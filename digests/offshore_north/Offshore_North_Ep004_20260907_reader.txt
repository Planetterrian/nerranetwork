Out in the fleet, The Ocean Race Atlantic sits at its midpoint after five days of a three-thousand-nautical-mile course.

The incident shows how a single equipment failure in heavy weather can erase hours or days of progress even when the hull itself stays intact.

Sixteen solo sailors started the Golden Globe Race from Les Sables d'Olonne on the sixth of September.

Sailorz reported the edition follows the original 1968–69 rules with no satellite navigation, no weather routing, and no outside support.

That brings us to Plain Sailing.

The physical demands on crews in a transatlantic race like The Ocean Race Atlantic come from constant motion and sleep disruption rather than any single storm.

When a low-pressure system passes, winds can jump from fifteen knots to over thirty knots within hours.

That forces repeated sail changes and reefing while the boat slams through short, steep waves.

On a sixty-foot IMOCA the motion is violent enough that simply moving from the cockpit to the galley or trying to prepare a freeze-dried meal becomes a full-body effort.

Crews often report going twenty-four hours or more without a proper hot meal or more than a few minutes of continuous rest.

The six-boat fleet in this edition met exactly those conditions on days four and five.

Sleep comes in short fragments because the boat must keep moving at high speed through the night.

Even with an autopilot holding course, someone must stay alert for sudden wind shifts or equipment strain, and the noise of water slamming against the hull makes deeper rest difficult.

Over five days these effects compound.

Reaction times slow, small errors in sail trim or routing add up, and the mental load of deciding whether to push harder or conserve the boat grows heavier.

The leading trio of Meilhat, Clapcich and Shiraishi used the low-pressure system as a turning point.

They sailed a route that placed them ahead of the worst weather while the rest of the fleet remained inside it.

Their decision to accept a slightly longer route for clearer air and better rest shows how these cumulative stresses shape strategy.

The gap of more than four hundred thirty miles that opened after five days is the direct result of those choices made under fatigue.

In ocean racing the boat that manages its crew's energy most effectively often gains the largest advantage once the weather settles.
