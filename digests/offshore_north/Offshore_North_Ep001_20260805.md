## What This Show Is
Offshore ocean racing is one of the great endurance sports on earth and is reported almost entirely in French. English-speaking fans can find results but rarely the meaning behind them. This show closes that gap with weekly context rather than literal translation.

Every four years a small number of people sail alone, without stopping and without assistance, around the world. No Canadian has ever finished that race. Scott Shawyer and Canada Ocean Racing are attempting to change that at the Vendée Globe that starts on 12 November 2028. The show follows that campaign week by week and uses it as a door into the wider offshore world.

Each episode covers three things: what Canada Ocean Racing reported and what it changes for the 2028 start; the most consequential offshore stories from the past seven days with their real implications; and one concept explained plainly so listeners can understand the sport rather than just its results. A short countdown closes every show.

Sources are named and linked every week. No hype is added, no play-by-play is given that will be stale by Monday, and nothing is stated that the sources do not support. Uncertainty is flagged when it exists. The audience is assumed to be enthusiastic but not expert, and is never treated as though those two things are the same.

### The Fleet
The Ocean Race Atlantic 2026 starts on 1 September with six IMOCA teams leaving New York for a 3,600-nautical-mile sprint to Lorient, France. Every crew must consist of two women, two men and one on-board reporter, making this the first 50/50 gender-parity crewed transatlantic race in offshore sailing. The event serves as the first full test of both the new boats and the parity rule ahead of the 2027 around-the-world edition.

Two new-generation IMOCAs are already sailing. Malizia 4 is the product of a three-team sistership collaboration between Team Malizia, TR Racing and Team Banque Populaire; it was designed by Antoine Koch with Finot-Conq and Gsea Design, is narrower and flatter than its predecessor, and required more than 85,000 hours of build time. Skipper Boris Herrmann described the first week of sailing as “love at first sail.”

DMG MORI Global One follows a different path. Designed by Guillaume Verdier and built by Multiplast, its hull resembles the central hull of a trimaran; the brief given to the designer by skipper Kojiro Shiraishi was “free mind, free ideas, no limits.” The boat is intended to foil earlier and slam less than conventional designs. ## Plain Sailing
An IMOCA is a 60-foot monohull built to a box rule that allows wide design freedom while enforcing safety and cost limits. The class is the only one currently used for the Vendée Globe solo non-stop round-the-world race.

Modern IMOCAs carry two large daggerboards that act as foils. When the boat heels, one board lowers and generates upward lift, reducing the amount of hull in the water. Less wetted surface means less drag, so the boat can sail faster in the same wind. The trade-off is that the boat becomes more sensitive to steering and wave impact; a poorly managed foil can slam hard enough to damage the structure or slow the boat more than a conventional keel would.

The newest boats add further variables. Malizia 4 is described as narrower and flatter than its predecessor, shifting how it balances in waves. DMG MORI Global One uses a central-hull shape intended to let the foils engage earlier. Both approaches still must survive the same Southern Ocean conditions that have broken previous generations of boats.

Designers balance several competing goals inside the same rule set. The boat must be light enough to fly on its foils yet strong enough to withstand repeated high-speed impacts with waves. It must be narrow enough to reduce drag yet wide enough to carry the righting moment needed when the foils are not fully lifting the hull. It must be stiff enough to keep its rig and foils working efficiently yet flexible enough to absorb shocks without breaking. These choices are made with the knowledge that every kilogram saved in one area must be paid for somewhere else, either in added structure or in reduced equipment.

The foil system itself introduces new variables. The boards must be strong enough to carry the entire weight of the boat when it is flying, yet they must also retract or be protected when the boat is sailing upwind or in heavy weather. The angle and shape of the foil determine how early lift begins and how much side-force it produces; a foil that generates too much lift too soon can make the boat unstable, while one that generates too little leaves the hull dragging and slow. Skippers therefore spend hundreds of hours testing different board settings and ride heights before a major race.

Wave impact adds another layer. When a foiling IMOCA drops off a wave, the sudden change in load can exceed the design limits of the structure if the boat is not perfectly trimmed. Earlier generations of boats suffered broken daggerboards and damaged hulls in the Southern Ocean; the new generation tries to reduce those loads by changing hull shape or by allowing the foils to engage at lower speeds. Whether those changes succeed will be tested first in the September transatlantic race and then, more severely, in the Southern Ocean during the 2027 around-the-world event.

The single sentence to repeat: an IMOCA sails fastest when its foils lift enough hull out of the water to cut drag without letting the boat become uncontrollable in the next wave.
