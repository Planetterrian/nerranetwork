## The Canadian Boat
As of the team’s most recent updates on 6–7 August 2026, no new position or movement details have been posted on the campaign site or YouTube channel.

The Toronto Star reports that Scott Shawyer has purchased a second-hand IMOCA 60 for the Canada Ocean Racing Vendée Globe 2028 campaign. The article frames the purchase as a concrete step toward becoming the first Canadian to finish the solo non-stop race. It notes the yacht cost roughly six million dollars and highlights the event’s history of claiming lives and breaking dreams. Shawyer is described as preparing anyway, drawing on his background in business and engineering rather than the traditional French offshore pathway.

This places a boat with documented ocean-racing results under Canadian ownership, shifting the campaign from planning to preparation with a hull already proven at the front of the fleet. The move supplies a concrete platform on which the team can now focus refit decisions, crew training and qualification planning for the 2028 start. ### The Fleet
Johnny Vincent’s Volvo 70 *Pace* took monohull line honours in the 50th anniversary RORC Round Britain and Ireland Race after eight days of racing that mixed high-speed sailing with complex navigation. The result marks the completion of a personal offshore project for the owner on a boat built for sustained ocean performance. The race combined raw speed requirements with demanding routing choices across varied coastal and open-water legs.

This shows that well-prepared older designs can still deliver outright wins in long-distance handicap events when routing and reliability are prioritised. A Volvo 70 remains competitive in modern fleets when the emphasis stays on consistent performance rather than the latest foil technology. Source: [sail-world.com](https://www.sail-world.com/news/298917/?source=rss)

Scuttlebutt Sailing News published an essay by offshore coach Stuart Greenfield arguing that almost every offshore sailor begins on someone else’s boat at someone else’s expense, and that this pattern has changed little since the 1970s. Greenfield notes he began racing offshore properly in 1974 and has now completed fourteen Fastnets. The piece calls for deliberate programmes to lower the cost barrier for the next generation of sailors.

Without such programmes, the pool of experienced offshore crew remains narrow, limiting the number of skippers who can realistically step up to IMOCA-level campaigns. The essay frames the issue as structural rather than individual, pointing to the hidden costs that have persisted across five decades. ## Plain Sailing
An IMOCA 60’s racing history travels with the boat even after ownership changes. EMIRA IV, the hull now sailed by Scott Shawyer, was built as *11th Hour Racing Team — Mālama*. It won The Ocean Race in 2023 under Charlie Enright, then finished third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe with Sébastien Simon despite a broken foil from early December onward. Simon also set a solo 24-hour speed record on the same boat. The vessel measures over eighteen metres, carries a roughly fifteen-foot keel, and has demonstrated sustained speeds above forty knots in open ocean conditions.

That record of results matters because the Vendée Globe demands a boat that can survive the Southern Ocean at high speed for weeks. A hull that has already completed a full lap with only one foil demonstrates structural margins and design choices that directly affect what a new skipper can attempt in 2028. The boat’s earlier success in The Ocean Race shows it can handle fleet racing with multiple crew changes and stopovers, while its Vendée performance under damage reveals how it behaves when pushed hard in the most remote stretches of the course.

When a campaign buys a boat with this pedigree, it acquires not just carbon and sails but a documented performance envelope. The new owner can study exactly how the boat behaved in 40-knot Southern Ocean conditions and in the North Atlantic, then decide which systems to keep, upgrade, or replace before the next start line. Previous campaigns have already solved many of the integration problems that arise when foils, rigs and electronics operate together at the edge of the design envelope. That institutional knowledge reduces the number of unknowns a new team must resolve during its own refit and training blocks.

The boat’s path also illustrates how IMOCA hulls move through the professional fleet. After its Ocean Race victory it passed to Simon as *Groupe Dubreuil*, then to Canada Ocean Racing in early 2025 and was renamed EMIRA IV. Each transfer carries forward the accumulated data on speed, durability and handling. A new skipper therefore inherits both the physical asset and the lessons already learned at the front of the fleet.
