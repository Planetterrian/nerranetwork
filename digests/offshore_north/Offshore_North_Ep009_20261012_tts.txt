The summer training block in Canada gave him time to learn the boat's systems, but an unexpected energy-systems issue on the return delivery and a very short qualification window complicated the final steps.

The post explains that getting EMIRA IV to Canada for a summer of training was a major project that gave Scott valuable time onboard to learn how the boat performs, understand her systems, and build muscle memory.

The plan was to return to the U K, complete the most important work, and then set off on the qualifying passage for November’s Route du Rhum, which was to be his first solo race on EMIRA IV.

On the attempt, seasickness hit fast while trying to get the computer systems working, and in the busy Channel waters he had to assess the risks and turn back.

Time for Plain Sailing.

An IMOCA 60 carries two very different foil systems.

Daggerboards are vertical blades that slide up and down to reduce leeway and add lift when the boat heels.

They work in a wide range of conditions and are simpler to manage.

True foils are L-shaped or T-shaped appendages that generate enough upward force to lift the entire hull clear of the water once the boat reaches a certain speed.

On a foiling IMOCA the transition happens around twelve to fifteen knots.

Above that the drag drops sharply and the boat can sustain 30-plus knots for hours.

The trade-off is that a foil boat is more sensitive to setup and more vulnerable if one foil is damaged, which is why some teams still choose daggerboards for their first seasons on the class.
