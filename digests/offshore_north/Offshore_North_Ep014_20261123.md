> **Swedish skipper Pär Nyman was dismasted late on November 22 in the Golden Globe Race and is now jury-rigging toward port.**

### The Fleet
Sixteen solo skippers began the 2026-27 Golden Globe Race on September 6 from Les Sables-d’Olonne in France. The event uses a retro format limited to 32- to 36-foot production boats designed before 1988 and sailed without modern electronics or outside assistance. Swedish skipper Pär Nyman, holding sixth place aboard the Rustler 36 Lazy Otter, lost his mast in heavy weather late on November 22 roughly 1,000 nautical miles southeast of Cape Town. Winds were reported from the west at 35 knots, shifting southwest with gusts to 50 knots and seas of four to five metres. Nyman cut the rig and mast free with no reported hull damage and signalled that he required no outside help. He plans to use three spinnaker poles as a jury rig and make northward progress when conditions allow, aiming first for latitudes near 30 degrees south where winds may become more favourable. Two route options are under consideration: at least 1,000 nautical miles northeast followed by roughly 1,500 nautical miles toward the African coast, or a direct passage of about 3,500 nautical miles to Perth in Western Australia. The dismasting leaves nine boats still racing from the original sixteen starters. Damien Guillou holds a 1,350-nautical-mile lead in first place and was making 7.2 knots toward an expected Hobart film-drop arrival around December 15. Seven other retirements have already occurred this edition, including Selim Yalcin on September 26, Pat Lawless on October 17, Louis Kerdelhue on November 4, Guy deBoer on November 7, Andrea Lodolo on November 7, and Mara Løvenskiold Kveseth on November 22. The first Golden Globe in 1968 saw nine starters and only one finisher, Sir Robin Knox-Johnston. A dismasting at this stage in a retro-class solo round-the-world race ends any realistic chance of finishing under the event’s strict no-modern-technology rules and forces a long, slow passage under jury rig with limited ability to make progress upwind.

The Ocean Race announced a long-term partnership that moves its operational headquarters and Atlantic base to Porto, Portugal, from 2028 through 2040. The agreement was launched at the Porto Historical Customs Building and involves Turismo de Portugal, Turismo do Porto e Norte de Portugal, and Porto City Council. Key elements include a new operational headquarters in a historic waterfront building near Parque da Cidade, a Festival of Sailing in 2028, starts for three editions of the round-the-world race from Porto in 2030, 2034 and 2038, at least six additional international ocean events across Portugal through 2040, and a new Ocean Immersive Museum exploring the ocean’s story, the race, its sailors, and Portugal’s maritime heritage. The partnership also supports the Racing for the Ocean sustainability programme with summits, science, education and ocean-literacy initiatives. Race Chairman Richard Brisius described the day as historic for both Porto and the event. Shifting the race’s main Atlantic hub changes the logistical and commercial centre of gravity for the event for more than a decade and gives Portugal a sustained platform for ocean-racing infrastructure.

The 105-foot Ultim trimaran Banque Populaire XI, which capsized in the mid-Atlantic during the Route du Rhum, has been righted in the Azores and is now under tow for the 1,300-nautical-mile return to its base in Lorient. The recovery removes an immediate safety hazard from the race area and returns the boat to its shore team for assessment. A major capsize and subsequent righting in the Ultim class highlights how quickly a single structural or handling failure can end a campaign even on the largest and most heavily supported boats.

### The Canadian Boat
As of 23 November 2026 the team’s YB tracker shows EMIRA IV at 50.47°N, 1.28°W. On 21 November Canada Ocean Racing posted that Scott Shawyer and Alan Roberts will sail the foiling IMOCA double-handed in the RORC Transatlantic Race starting 10 January 2027 from Lanzarote to Antigua. The post notes the boat’s potential for significantly higher downwind speeds than their previous IMOCA and frames the crossing as valuable open-ocean experience ahead of the Vendée Globe 2028. With fifty days until the start, the team is preparing for a very different kind of race. Scott and Alan previously set a new double-handed course record in 2023. This time they will race Emira IV, a foiling IMOCA capable of significantly higher speeds at certain points of sail. The RORC Transatlantic will give them their first opportunity to really test her performance over a long open-ocean passage. The fleet starts on 10 January 2027 and races approximately 3,000 nautical miles from Marina Lanzarote in the Canary Islands to Antigua in the Caribbean. The Royal Ocean Racing Club organises the race. It is a major fixture on the offshore calendar and attracts international teams chasing a fast Atlantic crossing. The challenge is a familiar one for Shawyer and Roberts, but there is plenty to work out. They will be studying sail choices, looking at how Emira IV performs in different wind conditions and trying to find the fastest route across. Weather and routing are a big part of that puzzle. Shawyer recently produced a six-part YouTube series analysing the 2026 Route du Rhum. The short videos cover the weather systems, routing options and tactical decisions facing the IMOCA skippers. Emira IV is currently in Gosport, UK, undergoing adjustments and repairs ahead of the crossing. The team will deliver her to Lanzarote in December for final preparations before the start. The priority is to finish safely and learn more about the boat. Conditions permitting, they also want to improve on their 2023 crossing time of 9 days, 12 hours and 26 minutes. The boat is close to 50 percent faster at some points of sail, so if they get good weather conditions they would like to beat their previous time.

### Plain Sailing
An IMOCA 60 is designed to be self-righting after a capsize. The keel carries several tonnes of ballast at the bottom of a long, weighted fin. When the boat is inverted that ballast sits above the centre of buoyancy and creates a strong righting moment that rolls the hull upright again. The rule exists because a solo skipper cannot leave the boat to perform an external recovery and must be able to continue racing after a knockdown or full inversion. In practice the system has allowed several Vendée Globe and Route du Rhum skippers to resume racing after capsizes that would have ended earlier generations of boats.
