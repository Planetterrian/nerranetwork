Every four years a handful of people set out alone to sail around the world without stopping or taking outside help, and one Canadian is working toward becoming the first to finish that race.

On the Canadian boat.

The team's YB tracker shows EMIRA IV alongside in Gosport, UK, as of 19 October 2026, and it records no movement outside the berth in the preceding thirty days.

On 8 October the team posted that Scott Shawyer will not start the Route du Rhum.

The post lays out an energy-systems failure on the delivery back from Canada that limited the boat's function, a compressed qualification window once the systems were restored, and seasickness that struck while Shawyer worked on the onboard computer in 25 knots upwind in the English Channel.

He turned back rather than continue in busy shipping lanes with incomplete alarms and navigation tools.

The energy issue had already forced an earlier return to port during the transatlantic delivery, and the subsequent repair and computer upgrade work left too little margin to complete and test every safety system before the qualification deadline.

Shawyer has spoken openly about his history with seasickness, and on this attempt the symptoms arrived quickly while he was focused on the computer in a high-traffic area where reliable radar, AIS, and collision-avoidance overlays are essential.

The decision to abort preserved the boat and the longer-term goal of a Vendée Globe campaign rather than risking a compromised start to the 2026 season.

That choice keeps the focus on the 2028 edition, where every completed Grade 2 solo race on EMIRA IV will count toward both the required qualifier and the Globe Series points table.

A 14 October YouTube short from the team shows the boat under sail in mostly dry conditions.

The short clip captures the boat moving in light air with the wing mast and foils visible, confirming that systems are operational again after the earlier faults.

The video adds no new position data beyond the tracker but demonstrates that the boat is ready for further work once the Route du Rhum window closes.

The choice to turn back in the Channel lines up with the same kind of three-in-the-morning risk call a pilot makes when systems and weather do not line up.

Now, the fleet.

Ferrari unveiled its 100-foot foiling monohull this week at a yard near Pisa.

The Verdier design is finished in yellow and matt dark grey, measures 30.5 metres long and up to 23.5 metres wide with foils deployed, and is described as the world's first energy self-sufficient foiling offshore monohull.

Construction took place inside two unremarkable sheds along the Navicelli Canal, with the team fitting out the space themselves after arriving four years earlier.

The hull features the signature bustle, a central trimaran-style structure running bow to stern that supports the rest of the hull, a concept Verdier previously applied to the AC75 and to recent IMOCAs such as DMG Mori Global One and Groupe Dubreuil.

More than twenty CFD iterations refined the final shape.

First sailing trials are set for the end of the first quarter of 2027.

The project required journalists to sign non-disclosure agreements and cover phone cameras before entry, underscoring the commercial sensitivity around a brand entering the offshore space at this scale.

That launch shows how far the push for self-sufficient, high-performance offshore designs has moved beyond the current IMOCA fleet.

The Vendée Globe Foundation opened its 2027 call for projects on 16 October.

Associations and research bodies have until 31 October to apply for up to 50,000 euros a year to support ocean knowledge, protection or restoration work.

The fund can support a project for up to three years and will not exceed 35 percent of total project cost, arriving only after other financing is secured.

Past grants have funded citizen-science algae surveys along the French coast with BioLit and Planète Mer, an algae-focused sailing expedition by Blutopia, and environmental DNA sampling in New Zealand waters by Citizens of the Sea.

The call is open to non-profit structures in France and internationally, covering research, innovation, medical, and restoration topics.

The fund ties the race's visibility directly to ongoing ocean research rather than one-off events.

Time for Plain Sailing.

An IMOCA skipper at sea never gets a full night's sleep.

The boat is moving at highway speeds through the dark, and the only way to keep it safe is to break rest into short fragments, usually around twenty minutes at a time.

The autopilot handles most of the steering, but the skipper still has to wake, check the instruments, decide whether the route still makes sense, and then try to sleep again before the next decision point arrives.

That pattern repeats for weeks.

It is why every system on board — power, navigation, collision avoidance — has to work without constant human oversight, and why a single computer fault or power glitch can force a hard choice about whether to keep going.

The twenty-minute cycle is the real limit on how long one person can stay in command of a sixty-foot boat alone.
