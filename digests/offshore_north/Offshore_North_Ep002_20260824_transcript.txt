The Golden Globe race and the Vende Globe both send one person alone around the world without stopping
yet they test entirely different skills. The Golden Globe uses only boats designed before 1988
and forbids GPS, satellite phones, or weather routing computers. So skippers navigate with a
sextant and paper charts the way the original 1968 competitors did. The Vende Globe by contrast
is sailed in current generation foiling IM-OCA's that can exceed 40 knots and carry the full sweet
of modern satellite systems. Because the boats are slower and the information poorer,
a Golden Globe skipper's daily decisions rest on what can be seen from the deck and what the
and paper weather maps suggest. That constraint turns every cloud build up and wind shift into a
high stakes calculation made without the safety net of real-time forecasts or outside assistance.
A skipper may spend weeks without speaking to another human, rationing food, water, and energy
while the boat moves at perhaps one third the speed of an IM-OCA. The absence of electronic
autopilots and routing software also means the sailor must steer and trim continuously during
daylight hours and accept whatever weather arrives rather than threading between systems.
The result is a race whose difficulty lies less in raw speed than in sustained self-reliance over
roughly eight months at sea. A listener can picture it as the difference between driving a modern rally
car with live telemetry and driving a 1960s vehicle across the same continents using only a road
atlas and a compass. The Golden Globe therefore measures endurance of isolation and judgment under
deliberately limited tools while the Vende Globe measures the ability to extract maximum performance
from the most advanced offshore machines ever built. Both formats reward the same core trait,
making correct decisions when no one else can help, but they remove different variables to isolate
that trait. One removes technology, the other removes company. A listener who finishes this explanation
can state that the Golden Globe's eight-month duration and pre-1988 boats create a test of pure
