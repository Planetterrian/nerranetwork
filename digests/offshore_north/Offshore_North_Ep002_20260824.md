# Offshore North
**Week ending:** August 24, 2026
> **Sixteen solo skippers have gathered in Les Sables-d’Olonne to begin final preparations for the 2026 Golden Globe Race, the only round-the-world event sailed in 1960s-era boats without modern electronics or outside assistance.**

## The Canadian Boat
As of the campaign’s most recent public update on 17 August 2026, EMIRA IV’s exact position after its 8–9 August departure from Collingwood remains unconfirmed in this week’s sources.

Canada Ocean Racing’s website, news section and YouTube channel were each checked; the newest item on all three is dated 17 August 2026 and concerns a profile of the campaign’s motivation.

**Standing item:** EMIRA IV is the IMOCA 60 previously raced as 11th Hour Racing Team — Mālama that won The Ocean Race 2023 under Charlie Enright. The same hull was later campaigned by Sébastien Simon as Groupe Dubreuil and finished third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe despite a broken foil reported in early December. Simon also set a solo 24-hour speed record aboard the boat before it was handed over to Scott Shawyer in early 2025 and renamed. Under Canada Ocean Racing the boat placed sixth in The Ocean Race Europe 2025 and was forced to abandon the 2025 Rolex Fastnet Race. This pedigree means every future solo mile Shawyer sails on EMIRA IV both accumulates Globe Series points and satisfies the published Grade 2 qualifier requirement for the 2028 Vendée Globe. ### The Fleet
Sixteen solo skippers are now assembled in Les Sables-d’Olonne two weeks before the start of the McIntyre Golden Globe Race 2026. The event requires each entrant to sail a production boat built before 1988, using only the navigation and safety equipment available in the original 1968–69 Golden Globe. After a final prologue won by Damien Guillou, the fleet enters its last preparation phase for a non-stop, unassisted circumnavigation that will last roughly eight months. Skippers must rely on sextant, paper charts and barometer readings with no satellite weather routing or outside assistance permitted. The race returns the sport to the conditions faced by the nine original competitors who departed Plymouth in 1968. Source: [courseaularge.com](https://www.courseaularge.com/golden-globe-race-16-skippers-aux-sables-dolonne-avant-le-grand-depart.html)

Teams are gathering in Newport, Rhode Island, ahead of The Ocean Race Atlantic. The event forms the next stage of The Ocean Race’s two-year global calendar and will be sailed by crews that include the DMG MORI Sailing Team, whose campaign was profiled this week in an official video release. The Ocean Race Atlantic continues the series’ emphasis on crewed offshore racing across the North Atlantic, providing competitive miles and media exposure for teams preparing longer campaigns. Newport’s selection as a stopover leverages the port’s established infrastructure for high-performance yacht preparation and its history of hosting major offshore events. Source: [theoceanrace.com](https://www.theoceanrace.com/en/video/atlantic/15880_Meet-DMG-MORI-Sailing-Team)

Alexia Barrier’s IDEC Sport team has begun its Route du Rhum 2026 preparation programme. The solo transatlantic race starts 1 November from Saint-Malo; Barrier’s campaign is one of the early public efforts to secure a berth in the IMOCA class for the 13th edition of the event. The team’s announcement frames the work as the formal beginning of an adventure that will see the boat and skipper tested across the Atlantic in the largest mixed-class offshore start in the sport. Route du Rhum participation also offers Barrier an opportunity to gain the solo offshore experience required for future qualification pathways. ## Plain Sailing
The Golden Globe Race and the Vendée Globe both send one person alone around the world without stopping, yet they test entirely different skills. The Golden Globe uses only boats designed before 1988 and forbids GPS, satellite phones, or weather-routing computers; skippers must navigate with a sextant and paper charts the way the original 1968 competitors did. The Vendée Globe, by contrast, is sailed in current-generation foiling IMOCAs that can exceed 40 knots and carry the full suite of modern satellite systems. 

Because the boats are slower and the information poorer, a Golden Globe skipper’s daily decisions rest on what can be seen from the deck and what the barometer and paper weather maps suggest. That constraint turns every cloud build-up and wind shift into a high-stakes calculation made without the safety net of real-time forecasts or outside assistance. A skipper may spend weeks without speaking to another human, rationing food, water and energy while the boat moves at perhaps one-third the speed of an IMOCA. The absence of electronic autopilots and routing software also means the sailor must steer and trim continuously during daylight hours and accept whatever weather arrives rather than threading between systems. 

The result is a race whose difficulty lies less in raw speed than in sustained self-reliance over roughly eight months at sea. A listener can picture it as the difference between driving a modern rally car with live telemetry and driving a 1960s vehicle across the same continents using only a road atlas and a compass. The Golden Globe therefore measures endurance of isolation and judgment under deliberately limited tools, while the Vendée Globe measures the ability to extract maximum performance from the most advanced offshore machines ever built. Both formats reward the same core trait—making correct decisions when no one else can help—but they remove different variables to isolate that trait. One removes technology; the other removes company. A listener who finishes this explanation can state that the Golden Globe’s eight-month duration and pre-1988 boats create a test of pure self-reliance that modern IMOCA racing deliberately replaces with speed and connectivity.
