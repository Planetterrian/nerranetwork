now, the fleet

The Ocean Race Atlantic 2026 starts 1 September with six IMOCA entries sailing three thousand six hundred nautical miles from New York to Lorient.

Every crew must include two women, two men and one on-board reporter.

That creates the first 50/50 gender-parity offshore transatlantic race.

The event serves as the main pre-event test before The Ocean Race round-the-world course begins in January 2027.

Two new-generation IMOCAs already sailing will use the race as their first serious ocean test.

Malizia 4 came out of a three-team sistership collaboration between Team Malizia, TR Racing and Team Banque Populaire.

It was designed by Antoine Koch with Finot-Conq and Gsea Design.

The boat is narrower and flatter than its predecessor and carries more than eighty-five thousand hours of build time.

Skipper Boris Herrmann called the first week of sailing on the boat love at first sail.

DMG MORI Global One follows a separate design route by Guillaume Verdier and was built by Multiplast.

Its hull resembles the central hull of a trimaran and is intended to foil earlier while reducing slamming.

This race therefore supplies the first direct comparison between the two new boats under identical 50/50 crew rules and identical ocean conditions before the longer 2027 campaign.

time for Plain Sailing

Gender parity rules require mixed crews on offshore boats.

In The Ocean Race Atlantic 2026 the rule is explicit: two women, two men and one on-board reporter on every entry.

The requirement is written into the notice of race and applies to every team that wants to start.

The rule changes how campaigns select sailors and how they train.

A skipper can no longer fill a five-person watch system with the four most experienced offshore sailors available.

At least two of those sailors must be women who meet the same physical and skill thresholds as their male teammates.

That constraint affects hiring timelines, training blocks and even boat layout, because watch systems and berthing must now accommodate the mandated balance rather than the fastest available combination.

The practical effect is that teams must develop a wider bench of qualified offshore sailors earlier in the campaign cycle.

For the two new IMOCAs already launched, the rule also means their first serious ocean miles will be sailed by crews that were assembled under the parity constraint rather than under an open selection process.

The single sentence a listener could repeat is this: the rule forces every team to solve the same talent-pool problem at the same time.

An IMOCA is a sixty-foot monohull built to a strict class rule that permits foils, water ballast and other performance features while capping overall length, beam and other dimensions.

The class rule itself does not dictate crew composition.

That decision belongs to each race organiser.

When an organiser adds a gender-parity clause, the constraint travels with the entry list rather than with the boat design.

Teams therefore face two parallel problems.

They must still optimise the boat for solo or short-handed sailing in later events, yet they must also prove the same boat works with a five-person mixed crew on the Atlantic course.

The on-board reporter counts toward the total but does not sail the boat, so the four sailing positions must still satisfy the two-plus-two split.

This creates a narrow window in which teams can test new equipment and new crew combinations simultaneously.

Because the race starts on 1 September, any shortfall in qualified female offshore sailors must be solved before that date.

There is no later qualifying event that can substitute for the experience these crews will gain.

The same calendar pressure applies to every entry.

That is why the rule is described as the first 50/50 gender-parity crewed transatlantic race in offshore sailing.
