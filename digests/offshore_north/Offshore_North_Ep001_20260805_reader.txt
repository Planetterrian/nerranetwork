Time for Plain Sailing.

An IMOCA is a 60-foot monohull built to a box rule that allows wide design freedom while enforcing safety and cost limits.

The class is the only one currently used for the Vendée Globe solo non-stop round-the-world race.

Modern IMOCAs carry two large daggerboards that act as foils.

When the boat heels, one board lowers and generates upward lift, reducing the amount of hull in the water.

Less wetted surface means less drag, so the boat can sail faster in the same wind.

The trade-off is that the boat becomes more sensitive to steering and wave impact.

A poorly managed foil can slam hard enough to damage the structure or slow the boat more than a conventional keel would.

The newest boats add further variables.

Malizia 4 is described as narrower and flatter than its predecessor, shifting how it balances in waves.

DMG MORI Global One uses a central-hull shape intended to let the foils engage earlier.

Both approaches still must survive the same Southern Ocean conditions that have broken previous generations of boats.

Designers balance several competing goals inside the same rule set.

The boat must be light enough to fly on its foils yet strong enough to withstand repeated high-speed impacts with waves.

It must be narrow enough to reduce drag yet wide enough to carry the righting moment needed when the foils are not fully lifting the hull.

It must be stiff enough to keep its rig and foils working efficiently yet flexible enough to absorb shocks without breaking.

These choices are made with the knowledge that every kilogram saved in one area must be paid for somewhere else, either in added structure or in reduced equipment.

The foil system itself introduces new variables.

The boards must be strong enough to carry the entire weight of the boat when it is flying, yet they must also retract or be protected when the boat is sailing upwind or in heavy weather.

The angle and shape of the foil determine how early lift begins and how much side-force it produces.

A foil that generates too much lift too soon can make the boat unstable, while one that generates too little leaves the hull dragging and slow.

Skippers therefore spend hundreds of hours testing different board settings and ride heights before a major race.

Wave impact adds another layer.

When a foiling IMOCA drops off a wave, the sudden change in load can exceed the design limits of the structure if the boat is not perfectly trimmed.

Earlier generations of boats suffered broken daggerboards and damaged hulls in the Southern Ocean.

The new generation tries to reduce those loads by changing hull shape or by allowing the foils to engage at lower speeds.

Whether those changes succeed will be tested first in the September transatlantic race and then, more severely, in the Southern Ocean during the 2027 around-the-world event.

An IMOCA sails fastest when its foils lift enough hull out of the water to cut drag without letting the boat become uncontrollable in the next wave.
