That brings us to Plain Sailing.

The Ocean Race Atlantic requires every boat to carry exactly two women and two men.

That 50/50 rule is not a suggestion; it is written into the notice of race and checked at the dock before the start.

In practice it forces teams to build four-person watches that balance strength, weight, technical skill and decision-making across gender lines on a boat that still demands the same physical loads as any IMOCA—grinding winches, handling 40-knot squalls, and executing sail changes while the hull is flying on one or two foils.

Because the race is crewed rather than solo, the skipper’s role shifts from sole decision-maker to coordinator who must integrate four sets of observations and fatigue patterns.

On a solo boat the skipper alone reads the weather, chooses the sail plan, and decides when to push or conserve; here those tasks are distributed, which changes both the speed of decisions and the risk of conflicting inputs at three in the morning.

The format therefore tests both the human system and the technical system.

Teams such as the Clapcich–Meilhat pairing and the all-Kiwi crew under Colman will reveal whether national or cross-border combinations handle that coordination differently.

The first edition therefore functions as a live experiment: results will show whether the gender-balance rule produces measurably different speeds, safety records or breakage rates compared with the all-male or open crews that dominate most IMOCA events.
