Modern Iomika's carry two large dagger boards that act as foils, when the boat heals, one board lowers and generates upward lift, reducing the amount of hull in the water.
Less wetted surface means less drag so the boat can sail faster in the same wind. The trade-off is that the boat becomes more sensitive to steering and wave impact.

Designers balance several competing goals inside the same rule set. The boat must be laid enough to fly on its foils yet strong enough to withstand repeated high speed impacts with waves.
It must be narrow enough to reduce drag yet wide enough to carry the riding moment needed when the foils are not fully lifting the hull.
It must be stiff enough to keep its rig and foils working efficiently, yet flexible enough to absorb shocks without breaking.
These choices are made with the knowledge that every kilogram saved in one area must be paid for somewhere else, either in added structure or in reduced equipment.
The foil system itself introduces new variables. The boards must be strong enough to carry the entire weight of the boat when it is flying, yet they must also retract or be protected when the boat is sailing upwind or in heavy weather.
The angle and shape of the foil determine how early lift begins and how much side force it produces. A foil that generates too much lift too soon can make the boat unstable, while one that generates too little leaves the hull dragging and slow.
Skippers therefore spend hundreds of hours testing different board settings and ride heights before a major race. Wave impact adds another layer.
When a foiling iomica drops off a wave, the sudden change in load can exceed the design limits of the structure if the boat is not perfectly trimmed.
Earlier generations of boats suffered broken dagger boards and damaged hulls in the southern ocean. The new generation tries to reduce those loads by changing hull shape or by allowing the foils to engage at lower speeds.
Whether those changes succeed will be tested first in the September transatlantic race and then more severely in the southern ocean during the 2027 around the world event.
