In 1980, Emancio Ortega opened the first Zara store in Acaruña, Spain.
His goal was straightforward.
Move a new dress design from sketch to hanger in weeks rather than months.
Shoppers who had once waited for seasonal collections could suddenly buy copies of Paris
or Milan looks at prices that fit a factory wage.
The system worked so well that within two decades the model had spread across Europe and beyond.
Yet the same speed that delivered choice also locked the industry into constant overproduction
and disposal. Zara and H&M set out to make runway fashion affordable for ordinary shoppers.
They created an industry that now produces 92 million tons of textile waste a year,
while emitting 8-10% of global greenhouse gases.
Emancio Ortega came from modest circumstances in Acaruña.
He and the early teams at Indytex, along with executives at H&M in Sweden,
were responding to a clear post-war reality.
Most clothing remained expensive relative to income and slow to reflect changing tastes.
Department store cycles still ran on six-month lead times.
That left working families with limited variety and little ability to refresh wardrobes without
significant cost. By shortening design to store timelines through local production clusters and
rapid feedback from store managers, the companies believed they could deliver dignity and self-expression
to a broader public. The approach aligned with the era's broader faith in flexible manufacturing
and consumer access. Those same ideas had already lifted living standards in food and electronics.
Ortega saw affordable fashion as a practical extension of that progress.
Zara opened its second store in 1975 and began refining a quick response system in the early 1980s.
That system moved production closer to its Spanish distribution centers.
H&M scaled a similar model across northern Europe during the same decade.
It sourced from lower cost factories as trade rules eased. By the mid-1990s,
both firms were reporting double-digit annual sales growth. Zara reached 1000 stores worldwide
by 2004. Store managers sent daily sales data that factories turned into new garments in as
little as three weeks. Early observers in business journals praised the reduction in unsolved
inventory and the widening of style access. A few labor economists noted that compressed timelines
would eventually push suppliers toward lower wage regions. The dominant narrative at the time
centered on efficiency and consumer benefit. The same just-in-time logic that cut lead times also
rewarded volume. Each new collection had to be produced quickly and cheaply. Orders therefore
shifted to factories in Bangladesh, Vietnam and Cambodia. Wages there often fell below local
living standards. Because styles changed weekly, garments were designed with shorter physical
lifespans. Polyester blends that were inexpensive to knit became dominant. Those blends later proved
difficult to recycle at scale. Consumers responded to low prices by buying more and discarding sooner.
That behavior generated the 92 million tons of annual textile waste now tracked by the Ellen
MacArthur Foundation and national environmental agencies. The emissions total of 8-10% of global
greenhouse gases stems directly from this loop. Energy-intensive fiber production, long haul shipping
of short-lived items, and methane from landfill decomposition all play a part. Second order effects
appeared in water tables around die houses. They also appeared in the bargaining power of suppliers
locked into contracts that penalized delays. Third order effects emerged as fast fashion habits
normalized, disposability across income levels. Slower, higher quality alternatives began to appear
economically irrational to many households. By the late 2010s, brands began publishing sustainability
reports and piloting take-back programs. The European Union introduced its 2022 strategy for sustainable
and circular textiles. That strategy aimed at mandatory recycled content and extended producer
responsibility. Some factories improved safety after the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse. Overall production
volumes continued to rise with the arrival of sheen's on-demand model. Attempts to slow the cycle
through resale platforms and rental services have grown. They have not yet offset the underlying
incentive to manufacture at ever lower unit costs. Current estimates suggest global fiber
production will still increase through 2030 unless binding limits on virgin polyester are adopted.
When speed and price become the primary metrics of success, systems naturally optimize for throughput
rather than durability or material recovery. Incentive structures that reward rapid turnover
will locate the cheapest available labor and the least durable inputs unless those choices
carry explicit costs. The fast fashion story therefore invites anyone designing access-oriented systems
to model what happens when the marginal unit becomes almost free. Whether the domain is housing,
food delivery, or digital goods, the same dynamic appears. What second-order behaviors would your
own organization's efficiency targets quietly encourage if left unexamined? That's the case for today.
