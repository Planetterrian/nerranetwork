> **Over 75,000 ferrets released across New Zealand to control rabbits instead reduced native ground-nesting birds.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1881 the Rabbit Nuisance Act gave ferrets official protection as natural enemies of the rabbit and empowered inspectors to enforce control on landowners. Pastoral runs in the southern South Island had already seen wool clip and lambing rates fall sharply from rabbit damage by the mid-1870s. This was designed to cut rabbit numbers on grazing land. Instead, it caused measurable declines in kiwi, weka, blue duck and other endemic species.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Pastoralist Cuthbert Cowan brought the first documented private shipment of ferrets to his Southland property in 1876 after rabbits had already begun to reduce sheep numbers on his runs. Rabbits had arrived with whalers and early settlers in the 1820s through 1850s and multiplied rapidly once pastoral farming expanded. By the early 1870s burrowing and pasture loss were cutting wool production and land values across Otago and Southland. Government rental income from affected runs dropped from £2,288 to £619 by 1881. Superintending Rabbit Inspector Benjamin P. Bayly and local runholders therefore turned to ferrets as a cheaper alternative to repeated poisoning and trapping campaigns. The 1881 Rabbit Nuisance Act formalised this approach by shielding mustelids from harm and directing inspectors to promote their use. At the time the decision rested on the observation that ferrets killed rabbits efficiently in Britain and on the urgent need to protect the colony’s dominant export income. Runholders like Cowan had already demonstrated on their own land that ferrets could be bred locally and released without waiting for slow and expensive imports from England, which often arrived weakened after months at sea. The arithmetic looked straightforward: each ferret released could in principle remove dozens of rabbits per season while avoiding the recurring labour costs of human trappers who had to cover thousands of hectares of open tussock. Because the 1881 Act also imposed fines of £10 on anyone who killed a protected mustelid, landowners had a legal incentive to tolerate the new arrivals rather than treat them as another pest. Officials therefore viewed the programme as an extension of familiar British game-keeping practice rather than an untested experiment.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The Department of Agriculture, formed in 1892, placed rabbit control under its own inspectors and spent roughly a quarter of its 1895 budget on the work. Government contracts purchased 21,760 ferrets in 1887–88 alone, while Otago stations recorded more than 7,000 releases in 1890. Amuri Rabbit Board liberated 1,000 ferrets plus stoats, weasels and cats in 1888–89. Private breeding stations supplied additional animals at up to ten shillings each for release on Crown pastoral leases. Historian R. M. Burdon later estimated total ferret liberations reached upward of 75,000 between 1870 and 1920. Early reports noted local drops in rabbit numbers on some runs, which encouraged further shipments even as stoats and weasels were added in at least 25 separate consignments between 1883 and 1892. Inspectors tracked the numbers released per station and recorded survival rates on the open plains, where cover was scarce compared with English hedgerows; they responded by increasing the scale of releases rather than questioning the method. When initial ferret stocks proved insufficient, local breeding colonies were established on several South Island runs so that young animals could be produced on site and released the same season, cutting both transport losses and costs. The same records show that stoats and weasels were added once ferret numbers alone appeared inadequate to match rabbit breeding on the vast leasehold blocks.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Ferrets proved less specialised than hoped and readily took ground-nesting birds whose populations were already thinned by ship rats and cats. University of Waikato researcher Carolyn King’s review of 1880s newspapers and committee evidence shows that mustelids preyed on kiwi, weka and blue duck chicks as readily as on rabbits. The same 1884 Joint Committee testimony that recorded Cowan’s 26,000-rabbit kill also documented continued pasture damage once rabbit numbers rebounded. Because ferrets could not keep pace with rabbit breeding rates on open tussock country, landowners continued to press for more releases while native bird populations declined without similar public notice. At least 7,838 stoats and weasels arrived in the same period, adding further predation pressure. By 1900 the combined mustelid population was established across the South Island, contributing to the mainland extinction of kākāpō. Rabbit boards formed after 1947 still inherited a landscape where the original biological-control programme had failed to prevent either the target pest or the non-target losses. The causal sequence ran from the 1881 legal protection of ferrets, through mass releases that exceeded 75,000 animals, to the discovery that the predators’ diet was broader than the single pest they were meant to suppress. On the open Canterbury and Otago plains, rabbits could retreat into burrows or disperse across hundreds of hectares, while ground-nesting birds had no such refuge once a ferret located a nest. The 1881 Act’s protection clause prevented any systematic removal of the mustelids even after early signs of bird losses appeared in station diaries. Because the inspectors’ performance was measured by rabbit counts on pastoral leases rather than by counts of surviving native species, the broader ecological cost remained outside the formal accounting.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Official government purchases of ferrets for release ended in 1889, though Rabbit Boards and private landowners continued liberations into the twentieth century. Later legislation shifted emphasis to organised poisoning and, after 1989, to regional councils that inherited both rabbit and predator problems. Ferrets remain widespread and continue to limit recovery of several endemic birds. The Department of Conservation lists them among the introduced predators whose presence compounds the effects of earlier rat and cat introductions. No subsequent programme has fully reversed the combined impacts on ground-nesting species. The shift to poisoning reduced some rabbit pressure but left the established mustelid populations untouched, so the secondary predation on birds persisted even as rabbit boards were later disbanded.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Benjamin Bayly’s inspectors protected and released ferrets under the 1881 Act, the rule rewarded any animal that killed rabbits on pastoral leases without measuring effects on other species. Later Rabbit Board funding formulas continued to reward rapid visible kills over long-term ecosystem accounting. Decision-makers today can test whether a proposed control agent’s diet and habitat range have been measured against all resident species before large-scale release. The same test applies when modern agencies consider new biological agents for any single pest.
