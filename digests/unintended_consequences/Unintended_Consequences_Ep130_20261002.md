> **Europe's diesel tax breaks cut CO2 but filled city streets with nitrogen dioxide instead.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the early 2000s, drivers across Western Europe found diesel cars suddenly cheaper to tax and fuel than their petrol equivalents. Governments had designed the incentives to lower carbon dioxide output from the transport sector. Instead the policy produced higher roadside levels of nitrogen dioxide that breached legal limits in most large cities.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
European policymakers in the late 1990s faced rising concern over greenhouse-gas emissions from road transport. They viewed diesel engines as a ready technical solution because those engines delivered more kilometres per litre of fuel and therefore emitted less CO2 per kilometre than comparable petrol engines. The arithmetic was straightforward: a vehicle that travels the same distance on less fuel releases fewer carbon atoms into the atmosphere, directly addressing the dominant climate metric of the era. The United Kingdom’s 2002 company-car tax reform formalised this logic by tying the tax rate directly to official CO2 grams per kilometre figures. At the time, regulators treated the lower CO2 number as the decisive environmental metric and did not assign equal weight to other tailpipe pollutants. The approach aligned with the prevailing climate-policy priority of the period and appeared technically straightforward to implement through existing fuel-duty and vehicle-tax schedules.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Most Western European countries reduced excise duty on diesel relative to petrol and adjusted annual circulation taxes to favour diesel models. In the United Kingdom the 2002 company-car tax change accelerated the shift among business fleets, which in turn influenced the wider used-car market. Early data showed the intended result: diesel’s share of new-car registrations in Western Europe climbed from under one-quarter to more than half by 2011. Proponents pointed to measurable reductions in fleet-average CO2 and welcomed the contribution to national climate targets. A few air-quality specialists noted that diesel combustion produced substantially more nitrogen oxides and particulate matter, yet these warnings received limited policy attention while the CO2 metric remained the dominant target. Because the tax schedules operated through annual fiscal adjustments rather than technology mandates, the fleet composition changed gradually but steadily as older petrol cars were replaced.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Diesel engines emit several times more nitrogen oxides under real-world driving conditions than the laboratory test cycles used for type approval. Because the tax incentives rapidly increased the number of diesel vehicles on the road, total NOx output from passenger cars rose even as CO2 declined. Consider the arithmetic: when diesel’s share moved from roughly 25 percent to over 50 percent, the proportion of higher-NOx vehicles in the fleet approximately doubled. Each additional diesel car replaced a lower-NOx petrol car, so the aggregate emissions of nitrogen oxides increased even though individual vehicles met the CO2-based tax criteria. Roadside monitoring stations in London, Paris, Berlin and other major cities began recording persistent breaches of the European Union nitrogen dioxide limit values. The 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal later revealed that many diesel cars had been equipped with software that reduced NOx only during official tests, confirming that real-world emissions were often several times higher than certified figures. The resulting pollution contributed to respiratory health burdens in densely populated urban corridors. Cities responded with low-emission zones and, later, diesel scrappage schemes, yet the fleet composition created by more than a decade of favourable taxation could not be altered overnight. Second-order effects included accelerated turnover of older diesel vehicles into secondary markets in Eastern Europe and pressure on public budgets to fund new mitigation infrastructure. Third-order effects appeared in municipal health spending and in the uneven distribution of air-quality improvements across neighbourhoods.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the mid-2010s several of the same governments that had introduced the tax advantages began reversing course through higher diesel taxes, city-centre bans and purchase incentives for petrol and electric alternatives. Scrappage programmes offered payments to owners willing to retire older diesel cars, while low-emission zones restricted access for higher-polluting models. These corrective measures themselves generated new distributional effects, disproportionately affecting lower-income households that had purchased the once-subsidised diesel vehicles. Air-quality data in some cities improved gradually after the fleet began to turn over, yet legacy effects on public health and municipal spending persisted. The episode illustrated how a single-metric incentive can embed a technology deeply before secondary pollutants become visible in regulatory statistics.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Incentive structures that optimise for one measurable outcome tend to shift unmeasured burdens elsewhere in the system. When regulators anchor policy on a narrow proxy such as CO2 grams per kilometre, they create strong economic signals that later prove difficult to unwind once capital stock has adjusted. Complex technical systems therefore reward decision-makers who examine the full set of pollutants and real-world operating conditions rather than laboratory test values alone. The same pattern appears today whenever subsidies or taxes are tied to a single performance number without parallel safeguards for other externalities.
