Today's profile, a policy that did the opposite of what its designers intended.
In 1995, Jerry DeWayne Williams took a slice of pepperoni pizza from a group of children
at a Rodondo Beach restaurant. He received a sentence of 25 years to life under California's new
Three Strikes Statute. The law had been written to incapacitate repeat violent offenders after high
profile murders. Yet Williams' prior convictions were for robbery, not homicide or rape.
Within a few years, the same statute produced a 50-to-life term for Leandro Andrade after he
shoplifted nine videotapes from two K-Mart stores in Ontario, California. The modern Three Strikes
Movement began in Washington State in 1993, with initiative 593. It was drafted after the
murder of Diane Balaciats by a repeat offender on work release. California followed in 1994
with Proposition 184. That measure was placed on the ballot after the abduction and killing of 12-year-old
polyclass by Richard Allen Davis, who had an extensive prior record. Sponsors argued that a small
number of habitual offenders committed a disproportionate share of serious crimes. They believed
mandatory long sentences would both incapacitate those individuals and deter others.
At the time, FBI data showed violent crime rates still near their early 1990s peak,
and public fear was intense. Polls indicated overwhelming support for any measure labeled
tough on crime. Legislators and voters therefore treated the policy as a straightforward
application of selective incapacitation theory that criminologists had discussed since the 1970s.
Washington's law took effect in December 1993. California has passed with 72% of the vote in
November 1994 and applied retroactively to existing felony convictions. Prosecutors in both states
gained wide discretion over which prior felonies counted as strikes. California's version defined
a strike as any felony rather than only serious or violent ones. Early evaluations in 1995 and 1996
reported drops in felony filings for individuals already labeled two strike defendants.
Proponents cited those drops as evidence of deterrence. Skeptics at the time, including some district
attorneys and the California Legislative Analysts Office, warned that the broad definition of strikes
would sweep in property and drug cases once offenders accumulated three felonies of any kind.
By 2002, California's prison population had roughly doubled from its 1994 level,
reaching more than 160,000 inmates. Three strike sentences accounted for a substantial share
of the growth. Because the law treated many non-violent felonies as third strikes,
an estimated 65% of California three strikes prisoners were serving time for property, drug,
or other non-violent offenses. The pizza case of Jerry DeWayne Williams and the videotape case of
Leandro Andrade became national symbols. Andrade's sentence was upheld by the US Supreme Court in 2003.
The causal mechanism was mechanical. Once two prior strikes existed, any new felony, no matter
how minor, triggered a sentence of 25 years to life with limited good-time credits.
This removed ordinary plea bargaining leverage for low-level cases and produced long sentences for
conduct that previously drew county jail time or short prison terms. Second order effects included
sharply higher corrections budgets, overcrowding triggered federal court intervention in 2009.
Prison beds were diverted away from the very violent offenders the law had targeted.
County prosecutors also adapted by filing more cases as felonies to create future strikes
further widening the net. By 2010, voter and legislative concern over costs and fairness produced
Proposition 36. Voters approved it in November 2012 by a 69% margin.
The measure narrowed third strike eligibility to serious or violent felonies and allowed
resentencing for some inmates already serving life terms. Roughly 2,000 people received reduced
sentences after the reform. Thousands more remained incarcerated under the original statute.
The rollback itself generated new litigation over retroactivity and eligibility criteria.
California's prison population later declined. The state still operates under federal
population caps first imposed during the three strikes era.
Broad mandatory sentencing rules interact with existing plea practices and charging discretion
in ways that are difficult to predict from the statute's text alone.
When a single additional conviction can multiply sentence length by an order of magnitude,
even modest changes in what counts as a felony produce large shifts in incarceration volume.
Modern risk assessment tools and diversion programs attempt to target the same goal of selective
incapacitation with narrower eligibility criteria. The three strikes experience suggests those
tools must be evaluated against actual filing and plea data rather than intended categories.
How might today's proposals for automatic sentence enhancements for repeat offenses fair
under similar real world charging dynamics? That's unintended consequences for today.
