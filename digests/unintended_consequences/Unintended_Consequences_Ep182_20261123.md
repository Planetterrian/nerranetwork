> **India's 2016 demonetisation removed 86 percent of currency notes overnight, yet 99.3 percent returned to banks by June 2018.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On 8 November 2016 Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced that 500- and 1,000-rupee notes would cease to be legal tender at midnight. The Reserve Bank of India later recorded that 99.3 percent of those notes, worth 15,310.73 billion rupees out of 15,417.93 billion in circulation, had been deposited by 30 June 2018. This was designed to trap unaccounted cash outside the banking system; instead nearly all of it re-entered.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Prime Minister Narendra Modi made the decision after months of internal discussion. He had seen persistent complaints about counterfeit high-value notes and the use of cash to hide income from tax authorities. On the evening of 8 November he told the nation that the two denominations represented the bulk of currency in circulation and that their sudden invalidation would strike at black money, terror financing and fake currency. The RBI board had met that same afternoon and approved the step, though its minutes noted that most black money was held in real estate or gold rather than cash. At the time the move appeared consistent with earlier drives to expand the formal economy and with public frustration over tax evasion. Modi and his advisers expected that large sums of illicit cash would simply never be presented at banks. The policy therefore targeted a visible, measurable stock of paper currency rather than harder-to-trace assets. Because the notes accounted for 86.9 percent of the value of all currency then in circulation, officials calculated that invalidating them would force holders of hidden wealth to reveal themselves or forfeit the money outright. The arithmetic seemed straightforward: if the notes could not be exchanged without entering the banking system, any unaccounted holdings would effectively disappear from the economy.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Banks and post offices opened the next morning to receive the old notes for deposit or limited exchange. Long queues formed across the country as people waited to exchange or deposit their holdings. The government set weekly withdrawal caps and encouraged the use of digital wallets and bank transfers. Early reports showed a sharp drop in cash transactions and a temporary rise in digital payments. Proponents pointed to the queues themselves as evidence that hoarded cash was surfacing. Within the RBI, board members had already recorded concern that the move would produce only a short-term negative effect on GDP and would not materially affect black-money holdings outside cash. Those cautions remained internal until an RTI request later made the minutes public. The board had also observed that counterfeit notes represented only about 400 crore rupees against the total stock of currency, a fraction too small to justify the scale of disruption on its own. Implementation therefore rested on the assumption that the bulk of the cancelled notes would remain outside the formal system rather than on any detailed plan for how the banking network would absorb the sudden volume of deposits.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The Reserve Bank of India’s August 2018 annual report showed that 15,310.73 billion rupees of the demonetised notes had returned, leaving only 107.2 billion rupees unaccounted for. Because most undeclared wealth was not held as cash, the expected destruction of black money did not occur; economist Arun Kumar later noted that cash typically forms only about one percent of total assets. Economists Gabriel Chodorow-Reich, Gita Gopinath, Prachi Mishra and Abhinav Narayanan examined district-level data and estimated that employment and night-light output fell roughly two percentage points below their expected paths in the final quarter of 2016, with the shortfall fading within months. Districts that received fewer new notes experienced sharper drops in economic activity and faster subsequent adoption of digital payments. The cash shortage forced many small businesses and daily-wage workers to accept lower activity or shift to informal credit arrangements. Meanwhile the sudden policy shock accelerated the rollout of the Unified Payments Interface, which had been planned earlier but now faced less resistance from merchants already forced to handle cards or wallets. Tax-filing numbers rose from 35.10 million in FY 2014-15 to higher levels in later years, yet the Central Board of Direct Taxes and later analyses attributed most of the increase to subsequent measures such as GST and improved data matching rather than the note ban alone. The RBI board’s pre-decision warning that demonetisation would not touch the main stores of black wealth proved accurate once the deposit data arrived. When the notes returned at a 99.3 percent rate, the arithmetic revealed that the policy had effectively converted almost the entire stock of high-value currency into bank deposits rather than extinguishing it. That conversion occurred because holders of legitimate cash faced the same deadline as anyone else and therefore deposited their notes to avoid total loss, while those holding illicit cash could launder it through the same channels without detection at the point of deposit. The contraction in output followed directly from the temporary mismatch between the volume of notes removed and the slower pace at which new notes were printed and distributed; businesses that relied on daily cash turnover simply scaled back operations until replacement currency arrived. The faster digital adoption in cash-scarce districts showed that the policy changed payment habits only where the shortage was most acute, not because the underlying preference for cash had shifted permanently.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
No formal reversal was issued; the government instead highlighted secondary gains in digital payments and tax compliance. Cash-to-GDP ratios fell briefly then climbed back toward earlier levels. UPI transaction volumes continued their steep rise, supported by falling smartphone prices and expanding broadband, though the acceleration predated and outlasted the note ban itself. Official statements in Parliament in 2020 acknowledged that the government possessed no precise estimate of total black money either before or after the exercise. Ten years later the episode is remembered chiefly for the short-run disruption and for the confirmation that currency-based hoards were a small fraction of hidden wealth. The absence of any official black-money estimate meant that claims of success rested on indirect indicators whose causal link to the note ban remained contested in the data reviews published a decade later.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a policy targets only one narrow store of value, actors who hold wealth in other forms face little pressure to disclose it, as the RBI board minutes already indicated on the day of the decision. Measuring success by the volume of notes returned therefore rewards the appearance of compliance rather than the reduction of hidden assets. Any future attempt to shrink an informal sector must therefore track the actual composition of that sector’s balance sheets before assuming that one instrument, such as cash, carries the decisive share. How might today’s digital-payment mandates change if regulators first mapped where illicit funds actually sit rather than where they once circulated?
