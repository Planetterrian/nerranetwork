> **Cola sales at one Dutch border store doubled to 46,800 pallets after Germany's 2003 deposit on cans began.**

---

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On 1 January 2003, Environment Minister Jürgen Trittin’s new deposit rule took effect on single-use cans and bottles for beer, mineral water and carbonated soft drinks. German shoppers immediately began driving across the borders into Poland, the Netherlands and Belgium to buy the same products without the 25-cent or 50-cent Pfand. Retail shelves inside Germany emptied while border supermarkets in Słubice and Vaals reported sales surges of up to 70 percent.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Jürgen Trittin, then Bundesumweltminister, had watched the share of reusable containers fall below the 72 percent threshold set by the 1991 Packaging Ordinance. When two official surveys confirmed the drop, the ordinance automatically triggered the deposit requirement on 1 January 2003. Trittin and his ministry saw the Pfand as a direct incentive that would steer consumers back toward refillable glass and PET bottles already used by many German breweries and mineral-water firms. The policy also promised to keep containers out of residual waste and into organized recycling streams. At the time, roughly 250,000 jobs were tied to the existing refillable systems, and Trittin argued that protecting those jobs required decisive action against single-use growth. The deposit amounts were set at 25 cents for containers up to 1.5 liters and 50 cents above that size, applying only to the affected categories of beer, water and carbonated drinks. Proponents expected the measure to strengthen domestic reuse infrastructure that had been built over the previous decade. The arithmetic was straightforward: a consumer facing an extra 25 or 50 cents at the German checkout would compare that surcharge against the zero-deposit price across the border and, for many households living within a short drive, the foreign option would win on cost alone. This calculation rested on the assumption that the deposit would operate inside a closed national market where every purchase and every return stayed within the same set of rules.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The deposit began nationwide on the first day of 2003 even though no single return system yet existed across all retailers. Early return points operated as separate “island solutions” run by individual chains, so consumers could not reliably redeem containers bought at one store in another. Industry groups challenged the rule in court, but the Bundesverfassungsgericht upheld it weeks before the start date. Trittin publicly urged retailers and producers to stop resisting and to build the necessary logistics instead. Within days, German border residents noticed that supermarkets just across the line in Poland and the Netherlands still sold identical cans and bottles without any deposit. Customs officers in Cottbus recorded the first increase in private imports of single-use containers almost immediately. Because the return obligation applied only at the point of sale inside Germany, a retailer in Aachen or Frankfurt an der Oder had to charge the Pfand and later arrange its refund, while a retailer in Vaals or Słubice faced neither charge nor refund. This asymmetry in administrative burden and cash flow made the foreign price visibly lower even before any consumer crossed the border.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Shoppers from as far away as Berlin began making regular trips to Słubice, where a 1.5-liter bottle of lemonade cost about 25 cents without Pfand. One small Polish store reported that German customers routinely bought ten bottles each, and larger supermarkets saw overall sales of deposit-free drinks rise by as much as 70 percent. In Vaals on the Dutch border, the C1000 supermarket recorded cola-can volume jumping from 24,500 to 46,800 pallets in a single year. German retailers near the frontiers watched their own shelves empty because they alone had to charge and later refund the deposit. Because early return systems were fragmented, many consumers found it simpler to keep buying abroad than to navigate domestic redemption. Polish and Dutch producers, whose one-way containers faced no deposit at home, gained market share at the expense of German refillable lines. The European Parliament later noted that Dutch, Belgian and Austrian exporters lost nearly their entire German market share and that roughly 1,000 jobs were lost across those countries with 5,000 more at risk. Consumers who bought abroad could not return the containers through Germany’s dual system, so many ended up in ordinary household waste rather than the yellow bin. The policy had therefore shifted purchasing geography and cross-border traffic volumes without immediately raising overall recovery rates for the containers that were now being imported. The causal sequence ran from the deposit rule to the price differential, then to the decision by border households to treat the foreign supermarket as their regular supplier, and finally to the physical movement of volume that left German refillable systems with fewer customers while imported one-way containers entered German waste streams outside the deposit loop. Each additional kilometer driven to the border represented a second-order effect the original ordinance had not modeled.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
In April 2004 the European Commission formally asked Germany to remove the disproportionate barrier the deposit system created for imports. Retailers and importers pressed for a nationwide clearing system that would allow any retailer to accept any container. By October 2003 a unified return network had already begun to appear, and further legal adjustments followed in 2004. The Commission’s intervention forced Germany to guarantee equivalent access for foreign one-way packaging, which reduced but did not eliminate the price differential at the border. Today the deposit applies to a broader range of single-use containers and operates through the centralized Deutsche Pfandsystem GmbH, yet cross-border shopping for cheaper non-deposit goods remains a noted side effect whenever neighboring countries maintain lower or zero deposits. The later addition of a single clearing house addressed the “island solutions” problem inside Germany but left untouched the underlying difference in deposit obligations between member states.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Trittin tied the deposit only to containers sold inside Germany, the rule rewarded any retailer who could sell the same container just outside the jurisdiction. The resulting price gap turned an environmental incentive into a transport incentive for border households. Policymakers who later added a single nationwide redemption network reduced that gap but still left the underlying price difference intact for shoppers willing to cross frontiers. Any deposit or tax applied to only one side of an open border therefore shifts volume rather than simply changing container choices. How might today’s single-use plastic levies account for the same cross-border movement before they are introduced?
