> **D.A.R.E. placed uniformed officers in classrooms to teach millions of children to refuse drugs, yet repeated evaluations found the program produced little measurable change in behavior.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the spring of 1984, Los Angeles Police Department officers stood at the front of fifth-grade classrooms holding plastic bags of marijuana and cocaine while students recited pledges to stay drug-free. The program, called Drug Abuse Resistance Education, had been launched the previous year by LAPD chief Daryl Gates in partnership with the Los Angeles Unified School District. It was designed to reduce youth drug use through direct, authoritative instruction. Instead, the same approach that felt intuitively right to parents and police would later be shown by multiple studies to leave actual drug-use rates essentially unchanged.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
D.A.R.E. emerged in 1983 amid the crack-cocaine surge and the broader federal War on Drugs. Gates and school officials believed that placing trained officers in elementary and middle schools would give children credible, firsthand information about the dangers of substances before peer pressure took hold. At the time, most anti-drug efforts relied on occasional assemblies or pamphlets; embedding officers for seventeen weekly sessions seemed like a logical escalation that combined law-enforcement authority with classroom access. Policymakers viewed the program as a preventive public-health measure rather than simple enforcement, and early anecdotal reports from participating schools suggested students were engaged and parents felt reassured. The decision reflected the prevailing belief that clear, repeated messaging from respected adults could alter risk behavior in much the same way safety campaigns had reduced drunk driving.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Within two years the curriculum had spread beyond Los Angeles to dozens of California districts, and by 1988 it operated in more than a thousand U.S. communities. Federal and state grants covered officer training and materials, while local police departments supplied personnel on duty time. Proponents pointed to high enrollment numbers—reaching an estimated 26 million students by the mid-1990s—and to visible symbols such as graduation certificates and red T-shirts that signaled community commitment. A few early internal reviews noted short-term gains in students’ stated intentions to avoid drugs, which program advocates cited as proof of concept. Skeptics inside public-health circles warned that the curriculum’s heavy emphasis on drug identification and refusal scripts lacked interactive skill practice, yet those cautions received little traction while political support remained strong.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Large-scale evaluations beginning in the early 1990s consistently found no statistically significant reduction in students’ later drug use. A 1994 meta-analysis by Ennett and colleagues, published in the American Journal of Public Health, reviewed eight methodologically sound studies and concluded that D.A.R.E.’s effects on behavior were negligible. Some smaller studies even recorded modest increases in curiosity about substances after students received detailed descriptions of drug effects and paraphernalia. The causal pathway appears straightforward: the curriculum relied primarily on didactic lectures and fear-based messaging rather than on practicing refusal skills under realistic social pressure, an approach already known to be less effective in behavior-change research. Because the program consumed classroom time and local budgets, schools sometimes deferred other prevention activities that later proved more promising. Politically, the program’s popularity among parents and elected officials created a feedback loop in which cancellation carried higher immediate costs than continuation, even after negative findings accumulated. Over time, the gap between measured outcomes and public perception widened; many communities retained D.A.R.E. long after federal agencies had quietly stopped listing it as an evidence-based model.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the early 2000s, mounting evidence prompted several districts to drop the original curriculum. In 2009 the D.A.R.E. organization itself adopted a new evidence-informed program called keepin’ it REAL, developed at Pennsylvania State University and built around interactive storytelling and peer-resistance strategies rather than officer lectures. The transition preserved the visible police-school partnership while altering the pedagogical core. Some communities that had already invested in training continued the older format for several additional years. Today keepin’ it REAL operates in a smaller number of sites, and rigorous evaluations of the revised program show modest positive effects on decision-making skills, though at a far smaller scale than the original D.A.R.E. reached.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
An intervention’s visibility and political durability can remain high even when its measured impact on the target behavior is low. Early adoption of rigorous, independent outcome evaluation—rather than reliance on enrollment numbers or participant satisfaction—helps surface ineffective designs before they become entrenched. When an activity produces clear organizational or symbolic benefits for the adults involved, those benefits can decouple from results for the intended beneficiaries. The D.A.R.E. story therefore raises a practical question for any new prevention or education effort: what independent data would be required to continue, modify, or retire the program, and who is positioned to act on that data before sunk costs and community attachment grow too large?
