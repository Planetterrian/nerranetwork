> **By 2012 the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program had distributed 4.49 million hectares yet tenancy persisted on reclassified lands.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On June 30, 2021, the Philippine Supreme Court ruled in G.R. No. 226852 that a two-hectare parcel in Barangay Makiling, Calamba, Laguna, owned by Asia United Bank, qualified for exemption from the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program because a prior local zoning ordinance had reclassified it as industrial. Renato Tañon and the heirs of Pio Candelaria, who had worked the land, could not prove vested tenancy rights before the zoning change and therefore received no redistribution. The 1988 law had set a five-hectare retention limit and ordered shares for tenants on large estates; instead, the exemption mechanism left those tenants on the same plots under unchanged control, because the Court treated the Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board certification of industrial zoning as conclusive evidence that removed the parcel from redistribution before any tenancy claim could be litigated.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
After the 1986 People Power Revolution removed Ferdinand Marcos, the new government under President Corazon Aquino faced intense pressure to break up the concentrated rural landholdings that had fueled decades of unrest. Congress passed Republic Act No. 6657, the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Law, on June 10, 1988, capping private ownership at five hectares per landowner and directing the Department of Agrarian Reform to distribute excess land to tenant farmers and landless workers. The statute also required that tenants on retained or exempt parcels receive leasehold protections so they would no longer face arbitrary eviction or share-tenancy abuses. Lawmakers viewed these caps and protections as the practical way to extend the earlier Presidential Decree 27 reforms while respecting constitutional property rights and avoiding the economic disruption of sudden plantation seizures. At the time, the policy appeared measured and administratively feasible because it built on existing local government powers to reclassify land for non-agricultural uses, a step that seemed necessary to allow orderly urban growth without immediately dismantling every commercial farm. Proponents reasoned that the five-hectare limit would still leave owners with viable holdings while the leasehold rules on exempt land would gradually improve tenant security through regulated rents and written contracts.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The Department of Agrarian Reform began issuing Certificates of Land Ownership Award starting in late 1988, and by 2012 it reported having distributed 4.49 million hectares to roughly 2.8 million beneficiaries. Early official statements highlighted the transfer of rice and corn lands as a clear success. The 1994 amendments under Republic Act 7881 carved out fishponds, prawn farms, and certain livestock operations, reflecting lobbying from commercial producers who argued that forced redistribution would destroy export industries. Skeptics inside the agrarian-reform bureaucracy warned that the exemption clauses and the requirement for Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board ratification of local reclassifications would invite strategic re-zoning before coverage notices could be served. Implementation therefore proceeded parcel by parcel, with municipal councils able to pass zoning ordinances that shifted land from agricultural to industrial or residential categories, after which the central board’s certification locked in the exemption even if tenants had already begun leasehold negotiations. The law’s own text in Section 10 listed explicit exemptions for non-agricultural uses, so local governments treated reclassification as a routine planning tool rather than an evasion tactic.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Landowners responded by securing municipal zoning ordinances that reclassified agricultural parcels as industrial, residential, or commercial before the Department of Agrarian Reform could place them under coverage. In the Calamba case, Asia Trust Development Bank obtained a February 2007 HLURB certification that the two-hectare grassland site had already been zoned industrial; the Supreme Court later treated that certification as conclusive. Similar reclassifications removed hundreds of hectares in Sariaya, Quezon, even after some tenants had received Certificates of Land Ownership Award. Because the law permitted conversion to high-value commercial crops without triggering redistribution, many estates simply shifted from rice to bananas or pineapples while retaining the same workforce under leasehold contracts. Tenants therefore remained on the identical plots, paying rent or delivering shares to the same families or corporate successors, only now under the formal label of “exempt” land. The World Bank’s 2009 technical paper documented that these mechanisms, combined with slow processing and credit constraints on new owners, left ownership concentration largely intact in several provinces. Second-order effects included depressed local land markets, because banks hesitated to lend against titles that might later face exemption claims, and continued rural poverty that the original redistribution had been meant to relieve. A further consequence appeared when new owners who did receive land found themselves unable to consolidate fragmented holdings into efficient units, since the same reclassification route that protected large owners also discouraged investment in irrigation or machinery on smaller redistributed plots. The causal chain ran from the statute’s simultaneous grant of redistribution power and local zoning authority, through the timing advantage that allowed preemptive reclassification, to the survival of tenancy relations under leasehold rules that the law itself preserved on exempt parcels.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
The 2021 Supreme Court decision reaffirmed the pre-CARP reclassification route and required future claimants to prove tenancy rights existed before any zoning change. Successive administrations have extended the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program’s funding deadlines rather than closing the exemption loopholes. Some municipal governments have tightened zoning review, yet the ResearchGate evaluation of thirty years of implementation notes that conversion applications continue to outpace actual redistribution in peri-urban zones. No large-scale reversal of earlier exemptions has occurred; the pattern of legal preservation of control remains the dominant outcome. The ResearchGate study further observes that poor targeting and land consolidation issues persisted because the original statute never created an independent mechanism to audit whether reclassifications served genuine development needs or merely shielded existing owners.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a statute sets a hard ownership cap but simultaneously authorizes local reclassification ratified by a central board, the actors who control the zoning process gain a reliable route around the cap. When leasehold rules remain in force on every exempt parcel, the same economic relationship between tenant and owner survives under a new legal heading. When proof of pre-existing tenancy rights is required after the fact, tenants who lacked formal documentation at the moment of reclassification lose any claim. Policymakers weighing similar redistribution rules today might therefore ask how any proposed exemption or conversion procedure will be timed relative to the moment coverage is declared.
