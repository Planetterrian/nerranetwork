Minky into the catch. This is Unintended Consequences, Episode 95. In the 1987 to 88
Austro-Summer, a Japanese fleet in the Antarctic took 273 Minky Whales. The paperwork called
its science. The Commercial Moratorium of the International Wailing Commission, the IWC,
had just come into force, written to drive catch limits to zero, and give battered stocks
room to recover. The Moratorium was designed to stop the killing. Instead, a research clause
drafted in 1946 opened a path that would put several hundred Minky on the Flensing
Deck every year for three decades. The path aimed at a species that earlier size limits
and species bands had once left almost untouched. The machinery that produced that outcome began
as an attempt to save an industry from itself. On December 2, 1946, in Washington, D.C., 15
nations signed the International Convention for the Regulation of Wailing. Its stated purpose
was dual, and at the time, the two halves did not look like a contradiction. The treaty
was meant to provide for the proper conservation of whale stocks and thus make possible the
orderly development of the wailing industry. The treaty created the IWC, and that body
could set seasons, sanctuaries, size limits, and quotas. The IWC could not, on its own,
ban wailing outright. The men who wrote the convention did not think they were writing
a prohibition. Those men were trying to stop a repeat of the 1930s free-for-all that had
already gutted some southern ocean stocks. Quotas, size limits, seasons, and sanctuaries
looked like enough. By the 1970s, that optimism was hard to defend. Blue whales had been
protected in 1966 after a collapse so complete that further hunting was pointless. Humpbacks
had been closed in the mid-1960s. Finn and Say Quotas were ratcheted down as factoring
ships scraped through what was left. The 1972 United Nations Conference on the Human
Environment in Stockholm called for a 10-year moratorium. Environmental campaigns in the
United States, Britain, Australia, and New Zealand turned whales into a public cause. Inside
the IWC, conservation-minded members saw a zero-catch limit as the only way to break a pattern.
Among them was the scientist Sidney Holt, who had spent years arguing that the commission's
own quotas were too high. The IWC had long been described as a Whalers Club, setting numbers
the water could not sustain. In 1982, at the commission's meeting in Brighton, the members
voted to add paragraph 10E to the schedule. Catch limits for commercial whaling would
be zero, beginning in 1986, kept under review with a comprehensive assessment by 1990.
The vote was 25-7. The intention was recovery. The information on the table was grim, and
the people using it were not trying to humiliate Japan or Norway. Those delegates were trying
to stop a repeating failure. The moratorium took effect for the 1985-86 season. It was
never a clean global stop. Under Article 5 of the 1946 convention, a member that objects
within 90 days is not bound. The convention itself gave members that window. Japan, Norway,
the Soviet Union, and Peru all objected. What closed the commercial path for Japan was not
only Brighton, but American fisheries law. The Pellia Amendment and the Pachwood Magnuson
Amendment allowed Washington to certify nations whose whaling diminished the effectiveness
of the IWC, and then to cut their allocations in United States waters. Japan's distant
water fishing fleets had a great deal to lose. In 1988, Japan withdrew its objection
to the commercial zero quotas. Japan did not withdraw from Antarctic whaling. Article
8 of the same 1946 convention says that any contracting government may grant to any of
its nationals a special permit authorizing that national to kill, take, and treat whales
for purposes of scientific research. Whales taken under such a permit shall be exempt
from the operation of the convention. The clause had been written for small, bonafide samples.
The kind of work a biologist might do with a handful of animals. In 1987, the Institute
of Citation Research was established in Tokyo. For the 1987-88 season, the fisheries agency
issued permits for the Japanese Whale Research Program under special permit in the Antarctic,
or JARPA. The first season's catch was 273 Antarctic Minky Whales. Proponents of the
moratorium called the zero limit a pause the stocks had earned. Japanese officials argued that
Antarctic Minky were abundant, that the IWC's own promised assessment required data,
and that lethal sampling was the way to get age, reproductive condition, and stock structure.
From the first season, critics said the sample sizes look like a quota, and that the meat
would be sold. Both things were true. Japanese law required the byproduct to be processed,
and the proceeds offset the cost of the research. The Nishin Maru and her catcher boats kept
steaming south. The Minky Harvest that JARPA locked in was not a sudden invention of 1987.
It was the end of a substitution chain that the IWC's own earlier protections had set in motion.
Antarctic Minky Whales, balanoptera boner-ensis, run 7-10 meters long. Antarctic Minky had been
of little commercial interest when blue, thin, and sperm whales filled the factory ships.
Original schedule size limits were written around the great roar quals. As long as a larger
animal was legal and profitable, a Minky was a nuisance, not a target. That changed as the larger
species were protected or commercially exhausted. In 1979, the IWC banned factory shift wailing for
all species except Minky. The rule was meant as a conservation step. In practice, the 1979
rule channeled remaining pelagic effort onto the one animal still allowed. Japanese and Soviet
fleets had already begun taking Antarctic Minky in volume in the early 1970s. Within a decade,
a species that had been a footnote became the mainstay of southern ocean pelagic whaling,
with combined catches in the thousands in some seasons. Species specific mercy for the famous whales
had intensified pressure on the substitute. The 1986 moratorium was supposed to stop even that.
What the moratorium stopped was the commercial label. IWC catch reports show just under 6,800
Antarctic Minky taken during DARPA's 18 seasons. That is typically around 300 a year at first.
A full research season every year after commercial catch limits were supposed to be zero.
The take later rose toward 400 after the sample size was increased in the mid-1990s.
In 2005, Japan launched DARPA 2 with a stated annual target of 850 Antarctic Minky,
plus fin and humpback whales. Actual DARPA 2 Minky catches often fell short of that target.
Several thousand animals were taken over the life of the program by most accounts,
in part because protest vessels in the southern ocean made the work harder.
The meat still entered commercial channels in Japan. A policy written to reduce
citation removals to zero had redirected them into a government permitted research take,
whose scale was a matter of permit design, not of a recovered market.
Second order effects piled up far from the ice. Inside Japan, whale meat was no longer the post-war
protein staple it had been in the 1950s and 1960s, when school lunches and scarcity made it
ordinary. By the 1990s and 2000s, domestic demand was falling. Inventories accumulated in cold
storage. Some product was steered into institutional meals to clear stock. The hunt did not shrink
in proportion, because the program was no longer only about protein. The program had become an
institution, the Institute of Citation Research, the Factory Ship, the Catcher Cruise,
the Fisheries Agency's position at the IWC. Whaling hardened into a question of sovereignty
and of not being seen to yield. Coastal towns with a whaling history, Shimonoseki,
Ayukawa, later Kushiro, experienced the moratorium as an attack on livelihood and culture,
even as most Japanese consumers quietly stopped eating the meat.
At the IWC, the moratorium and the scientific permit dispute polarized the room for a generation.
Conservation members treated zero as the point. Japan and a group of allies treated sustainable
use as the point. Japan and those allies argued that Antarctic Minky numbered in the hundreds
of thousands. Estimates the scientific committee itself revised and contested over the years,
with substantial uncertainty that remains. Japan supported IWC membership for countries with
little or no whaling history, building a voting block against new sanctuaries and for a return
to managed quotas. The 1994 Southern Ocean Sanctuary passed. Japan objected and was not bound.
Diplomacy with Australia and New Zealand soured. In the 2000s, the Southern Ocean became a stage
for clashes between the Nishin Maru and Sea Shepherd, footage that played to both domestic audiences.
A 2010 attempt at a grand bargain, limited commercial quotas in exchange for an end to scientific
permits collapsed. The organization created to manage whales spent decades managing a feud.
The causal chain is not mysterious. Protect the large species and effort moves to the small one.
Write a commercial's euro without rewriting or policing the research exception,
and the exception becomes the season. Fun the exception with the sale of meat, and the research
program acquires a commercial metabolism. Leave coastal communities and a national bureaucracy
without an off-ramp, and a protein industry becomes an identity industry. None of that required foolishness
in 1946 or in 1982. It required a treaty that tried to be both a conservation instrument
and a whaling instrument, then a prohibition that used one half of the text against the other.
On March 31, 2014, the International Court of Justice in Australia versus Japan,
with New Zealand intervening, found that Jarpatou was not for purposes of scientific research
under Article 8. The vote was 12 to 4. Japan cancelled the next Antarctic season,
then redesigned the work as New Rep A, with a smaller proposed sample and fresh paperwork.
The IWC still would not agree to implement a revised management procedure that would have allowed
commercial quotas under strict rules. On December 26, 2018, Chief Cabinet Secretary Suga Yoshihide
announced that Japan would withdraw from the IWC. The withdrawal took effect on June 30, 2019.
The following day, Japan resumed commercial whaling in its exclusive economic zone and territorial
waters, with self-set quotas for minkey, brides, and seawales. The numbers ran in the dozens to
low hundreds across those species, a fraction of the old Antarctic scientific take. The Antarctic
program ended. The Nishin Maru came home. There is a meta-irony here that the 1982 delegates
could not have priced in. Staying inside the convention, armed with Article 8, had sustained an
Antarctic minkey harvest for more than 30 years. Leaving the convention ended that harvest. Leaving
also removed Japan from the multilateral body that still sets the global rules for everyone else.
Norway and Iceland had never fully accepted the moratorium in the North Atlantic. Norway and
Iceland continue to take minkey under objection or reservation. The IWC's zero-catch limit remains
in force for members. Antarctic minkey are no longer taken by Japan. Whale meat in Japanese
markets still struggles to find buyers. The substitution problem did not vanish so much as shrink
and move in shore. Three principles travel well beyond the southern ocean. First, species-specific
protections reallocate pressure onto substitutes unless someone accounts for effort displacement.
Close one stock, one chemical, one gear type, and the boats and the capital do not evaporate.
Effort looks for the next legal target. Size limits and the 1979 minkey exception did not fail
by accident. Those rules succeeded at moving the fleet. Second, an exception written for a narrow
good purpose will be used for a wider one when the main rule binds. Article 8 was not a trick in 1946.
Article 8 was not designed as a parallel commercial season, and that is what it became. A research
clause, a traditional use clause, a 90-day objection window, any door left a jar will carry traffic
the drafters never pictured. Third, a ban that does not deal with institutions, identity,
and the residual market may relabel an activity rather than end it, and may make it harder to unwind.
Once crews, agencies, and towns have organized around the exception,
shutting the activity down is no longer a market decision. The useful question is where the
pressure goes when a rule closes one door. Fisheries managers meet the same pattern when a closure
on one stalk lights up another. Treaty lawyers meet that pattern whenever a research, traditional,
or scientific clause sits beside a prohibition. If you are designing a ban this year on a gear-type,
a chemical, a trade, it is worth asking before the vote which substitute you have just made valuable
and which paragraph of your own text will still be open at 90 days. That's unintended consequences
