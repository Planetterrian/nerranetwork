> **In 2013 German lignite generation reached its highest level since 1990 even as renewables set new records.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
Germany’s feed-in tariffs under the Renewable Energy Sources Act had driven renewable generation to 24.1 percent of gross electricity in 2013. Yet that same year lignite-fired plants produced more than 162 billion kilowatt-hours, the highest total since 1990. The policy designed to cut carbon emissions had instead increased them in 2012 and 2013.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
German lawmakers passed the Renewable Energy Sources Act in 2000 to accelerate wind, solar and biomass after years of slow progress under earlier support schemes. They had watched Denmark’s early wind programs and wanted a reliable mechanism that would guarantee investors a fixed price for every kilowatt-hour fed into the grid. The goal was straightforward: replace fossil and nuclear generation with renewables as quickly as possible while keeping the lights on. At the time, wholesale electricity prices were high enough that adding zero-marginal-cost renewables was expected to lower consumer bills over the long run. Policymakers also believed the nuclear phase-out agreed after Fukushima would be covered by the growing renewable fleet. Claudia Kemfert, then at the German Institute for Economic Research, later noted that the missing piece was an adequate price on carbon dioxide. The architects therefore focused on deployment volume rather than on the relative operating costs of the remaining thermal plants. One objection often raised later is whether they should have foreseen the interaction with the EU Emissions Trading System, but the ETS price had been designed separately and was expected to tighten on its own schedule. In practice the two instruments operated on different timelines and different price signals.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The feed-in tariff system expanded rapidly after 2000 and again after 2009 when solar tariffs were still generous. By 2013 renewables supplied 152.4 billion kilowatt-hours, up from far lower figures a decade earlier. Proponents pointed to falling technology costs and rising public acceptance. Skeptics inside the utility sector warned that the merit-order effect would eventually push conventional plants out of the money, but few predicted which plants would be displaced first. The European Union Emissions Trading System price had already fallen below ten euros per tonne by 2011 and hovered near seven euros in 2013. At the same time, cheap United States coal displaced by domestic shale gas flooded European markets. These price signals were visible in day-ahead markets but were not part of the original renewable-support design. A further complication was that lignite plants, once built, had very low variable costs because the fuel sat in open-pit mines next to the stations, so even modest wholesale price drops left them profitable while gas plants with pipeline fuel costs did not.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Agora Energiewende’s April 2014 report documented the result: a fuel switch from gas to coal inside Germany and in neighboring countries. Because renewables have zero marginal cost they set the clearing price in more hours, driving wholesale prices down. Gas plants, with higher fuel costs, became unprofitable first and reduced output. Lignite and hard-coal plants, whose fuel and carbon costs were lower, remained in the money and increased generation. Patrick Graichen of Agora Energiewende told reporters that German coal plants were displacing gas plants not only domestically but also in the Netherlands. Lignite output reached 162 billion kilowatt-hours in 2013, the highest figure recorded since reunification. Carbon-dioxide emissions rose in both 2012 and 2013 despite the renewable growth. The nuclear phase-out itself did not drive the increase; Agora’s analysis showed that renewable additions had fully offset the lost nuclear generation. The second-order effect was record electricity exports, much of it coal-fired, which further raised domestic emissions. Economists noted that an EU carbon price near seven euros per tonne was simply too low to change the relative cost of lignite versus gas. One might ask whether the nuclear phase-out gap alone explains the coal surge, yet the numbers show renewables covered that gap while the additional coal burn came from the changed dispatch order among the remaining thermal fleet. The third-order effect was that utilities kept older lignite units online longer because they still cleared the market, delaying any early retirement that higher carbon prices might have triggered.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By late 2014 the picture began to shift. Renewables reached 25.8 percent of gross generation and became the single largest source for the first time, ahead of lignite at 25.6 percent. Total emissions fell roughly five percent that year as milder weather and further renewable growth reduced coal and gas burn. Policymakers responded with the 2014 EEG reform that slowed tariff digression and introduced auctions. Agora’s Patrick Graichen argued that a tighter emissions cap or a national carbon price floor would still be needed to force coal reductions. No immediate reversal of the feed-in tariff occurred; instead the system evolved toward market-based procurement while the underlying merit-order dynamic remained. The reform addressed deployment speed but left the carbon-price gap intact, so the same relative-cost logic continued to favor lignite whenever gas prices stayed high.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a support rule rewards only the volume of zero-marginal-cost generation without adjusting the carbon price that governs dispatch, the plants with the lowest running costs among the remaining fleet keep operating. Germany’s experience shows that the same price drop that makes renewables competitive can also extend the life of the cheapest fossil plants if their carbon cost stays low. Any new clean-energy mandate should therefore be paired with an explicit adjustment to the relative cost of the dirtiest remaining resources, or the displacement will favor those resources over cleaner but more expensive ones. How might today’s capacity markets or clean-energy standards change if they incorporated that same relative-cost test?
