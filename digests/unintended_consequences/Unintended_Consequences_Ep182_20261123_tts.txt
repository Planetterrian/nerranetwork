India removed eighty-six percent of cash overnight, yet banks took nearly all of it back.

This is Unintended Consequences, episode one hundred eighty-two.

By the thirtieth of June, twenty eighteen, the Reserve Bank of India, the RBI, had finished counting the cancelled five-hundred- and one-thousand-rupee notes. Fifteen thousand three hundred ten point seven three billion rupees had been deposited out of fifteen thousand four hundred seventeen point nine three billion in circulation, a return of ninety-nine point three percent.

Only one hundred seven point two billion rupees never came back.

The invalidation was designed to trap unaccounted cash outside the banking system. Holders of legitimate cash and holders of illicit cash faced the same deadline, so both deposited to avoid a total loss. Illicit notes could pass through the same channels without detection at the point of deposit.

The policy converted almost the entire stock of high-value currency into bank deposits rather than extinguishing it.

On the evening of the eighth of November, twenty sixteen, Prime Minister Narendra Modi told the nation that those two denominations would cease to be legal tender at midnight. He had reached the decision after months of internal discussion. Persistent complaints about counterfeit high-value notes, and about cash used to hide income from tax authorities, sat behind it.

He said the two denominations represented the bulk of currency in circulation, and that sudden invalidation would strike at black money, terror financing, and fake currency.

The RBI board met that same afternoon and approved the step. Its minutes noted that most black money was held in real estate or gold rather than cash. At the time the move appeared consistent with earlier drives to expand the formal economy and with public frustration over tax evasion. Modi and his advisers expected that large sums of illicit cash would simply never be presented at banks.

The policy targeted a visible, measurable stock of paper currency rather than harder-to-trace assets. Because the notes accounted for eighty-six point nine percent of the value of all currency then in circulation, officials calculated that invalidating them would force holders of hidden wealth to reveal themselves or forfeit the money.

If the notes could not be exchanged without entering the banking system, any unaccounted holdings would effectively disappear from the economy.

Banks and post offices opened the next morning to take the old notes for deposit or limited exchange. Long queues formed across the country as people waited. The government set weekly withdrawal caps and encouraged digital wallets and bank transfers. Early reports showed a sharp drop in cash transactions and a temporary rise in digital payments.

Proponents pointed to the queues themselves as evidence that hoarded cash was surfacing.

Inside the RBI, board members had already recorded a different reading. The move, they noted, would produce only a short-term negative effect on gross domestic product and would not materially affect black-money holdings outside cash. Those cautions stayed internal until a Right to Information request later made the minutes public.

The board had also observed that counterfeit notes represented only about four hundred crore rupees against the total stock of currency, a fraction too small to justify the scale of disruption on its own.

Implementation rested on the assumption that the bulk of the cancelled notes would remain outside the formal system, not on a detailed plan for how the banking network would absorb the sudden volume of deposits.

Because most undeclared wealth was not held as cash, the expected destruction of black money did not occur. Economist Arun Kumar later noted that cash typically forms only about one percent of total assets. The board's pre-decision warning, that the step would not touch the main stores of black wealth, matched the deposit data once that data arrived.

Economists Gabriel Chodorow-Reich, Gita Gopinath, Prachi Mishra, and Abhinav Narayanan examined district-level data and estimated that employment and night-light output fell roughly two percentage points below their expected paths in the final quarter of twenty sixteen. The shortfall faded within months.

Districts that received fewer new notes saw sharper drops in economic activity and faster later adoption of digital payments. The cash shortage forced many small businesses and daily-wage workers to accept lower activity or shift to informal credit. The contraction followed from the mismatch between the volume of notes removed and the slower pace at which new notes were printed and distributed.

Businesses that relied on daily cash turnover scaled back until replacement currency arrived. Payment habits changed where the shortage was most acute, not because the underlying preference for cash had shifted for good.

The shock accelerated the Unified Payments Interface, UPI, which had been planned earlier and now faced less resistance from merchants already forced onto cards or wallets. Tax-filing numbers rose from thirty-five point one zero million in financial year twenty fourteen to fifteen to higher levels in later years.

The Central Board of Direct Taxes, and later analyses, attributed most of that increase to subsequent measures such as the Goods and Services Tax, GST, and improved data matching, rather than to the note ban alone.

No formal reversal was issued. The government highlighted secondary gains in digital payments and tax compliance. The ratio of cash to gross domestic product fell briefly, then climbed back toward earlier levels. UPI transaction volumes continued their steep rise, supported by falling smartphone prices and expanding broadband, though that acceleration predated the note ban and outlasted it.

Official statements in Parliament in twenty twenty acknowledged that the government possessed no precise estimate of total black money either before or after the exercise. Ten years later the episode is remembered chiefly for the short-run disruption and for the confirmation that currency-based hoards were a small fraction of hidden wealth.

Without an official black-money estimate, claims of success rested on indirect indicators whose link to the note ban remained contested in the data reviews published a decade later.

When a policy targets only one narrow store of value, actors who hold wealth in other forms face little pressure to disclose it, which is what the RBI board minutes already indicated on the day of the decision. Measuring success by the volume of notes returned therefore rewards the appearance of compliance rather than the reduction of hidden assets.

Any future attempt to shrink an informal sector must track the actual composition of that sector's balance sheets before assuming that one instrument, such as cash, carries the decisive share. A short-run drop in activity can be real and still say nothing about whether hidden wealth moved.
