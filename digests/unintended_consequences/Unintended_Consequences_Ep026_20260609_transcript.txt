In the spring of 2006, a fifth grade teacher in a suburban Texas district stopped holding
weekly science experiments.
She replaced them with daily drills on released items from the state math and reading tests.
The change was not her choice.
Under the rules of the 2001 No Child Left Behind Act, her schools continued receipt of federal
Title I money now depended on the percentage of students who reached proficient on those
specific exams.
This policy had been written to guarantee that no child would be overlooked.
Instead, it produced classrooms where entire subjects and most non-tested skills quietly
disappeared from the schedule.
The legislation that became No Child Left Behind was shaped by a bipartisan coalition
that included President George W. Bush, Representative John Banner, and Senator Edward Kennedy.
Its central goal was to close long-standing achievement gaps between low-income students,
students of color, and their more advantaged peers.
Earlier federal efforts, such as the 1965 Elementary and Secondary Education Act, had distributed
money with few requirements to show results.
By the late 1990s, state-level experiments with standards and testing in Texas and North
Carolina appeared to raise scores for previously low-performing groups.
Policy makers therefore concluded that attaching clear numeric targets and consequences to federal
dollars would finally force schools to focus on the children they had historically neglected.
Congress passed the bill in December 2001, and President Bush signed it into law on January
8, 2002. Every state had to administer annual reading and mathematics tests in grades
3 through 8 and once in high school. Then schools had to report results by race, income, disability
status, and English learner designation. Schools that failed to make adequate yearly progress
for two consecutive years faced a sequence of interventions culminating in possible staff
replacement or conversion to a charter school. Early data from 2003 and 2004 showed rising
percentages of students reaching proficient levels in several states. Supporters cited that rise
as evidence that the new pressure was working. A smaller number of analysts noted that schools were
already shifting instructional time away from social studies, science, and the arts to protect
their math and reading scores. Because the law measured only reading and mathematics proficiency,
many districts reallocated minutes in the school day from every other subject. A 2007 survey
by the Center on Education Policy found that 62% of districts had increased time for tested subjects
while 44% had cut time for social studies, science, art, or music. Teachers reported spending weeks
each spring on test preparation packets rather than on writing projects or laboratory work. In some
states, the pressure produced outright cheating. The 2011 Atlanta investigation revealed that dozens
of educators had altered answer sheets to protect their schools from sanctions. The single metric
of percent proficient also encouraged schools to focus resources on students just below the passing
line. Those already certain to pass or those far below it received less attention. Over time, the
narrowed curriculum became self-reinforcing. New teachers entered the profession having experienced
little else themselves. Parents and community members noticed the loss of field trips, recess,
and elective classes. Yet the federal funding rules gave local leaders little room to restore what
had been removed. By 2011, both parties in Congress acknowledged that the adequate yearly progress
rules were unworkable for nearly all schools. The Obama administration began granting waivers that
relaxed some requirements. In December 2015, President Obama signed the Every Student Succeeds Act.
That law returned most decisions about goals, interventions, and accountability systems to the states.
The new law retained annual testing, but eliminated the rigid federal timeline of sanctions.
Some states quickly restored requirements for social studies and science testing.
Others kept the narrowed focus on reading and mathematics.
10 years later, most districts still administer more standardized tests than they did before 2002.
Debates continue over how much instructional time should be protected for subjects that do not
carry federal consequences. When a single quantitative target carries large financial or career
stakes, organizations will optimize for that target even when doing so undermines the broader mission.
