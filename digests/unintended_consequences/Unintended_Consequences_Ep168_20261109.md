> **GI Bill mortgages raised homeownership from 44 to 62 percent, steering veterans into car-only suburbs.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
The 1944 Servicemen’s Readjustment Act offered returning veterans low-interest, no-down-payment home loans that banks and builders directed toward new single-family tracts built beyond existing rail lines. By 1960 homeownership had climbed from 44 percent to 62 percent, yet most of those new owners found themselves dependent on automobiles for every daily trip. The policy that aimed to give veterans stable housing instead produced settlement patterns that required cars and discouraged rail investment for decades afterward.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
After the Great Depression left more than half of U.S. mortgages in default and wartime production had drawn millions into uniform service, Congress passed the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act on June 22, 1944. Lawmakers sought to prevent a repeat of the 1932 foreclosure wave and to reward sixteen million returning servicemen with practical tools for civilian life. The act’s home-loan guaranty program, administered through the Veterans Administration, promised private lenders repayment if veterans defaulted, allowing banks to offer thirty-year amortized mortgages with no down payment. At the time this seemed a straightforward way to convert wartime sacrifice into long-term household stability without direct government construction of housing. Supporters viewed the loans as an extension of the same federal credit support already begun by the Home Owners Loan Corporation and the Federal Housing Administration in the 1930s. The arithmetic was simple: a zero-down, thirty-year term cut the monthly payment enough that a factory wage could cover both principal and interest on a modest new house, something five-year balloon mortgages had never allowed.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Lenders quickly channeled the new guarantees into large-scale developments on farmland outside city limits. On Long Island, developer William Levitt began Levittown in 1947, completing thousands of Cape Cod houses using assembly-line methods that kept prices low enough for veterans to buy with GI Bill financing. Similar tracts appeared in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Early reports from the Veterans Administration showed rapid uptake; by the early 1950s millions of loans had closed. Proponents pointed to rising homeownership figures and the visible creation of new neighborhoods as proof the program worked. Few planners at the time questioned whether the resulting locations would remain connected to jobs once streetcar and commuter-rail service thinned. Builders could purchase acreage at agricultural prices, grade it once, and pour concrete slabs in sequence; each step lowered the per-unit cost that the VA guarantee then translated into an affordable monthly payment for the buyer.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Because the loans financed new construction rather than existing urban stock, capital flowed to greenfield sites where land was cheap and rail lines were absent or distant. Levittown and its imitators therefore grew as clusters of single-family houses served only by roads. The 1956 Federal Highway Act then added 41,000 miles of interstate routes that made those locations reachable by car, further rewarding the outward pattern. As white veterans moved, northern cities lost tax base while suburban municipalities invested in roads instead of transit. Rail operators, facing declining ridership, cut service, which in turn made car ownership even more necessary for the next wave of buyers. Families without access to the GI Bill benefits, including most Black veterans denied loans by discriminatory bank practices, remained in cities whose infrastructure received less new investment. The result was not merely more driving but a durable settlement form in which jobs, schools, and stores were spaced for automobile travel rather than walking or rail. Estimates from the American Economic Association study attribute roughly one-quarter of the homeownership rise among affected cohorts directly to the timing and terms of these loans, confirming how credit rules reshaped where and how people lived. One might object that families simply preferred detached houses; yet pre-war streetcar suburbs had already offered that option at higher densities and with rail access, while the GI Bill terms removed the price penalty that had once kept development closer to existing lines. The same loan guarantee that lowered the buyer’s monthly cost also removed any incentive for builders to pay the premium for land near stations, because the VA did not require proximity to transit as a condition of the guaranty.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the late 1960s the social costs of car-dependent sprawl—congestion, air quality, and uneven access—were visible enough that federal policy began to shift. The 1968 Fair Housing Act and later transit funding measures attempted to open suburban opportunities and support alternatives to driving, yet the physical layout of existing tracts remained fixed. Subsequent mortgage programs continued to favor single-family detached homes, preserving the same incentive structure. Today the majority of U.S. housing units built between 1945 and 1970 still sit in low-density corridors whose residents drive for most trips, illustrating how the original credit design continues to shape daily mobility. Attempts to retrofit rail or bus rapid transit into these corridors now face the higher per-rider cost that low-density spacing creates, a direct arithmetic consequence of the site-selection logic the GI Bill loans first rewarded.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When the Veterans Administration guaranteed thirty-year loans without location criteria, private builders responded by siting new supply on the cheapest available land, which lay beyond rail corridors. Any future housing-credit rule that omits explicit transit-access requirements will again favor whichever sites minimize short-term construction cost. The same mechanism appears whenever mortgage terms or tax treatment reward new detached units over infill near existing stations; the GI Bill case shows the pattern can lock in within a single generation. How might today’s housing programs change if every loan guarantee carried a simple test for proximity to frequent transit?
