> **Smokey Bear urged Americans to stamp out every spark — and turned manageable forests into fuel for megafires that now consume the agency’s budget.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On August 20, 1910, the Big Burn raced across three million acres of Idaho and Montana in two days, killing eighty-seven people., killing eighty-seven people and convincing federal foresters that any uncontrolled fire threatened both timber and towns. In the decades that followed, the U.S. Forest Service placed a bear in a ranger hat on posters whose slogan soon became the single command “Only YOU can prevent forest fires.” The policy that followed treated every ignition as an emergency to be extinguished by 10 a.m. the next day, an approach that succeeded in reducing the number of small fires for decades while quietly rebuilding the very material those fires had once consumed.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The 1910 catastrophe and a series of wartime conflagrations in the 1940s convinced Forest Service leaders that the nation’s remaining commercial timber stands could not survive another uncontrolled burn. In 1935 the agency formalized the “10 a.m. policy,” directing every district to have a fire under control by ten o’clock the morning after it was reported. Smokey Bear, introduced in 1944 by the Forest Service and the Wartime Advertising Council, translated that operational rule into a public ethic: fire itself was the enemy. At the time the choice appeared straightforward; commercial forestry, rural economies, and emerging recreation all depended on keeping existing stands intact rather than letting them burn. Decision-makers worked from the best available data on merchantable timber volume and the visible destruction left by crown fires, not from long-term studies of fire’s ecological role.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Posters, radio spots, and classroom materials reached a large majority of American households within a decade. The Forest Service expanded its firefighting capacity with smokejumpers, aerial tankers, and a growing network of lookouts. Annual burned acreage nationwide dropped from tens of millions of acres in the pre-war decades to a small fraction of that within a generation. Early reports celebrated the policy as proof that aggressive suppression could protect both watersheds and the timber base needed for postwar housing. A few range scientists in the Southwest noted that frequent low-intensity fires had maintained open ponderosa stands, yet their observations remained outside the agency’s dominant timber-management framework.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Without the regular, low-intensity burns that had cleared needles and brush for centuries, surface fuels accumulated at rates of roughly one to two tons per acre per decade in many western forests. When ignitions finally escaped initial attack, they encountered continuous ladders of dead wood that carried flames into the canopy, producing crown fires whose heat sterilized soils to depths of several inches. Within decades, stands that had once been open forest carried many times their historical density of trees, each competing for the same water and nutrients. The 1988 Yellowstone fires, which burned nearly 800,000 acres of the park despite the agency’s full suppression effort, marked the first nationally visible demonstration that decades of exclusion had altered fire behavior., marked the first nationally visible demonstration that decades of exclusion had altered fire behavior. Subsequent megafires—the 2002 Biscuit Fire, the 2013 Rim Fire, and the 2020 August Complex—each consumed hundreds of thousands of acres and released carbon stores accumulated over a century of fire absence. and released carbon stores accumulated over a century of fire absence. The Forest Service’s fire-suppression budget, roughly 16 percent of total agency spending in 1995, rose above 50 percent by 2020, diverting resources from reforestation and thinning projects that might have reduced future risk.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
In 1968 the Forest Service began limited experiments with prescribed fire in the South and Southwest, and the 1976 National Forest Management Act formally recognized fire as a natural process on some lands. Yet the accumulated fuel load made reintroduction difficult; escaped prescribed burns such as the 2000 Cerro Grande fire near Los Alamos prompted stricter rules and liability concerns that slowed the practice. Today the agency treats a few million acres annually with prescribed fire or mechanical thinning, while tens of millions of acres remain in need of treatment. Recent legislation, including the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, has allocated new funding for fuel reduction, but the scale of the backlog and the narrow window of acceptable weather conditions continue to constrain progress.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Systems that reward the immediate absence of a visible harm often accumulate the conditions for larger harm later. When success is measured solely by the count of fires extinguished rather than by the state of the fuel complex, organizations lose the feedback that would prompt earlier course corrections. The same pattern appears whenever a single metric—student test scores, quarterly earnings, or zero visible homelessness—drives policy without reference to the broader system those metrics are meant to serve. The remaining question is whether institutions can shift resources toward the slower, less photogenic work of restoring process before the next ignition arrives.
