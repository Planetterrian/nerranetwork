Today's story is one of those rare examples where the cure was almost worse than the disease,

Seatbelt laws save drivers by the thousands, yet feeling safer may have led some to drive
faster and shift risk onto everyone else.
In the spring of 1985, New York became the first American state to require drivers and
front seat passengers to buckle up.
Within months, observed belt use jumped from roughly 16% to over 50% in many areas.
At the same time, some traffic engineers quietly noted that average speeds on certain highways
began to edge upward, and the number of pedestrian injuries in several cities did not fall as
sharply as the drop in driver fatalities might have predicted.
The question that lingered was whether those two patterns were connected, or whether
the rise in speeds was simply the result of unrelated changes in traffic volume or road
conditions that happened to coincide with the new rules.
The three-point seat belt itself was the work of Volvo engineer Niels Bolin, who filed
the patent in 1959 after studying crash data that showed unbelted occupants were frequently
ejected or slammed into dashboards.
Swedish and American regulators later required belts in new cars, and by the late 1960s
every new passenger vehicle sold in the United States came equipped with them.
Public health advocates and safety researchers saw the device as a straightforward engineering
solution to a well-documented problem.
Roughly 50,000 Americans were dying in crashes each year, most of them unbelted.
The logic was simple and humane, prevent the second collision inside the vehicle.
Few people questioned whether protecting the person behind the wheel might alter that person's
behavior toward others on the road. Regulators at the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration
focused on the measurable physics of deceleration forces distributed across the pelvis and shoulder,
rather than on any possible downstream change in driver psychology.
At the time, the dominant model treated the driver as a fixed element whose behavior
would remain constant once the car's interior safety features improved.
One objection that occasionally surfaced was whether people would simply stop wearing the belts
once the novelty wore off, but the early data from places that made wearing compulsory
suggested compliance rose quickly when enforcement was present.
Australia's state of Victoria led the way with the world's first mandatory wearing law
in December 1970. Belt use rose quickly and driver death rates fell.
Similar statutes spread through Europe and Canada in the 1970s and 1980s.
In the United States, New York's 1984 law was followed by more than 30 states within five years.
Early evaluations from Victoria and from several US states reported 10-15% reductions in driver
and front seat passenger fatalities. Those numbers aligned with laboratory crash tests.
A few economists, however, warned that the same sense of security might change driving habits.
University of Chicago professor Sam Peltzman published a 1975 paper arguing that earlier federal
safety standards had already produced measurable risk compensation.
Proponents of the laws countered that any behavioral shift would be small compared with the direct
mechanical protection the belts provided. They pointed to the rapid rise in observed usage rates
as evidence that enforcement was working as intended. The counter argument often took the form
of asking how large any offset could really be when the mechanical benefit was so clearly documented
in controlled tests. The reply from the compensation side was that the offset did not need to erase
the entire gain to still matter for how risk was shared across different groups of road users.
Peltzman's analysis suggested that roughly half the expected safety gain from mandated equipment
was offset by more aggressive driving. Later studies of belt laws produced smaller but still detectable
offsets. Estimates range from 10-40% of the life-saving effect depending on the data set and the
measure used. Some researchers documented modest increases in average speeds or following distances
after laws took effect. Others found rises in single vehicle crashes or in injuries to pedestrians
and cyclists. The proposed mechanism was behavioral rather than mechanical. Drivers who felt the belt
and the car's other safety features had their back accepted slightly higher levels of risk.
Because the offset fell most heavily on people outside the vehicle, the net social benefit
remained strongly positive. Tens of thousands of occupant lives were saved annually in the United
States alone. Yet the distribution of risk had shifted. Cyclists in particular noticed the change
in some cities. One 1990 study in California found that counties with higher belt use also recorded
higher rates of cyclist injuries per mile traveled. Though causation remained contested and other
factors such as rising traffic volume played a role. The arithmetic works roughly like this.
If a law is projected to prevent 1000 occupant deaths, but drivers respond by increasing speed enough
to cause an additional 200-300 crashes involving vulnerable road users, the gross gain is still large
while the externalized portion of the risk becomes visible in separate data sets. Critics of the
compensation hypothesis correctly note that overall fatality rates continue to decline after belt
laws. That aggregate improvement does not rule out a partial offset. It simply shows that the direct
protective effect outweighed the behavioral response. The deeper question is whether the same drivers who
now survive their own crashes were also the ones whose marginal increase in speed or inattention
produced injuries elsewhere in the system. Highway agencies responded not by repealing belt laws,
but by pairing them with speed limit enforcement, automated cameras, and public campaigns aimed at
pedestrians and cyclists. Airbag mandates in the 1990s triggered a second round of the same debate.
Again, the data showed large net reductions in fatalities despite some behavioral adjustment.
Today, every US state except New Hampshire requires adult belt use.
Observed wearing rates exceed 90% in most places. Researchers continue to test risk compensation
hypotheses with naturalistic driving data and randomized trials of new safety technologies
such as automatic emergency braking. The pattern that emerges is consistent but modest.
Safety features save far more lives than they indirectly endanger, yet they rarely deliver their
full theoretical benefit once human behavior adjusts. Enforcement and infrastructure changes have
become the standard tools for containing the size of any offset. Any safety intervention that lowers
the perceived cost of risky behavior will on the margin invite more of that behavior. The size of
the response varies but is rarely zero, because the people who gain protection and the people who
bear new risk are often different groups. Even a policy with large overall benefits can redistribute
harm in ways that deserve measurement. The practical question for designers and regulators is therefore
not whether compensation occurs, but how large it is and whether flanking measures such as enforcement,
infrastructure, or feedback to the driver can keep the offset small. When the next generation of
vehicle automation promises near perfect crash avoidance, the same test will apply.
