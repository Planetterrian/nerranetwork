In two thousand nine, ADM Elektrik began mounting special life platforms on distribution poles in Söke, Aydın, to give white storks safe nesting sites away from live conductors.

The measure spread to other distribution companies and, by the tenth year of the program, covered Aydın, Denizli and Muğla.

Engin Öztürk, environment and urbanization general director in Edirne province, described the problem officials saw in villages along Saros Bay.

Storks returning each spring built nests directly on cross-arms and insulators, where their wings or nesting material touched energized parts.

In November twenty twenty the Provincial Directorate of Environment and Urbanization began fitting portable nesting platforms on poles together with teams from the local electricity distribution company.

The stated goals were to stop birds from being caught in electrical current, to cut the number of malfunctions on rural lines, and to let repair crews work more efficiently when faults did occur.

At the time the region was known to host about fifty thousand migratory birds, so keeping nests off live equipment looked like a straightforward way to protect both wildlife and supply reliability.

ADM Elektrik had already given that two thousand nine effort the name A New Home for Storks, placed platforms on poles in western Turkey, and later reported that the Ministry of Energy and Natural Resources had asked other distributors to copy the approach.

Officials treated the platforms as an accommodation that worked with the birds' known habit of returning to the same site each year, rather than an attempt to remove poles from the landscape.

By twenty twenty the Edirne teams had installed the first portable nests on poles in protected breeding areas.

In the Thrace region, TREDAŞ, the distribution company owned by IC Holding, began its own platform program around the same period and, by the mid-twenty twenties, had placed one thousand one hundred ninety-six platforms along its lines while also adding conductor insulation.

ADM Elektrik and its affiliate GDZ Elektrik continued annual pre-migration maintenance on platforms in Aydın, Denizli and Muğla, cleaning debris and adding insulation near nests.

Those companies reported fewer emergency call-outs for nests that had previously shorted lines.

National media gave the work positive coverage and framed it as an example of utility and nature cooperation.

Partnerships formed with Trakya University and Ege University for monitoring, and the Ministry of Energy circulated the ADM model to other operators.

Once many storks used the new platforms, nest density on individual poles rose sharply, because each platform offered a stable, elevated site that several pairs sometimes adopted or that successive seasons enlarged.

TEPAV's October twenty twenty-six policy note on storks, power lines and fire risk observed that these concentrated nests increased the mass and wind load on single structures, so that during storms a nest could shift, drop wet material onto conductors below, or cause flashovers that earlier scattered nests had been less likely to produce.

The same note recorded that bird-related contact remained a leading cause of faults on Turkish high-voltage lines, with one technical study counting two thousand six hundred forty-five such events between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-four, most attributed to migrating white storks.

Because platforms kept birds on the network rather than moving them elsewhere, the electrocution risk moved from the cross-arm to the edge of the platform or to neighboring equipment when fledglings fluttered.

Rural customers served by lighter distribution lines experienced more frequent and longer outages when a single large nest faulted a pole that now carried several nests instead of one.

TREDAŞ data later showed that insulation and platforms together reduced deaths at monitored eagle nests, yet the density effect on stork nests persisted where platforms were the main intervention.

Heavier nests required more frequent mechanical checks, which raised maintenance costs, and storm-related faults triggered automatic reclosers that sometimes started small fires in dry vegetation beneath the lines.

The original decision to treat each pole as an independent nesting site had not accounted for the way attractive platforms would cluster activity that had once been dispersed across many unsuitable locations.

By late twenty twenty-six TEPAV recommended that insulation, markers, safe perches and platforms be used together rather than platforms alone, and that national risk maps incorporate migration routes.

TREDAŞ expanded its program to include G P S tracking of birds and additional conductor covering, reporting no electrocutions at forty-six monitored imperial eagle nests in twenty twenty-five.

ADM Elektrik and GDZ Elektrik continued annual platform maintenance and insulation work, now framed as part of a broader effort that also addressed climate-driven shifts in stork routes.

No large-scale removal of platforms has been reported, and operators treat the structures as permanent habitat features that must be managed with other engineering controls.

Decision-makers who change only the most visible contact point have to model how that change redistributes nests across the rest of the network, not only whether deaths at the old contact point decline. Fault probability on the pole that becomes the preferred site can rise even when the wildlife tally improves.

Counting saved birds and counting reliable minutes of rural supply are different ledgers, and a program scored on only one of them can look successful while the other slips. Utilities modernizing lines now face that test whenever a retrofit makes one structure safer or more attractive than its neighbors.

In today's grid-modernization projects, does the chosen retrofit alter where animals or people gather, or does it merely move the original hazard to a new location?
