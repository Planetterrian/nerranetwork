> **Planting 10,000 hectares of conifers yearly in Ireland drained peat bogs and released stored carbon.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1948 Seán MacBride campaigned for a national target of planting 10,000 hectares of trees each year to create rural jobs and a domestic timber supply. The policy was later carried out on large areas of blanket bog west of the Shannon, where drainage was required before the trees could grow. This was designed to increase forest cover and meet timber goals. Instead, it caused widespread lowering of water tables and continuous release of carbon from the peat.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Bulmer Hobson first outlined the idea in a 1936 article in Ireland To-Day, arguing that fast-growing conifers could turn the western Gaeltacht into a prosperous national forest. Seán MacBride took up the same goal when he ran in the 1948 general election and later, as Minister for External Affairs, secured cabinet support for the 10,000-hectare annual planting rate. At the time Ireland planted only about 2,400 hectares a year, and the country still imported most of its timber. MacBride and his Fine Gael colleague Gerard Sweetman presented the programme as a way to bring industry and employment to remote rural districts that had few other economic options. The land west of the Shannon was cheap and available, and non-native Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine were known to grow quickly once established. No one at the time measured the carbon stored in the underlying peat or considered how drainage would affect it. The arithmetic was straightforward: each additional hectare planted moved Ireland closer to self-sufficiency in wood products while creating measurable employment in planting crews, nurseries and haulage. Post-war rural Ireland offered limited alternatives, so directing state funds toward visible hectares and jobs made sense on the information then available.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
In 1951 the state purchased heavy ploughs and tractors that allowed foresters to drain deeper into blanket bog and higher up the hills than before. Nearly half the new plantations went onto these western peat soils. One early site was the 343-hectare block in the townlands of Derryclare and Cloonnacarton in the Inagh valley of Connemara, where furrows were cut, water was channelled away, and rock phosphate was spread before the spruce and pine were planted. The Department of Agriculture later formalised grant schemes that paid up to 100 percent of establishment costs plus annual premiums for conifer planting. Early reports counted the hectares planted and the jobs created in nurseries and fencing crews; those numbers rose steadily through the 1950s and 1960s. The machinery let crews create continuous drainage channels spaced to keep the root zone aerated long enough for seedlings to survive the first winters. Because the grants covered every step from ploughing to fencing, landowners faced no direct cost for the drainage work itself. Success was measured in the annual tally of new hectares and the volume of timber projected decades ahead, not in changes to water levels or soil chemistry.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Drainage lowered the water table across the planted bogs, allowing oxygen to reach the peat and begin its oxidation. At the Connemara site the disturbed peat now emits up to 1,200 tonnes of carbon each year, according to measurements reported by The Irish Times in 2024. Dr Kenneth A. Byrne of the University of Limerick told the Joint Committee on Environment and Climate Action in November 2022 that peat soils make up 38.7 percent of Irish forests and that forested blanket peatlands emit 1.68 tonnes of CO2 per hectare per year. The same drainage networks dried out adjacent unplanted bog, reducing its sponge-like capacity to hold water and support specialised plants. Phosphorus runoff from the fertiliser applications acidified streams and harmed Atlantic salmon and freshwater pearl mussel populations. Windthrow increased on the exposed, poorly rooted trees, and the remaining timber often proved low-value. Enable Research noted in March 2026 that 80 percent of Irish peatlands are now degraded, with state-supported afforestation listed as a continuing pressure. The Irish Independent reported in April 2025 that all Irish peatland habitats already hold unfavourable conservation status, with afforestation identified as one of the main drivers. The causal sequence begins with the decision to plant on the lowest-cost land; that land happened to be peat whose carbon had remained locked away only while water excluded oxygen. Once drains lowered the water table by even a few tens of centimetres, microbial activity accelerated and the stored carbon began converting to CO2 at a steady rate. Tree growth sequesters some carbon above ground, yet the soil emissions continue year after year regardless of whether the timber is harvested or blown down. Neighbouring unplanted areas lose their saturated condition because the drainage ditches do not stop at plantation boundaries, so the drying effect spreads outward. Runoff carrying the added phosphate reaches streams already stressed by acidification, reducing populations of species that require stable water chemistry. Over decades the cumulative result is a landscape whose original carbon store is diminished while the planted trees deliver lower-than-expected timber volumes.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Restoration projects now block drains and remove trees on selected sites, yet The Irish Times observed in 2024 that simply felling the conifers does not quickly restore the original hydrology or carbon sink function. A study of 8,000 hectares of low-productivity Coillte peatland forests found that rewetting can temporarily increase emissions before any long-term benefit appears. Ireland has been referred to the EU Court of Justice for failing to protect bog habitats, and current grant schemes still offer payments for some conifer planting on marginal land. Researchers continue to weigh whether soil-carbon losses on any given site can be offset by tree growth, with results depending on local drainage patterns and adjacent land use. Blocking drains on one parcel can raise water levels on neighbouring farmland, creating new conflicts over land usability. The same grant structure that once rewarded drainage now funds some restoration work, yet the underlying rule of paying per hectare planted remains in place for other forest types.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a subsidy pays per hectare planted and sets volume targets without measuring soil carbon change, landowners rationally choose the cheapest land and the drainage methods that let the trees survive. In this case the cheapest land was peat bog whose stored carbon became an uncounted cost once water was removed. Decision-makers today can test any area-based incentive against the measurable carbon or water effects it will trigger on that exact soil type before the first grant is approved.
