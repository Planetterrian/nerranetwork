On May 1, 2016 Prime Minister Narendra Modi stood in Baliya, in Uttar Pradesh and launched the Pradhan Mantri Ujwala Yojana.
The promise was free-liquified petroleum gas connections, LPG, for 50 million poor households.
Women would no longer have to cook over wood and dung.
The problem the scheme was built to answer was not abstract.
Indoor air pollution from solid fuels was estimated to cause hundreds of thousands of premature
deaths each year.
Women and children took the hardest hit because the smoke lived in the room where the meal
was made.
Moti's government, like earlier administrations, treated LPG as the practical, clean fuel already
sitting inside most districts.
Cylinders were already moving to rural dealers.
Distribution networks already reached most blocks.
What looked missing was the first payment, not a new market and not a new forest policy.
So the scheme aimed at that first barrier.

The Comptroller and Auditor General later looked at what happened after the connection arrived.
Among beneficiaries who had held a connection for more than a year, average annual refills
stood at 3.66 cylinders as of March 31, 2018.
By December 31, 2018, that average had fallen to 3.21.
A refill lasts roughly a month for a typical household.
Three cylinders therefore covered only about three months of cooking.
The other nine months had to be fueled somehow.
A study of 4,994 households across 500 villages in the Central Indian Highlands landscape,
published in 2021 in Energy Research and Social Science, found LPG owning families still gathering
and burning firewood. Researchers called the pattern fuel stacking, households kept the
free connection, purchased LPG became a supplement, not a replacement. Three pressures held the
older fuel in daily rotation, refill costs, irregular cash income, and would nearby that carried a
price of zero. When a household's cash arrives in one or two harvest sales, several hundred rupees for
a cylinder in a lean month sits next to school fees and medical costs. Those bills do not wait for the
next crop. Wood that can be gathered in an afternoon has no cash price. On most days
the wood wins. The survey also showed that households with more forest within easy
walking distance were more likely to keep collecting every day, even after the LPG connection
arrived. The marginal cost of another trip remained zero. Distance to trees mattered as much
as the paper in the woman's name. That is a second order effect the connection count
could not see. The scheme had prioritized poor and forest fringe households because the
smoke burden was heaviest there. Those were also the places where free wood was closest,
and where cash was most seasonal. The groups named first for the hardware were the groups for
whom daily replacement was hardest. There was a partial gain, and it should be said plainly.
Longer ownership correlated with fewer collection hours. For some families, total firewood volume
did fall. The drop was gradual. It never reached full displacement for the great majority.
A connection could shave hours off a wood trip and still leave a wood fire as the ordinary
way to cook. Health gains that depend on the fire going out do not arrive on the same schedule
as a handover. The audit found another break in the chain. 198,000 beneficiaries averaged more than
12 cylinders a year. That volume is improbable for a domestic kitchen. It suggested some cylinders
were being diverted to commercial kitchens. Multiple daily refills at single bottle connections
pointed the same way. Some subsidized fuel left the domestic system entirely. That is a third
order effect. Cylinders diverted to commercial use are cylinders no longer available for the
household's the scheme named. Any simple count of health gains from connections delivered becomes
harder to read, because some of the subsidized fuel never stayed in the room where the smoke was
supposed to fall. None of this required bad faith at the launch in Bollyah. It required
a price, a season, and a forest that the connection record did not contain. The audit prompted
tighter checks on refill patterns. Emphasis shifted, at least in the language of review,
toward sustained use rather than connection counts alone. Later government updates still reported
cumulative connections past the original 80 million target. Independent surveys kept finding
the same stacking in forest adjacent districts.
No large-scale reversal took the hardware back.
State programs began experimenting instead.
Targeted subsidies for additional cylinders, community-level alternatives to firewood.
The core design stayed as it was.
Pay once for access, then charge a market price for the fuel.
Follow-up surveys in the same Central Indian Highlands landscape kept recording the same
split.
The probability of daily LPG use stayed lowest among the poorest households, and the
most forest adjacent households. Those were exactly the groups the scheme had put first
in line for the initial connection. The fix did not undo the metric that had made the
program look finished.
Titer checks can catch a suspicious refill pattern. They do not, by themselves, put cash in a
lean month, or put a price on an afternoon in the forest. Experiments with extra cylinder
subsidies and community firewood alternatives were an admission that the second cylinder
was the real test.
They were also still small next to a national design that treated the first handover
as the intervention.
Where things stood after the audit was not a cancelled scheme.
It was a scheme still adding names, besides surveys that still found wood and daily use,
in the places the names had been meant to protect.
