In twenty eighteen the House of Commons Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee, the EFRA Committee, examined hospital records.

The nineteen ninety-one Dangerous Dogs Act had banned four types outright and placed strict controls on any others thought to be fighting dogs.

The measure was written to remove dangerous animals from British streets.

Instead the stock of powerful dogs simply changed shape.

To see why that happened, you have to stand where ministers stood in the spring of nineteen ninety-one.

The Act was drafted after a cluster of serious attacks.

Ministers faced public pressure to act quickly on what appeared to be a new and identifiable threat.

That pressure was not abstract.

People had been hurt. Families were frightened. The dogs in the worst cases seemed to share a look.

The four types named were the pit bull terrier type, the Japanese Tosa, the Dogo Argentino, and the Fila Brasileiro.

Those four were seen as the core of the problem.

An exemption register was added so that existing, well-managed dogs could stay with their owners.

The conditions were muzzling, insurance, and neutering.

At the moment of passage the policy looked like a targeted, evidence-based response to a narrow set of animals.

It did not look like a broad attack on all large dogs.

The arithmetic seemed straightforward.

Remove the animals whose appearance matched the recent fatalities, and the risk would fall in proportion.

Officials could point to physical markers.

Broad skulls. Muscular frames. Short coats.

Because the markers were observable on the street, enforcement could begin without waiting for behavioural records that many owners did not keep.

That practical point mattered.

A law that needed a paper trail of how every dog had been housed would have been slow to start.

A law that a constable could apply by looking at a dog could start at once.

The exemption scheme softened the measure further.

The design tried to avoid the charge that every animal of a given appearance would be seized regardless of history.
