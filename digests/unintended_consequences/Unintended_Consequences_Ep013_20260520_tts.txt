This is a show about what happens when smart, well-meaning people try to fix something — and the world fixes back.

A designer removed one click to make browsing smoother in 2006 — and helped build the endless feeds that now shape how billions spend their time.

In the spring of 2006, Aza Raskin sat at a desk testing a new way to move through long lists of search results.

Instead of clicking a “next” button to load another page, the content would simply continue as the user scrolled downward.

The change felt like a small courtesy, a way to keep someone from breaking their train of thought.

Within a decade the same pattern, paired with automatic video playback, would turn brief visits into hours-long sessions across phones and laptops worldwide.

Aza Raskin, then a young interface designer, wanted to reduce the mechanical friction that interrupted reading or searching.

At the time most websites still required users to click pagination links to see more results, an action that felt dated once broadband connections became common.

Raskin saw the extra click as an unnecessary interruption rather than a deliberate pause.

YouTube engineers later applied a similar logic in 2015 when they introduced auto-play.

They hoped to spare viewers the small decision of choosing the next video.

Both groups worked inside companies that measured success by how easily people could keep moving through content.

Their choices reflected the dominant design principle of the era — remove every obstacle that might cause a user to stop.

Infinite scroll first appeared in internal prototypes at Mozilla and was soon adopted by other sites that needed to display large result sets.

Early tests showed users reached more items without the visible break of a new page load.

YouTube rolled auto-play out globally in 2015 after smaller experiments on its recommendation bar.

Product teams reported that completed view counts rose quickly because the next video began before the viewer could close the tab.

At both companies the features were presented internally as straightforward improvements to user experience.

Few public objections surfaced in the first years.

The changes aligned with the broader industry move toward continuous, mobile-friendly interfaces.

Once the two mechanisms operated together, the absence of any stopping cue produced longer sessions than either team had modeled.

A user who intended to watch one clip or check a single update could continue without ever forming a new intention to stop.

Platforms optimized recommendation systems to exploit this continuous state, selecting the next item for maximum likelihood of another view.

Over time the design contributed to measurable shifts in daily time spent on social and video platforms, with some internal metrics showing average session lengths doubling within two years of wider adoption.

Second-order effects appeared in attention patterns.

People reported difficulty returning to tasks after opening an app for a quick check.

Parents and educators noted younger users struggling to regulate screen time when the interface itself supplied no natural endpoint.

Raskin later described watching his own creation spread and realizing the scroll never asked whether the user still wanted to keep going.

The original goal of removing friction had removed the very moments at which someone might reconsider their choice.

By the late 2010s Raskin began speaking publicly about the regret, describing infinite scroll as an invention he wished he could retract.

Some platforms introduced optional time-limit reminders and “take a break” prompts, though these features remained secondary to the default continuous experience.

YouTube added an auto-play toggle after user complaints, yet the setting stayed on for most accounts.

Researchers and advocacy groups now study the cumulative impact of frictionless design on sleep, productivity, and mental health, while product teams continue to test small variations that preserve engagement.

The core pattern persists across new services because the underlying incentive to increase time-on-platform has not changed.

Design choices that lower the cost of continuing an activity will reliably increase how long people stay with it, even when no one intends that outcome.

Once a system removes every natural breakpoint, later attempts to restore limits must compete against habits already formed.

Teams that treat each incremental reduction in friction as neutral should ask what behavior the new default will reward at scale.

Consider the quiet rollout of always-on recommendation feeds inside workplace collaboration tools today.

The same logic that once removed a single click now removes the moment an employee might decide to step away from the screen after answering one message.

What signal would you watch for, inside the next convenience you are about to ship, that tells you the default has already begun to shape how long people remain?
