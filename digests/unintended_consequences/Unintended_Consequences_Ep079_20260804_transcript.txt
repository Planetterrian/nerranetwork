Maya Bay's coral covered declined by roughly 80% in the decade after the film,
according to surveys by Thailand's Department of National Parks. The causal chain was straight
forward. Zero or near-zero marginal cost for each additional visitor, combined with visible
social proof on social media, produced daily loads that exceeded the ecological and
infrastructural thresholds of the sites. An increase from a few hundred visitors to 5,000
represents roughly a 10-fold to 20-fold rise in daily foot traffic, boat moorings, and chemical
inputs. All of this was concentrated on a cove whose coral and sediment systems had evolved
under far lower disturbance rates. Residents left historic centers removing the very local life
that many tourists had hoped to encounter. Emergency closures then shifted pressure to
neighboring sites that lacked even basic management plans. Revenue accrued to short-term
operators and platforms while the costs of erosion control, rescue services, and habitat repair
remained with public agencies and the remaining residents. Thailand closed Maya Bay in June 2018
for four years. It reopened in 2022 with a daily cap of 375 visitors and a requirement that

in 2024, paired with a reservation system. Enforcement remains limited to a few entry points.
Iceland has added parking fees and timed entry at several highland sites and has funded
trail hardening with a portion of the revenue. Peru now requires advanced tickets for Machu Picchu
and has caped daily entries near 4000. These measures have reduced peak loads at the most
stressed locations. Yet they have also concentrated demand on still open alternatives and created administrative
costs that smaller municipalities struggle to meet. Some residents note that the fees generate
revenue without restoring the quieter character of the places before mass visitation began.

the cost of each additional visitor falls almost entirely on the resource itself rather than on
the visitor or the platform that directs them there. Pricing, quotas, and physical limits can
