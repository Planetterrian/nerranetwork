The law grew out of decades of rural unrest.

Repeated attempts at land reform had left tenancy rates high.

Families who tilled large estates still did not hold them.

In June of nineteen eighty-eight, Congress passed the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Law to transfer those estates to the families who worked them.

President Corazon Aquino signed Republic Act sixty-six fifty-seven on the tenth of June, nineteen eighty-eight.

Her own family owned Hacienda Luisita, in Tarlac.

The nineteen eighty-six People Power revolt had raised expectations that concentrated landholdings would finally be broken up.

Signing a redistribution statute, with that family name attached to one of the estates in view, was the political answer to those expectations.

Lawmakers still had a practical fear.

They believed compulsory subdivision of integrated plantations would disrupt production.

New owners, they argued, might be left without credit or equipment.

Many estates already ran as integrated sugar or rice operations, with shared irrigation and shared milling.

Breaking those links risked lowering output at a moment when the post-Marcos economy needed steady agricultural exports.

So Section thirty-one offered another route.

A corporate plantation could stay intact.

Workers would receive minority shares, and a production bonus, instead of title to particular fields.

The Department of Agrarian Reform and the Presidential Agrarian Reform Council treated that option as one legitimate path to the law’s stated goal of land to the tiller.

The arithmetic they worked with was straightforward.

A single large corporation could issue shares representing the land’s value.

Those shares could be spread across thousands of workers over thirty years.

The corporation could still meet production targets that small plots might not sustain.

By allowing the stock route, the framers hoped to avoid political backlash from landowners.

They also hoped to avoid the administrative burden of surveying and titling every hectare at once.

Read on its own terms, the clause was a compromise between speed, efficiency, and a promise of ownership.

It assumed that a share in the company that held the land was close enough to a share of the land itself.

Hacienda Luisita, Incorporated submitted its stock-distribution plan in nineteen eighty-nine.

A referendum that year recorded ninety-two point nine percent approval among farmworker beneficiaries, according to Department of Agrarian Reform records.

That figure, as the department recorded it, became the democratic warrant for the plan.

Workers received thirty-three point two nine six percent of the shares, distributed over thirty years, plus a three percent production share.

The share percentage was calculated from the ratio of the land’s declared value to the corporation’s total equity.

Farmworkers technically owned a slice of the company rather than individual parcels.

The remaining equity stayed with the original shareholders.

The Cojuangco family therefore continued to elect the board and to set land-use policy.

The three percent production share was an annual cash distribution tied to gross sales.

It was meant to give workers immediate income while the stock vested.

The Presidential Agrarian Reform Council approved the plan.

Similar arrangements were later adopted on other corporate estates.

Government reports at the time highlighted the rapid enrollment of large holdings under the new mechanism.

Early tallies counted these plans as successful coverage under the law.

The paperwork showed workers listed as beneficiaries on corporate books.

From the agency side, that listing looked like progress.

A vast estate had entered the program without a survey crew walking every boundary, and without splitting mills and canals into fragments.

From the family side, the corporation remained one decision-maker.

Majority voting control never left the original owners.

Skeptics of physical breakup had a clean reply in those early years: production links stayed whole, and workers had both a vesting schedule and a cash bonus tied to sales.

What the enrollment numbers could not show was who would sign the next permit.

Because the stock plan left the Cojuangco family with majority voting control, day-to-day management stayed with the original owners.

So did land-use decisions.

The causal chain ran through the voting arithmetic.

With only about one-third of the shares, workers could never outvote the family on whether to sell or rezone land.

When the corporation later sought conversion permits for non-agricultural use, the minority stake gave workers no formal seat at the table.

Portions of the estate were reclassified for industrial parks and sold.

Additional hectares were taken for the Subic-Clark-Tarlac Expressway.

That expressway alone removed several hundred hectares from agricultural production.

The compensation paid to the corporation flowed to the majority shareholders.

Philippine Institute for Development Studies researchers Marife Ballesteros and colleagues later documented how the same mechanism worked beyond this one estate.

Together with conversion permits, and with registration of excess land to relatives, it allowed several estates to avoid physical redistribution.

Farmworkers who held minority shares received dividends that fluctuated with corporate performance.

They did not receive secure title they could mortgage or pass to heirs.

Workers who had expected eventual land titles found themselves holding paper whose value depended on corporate profitability rather than on the rising price of farmland itself.

Over time, some families sold their vested shares for immediate cash.

Those sales concentrated ownership further back inside the original corporate structure.

A two thousand four strike by the Alyansa ng mga Manggagawang Bukid ng Hacienda Luisita brought renewed attention to the gap between promised benefits and actual control.

The pattern was not a one-off failure of paperwork.

The law’s alternative compliance path rewarded owners who maintained corporate form while the physical land stayed under unified management.

On other estates where stock plans were approved, the same reward applied.

Issue the minority slice.

Count the workers as covered.

Decide, as the board, what the land becomes.

That is a second-order effect of measuring success by enrollment rather than by who holds the vote.

A third-order effect followed when land left agriculture.

Once parcels were converted, leased, or taken for a highway, the thing workers might someday have titled was no longer the same estate they had been told they were joining.

Dividends could rise or fall with whatever the corporation earned after that change.

They could not restore a field that had already been rezoned.

The Presidential Agrarian Reform Council’s two thousand five revocation order said the stock plan had not done what the statute required.

The Supreme Court’s rulings in two thousand eleven and two thousand twelve confirmed that the plan had not transferred effective ownership.

Those decisions did not invent a new theory of control.

They read the voting arithmetic that had been visible since the shares were issued.

After the Supreme Court decisions, compulsory acquisition proceeded on the remaining agricultural land.

The last Certificates of Land Ownership Award for Hacienda Luisita were issued in twenty eighteen.

That distribution occurred under compulsory acquisition orders that valued the land as of the nineteen eighty-nine taking date.

Compensation therefore reflected earlier prices rather than current market rates.

Even after the shares were set aside, some converted parcels had already been sold or leased for industrial use.

The redistributed agricultural remainder was smaller than the original estate.

The Ballesteros study for the Philippine Institute for Development Studies found that the overall program had distributed roughly four point seven million hectares by twenty sixteen.

Yet many large estates remained intact through the very options the nineteen eighty-eight law had permitted.

The review noted that aggregate hectares distributed looked substantial.

The persistence of large contiguous holdings showed that evasion routes built into the original statute had limited the program’s structural impact.

Subsequent administrations tightened conversion rules.

They removed the stock-distribution provision from later extensions of the program.

The fix arrived after the alternative path had already done its work on the ground.

A revocation can unwind a plan that is still a plan.

It cannot un-sell a parcel that has changed hands, or put back hectares already taken for a road.

When Congress wrote the stock-distribution clause into the law, it rewarded landowners who could keep corporate control while satisfying the letter of redistribution.

Later regulators responded by revoking plans that left voting power unchanged.

That sequence shows that an ownership rule has to be measured by who actually decides land use, not by whose name appears on a beneficiary list.

The same pattern appears whenever a reform statute creates an alternative compliance path without requiring a transfer of decision rights.

Coverage can look complete on a ledger while the board that signs the permit never changes.

A cash bonus tied to sales can arrive on time and still leave the land-use vote where it started.

If you are watching a present-day housing, farm, or corporate-governance bill that offers shares, units, or paper stakes in place of the asset itself, the tell is simple.

Ask whether the new arrangement changes the identity of the person or the board that signs the next permit.

If it does not, the statute has created a record of inclusion without moving control.
