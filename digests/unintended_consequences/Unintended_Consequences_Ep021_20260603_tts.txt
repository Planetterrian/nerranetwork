In nineteen ninety five, Jerry Dewayne Williams took a slice of pepperoni pizza from a group of children at a Redondo Beach restaurant.

He received a sentence of twenty five years to life under California's new three-strikes statute.

The law had been written to incapacitate repeat violent offenders after high-profile murders.

Yet Williams's prior convictions were for robbery, not homicide or rape.

Within a few years the same statute produced a fifty-to-life term for Leandro Andrade after he shoplifted nine videotapes from two Kmart stores in Ontario, California.

The modern three-strikes movement began in Washington state in nineteen ninety three with Initiative five hundred ninety three.

It was drafted after the murder of Diane Ballasiotes by a repeat offender on work release.

California followed in nineteen ninety four with Proposition one hundred eighty four.

That measure was placed on the ballot after the abduction and killing of twelve-year-old Polly Klaas by Richard Allen Davis, who had an extensive prior record.

Sponsors argued that a small number of habitual offenders committed a disproportionate share of serious crimes.

They believed mandatory long sentences would both incapacitate those individuals and deter others.

At the time, FBI data showed violent crime rates still near their early-nineteen-nineties peak, and public fear was intense.

Polls indicated overwhelming support for any measure labeled tough on crime.

Legislators and voters therefore treated the policy as a straightforward application of selective incapacitation theory that criminologists had discussed since the nineteen seventies.

Washington's law took effect in December nineteen ninety three.

California's passed with seventy two percent of the vote in November nineteen ninety four and applied retroactively to existing felony convictions.

Prosecutors in both states gained wide discretion over which prior felonies counted as strikes.

California's version defined a strike as any felony rather than only serious or violent ones.

Early evaluations in nineteen ninety five and nineteen ninety six reported drops in felony filings for individuals already labeled two-strike defendants.

Proponents cited those drops as evidence of deterrence.

Skeptics at the time, including some district attorneys and the California Legislative Analyst's Office, warned that the broad definition of strikes would sweep in property and drug cases once offenders accumulated three felonies of any kind.

By two thousand two California's prison population had roughly doubled from its nineteen ninety four level, reaching more than one hundred sixty thousand inmates.

Three-strikes sentences accounted for a substantial share of the growth.

Because the law treated many nonviolent felonies as third strikes, an estimated sixty five percent of California three-strikes prisoners were serving time for property, drug, or other nonviolent offenses.

The pizza case of Jerry Dewayne Williams and the videotape case of Leandro Andrade became national symbols.

Andrade's sentence was upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court in two thousand three.

The causal mechanism was mechanical.

Once two prior strikes existed, any new felony, no matter how minor, triggered a sentence of twenty five years to life with limited good-time credits.

This removed ordinary plea-bargaining leverage for low-level cases and produced long sentences for conduct that previously drew county jail time or short prison terms.

Second-order effects included sharply higher corrections budgets.

Overcrowding triggered federal court intervention in two thousand nine.

Prison beds were diverted away from the very violent offenders the law had targeted.

County prosecutors also adapted by filing more cases as felonies to create future strikes, further widening the net.

By two thousand ten, voter and legislative concern over costs and fairness produced Proposition thirty six.

Voters approved it in November two thousand twelve by a sixty nine percent margin.

The measure narrowed third-strike eligibility to serious or violent felonies and allowed resentencing for some inmates already serving life terms.

Roughly two thousand people received reduced sentences after the reform.

Thousands more remained incarcerated under the original statute.

The rollback itself generated new litigation over retroactivity and eligibility criteria.

California's prison population later declined.

The state still operates under federal population caps first imposed during the three-strikes era.

Broad mandatory-sentencing rules interact with existing plea practices and charging discretion in ways that are difficult to predict from the statute's text alone.

When a single additional conviction can multiply sentence length by an order of magnitude, even modest changes in what counts as a felony produce large shifts in incarceration volume.

Modern risk-assessment tools and diversion programs attempt to target the same goal of selective incapacitation with narrower eligibility criteria.

The three-strikes experience suggests those tools must be evaluated against actual filing and plea data rather than intended categories.
