> **Post-war mortgage subsidies made homeownership reachable for millions of families — and built the car-dependent, racially segregated suburbs that later proved nearly impossible to change.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1949 a veteran named William Levitt bought potato fields on Long Island and began selling houses for $7,990 with no down payment for eligible buyers. The houses came with VA-guaranteed loans and FHA mortgage insurance, exactly the tools Congress had created to turn returning service members into stable homeowners. Within a decade the same pattern repeated across the country, producing both record homeownership rates and a landscape of single-family neighborhoods that required cars for every trip.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The policies grew out of the housing collapse of the 1930s and the need to reintegrate millions of veterans after 1945. The FHA, created in 1934, and the VA loan program authorized by the 1944 Servicemen’s Readjustment Act offered federal insurance and guarantees that lowered the risk for private lenders. Lawmakers saw homeownership as a source of family stability and economic security at a moment when rental housing was scarce and memories of bank failures remained fresh. The mortgage interest deduction, already on the books since the income-tax code of 1913, further reduced the effective cost of borrowing. At the time these measures looked like straightforward ways to expand access without direct government construction of housing. Officials expected the benefits to reach the broad middle of the workforce that had been shut out during the Depression.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1945 and 1960 the FHA and VA backed more than 10 million new homes, most of them in newly built subdivisions outside central cities. Developers such as Levitt & Sons used standardized plans and FHA minimum standards to produce thousands of units quickly, while local governments extended roads and utilities to the new tracts. Early results appeared strongly positive: the national homeownership rate rose from roughly 44 percent in 1940 to 62 percent by 1960. Congressional reports and housing-agency press releases described the programs as engines of broad-based wealth creation. A few planners and urbanists noted that the underwriting rules favored new construction over existing neighborhoods and that racial covenants were still common in the deeds, yet these observations stayed at the margins of policy discussion.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
FHA and VA appraisal standards treated racial homogeneity as a marker of neighborhood stability, so loans rarely reached Black buyers even when they could otherwise qualify. The result was a widening gap in household wealth that persisted for generations. At the same time the combination of cheap mortgage credit and federal highway construction after 1956 made distant greenfield sites economically attractive to builders. Families moved outward, retail followed, and local governments zoned almost exclusively for single-family homes to protect property values. Transit ridership in many metropolitan areas began a long decline as daily travel distances grew. By the 1970s the same policies that had produced rapid housing supply also locked in high per-capita infrastructure costs, elevated carbon emissions from commuting, and limited the ability of later planners to introduce buses or rail without crossing low-density subdivisions. Second-order effects appeared in school funding, which remained tied to local property taxes, and in municipal budgets that struggled to maintain roads and pipes spread across ever-larger footprints.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Fair-housing legislation in 1968 and the Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 attempted to correct racial exclusion, yet the spatial pattern of development was already fixed. Later efforts to add density or transit encountered resistance from residents who had purchased homes under the original rules. Some metropolitan areas experimented with growth boundaries and inclusionary zoning, but these measures operated inside a built environment shaped by decades of federal mortgage support. Today the mortgage interest deduction remains in place and continues to favor larger, newer homes farther from job centers, even as many cities struggle to finance transit extensions into the same low-density areas.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Incentive structures that lower the cost of one activity without accounting for its spatial footprint tend to produce durable physical outcomes that later generations inherit. Once capital is committed to dispersed infrastructure, reversing course requires either large new subsidies or politically difficult changes to property rights and taxes. The post-war housing programs show that design choices made for immediate access can constrain future options more tightly than their authors anticipated. When evaluating similar large-scale subsidies today, it is worth asking what physical and social patterns the incentives will reinforce over a fifty-year horizon rather than a five-year one.
