> **Automakers recast streets as car space and turned crossing them into a crime, reshaping cities for a century.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1923, Cincinnati police began ticketing adults for stepping off the curb anywhere except at a corner crosswalk, an act that had been ordinary for generations. The change followed intensive lobbying by automobile manufacturers and local motor clubs who argued that pedestrians, not drivers, were the main source of collisions. What began as an effort to reduce deaths on crowded streets ended by redefining the street itself as a place where people on foot no longer belonged by right.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
In the early 1920s, American cities faced sharply rising pedestrian fatalities as motor vehicle registrations climbed from roughly 8 million in 1920 to more than 20 million by 1927. City engineers, police officials, and insurance companies viewed unregulated street use as a public-health crisis; children still played in roadways and adults crossed wherever they chose. The National Conference on Street and Highway Safety, convened in 1924 under Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover, brought together traffic engineers and safety advocates who believed uniform rules could separate flows of movement and lower the death toll. Automobile trade groups such as the National Automobile Chamber of Commerce joined these meetings, contributing data on collisions and arguing that clear legal priority for vehicles would protect everyone. At the time the proposal seemed technical and neutral: codify where pedestrians should walk so drivers could anticipate them. No one in those rooms imagined the rules would later be used to justify the near-total redesign of urban space around the automobile.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1924 and 1930 dozens of cities passed ordinances that criminalized “jaywalking,” a term popularized by auto-club publicity that labeled mid-block crossing as rural ignorance. Los Angeles adopted one of the earliest strict statutes in 1925, followed by New York, Chicago, and Detroit; model legislation circulated by the American Automobile Association helped spread the language. Newspapers ran safety campaigns funded by motor interests that showed cartoon “jays” darting into traffic and being struck, while school programs taught children to treat streets as dangerous territory reserved for cars. Early data from cities that enforced the rules showed a measurable drop in mid-block collisions, which proponents cited as proof the policy worked. A few planners and downtown merchants warned that the laws would discourage foot traffic and favor suburban growth, but their objections were treated as minor compared with the visible reduction in downtown accidents.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Once jaywalking statutes were on the books, the legal balance of liability shifted decisively. Courts began treating any pedestrian outside a marked crossing as contributorily negligent, sharply reducing the number of successful lawsuits against drivers and insurers. With legal risk lowered, cities widened roadways, removed streetcar tracks, and added traffic signals timed for vehicle throughput rather than foot movement. The second-order effect was rapid: retail districts that had relied on easy pedestrian access lost customers to new drive-in shopping centers built at the urban fringe, accelerating the decentralization that later became suburban sprawl. By the 1950s, federal highway funding further embedded the assumption that streets existed primarily for cars; neighborhoods built before 1920 found themselves bisected by high-speed arterials where crossing on foot required long detours. Third-order consequences compounded over decades. Reduced street life lowered informal surveillance, contributing to the sense that downtowns were unsafe after dark. Public-health researchers later documented that the same infrastructure changes correlated with sharp declines in routine walking, a factor now linked to rising rates of obesity and cardiovascular disease. The original safety goal was never abandoned, yet the system that emerged produced a built environment in which the safest legal choice for most people was to drive even short distances.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the 1970s some transportation planners began questioning whether the 1920s rules had overshot their mark. Experiments with “shared space” designs in Europe and, later, in a handful of U.S. cities such as West Palm Beach and New York’s Times Square showed that removing strict separation could slow traffic and improve pedestrian safety without new criminal penalties. Most American municipalities, however, retained the jaywalking statutes; enforcement simply became sporadic. Recent state-level reforms in places such as Virginia (2020) and California (2022) have decriminalized mid-block crossing when it can be done safely, yet the physical legacy of wide lanes and limited crosswalks remains expensive to undo. The meta-irony is that many current “complete streets” and Vision Zero programs must now spend public funds to reverse design choices that were themselves sold as safety measures a century earlier.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a powerful interest group frames a safety problem in terms that align with its own technology, the resulting rules can quietly transfer rights and resources for generations. Complex urban systems rarely respond to single-variable interventions; reassigning legal priority on streets altered land-use patterns, retail economics, and daily physical activity in ways no 1924 conference table could have modeled. The durable lesson is to test any proposed reallocation of public space against the question of who will still be able to move freely once the new rules are enforced and the concrete has set.
