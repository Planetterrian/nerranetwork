In nineteen forty-eight Seán MacBride campaigned for a national target of planting ten thousand hectares of trees each year, to create rural jobs and a domestic timber supply.

Bulmer Hobson had first outlined the idea in a nineteen thirty-six article in Ireland To-Day. Hobson argued that fast-growing conifers could turn the western Gaeltacht into a prosperous national forest.

Seán MacBride took up the same goal when he ran in the nineteen forty-eight general election. Later, as Minister for External Affairs, MacBride secured cabinet support for that annual planting rate.

At the time Ireland planted only about two thousand four hundred hectares a year, and the country still imported most of its timber.

MacBride and his Fine Gael colleague Gerard Sweetman presented the programme as a way to bring industry and employment to remote rural districts that had few other economic options.

The land west of the Shannon was cheap and available. Non-native Sitka spruce and lodgepole pine were known to grow quickly once established.

No one at the time measured the carbon stored in the underlying peat, or considered how drainage would affect that store.

The arithmetic was straightforward. Each additional hectare planted moved Ireland closer to self-sufficiency in wood products while creating measurable employment in planting crews, nurseries and haulage.

Post-war rural Ireland offered limited alternatives, so directing state funds toward visible hectares and jobs made sense on the information then available.

In nineteen fifty-one the state purchased heavy ploughs and tractors that allowed foresters to drain deeper into blanket bog, and higher up the hills, than before.

Nearly half the new plantations went onto these western peat soils.

One early site was the three hundred forty-three hectare block in the townlands of Derryclare and Cloonnacarton, in the Inagh valley of Connemara. Furrows were cut, water was channelled away, and rock phosphate was spread before the spruce and pine were planted.

The Department of Agriculture later formalised grant schemes that paid up to one hundred percent of establishment costs, plus annual premiums for conifer planting.

Early reports counted the hectares planted and the jobs created in nurseries and fencing crews. Those numbers rose steadily through the nineteen fifties and nineteen sixties.

The machinery let crews create continuous drainage channels, spaced to keep the root zone aerated long enough for seedlings to survive the first winters.

Because the grants covered every step from ploughing to fencing, landowners faced no direct cost for the drainage work itself.

Success was measured in the annual tally of new hectares and the volume of timber projected decades ahead, not in changes to water levels or soil chemistry.

Drainage lowered the water table across the planted bogs. Oxygen reached the peat, and oxidation began.

That peat had kept its carbon locked away only while water excluded oxygen. The land chosen was the lowest-cost land, and the lowest-cost land was peat.

Once drains lowered the water table by even a few tens of centimetres, microbial activity accelerated, and the stored carbon began converting to carbon dioxide at a steady rate.

Tree growth sequesters some carbon above ground. The soil emissions continue year after year, whether the timber is harvested or blown down.

The same drainage networks dried out adjacent unplanted bog. The bog lost sponge-like capacity to hold water and to support specialised plants, because the ditches do not stop at plantation boundaries.

Phosphorus runoff from the fertiliser applications acidified streams and harmed Atlantic salmon and freshwater pearl mussel populations. The runoff reached water already stressed by that acidification.

Windthrow increased on the exposed, poorly rooted trees, and the remaining timber often proved low-value.

Byrne of the University of Limerick told the Joint Committee on Environment and Climate Action that peat soils make up thirty-eight point seven percent of Irish forests, and that forested blanket peatlands emit one point six eight tonnes of carbon dioxide per hectare per year.

The Irish Times reported the Connemara measurements in twenty twenty-four.

The Irish Independent reported in April twenty twenty-five that all Irish peatland habitats already hold unfavourable conservation status, with afforestation identified as one of the main drivers.

Enable Research noted in March twenty twenty-six that eighty percent of Irish peatlands are now degraded, with state-supported afforestation listed as a continuing pressure.

Restoration projects now block drains and remove trees on selected sites. The Irish Times observed in twenty twenty-four that felling the conifers does not quickly restore the original hydrology or the carbon sink function.

A study of eight thousand hectares of low-productivity Coillte peatland forests found that rewetting can temporarily increase emissions before any long-term benefit appears.

Ireland has been referred to the European Union Court of Justice for failing to protect bog habitats. Current grant schemes still offer payments for some conifer planting on marginal land.

Researchers continue to weigh whether soil-carbon losses on any given site can be offset by tree growth. The results depend on local drainage patterns and on adjacent land use.

Blocking drains on one parcel can raise water levels on neighbouring farmland, and that rise creates new conflicts over whether the land can still be used.

The same grant structure that once rewarded drainage now funds some restoration work. The rule of paying per hectare planted remains in place for other forest types.

A subsidy that counts hectares and projected volume, and does not count soil carbon, leaves the decisive cost off the ledger. The test has to use the soil that will actually be paid for, not a national average that hides the wet ground. Whoever approves the payment has to see the carbon and water result for that exact soil type before the first grant is approved.
