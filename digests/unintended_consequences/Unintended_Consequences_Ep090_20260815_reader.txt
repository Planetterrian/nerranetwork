Congress passed the Jones Act to guarantee a wartime-ready American merchant fleet — and ended up with a handful of aging ships and cargo moving by truck instead.

After World War I, American policymakers watched foreign-flag ships carry the bulk of U.S. exports.

They worried the nation would again be caught short of tonnage if another war broke out.

Senator Wesley L. Jones of Washington, chair of the Senate Commerce Committee, introduced the Merchant Marine Act of 1920.

The law closed the coastwise trade to any vessel that was not built in the United States, owned by U.S. citizens, registered under the U.S. flag, and crewed by American mariners.

The measure enjoyed broad support from shipyard unions, naval officers, and both parties.

It passed with little debate and was signed by President Wilson on the fifth of June, nineteen twenty.

At the time the logic appeared straightforward.

A protected domestic market would keep American yards busy, maintain a pool of trained seafarers, and ensure that the government could requisition tonnage without negotiating with foreign owners.

Lawmakers had just lived through the scramble of nineteen seventeen, when the United States had to charter foreign vessels and hastily convert passenger liners because domestic bottoms proved insufficient for the scale of troop movements to Europe.

The Jones Act took effect immediately for trade between the contiguous states, Alaska, Hawaii, and Puerto Rico.

In its first decade the protected trades did see new construction.

Bethlehem and Newport News yards delivered several large passenger-cargo liners for the intercoastal and Hawaii runs.

Proponents pointed to the steady employment of roughly fifty thousand American seafarers in the early nineteen twenties.

Skeptics in the shipping press noted that the new vessels were already more expensive to operate than foreign-flag competitors.

Those differentials were accepted as the necessary price of self-sufficiency.

Over the following decades the protected fleet did not expand.

It contracted.

Between nineteen forty-five and twenty twenty the number of Jones Act–eligible cargo vessels fell from more than one thousand two hundred to fewer than one hundred.

Most of them were built in the nineteen seventies or nineteen eighties and now approach or exceed the end of their economic lives.

A new U.S.-built container ship can cost three to four times as much as an equivalent vessel built in South Korea or China.

Ship operators responded by shifting cargo to trucks and rail wherever geography allowed.

Goods moving between the East and West coasts now travel overland rather than through the Panama Canal on American bottoms.

For island economies the effect was sharper.

A twenty nineteen Government Accountability Office review found that the cost of shipping a forty-foot container from Jacksonville to San Juan was roughly double the cost of shipping the same container from Jacksonville to the Dominican Republic.

Hawaii and Alaska reported similar mark-ups on fuel, building materials, and food.

Puerto Rican manufacturers lost ground to competitors in the Dominican Republic and Mexico who could use cheaper foreign-flag feeders.

With fewer ships, the maritime academies graduate officers who often find work on foreign-flag vessels or leave the industry.

The U.S. military has increasingly turned to foreign-flag vessels under the Maritime Security Program to meet surge requirements.

Reform efforts have surfaced periodically.

In twenty thirteen and again in twenty eighteen, members of Congress introduced bills to allow limited foreign-built vessels in the Puerto Rico trade.

Both measures died in committee after opposition from maritime unions and Gulf Coast shipyards.

Temporary waivers were granted after Hurricanes Maria and Irma, yet the waivers were narrow and short-lived.

As of twenty twenty-four the Maritime Administration lists only ninety-two privately owned Jones Act cargo ships, most over thirty years old.

No comprehensive redesign of the statute has occurred.

Shielding an industry from all external competition tends to raise its costs faster than its productivity.

The protected capacity can shrink even while the legal barrier remains in place.

When the protected activity is also the one the policy claims to value most, the contradiction becomes self-reinforcing.

Policymakers facing similar choices today might ask whether the instrument they are reaching for will still produce the capability they need once every competitor has been removed from the market.

The Jones Act’s history suggests that the answer depends less on the strength of the initial intention than on whether the chosen mechanism can adapt when the arithmetic of costs and demand changes.
