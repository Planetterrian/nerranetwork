> **Thomas Brocklehurst released two grey squirrels in 1876, displacing red squirrels across England at £37 million yearly cost.**

---

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1876 Victorian banker Thomas V. Brocklehurst released a pair of eastern grey squirrels he had brought from New York into Henbury Park near Macclesfield, Cheshire. The animals escaped and multiplied, carrying a parapoxvirus harmless to themselves but usually fatal to native red squirrels while also outcompeting them for food and habitat. Within decades red squirrel populations had collapsed across southern England and the Midlands. The single decision to add one attractive species to a private estate set off two separate pressures—disease and resource competition—that no one at the time measured or modeled. The outcome was not a gradual mixing of squirrel populations but a near-complete replacement in most lowland woodlands.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Thomas V. Brocklehurst, a banker who had traveled to America on business, brought the pair home because he found the animals attractive as pets once their novelty wore off on his estate. Other landowners followed the same impulse. The 11th Duke of Bedford, Herbrand Russell, kept grey squirrels at Woburn Park and gave animals to other estates, including Regent’s Park in London. At the time, estate owners viewed the introductions as harmless enhancements to private parks and gardens, much like the release of other ornamental species. No one had yet documented the virus transmission or the scale of competition that would follow. The releases occurred against a backdrop of Victorian enthusiasm for collecting and displaying exotic wildlife on country estates. Estate records show that owners tracked the visible presence of the new squirrels in their grounds but kept no systematic counts of native red squirrels already living there. Because the grey squirrels appeared healthy and bred readily in captivity, the assumption was that they would simply add color and activity without displacing anything already present.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Brocklehurst’s 1876 release was followed by further introductions listed by Monica Shorten and Christopher Lever. Ten squirrels from New Jersey stock were released at Woburn Park in 1890. One hundred animals went into Richmond Park in 1902. Ninety-one were released in Regent’s Park between 1905 and 1907. Additional releases occurred at sites in Berkshire, Staffordshire, Devon and Hampshire through 1929, after which the last recorded introduction took place and a prohibition order followed in 1937. Proponents at the time simply noted the squirrels’ visibility in parks; no coordinated monitoring existed. Even the smallest founding groups proved sufficient because grey squirrels reach breeding age in their first year and can produce two litters annually when food is plentiful. Landowners continued the practice because each new sighting was treated as confirmation that the animals were settling in successfully rather than as a signal to stop. The pattern of repeated small gifts from Woburn and similar estates created multiple independent nuclei rather than one expanding front.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Grey squirrels expanded from these centers, aided by further human translocations that DNA profiling of nearly 1,500 animals later traced. A 2000 study in Animal Conservation tested 223 apparently healthy grey squirrels and found 61 percent carried antibodies to the parapoxvirus, while only 2.86 percent of 140 red squirrels showed exposure and three of those displayed active disease. The Forestry Commission later recorded that grey squirrels strip bark from trees, reducing timber quality and imposing an estimated £37 million annual cost. Red squirrels disappeared from 70 of 73 surveyed forests by 1935 and from 19 of 77 by 1955 in districts examined by Southeastern Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies researchers. The virus acted as a reservoir maintained in the grey population, while direct competition for food and habitat accelerated the reds’ retreat to fragmented northern and island refugia. Human-assisted movements, documented by Imperial College London researchers, created genetically distinct populations far from natural dispersal range, including the Aberdeen group linked to Hampshire stock. The parapoxvirus spreads when greys and reds share feeding sites or when reds investigate dreys previously used by greys; reds develop skin lesions and systemic illness that greys do not. At the same time, grey squirrels can digest acorns and other hard mast more efficiently and cache larger stores, leaving less for reds during winter. Because the two pressures operated together, even woodlands that retained some tree cover saw red numbers fall below viable levels within twenty to thirty years of grey arrival. The DNA evidence further shows that natural dispersal alone could not account for the speed or pattern of spread; repeated human gifts multiplied the number of starting points and therefore the total area under pressure.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
A 1937 prohibition order ended deliberate releases. Modern management focuses on targeted grey squirrel control in priority red squirrel areas, which the Forestry Commission reports has allowed local red populations to stabilize. The same control work also reduces tree damage. Red squirrels remain protected and are now found mainly in northern England, Scotland, Ireland and a few southern islands. No reversal of the overall range shift has occurred. Control programs must be sustained year after year because any relaxation allows grey squirrels to re-enter from surrounding land. The economic case for continued action now rests on both biodiversity targets and the £37 million annual timber loss figure, so the original ornamental impulse continues to generate measurable costs more than a century later.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When estate owners chose to move small numbers of grey squirrels onto new ground, they rewarded the animals’ ability to exploit both novel food sources and an existing disease vulnerability in the resident species. When later managers tracked seroprevalence rather than visible symptoms alone, they identified the reservoir mechanism that single-species introductions had created. When DNA work distinguished human-transported populations from natural spread, it showed that repeated small gifts from Woburn and similar sites had multiplied the outcome far beyond the original releases. Each of these steps reveals that the critical variable was not the size of any single release but the cumulative effect of many independent decisions made without reference to pathogen carriage or competitive displacement. How might current decisions about moving or tolerating any one species account for hidden pathogen and competition effects before the next translocation occurs?
