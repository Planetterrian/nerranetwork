By the late nineteen nineties, red-light running killed more than eight hundred people a year in the United States and injured roughly two hundred thousand others, according to figures cited in the two thousand seven Virginia Transportation Research Council report.

Transportation engineers and public-safety officials in several states looked at that toll and saw a violation that was easy to name and hard to staff.

Automated cameras looked like a practical way to enforce signals without placing an officer at every intersection.

Jurisdictions such as Alexandria, Arlington, Fairfax County, Falls Church, and Vienna in Northern Virginia began installing cameras after the state legislature authorized their use.

The technology appeared to offer continuous enforcement at lower staffing cost.

It targeted a specific act: entering an intersection after the light turned red, the violation tied to the most severe angle collisions.

Early studies that did not adjust for regression to the mean suggested large safety gains, and those results reinforced the decision to expand the programs.

Officials reasoned that if a camera could change driver behavior at the exact moment the light changed, the most destructive crashes would decline even if total crash volume stayed the same or rose slightly.

That reasoning treated the angle crash as the harm worth buying down, and treated a small rise in other crashes as an acceptable remainder.

Between nineteen ninety-eight and two thousand four, the six Northern Virginia jurisdictions operated red-light cameras at selected intersections and collected seven years of crash data for later analysis.

Proponents pointed to immediate drops in red-light violations captured on video.

The Federal Highway Administration launched a larger multijurisdictional evaluation that applied empirical Bayes methods, seeking to isolate the cameras' effect from background trends.

Early press coverage and agency statements emphasized the reduction in angle crashes as evidence that the cameras were working as intended.

A smaller number of engineers and statisticians warned that prior evaluations had not controlled for regression to the mean, a statistical artifact that can exaggerate apparent safety improvements when sites are chosen because those sites recently experienced high crash counts.

The Virginia program therefore became one of the first large-scale tests in which both the intended angle-crash reduction and any offsetting rear-end increase could be measured against the same baseline data set.

Drivers approaching camera-equipped intersections braked more abruptly to avoid a citation, and following vehicles sometimes could not stop in time.

The Virginia Transportation Research Council's two thousand seven analysis of the same Northern Virginia corridors found rear-end crashes rose between twenty-seven and forty-two percent depending on the statistical method, with substantial variation across jurisdictions.

In Arlington, every crash category studied increased.

In Vienna, most categories declined.

When comprehensive crash costs were calculated, the aggregate result across the six jurisdictions showed a net increase in total crash costs, although injury-only costs fell modestly once three fatal angle crashes were set aside.

The Campbell Collaboration's two thousand twenty systematic review of thirty-eight studies confirmed an average nineteen percent rise in rear-end collisions once regression-to-the-mean adjustments were applied.

The causal chain ran directly from the enforcement incentive.

The camera punished red-light entry and rewarded a sudden stop, shifting crash type rather than eliminating harm.

Second-order effects included higher repair costs for property-damage crashes and occasional disputes over camera calibration and yellow-interval timing.

Rear-end collisions at these sites were more often low-speed events involving two vehicles already in the same lane, so average injury severity stayed lower than the angle crashes those rear-end collisions replaced.

The increase in frequency still raised total repair bills and insurance claims in several jurisdictions.

One jurisdiction recorded an annual increase of one hundred forty thousand eight hundred eighty-three dollars in comprehensive crash costs.

Another recorded an annual reduction of ninety-two thousand three hundred sixty-seven dollars.

The same camera technology produced opposite net outcomes depending on local traffic volume, signal timing, and approach geometry.

The Virginia Transportation Research Council concluded that cameras should be evaluated intersection by intersection rather than adopted as a blanket policy.

Some jurisdictions reduced or removed cameras where rear-end increases outweighed angle-crash reductions.

Other jurisdictions retained cameras at high-risk angle-crash locations.

Later national guidance incorporated site-specific crash-type trade-offs into camera-program design.

The two thousand twenty Campbell review noted that effects continued to vary with study quality and local conditions, leaving program managers to weigh local geometry, signal timing, and traffic volume before deciding on new installations.

Over time, several agencies began publishing the full before-and-after crash matrices for each candidate site so that decision makers could see both the angle-crash savings and the rear-end penalty in the same table before voting on installation.

Selecting intersections from recent red-light crash counts can pull a program toward places where a statistical rebound is already likely, and a camera then adds a second behavioral shift on top of that selection.

Monetizing every crash type, not only the crash the camera was built to stop, is what makes a modest net benefit visible at all, because the rear-end increase has to be subtracted from the angle-crash savings before anyone knows whether the ledger improved.

Requiring a before-and-after matrix at each site is a way to refuse a location where the rear-end penalty already exceeds the angle-crash gain, instead of treating one jurisdiction's savings as proof for the next corridor.
