> **Plastic bag bans cut visible litter but drove shoppers to buy more trash liners and thicker “reusable” bags that often lasted no longer.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 2015 a woman in a London supermarket reached for a pack of small white bin liners that had not existed on the same shelf two years earlier. The plastic carrier bag she once carried home and then used for kitchen waste now cost five pence at the till, so she bought dedicated liners instead. The policy that removed one thin bag from circulation had quietly added several new ones.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The bans and fees that spread through the 2010s grew out of clear evidence that single-use polyethylene bags were a visible and persistent form of litter. Marine biologists had documented bags in the digestive tracts of seabirds and turtles; municipal workers counted them among the most common items collected on beaches. Regulators in Ireland, the United Kingdom, California, and dozens of other jurisdictions therefore treated the bag as a low-cost target. A small price signal or outright prohibition looked like a straightforward way to change behavior without banning plastic itself. At the time, most households already reused grocery bags as bin liners, so officials expected the main effect to be fewer bags entering the waste stream altogether.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Ireland introduced the first nationwide levy in 2002 at fifteen cents per bag; consumption fell roughly 90 percent within months. England followed with a five-pence charge in 2015, later raised to ten pence. California enacted a statewide ban on single-use bags in 2016 after earlier local rules. Early data showed sharp drops in bag litter along waterways and in municipal clean-ups. Retailers reported handing out far fewer free bags, and environmental groups hailed the measures as quick wins. A few analysts noted that households might simply switch to other plastic products, but those cautions remained secondary to the visible reduction in discarded bags.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Once the thin grocery bag disappeared, many households began purchasing purpose-made trash-bin liners. In places that restricted grocery bags, sales of purpose-made bin liners rose noticeably, offsetting a share of the plastic that had been removed. In places that permitted thicker “bags for life,” shoppers often treated the new bags as single-use items anyway; the heavier gauge simply meant more plastic per discarded bag. Life-cycle assessments, including a widely cited 2011 UK Environment Agency study, calculated that a cotton tote bag must be reused at least 131 times to produce lower environmental impacts than the single-use polyethylene bag it replaced. Most cotton totes were used far fewer times before being lost or discarded. Because the original policy focused on one product category, it did not track or price the substitutes that consumers actually chose. The result was a partial shift in plastic tonnage rather than a net reduction, plus an increase in the number of separate plastic items manufactured and transported.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Some jurisdictions later adjusted their rules. England expanded its charge to all retailers and raised the fee. A handful of cities began requiring minimum recycled content or durability standards for reusable bags. Researchers published clearer guidance on reuse thresholds, and a few campaigns encouraged residents to keep and reuse the thicker bags they already owned. No large program has been fully reversed, yet the emphasis in newer policies has moved toward measuring total plastic mass and requiring retailers to report sales of alternative bags. The core design lesson—that substitutes must be measured alongside the targeted item—now appears in guidance from several national environment ministries.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Any intervention that removes one behavior necessarily prices or permits its substitutes, so the relevant unit of analysis is the full set of options people will actually adopt. Simple product bans therefore require follow-up data on what replaces the banned item, even when early litter counts look encouraging. The same principle applies to fees, deposit schemes, and material restrictions today: without tracking the second-order purchases, the policy can relocate rather than reduce the impact it set out to solve.
