Twentieth-century engineers built dams to power the West and control floods, then spent decades dismantling some so salmon could reach their spawning grounds again.

In the fall of 2011, workers on Washington’s Elwha River began the largest dam-removal project in U.S. history. They started by cutting large openings through the concrete of the one-hundred-eight-foot-high Glines Canyon Dam, a structure that had stood in place since nineteen twenty-seven and whose reservoir had submerged miles of riverbed.

The work was slow and methodical because crews had to manage the sudden release of decades of accumulated sediment without triggering uncontrolled downstream flooding. Within months, Chinook salmon that had been barred from seventy miles of upstream habitat began appearing again in the clear water above the old dam sites.

Those fish were returning to gravel beds that had been inaccessible since the dams first blocked their migration routes.

The structures had been erected to generate electricity and protect downstream farms. Their removal was now required to restore the very fish runs those farms and power plants had helped sustain for nearly a century.

The logic had reversed because the original benefits—kilowatt-hours delivered and flood peaks reduced—had been tallied in one set of ledgers while the costs to anadromous fish populations accumulated in another.

Federal agencies and regional boosters in the nineteen thirties saw untamed rivers as both threat and wasted resource. The Bureau of Reclamation, working under the New Deal, set out to build storage reservoirs that would irrigate arid land, generate cheap hydroelectricity, and reduce the devastating floods that had repeatedly inundated the Columbia and Klamath basins.

Engineers calculated that holding back spring runoff in large reservoirs would let water be released steadily through turbines during peak demand periods, turning seasonal river flow into a reliable supply of power measured in megawatts. President Franklin Roosevelt personally championed Grand Coulee Dam on the Columbia.

He argued that public power would modernize rural economies and lift families out of poverty. At the time, salmon runs appeared inexhaustible. State and federal fisheries managers believed fish ladders or hatcheries could offset any losses from new barriers. The overriding calculation was straightforward.

Stored water and falling water could be converted into measurable economic growth for a region still recovering from the Depression.

Construction began on Bonneville Dam in nineteen thirty-three and on Grand Coulee in nineteen thirty-three. Both were completed before the end of World War II. By nineteen forty-two Grand Coulee alone was producing more electricity than any other single installation in the United States and irrigating more than half a million acres of the Columbia Basin.

Similar projects followed on the Klamath River. The Bureau of Reclamation completed Copco Number One in nineteen eighteen and later added J.C. Boyle, Copco Number Two, and Iron Gate. Early reports from the nineteen forties and nineteen fifties documented rising farm output and falling flood damages. Local newspapers hailed the dams as permanent monuments to progress.

A handful of fisheries biologists noted declining upstream passage. Their concerns were treated as secondary to national defense and agricultural expansion during and after the war.

Once the dams were in place, anadromous fish lost access to the cold, gravel-bed tributaries where they had historically spawned. On the Columbia system, estimates suggest that roughly forty percent of historic salmon and steelhead habitat was blocked by the mid-nineteen sixties.

Adult fish that reached the base of Grand Coulee or the Elwha dams either turned back or exhausted themselves attempting to surmount concrete faces never designed for passage. Juvenile out-migrants faced reservoirs that slowed their journey to the sea and increased predation. Over successive decades, commercial and tribal harvests on the lower rivers fell sharply.

By the nineteen nineties several Columbia River stocks were listed under the Endangered Species Act. The causal chain ran through simple biology. Without access to natal streams, each generation produced fewer returning adults. This collapsed the nutrient cycle that had once carried marine-derived nitrogen into mountain forests.

Tribal communities whose seasonal economies and ceremonial life had revolved around predictable salmon runs experienced cultural as well as economic loss that compounded across generations.

By the late nineteen eighties, mounting legal pressure from tribes and environmental groups, combined with the high cost of maintaining aging structures and operating fish hatcheries with limited success, shifted the policy conversation. Congress authorized removal of the two Elwha River dams in nineteen ninety-two. Deconstruction began in twenty eleven and was largely complete by twenty fourteen.

On the Klamath, a twenty sixteen agreement led to the drawdown of the four lower dams beginning in twenty twenty-three. This became the largest river-restoration project attempted in the United States. Early monitoring shows increased spawning activity above the former dam sites. Full recovery of historic run sizes will take decades and remains uncertain.

The financial cost of removal—more than three hundred million dollars for the Elwha project alone—now appears on federal and state balance sheets that once recorded only construction and power revenues.

Infrastructure decisions embed ecological debts that surface only after the original builders have left office and the surrounding economy has reorganized around the new river regime. When the full chain of effects—from blocked migration to lost nutrients to cultural disruption—finally becomes visible, reversal can require resources comparable to the original construction.

The practical implication is that any large intervention should carry an explicit, funded mechanism for later reassessment rather than assuming benefits will remain net positive indefinitely. The question for today’s planners is which current systems, built with equally clear short-term gains, already contain similar long-term liabilities that will one day demand their own deliberate dismantling.
