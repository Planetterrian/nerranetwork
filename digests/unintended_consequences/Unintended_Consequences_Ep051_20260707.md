> **China's Three Gorges Dam promised flood control and clean power for hundreds of millions, yet its reservoir began stressing faults and destabilizing entire hillsides.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the summer of 2003, as the first phase of reservoir filling behind the Three Gorges Dam raised water levels more than 100 meters along the Yangtze, residents near the town of Wushan watched entire hillsides begin to move. Cracks opened in fields that had been farmed for generations, and small slides sent mud and rock into the rising water. The structure had been built to tame floods and generate electricity on a scale never attempted before; instead, the weight of the water itself started reshaping the surrounding geology.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The idea of a massive dam at the Three Gorges site dates to Sun Yat-sen’s 1919 proposal to improve navigation and irrigation on the Yangtze. Chinese leaders revisited the concept after devastating floods in 1954 that killed more than 30,000 people and displaced millions. By the 1980s, rapid economic growth created urgent demand for electricity and greater protection for the densely populated middle and lower Yangtze basin. Premier Li Peng, a hydraulic engineer by training, championed the project as essential infrastructure that would both prevent catastrophe and supply roughly one-tenth of the country’s power needs at the time. The National People’s Congress formally approved construction in 1992 after years of feasibility studies that emphasized flood-storage capacity of 22 billion cubic meters and a generating capacity of 22,500 megawatts. At the scale envisioned, the dam appeared to offer a single intervention capable of addressing multiple national priorities simultaneously.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Construction began in 1994 at Sandouping in Hubei Province. The reservoir was filled in stages, reaching 135 meters in 2003, 156 meters in 2006, and the final operating level of 175 meters in 2010. Official reports at the time highlighted successful power generation that began feeding the grid in 2003 and the relocation of roughly 1.13 million residents from 13 cities and hundreds of villages. Proponents cited reduced flood peaks downstream during the 2010 and 2012 monsoon seasons as early validation. Some geologists and environmental researchers inside and outside China had warned during the approval process that the unprecedented water load could affect seismic activity and slope stability, yet the project moved forward with monitoring programs intended to manage those risks.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Once the reservoir reached full capacity, the added weight of approximately 39 billion tons of water altered stresses on the crust beneath the 660-kilometer reservoir. Seismic stations recorded a measurable increase in small earthquakes, particularly along the Zigui and Badong segments of the Yangtze, with events clustered after rapid water-level rises. On the reservoir margins, saturation of previously dry slopes triggered hundreds of landslides; the 2003 Qianjiangping slide killed 24 people and blocked a tributary. Further downstream, the dam trapped most of the Yangtze’s sediment load, starving the river channel and coastal delta of material needed to maintain elevation against subsidence and sea-level rise. Towns and farmland that had been relocated to higher ground sometimes found themselves on slopes newly prone to failure because the relocated communities themselves altered drainage patterns. The combination of these effects—induced seismicity, repeated landslides, and sediment starvation—arose directly from the decision to impound such an enormous volume of water in a tectonically active, steeply incised valley.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Chinese authorities expanded monitoring networks and imposed seasonal water-level restrictions to reduce landslide risk during the monsoon. Some of the most unstable slopes received engineered stabilization, yet new slides continue to appear after heavy rains or rapid drawdowns. The sediment deficit has required periodic flushing operations that temporarily lower reservoir levels, trading power generation and flood storage for downstream channel maintenance. No reversal of the dam itself has been contemplated; instead, the operating regime has been adjusted iteratively. Today the structure still supplies roughly 100 terawatt-hours annually while providing measurable flood-peak reduction, even as geological management remains an ongoing operational cost.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Interventions that concentrate enormous mass or force in one location can shift stresses throughout surrounding physical systems in ways that are difficult to model completely beforehand. When the scale of a project approaches the magnitude of natural geological forces, second-order effects on slopes, faults, and sediment transport become first-order concerns rather than minor externalities. Decision-makers facing similarly large proposals today can usefully ask what existing stresses in the landscape or society will be amplified, not merely whether the primary objective will be met. How might we test the stability of an entire system before committing to changes whose physical consequences cannot be undone?
