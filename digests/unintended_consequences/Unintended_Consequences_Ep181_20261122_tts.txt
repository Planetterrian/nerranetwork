In Ketapang Regency, West Kalimantan, national zoning split each Peatland Hydrological Unit. Domes deeper than three meters were Peat Ecosystems with Protection Function. The shallow edges were Cultivation Function. The line was meant to separate conservation from cultivation.

Oil-palm canals went in on the permitted mineral soils and shallow peat. Those canals lowered the water table under the protected domes. Peat inside the protection lines dried, subsided, and burned.

The officials who drew those maps were working after the fires of twenty fifteen. Those fires burned two point six million hectares across Indonesia, and one third of that area was peat. The government created the Peatland Restoration Agency, BRG, and appointed Nazir Foead as its chief.

Foead and the agency worked from rules already on the books. Ministry of Agriculture Decree number fourteen of two thousand nine already banned new oil palm on peat that deep. Government Regulation seventy-one of two thousand fourteen capped drainage depth at zero point four meters.

BRG mapped eight hundred sixty-five Peatland Hydrological Units and required at least thirty percent of each unit, including the central domes, to remain in protection function. Officials reasoned that plantations kept to the outer, shallower zones would leave the carbon-rich domes wet and still allow economic use of the landscape.

A peat-conversion moratorium from twenty eleven, and restoration targets set for twenty twenty, followed the same logic. Draw a clear spatial line between protection and production.

At the time, the maps and the hydrological-unit framework appeared precise enough to enforce that separation on the ground. One objection at the planning stage asked whether water could move laterally across the boundary between shallow and deep zones.

The prevailing view held that the depth rule plus the drainage cap would keep the domes saturated, because the peat itself was treated as a stable barrier once the permitted areas stayed within legal limits.

Regency governments, including Ketapang, wrote the national zoning into local spatial plans through multistakeholder forums that brought plantation companies, smallholders, and forestry agencies into the same room. Roughly half the mapped domes remained unplanted, so BRG pushed to designate those unplanted areas as protected even inside existing concessions.

Companies on the permitted shallow fringes installed drainage canals and planted oil palm, following the water-table limits in the twenty fourteen regulation. Proponents pointed to the legal clarity of the protection-function category and to the commitment to finish restoration by twenty twenty.

Some local officials noted that the policy preserved space for both conservation and continued cultivation within the same hydrological unit.

The rollout treated each parcel's drainage as an independent engineering choice. Canals were dug to the allowed depth on the cultivation side. The assumption was that any seepage would be minimal, because the deeper peat held more water naturally. The meetings recorded agreement on the maps. Field verification of exact peat depths remained limited by the resolution of the surveys then available.

Drainage on the cultivation zones did not stop at the mapped boundary. Water flowed outward through the porous peat and dropped the water table inside the protected domes as well.

In the Ex-Mega Rice Project area, models showed that full drainage for oil palm would eventually place sixty-seven percent of the peat surface below flood level after one hundred years because of subsidence. Each centimeter of water-table drawdown allows oxygen to reach peat that had been anaerobic.

Oxidation then releases carbon dioxide and reduces the volume of the peat matrix by roughly the same amount, so the surface lowers. Repeated cycles compound until the dome begins to flood from below in wet seasons while remaining dry enough to burn in dry seasons.

Dry peat in the domes ignited during the annual dry season, and fires crossed back into the areas the zoning had intended to keep wet. In twenty sixteen, Nazir Foead observed that inaccurate maps still allowed licenses on deep peat, so drainage continued under the legal cover of adjacent concessions.

A twenty seventeen study in Wetlands Ecology and Management recorded twenty-eight percent encroachment of plantations onto protected peat despite that depth rule.

Carbon emissions from the drained and burned peat reached an estimated three hundred fifty to four hundred million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year. Smallholders and companies that had followed the zoning found their fields threatened by the same fires the policy was meant to prevent.

Restoration by BRG, which relied on canal blocking, could not keep pace with ongoing drainage from neighboring licensed areas. The boundary was hydrologically porous, so repeated fires erased the gain spatial separation had been meant to deliver. A further objection, that better enforcement of the drainage cap would solve the problem, ran into a harder fact.

Even legal drainage on one side of the line altered the hydraulic gradient across the entire unit and pulled water from the dome faster than natural recharge could replace it.

After the pattern became clear, BRG called for full protection of all remaining peat domes and for companies to forgo replanting once the first cycle ended. Multistakeholder forums in Ketapang produced a local policy instrument aimed at reconciling protection and production, yet implementation stayed limited by mismatched rules across national, provincial, and district levels.

Some plantations adopted zero-burning practices and tighter water management under guidance from the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil, RSPO, but the underlying drainage continued wherever mineral-soil or shallow-peat permits remained valid. By most accounts the restoration target for twenty twenty was not fully met.

Current efforts focus on aligning economic incentives and enforcing hydrological-unit planning more strictly, yet the same tension between adjacent land uses persists.

One practical adjustment under discussion is to treat the entire hydrological unit as the unit of permitting rather than individual concessions, so that any new canal on the shallow fringe would require offsetting water-table controls inside the dome.

The lines around those deep domes rewarded any drainage that stayed just outside them, so water tables inside the protected zones fell even when the canals were legal. The conservation share required on each hydrological unit rewarded continued planting on the rest of the unit, with no mechanism to keep that planting from altering the whole unit's water.

Applied separately to neighboring parcels, the depth rule and the drainage cap rewarded short-term planting decisions whose subsidence and fire costs crossed the parcel boundary. What was measured was compliance at the canal and at the map line. What moved was water through porous peat, faster than recharge could put it back.

A depth cap on one concession cannot hold a dome if the next concession is free to steepen the gradient. A protection share cannot stay saturated if the cultivated share may drain the same peat body. Nazir Foead and the regency forums were using the separation the existing decrees made available, and that separation looked enforceable because the peat was treated as a barrier.
