> **Seventy percent of Strait of Georgia coho catch came from hatcheries by 2001 despite lower survival than wild fish.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the early 2000s, biologists sampling coho salmon in the Strait of Georgia found that seven out of every ten fish carried a hatchery mark. The Salmonid Enhancement Program, launched in 1977 to increase overall abundance for fishers and coastal communities, had produced exactly that numerical success. Yet the same marked fish showed markedly poorer marine survival than their unmarked wild counterparts, and the proportion of wild fish in the population had fallen sharply. The marks themselves came from fin clips and coded-wire tags inserted before release, allowing every recovered adult to be traced back to a specific hatchery or river system. This tracing revealed not an addition of new fish on top of existing wild runs but a gradual substitution in which the wild component shrank as hatchery numbers rose.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Canada’s Department of Fisheries and Oceans created the Salmonid Enhancement Program in 1977 after years of declining Pacific salmon returns that hurt commercial and recreational harvests. Officials believed that building hatcheries and spawning channels would raise freshwater survival from the natural range of 2–25 percent to 70–80 percent, quickly rebuilding stocks that had been reduced by logging, dams, and fishing pressure. The plan drew on established hatchery practices already used in the United States and on the visible success of earlier Canadian facilities that had increased local runs. Rational managers working with the data then available saw a straightforward engineering solution: more juveniles released would mean more adults returning for everyone. Community groups and First Nations were invited to operate smaller facilities, tying the program to local economic development as well as conservation. At the time, the ocean was viewed as a vast, underused feeding ground whose capacity could absorb the extra production without measurable cost to existing wild populations; the arithmetic appeared simple because the only survival numbers tracked were those inside the hatchery raceways and the adult returns counted at terminal fisheries.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1977 and the late 1990s the program expanded to dozens of federal, community, and volunteer hatcheries across British Columbia and the Yukon. Releases of coho and chinook into the Strait of Georgia grew steadily, and by the mid-1980s managers reported higher catches in some terminal fisheries. Proponents pointed to the clear increase in marked fish recovered in commercial and sport harvests as proof the investment was working. A few biologists inside the department noted that hatchery fish might compete with wild juveniles for food and space, but the dominant view held that the ocean’s capacity was large enough to absorb the added production. Release strategies emphasized local broodstock and timing meant to reduce obvious genetic risks, measures described in a 2006 Fisheries and Oceans Canada review by Don MacKinlay. Early evaluations focused on the freshwater phase where the survival gain was dramatic and easy to measure; marine-phase performance received less scrutiny because the program’s primary success metric remained the number of adults returning to the river mouth or caught in the fishery.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
By 2001, coded-wire-tag and fin-clip data showed that hatchery-origin coho made up more than 70 percent of the Strait of Georgia catch and escapement, according to a North American Journal of Fisheries Management study that tracked the shift from the 1970s onward. The same fish, however, survived at substantially lower rates once they entered the ocean; one Cowichan River comparison found hatchery coho survival roughly one-third that of wild coho. A 2024 review by the Pacific Salmon Foundation and the American Fisheries Society summarized multiple SEP evaluations and concluded that hatchery coho and chinook frequently posted lower marine survival than wild fish, with limited evidence that the releases produced sustained increases in wild abundance. The mechanism was straightforward: hatchery juveniles, larger and more numerous at release, occupied rearing areas and prey resources that wild fish would otherwise have used, while also returning in numbers that crowded limited spawning gravel. Over successive generations the higher freshwater survival inside the hatchery created a growing numerical advantage that gradually replaced rather than supplemented the wild component. A broader literature on salmonid domestication, reviewed in Evolutionary Applications in 2008, showed that even one or two generations in captivity can reduce reproductive success in the wild through changes in behavior and physiology, though SEP-specific genetic studies have not yet quantified the same effect in British Columbia populations. The objection that hatchery fish simply added to total abundance overlooked the density-dependent limits in both the nearshore rearing zones and the spawning beds; each extra hatchery smolt that survived to compete reduced the growth or survival probability of the smaller wild smolts sharing the same limited food supply. As the proportion of hatchery fish on the spawning grounds rose, wild adults encountered more frequent interference during redd construction and egg deposition, further tilting the next generation’s composition. The net effect was that the program’s own output metric—marked fish in the catch—continued to look strong while the underlying wild productivity quietly declined.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the early 2000s managers reduced some coho releases and shifted emphasis toward conservation of wild stocks under Canada’s Wild Salmon Policy. In 2024 Fisheries and Oceans Canada issued new “Genetically based enhanced contribution guidelines” that require explicit evaluation of genetic risk before any hatchery program proceeds and that place conservation of wild salmon as the highest priority. The guidelines acknowledge that earlier enhancement objectives sometimes conflicted with that priority and instruct planners to use local broodstock, limit the proportion of hatchery fish on spawning grounds, and monitor fitness differences. Some terminal fisheries still rely on hatchery returns, so complete removal of the programs has not occurred. The net result is a more cautious, population-specific approach rather than the broad production targets of the 1970s and 1980s. Planners now calculate an allowable hatchery contribution for each individual population rather than treating all enhanced fish as interchangeable additions to a single regional total.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When the Salmonid Enhancement Program measured success by the number of hatchery juveniles released and adults recovered in the catch, the metric rewarded volume over the relative performance of wild fish sharing the same waters. When planners later required that any new release be evaluated against measurable effects on the fitness and abundance of the local wild population, the decision rule changed from “more fish is better” to “added fish must not reduce the wild component.” The same logic applies whenever an intervention counts only the output it directly controls and treats the surrounding natural population as an unlimited background resource. How might today’s restoration or recovery programs change if every added intervention were required to demonstrate no net loss to the unassisted wild baseline first?
