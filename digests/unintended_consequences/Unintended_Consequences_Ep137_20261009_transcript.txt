In the early 1840s, crews on the cincha islands off Peru's southern coast began cutting
into layers of seabird droppings that had accumulated for centuries. Some deposits reached nearly
200 feet deep. The Peruvian government declared a national monopoly in 1841 and signed consignment
contracts with foreign merchants to ship the material to farms in Britain, France, and the United
States. Peruvian leaders, especially General Ramone Castilla, saw the sudden European demand for
nitrogen-rich fertilizer as a way to stabilize public finances after years of political turmoil
and limited export options. The islands had long been known to local populations,
and Inca era rules had protected breeding colonies by restricting disturbance during
nesting seasons. In the 1840s, however, the priority was revenue. Guano could be sold at $25 to $50 per
ton without requiring Peru to develop new industries or infrastructure. Contracts with firms such as
Antony Gibbs and Sons were structured as profit-sharing arrangements that delivered immediate cash to
the Treasury while leaving extraction logistics to experienced traders. At the time, no one possessed
reliable surveys of total deposit volume or annual bird deposition rates, so the resource appeared
effectively unlimited. The first major contracts were awarded in the 1840s, with Gibbs handling
the bulk of shipments to Liverpool and other European ports. By the 1850s and 1860s,
annual exports from the Chincha Islands alone reached between 155,000 and 445,000 tons,
according to contemporary ledgers. The state used the proceeds to service foreign loans and
fund public works, and bond prices rose sharply after debt settlements in 1849.
When the initial partners proved costly, later governments shifted to direct sale agreements,
most notably the 1869 Dreyfus contract that tied Guano revenues directly to debt repayment.
Early reports noted only minor complaints about labor conditions and island dust.
The overriding narrative in Lima and London was one of mutual commercial success.
By the late 1860s, surveyors on the Chincha Islands recorded that many once-thick beds had
been reduced to bear rock, with extraction continuing at rates far above the slow natural
accumulation of new guano.
Peru exported roughly 11.5 million tons between 1841 and 1879, valued at around $750 million.
The biological source, three dominant seabird species, could not sustain that pace once
the ancient layers were gone.
Labor crews, including Chinese indentured workers, removed material so thoroughly that
nesting sites disappeared, and the birds began abandoning the islands in larger numbers.
State income, which had depended almost entirely on Guano, collapsed.
Peru defaulted on its foreign debt in 1876.
The same contracts that had channeled revenue to the treasury had created an incentive
to maximize short-term tonnage with no mechanism to limit total drawdown or protect future
bird colonies.
Second order effects included the rapid pivot to nitrate exports from the Atacama region,
which in turn contributed to the war of the Pacific, and the long-term decline of the
very seabird populations whose waste had created the original wealth.
After the Chinchu deposits were largely exhausted, Peru experimented with new marketing arrangements
and eventually created a state guano company in the 20th century to manage smaller, more
regulated harvests.
The islands never regained their former economic importance, and seabird numbers remained
depressed for decades.
Later conservation measures, including seasonal closures and fishing restrictions around
the islands, attempted to restore the colonies, yet the original open-access model had already
removed the century's old capital stock.
No later policy fully reversed the loss of that accumulated resource.
The shift to nitrates brought new revenues but also new conflicts over territory, illustrating
how one extraction regime can give way to another without restoring the biological foundation
that first created wealth.
