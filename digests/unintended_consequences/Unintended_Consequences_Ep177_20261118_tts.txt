Swiss avalanche shelters built after nineteen fifty-one put new housing on slopes once judged too hazardous.

The winter of nineteen fifty to nineteen fifty-one killed ninety-eight people and levelled entire clusters of buildings in Airolo, Andermatt, and Vals. Many of those structures were relatively new homes and shops, put up in the decades before despite local warnings.

After the avalanches destroyed roughly one thousand five hundred buildings across Switzerland, federal authorities demanded national avalanche registers and hazard maps by June nineteen fifty-two. The explicit goal was to cut future deaths and property loss by making avalanche terrain usable year-round.

The WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF answered by testing wood, aluminium, wire rope, steel, and concrete support systems on the Dorfberg slope above Davos from the mid-nineteen fifties onward. Professor Doctor R. Haefeli of ETH Zürich contributed designs for the first steel wire rope nets, field-tested on Schafberg near Pontresina in nineteen fifty-one.

Central government funding rose sharply, and practice shifted from low stone walls and terraces to engineered retaining structures. Provisional guidelines from nineteen fifty-five became the nineteen sixty-one official standard, Lawinenverbau im Anbruchgebiet.

Engineers at the time saw physical barriers and the emerging hazard maps as a rational way to turn restricted slopes into land that could hold permanent settlement without repeating the losses of nineteen fifty-one.

In the decades that followed, engineers installed more than five hundred kilometres of steel snow bridges, concrete galleries, and deflection dams to hold snow on starting zones and divert flows from roads and villages. Snow sheds such as the Seehorn gallery shielded railway lines. Retaining structures alone eventually reached approximately six hundred kilometres.

Colour-coded hazard maps followed. The first, for Gadmen, appeared in nineteen fifty-four. The Wengen map of nineteen sixty introduced red and blue zones that prohibited or conditioned new construction.

Municipalities and cantons used those maps and the new physical barriers together, treating the combination as enough to permit settlement where none had been allowed before. Early reports from the SLF noted that the barriers performed as modelled during moderate winters, and that record reinforced confidence in the approach.

Cantonal building offices began approving projects on slopes whose red-zone status had been downgraded once the retaining structures were certified. The approval process rested on the assumption that the defences would keep performing inside the design envelope even as the number of protected sites grew.

Once the barriers and maps were in place, new housing, hotels, and ski lifts appeared on slopes that local knowledge had kept empty. The absolute number of people and buildings inside potential avalanche paths rose even as the calculated risk per structure fell.

Concrete galleries and deflection dams more than twenty metres high protected traffic routes, and those same works created the perception that entire valleys had been rendered safe for permanent occupation. When extreme winters returned, the larger stock of exposed assets meant total damage figures could exceed those of nineteen fifty-one despite fewer individual failures.

The SLF later observed that many buildings put up after nineteen sixty stood in zones that would have stayed off-limits without the combination of maps and structures.

Second-order effects included higher maintenance costs for the defences themselves and pressure to extend protection farther upslope as development crept higher. Third-order effects appeared when ski-area operators and second-home buyers treated the engineered slopes as permanently stable, and construction accelerated during the tourism boom of the nineteen sixties and nineteen seventies.

The arithmetic ran this way. Each new retaining structure lowered the modelled probability for the parcels immediately downslope, and that lower figure justified the next round of building permits. The permits increased the total number of structures whose residual risk, however small, was now multiplied across a larger base.

Because the original hazard maps had been drawn before the barriers existed, later updates sometimes reclassified land only after development pressure had already shifted expectations about what counted as acceptable exposure.

Hazard mapping continued and was refined, yet the existing stock of buildings remained. Some cantons tightened red-zone rules. Others permitted object protection, such as concrete splitting wedges for individual structures.

The cost of securing one hectare with retaining structures reached roughly one million Swiss francs, and that figure prompted debate over whether further expansion should be subsidised. Current practice at the SLF combines the original structural inventory with stricter spatial planning that limits new exposure rather than attempting to armour ever-larger areas.

Planners now track the cumulative footprint of protected zones so that each added defence does not simply enlarge the area treated as developable. Maintenance budgets have also grown, because the steel and concrete elements need periodic inspection and replacement after heavy winters. That ongoing fiscal commitment was not fully anticipated when the first kilometres were installed.

When physical barriers were paired with hazard maps after nineteen fifty-two, the maps were read as permission rather than prohibition once the barriers existed. Nobody had to intend that reading for the boundary of acceptable development to move.

Decision-makers who fund protective infrastructure should require an explicit cap on new exposure inside the protected footprint before any construction begins.

Planners who update hazard zones after defences are built should also publish the cumulative count of people and buildings now located inside those zones, so growth in absolute risk stays visible even when the risk on each parcel looks smaller.

The interaction itself is the thing to watch. A physical measure and a regulatory map can shift what counts as buildable land without a vote to expand the settlement.
