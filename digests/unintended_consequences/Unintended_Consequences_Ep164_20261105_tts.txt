On June thirtieth, twenty twenty-one, the Philippine Supreme Court ruled in G.R. Number two two six eight five two. The parcel sat in Barangay Makiling, Calamba, Laguna. Asia United Bank owned it. A prior local zoning ordinance had reclassified it as industrial, and that reclassification qualified the land for exemption from the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program.

Renato Tañon and the heirs of Pio Candelaria had worked the land. They could not prove vested tenancy rights before the zoning change. They received no redistribution.

The Court treated a Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board certification of industrial zoning as conclusive. That certification removed the parcel from redistribution before any tenancy claim could be litigated. The exemption mechanism left those workers on the same plots under unchanged control.

The loss was not a surprise invented in twenty twenty-one. It followed a design choice made more than thirty years earlier, by people trying to stop exactly this kind of concentrated rural power.

After the nineteen eighty-six People Power Revolution removed Ferdinand Marcos, the new government under President Corazon Aquino faced intense pressure to break up the concentrated rural landholdings that had fueled decades of unrest. Congress answered on June tenth, nineteen eighty-eight, with Republic Act Number sixty-six fifty-seven, the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Law.

The statute capped private ownership at five hectares per landowner. It directed the Department of Agrarian Reform to distribute excess land to tenant farmers and landless workers. It also required that tenants on retained or exempt parcels receive leasehold protections, so they would no longer face arbitrary eviction or share-tenancy abuses.

Lawmakers viewed the caps and the protections as a practical way to extend the earlier Presidential Decree twenty-seven reforms while respecting constitutional property rights. Sudden plantation seizures looked, to them, like economic disruption the country could not absorb.

The policy appeared measured, and administratively feasible, because it built on powers local governments already had: the power to reclassify land for non-agricultural uses. That step seemed necessary if cities were to grow in an orderly way without dismantling every commercial farm at once.

Proponents reasoned that a five-hectare limit would still leave owners with viable holdings. On land that stayed exempt, regulated rents and written contracts would, they believed, gradually improve tenant security. The intention was security without seizure. The instrument was a balance between redistribution, retention, and local planning.

The Department of Agrarian Reform began issuing Certificates of Land Ownership Award in late nineteen eighty-eight. By twenty twelve it reported having distributed four point four nine million hectares to roughly two point eight million beneficiaries. Early official statements highlighted the transfer of rice and corn lands as a clear success.

Picture that area as farmland handed, on paper, to millions of households who had worked other people's estates.

The nineteen ninety-four amendments, Republic Act seventy-eight eighty-one, carved out fishponds, prawn farms, and certain livestock operations. Commercial producers had lobbied that forced redistribution would destroy export industries. The carve-outs reflected that argument.

Skeptics inside the agrarian-reform bureaucracy warned about a different door. Exemption clauses, plus the requirement that the Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board ratify local reclassifications, would invite strategic rezoning before coverage notices could be served. Implementation nevertheless proceeded parcel by parcel.

Municipal councils could pass zoning ordinances that shifted land from agricultural to industrial or residential categories. After that, the central board's certification locked in the exemption, even if tenants had already begun leasehold negotiations.

Section ten of the law listed explicit exemptions for non-agricultural uses. Local governments treated reclassification as a routine planning tool, not as an evasion tactic. The text gave them that reading. The people administering the program were using the authorities the statute had placed side by side.

Landowners responded by securing municipal zoning ordinances that reclassified agricultural parcels as industrial, residential, or commercial before the Department of Agrarian Reform could place them under coverage.

In the Calamba record, Asia Trust Development Bank obtained a February two thousand seven certification from the Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board that the grassland site had already been zoned industrial. The Supreme Court later treated that certification as conclusive.

Similar reclassifications removed hundreds of hectares in Sariaya, Quezon, even after some tenants had received Certificates of Land Ownership Award. The award on paper did not stop the category change.

The law also permitted conversion to high-value commercial crops without triggering redistribution. Many estates simply shifted from rice to bananas or pineapples and kept the same workforce under leasehold contracts. Tenants remained on the identical plots, paying rent or delivering shares to the same families or to corporate successors. The formal label was now exempt land.

A two thousand nine World Bank technical paper documented what those mechanisms produced together with slow processing and credit constraints on new owners. Ownership concentration remained largely intact in several provinces. The redistribution had moved millions of hectares.

In those provinces the pattern of who controlled the land had not broken in the way the nineteen eighty-eight statute was written to break it.

Second-order effects followed. Local land markets depressed, because banks hesitated to lend against titles that might later face exemption claims. Rural poverty continued, the poverty the original redistribution had been meant to relieve. A further consequence appeared among people who did receive land. New owners found themselves unable to consolidate fragmented holdings into efficient units.

The same reclassification route that protected large owners also discouraged investment in irrigation or machinery on smaller redistributed plots. A title that might be reclassified was a hard thing to borrow against, and a hard thing to improve.

The causal chain is specific. The statute granted redistribution power and local zoning authority at the same time. That pairing created a timing advantage: reclassification could be finished before coverage. Tenancy relations then survived under leasehold rules the law itself preserved on exempt parcels. The relationship did not have to be illegal to outlast the reform. It only had to be renamed.

The twenty twenty-one decision reaffirmed the pre-program reclassification route. Future claimants must prove that tenancy rights existed before any zoning change. Successive administrations have extended the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program's funding deadlines rather than closing the exemption loopholes. Some municipal governments have tightened zoning review.

A ResearchGate evaluation of thirty years of implementation notes that conversion applications continue to outpace actual redistribution in peri-urban zones.

No large-scale reversal of earlier exemptions has occurred. Legal preservation of control remains the dominant outcome. The same evaluation observes that poor targeting and land-consolidation problems persisted because the original statute never created an independent mechanism to audit whether a reclassification served genuine development needs or merely shielded existing owners.

Funding could be renewed. The timing gap did not have to be.

When a statute sets a hard ownership cap and, in the same text, authorizes local reclassification ratified by a central board, the actors who control the zoning calendar gain a reliable route around the cap. The cap is what the public hears. The calendar is what decides which parcels ever meet it. A municipal council can move a field from agricultural to industrial on a planning agenda.

A central board can then certify that change. Once the certificate exists, coverage arrives too late to matter. The measurement that counted was not acres owned on the day the law passed. It was the land-use category on the day a notice could be served.

When leasehold rules remain in force on every exempt parcel, the economic relationship between tenant and owner does not have to be rebuilt. It continues under a new legal heading. Rent replaces the share. A written contract replaces the old arrangement. The same family, or the same corporate successor, still receives the payment, and the same workers still stand on the same ground.

Security of a sort is there. The transfer of control is not. Lawmakers who wrote those leasehold protections were trying to stop eviction and abuse on land the state would not redistribute. The protections did that job. They also made the unreformed relationship lawful, durable, and easy to describe as compliance.

When proof of pre-existing tenancy rights is required after the fact, the tenant who lacked formal papers at the moment of reclassification loses the claim. Work on the land is not the same thing as a vested right the court will recognize. Documentation becomes the whole case, and documentation is what people without lawyers are least likely to have held on the day a zoning ordinance passed.

The burden falls on the party the statute was written to reach, and it falls after the category of the land has already changed.

Anyone weighing a redistribution rule now, a land cap, a housing set-aside, an affordability quota tied to a rezoning power, has a narrower question than whether the cap is sincere. How will any exemption or conversion procedure be timed against the moment coverage is declared, and who is positioned to finish the paperwork first?
