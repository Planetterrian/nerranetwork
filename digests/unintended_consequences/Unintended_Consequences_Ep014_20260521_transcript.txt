It's May 21st, 2026.
This is a show about what happens when smart,
well-meaning people try to fix something
and the world fixes back.
Justin Rosenstein built Facebook's like button
to let friends share quick appreciation
and it became the global yardstick for personal worth.
In the spring of 2007, he sat at his desk
inside the company's Palo Alto offices.
He sketched a small thumbs up icon
that could replace the longer process of typing a comment
like, that's great under a friend's post.
The goal was simple, remove friction
so that positive signals could travel faster
across the network.
Within two years, the button had spread
to every post on the platform.
Within a decade, it had become the default way
millions of people measured whether their day,
their appearance, or their work mattered at all.
Rosenstein and a small team of engineers
were responding to a concrete problem.
Facebook's early news feed was filling with updates,
yet most users scrolled past without reacting
because leaving a comment felt like too much effort.
The team wanted to preserve the friendly tone
of the early platform while making lightweight approval
effortless.
At the time, the dominant worry inside the company
was that people would stop sharing
if they received too little visible feedback.
A one-click signal seemed like a humane solution
that would encourage more connection rather than less.
Rosenstein later recalled that the group spent weeks
debating the exact wording and icon.
They ultimately rejected awesome and up-one
in favor of the single word like
because it felt warm and familiar.
The like button launched publicly on February 9th, 2009.
Early internal data showed an immediate increase
in user activity.
Posts that received likes were more likely
to receive comments later,
creating small, virtuous cycles.
Mark Zuckerberg publicly described the feature
as a way to make the world more open and connected.
Other platforms quickly copied the pattern.
Twitter introduced favorites the same year.
YouTube added thumbs-up ratings,
Instagram launched with a heart icon in 2010.
Inside Facebook, product teams began treating aggregate
like counts as a reliable proxy for content quality.
That approach shaped the first versions
of the algorithmic newsfeed introduced in 2011.
Because likes were both effortless and publicly visible,
they rapidly became the easiest available signal
of social standing.
Users began to notice that photographs or status updates
with higher like totals appeared higher in friends' feeds.
This created a feedback loop
that rewarded content engineered for quick approval.
Publishers and creators adjusted their output accordingly.
By 2013, many newsrooms were measuring story performance
by social shares and likes rather than traditional
readership metrics.
The same system that was meant to spread appreciation
also made the absence of likes conspicuous.
Teenagers reported comparing nightly like counts
as a form of social accounting.
Longitudinal studies later linked frequent checking
of these counts to increased anxiety
and depressive symptoms among adolescents.
Rosenstein himself observed that the button turned
every post into a small referendum on personal value.
Second order effects compounded.
Platforms optimize recommendation algorithms
around engagement velocity.
This favored emotionally charged material
over neutral or nuanced posts.
Third order effects reached the economics of journalism.
Outlets shifted resources toward listicles and reaction bait
because those formats reliably generated
the measurable signals advertisers demanded.
By 2017, Rosenstein had left Facebook
and begun limiting his own use of social media.
He told interviewers he kept his phone in grayscale
and avoided the platforms he had helped shape.
Instagram ran experiments in 2019
that hid like counts from other users in several countries.
The company cited concerns about social comparison.
Facebook itself introduced care and ha ha reactions in 2020,
partly to dilute the dominance of the original like.
None of these adjustments removed the underlying metric
from internal ranking systems.
Today like and equivalent signals remain central
to content recommendation across meta, TikTok and YouTube.
Public discussion of their psychological costs continues.
Simple low friction signals tend to become the dominant currency
in any system that measures them.
They are cheap to produce and easy to compare.
When those signals are attached to human identity
rather than to the content itself,
they reshape behavior at scale.
Designers and policy makers therefore face a recurring choice.
Either accept that the metric will drive the activity
or deliberately limit how visible and consequential
the metric is allowed to become.
The like button shows what happens
when that choice is deferred for more than a decade.
Consider the current wave of short form video platforms
that now surface content almost entirely
by counting quick taps and completion rates.
What signals are we allowing to stand in
for deeper measures of value there
and how visible should we let those signals become
before they start shaping what gets created next?
