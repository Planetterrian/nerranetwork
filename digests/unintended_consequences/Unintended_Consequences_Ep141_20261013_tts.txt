The explanation starts with the problem Watt actually faced, not with a theory of nations.

Britain's factories, mines, and transport systems needed cheaper power if they were to keep expanding.

The rollout did not replace one fixed stock of engines with more efficient ones.

A mill owner who once hesitated to install a steam engine because of coal bills now found it paid to run machines day and night.

The same logic scaled to railways.

Lower fuel cost per ton-mile let companies lay track into districts that could never have justified horse-drawn wagons or canals.

A line built because coal was cheap to burn became a reason to mine more coal, smelt more iron, and weave more cloth, all of which called for more engines.

Jevons noted that the very industries that adopted the efficient engines grew fastest.

The saving on each individual engine was swamped by the increase in the number of engines.

Contemporary observers who expected coal use to fall as engines improved were surprised by the opposite result.

They had counted only the reduction inside existing operations.

They had not traced how the same cost change altered the boundary between what was worth doing and what was not.

That boundary is the quiet part of the story.

An engine does not decide to consume more coal. A mine owner, a mill owner, a railway promoter decides that a job which failed last year's test now passes this year's test.

Multiply those decisions across Cornwall, across Lancashire, across every district a new line could reach, and the national tonnage stops looking like a fuel-saving program.

It looks like a construction program that fuel savings made possible.

Jevons set this down in eighteen sixty-five, in a book called The Coal Question.

He was not accusing Watt of a trick. He was following the decisions that cheaper power invited, one after another, until the sum no longer matched the hope attached to a single engine.

The Coal Question made Jevons briefly famous.

He was invited to give evidence before parliamentary committees on coal supplies.

The immediate policy response was modest.

There was some discussion of export duties, and of encouraging economy in domestic fires.

The larger pattern continued.

Later economists labeled the mechanism the Jevons paradox.

They tested it in lighting, in aviation, and in computing.

In each case the same sequence appeared.

An efficiency improvement reduced the resource needed for one task, lowered its price, and expanded total use.

No single reversal of the original engines occurred.

The insight entered economic textbooks instead, as a standing caution.

The caution is about assuming that technical savings automatically reduce resource consumption.

The pattern persisted because the underlying arithmetic remained unchanged across technologies.

Lower cost per unit of service leads to more units of service demanded.

That sentence does not depend on coal, or on pistons, or on a patent that expired in eighteen hundred.

It depends on a price falling, and on people noticing.

When James Watt and Matthew Boulton measured success by fuel burned per horsepower-hour, they rewarded designs that made each engine cheaper to run.

A cheaper engine was therefore worth installing in more places.

That rule, applied across Britain's growing industrial base, produced the measured rise in total coal tonnage that Jevons documented in eighteen sixty-five.

The metric was honest. It described the engine in front of them.

It did not describe the economy that would form around the engine.

If the ledger is national tonnage, or total electricity, those same decisions are the result.
