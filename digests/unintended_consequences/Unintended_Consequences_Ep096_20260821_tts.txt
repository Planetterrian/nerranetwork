Deposit insurance stopped the bank run — then paid for a decade of thrift gambling that cost taxpayers one hundred twenty-four billion dollars.

In March of nineteen thirty-three, Americans queued around the block to yank their savings out of banks that might not open tomorrow.

More than four thousand banks failed that year. Rumor alone could kill the next one down the street.

Half a century later, a money broker in New York could wire millions to a Texas savings-and-loan he had never seen.

The advertised rate was a quarter-point higher, and every dollar was guaranteed by the United States.

One picture is visceral panic. The other is a rate sheet, a wire transfer, and a government promise.

Deposit insurance was designed to kill the panic that destroyed thousands of banks.

Instead, it taught depositors not to ask questions, and it handed failing thrifts a taxpayer-backed chip with which to gamble.

The nineteen thirty-three picture is why the guarantee was born. The nineteen eighties picture is what the guarantee quietly taught people to stop doing.

The Banking Act of nineteen thirty-three, signed by Franklin Roosevelt on June sixteenth, created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the FDIC, because the alternative had just been lived in public.

Between nineteen twenty-nine and nineteen thirty-three, roughly nine thousand American banks suspended operations. In nineteen thirty-three alone, more than four thousand failed.

Depositors who got to the window too late lost their money. The rumor of a rumor was enough to finish the next bank down the street.

Representative Henry B. Steagall of Alabama had been pushing a federal guarantee for years. His rural constituents had watched local banks vanish.

Roosevelt himself had doubts. He worried that a government promise would invite careless banking and, eventually, a raid on the Treasury.

The American Bankers Association fought the idea as a subsidy for weak institutions. They were not being frivolous.

Those objections were the arguments of people who had watched banks from the inside, and they were thinking about incentives, not just headlines.

But in the spring of nineteen thirty-three the political fact was simpler than the theory.

People would not put money back into banks they could not trust, and the payment system of the United States had already stopped.

Steagall’s rural constituents and that frozen payments system won the argument.

Deposit insurance — initially twenty-five hundred dollars per account when the temporary plan took effect on January first, nineteen thirty-four — was the price of getting deposits to return.

A companion fund, the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation, or FSLIC, followed in nineteen thirty-four under the National Housing Act.

It wrapped the same logic around the thrifts that financed ordinary home loans.

The intention was narrow and humane. Stop the run, restore the checking account, let a solvent bank survive a false rumor.

It worked, almost immediately, at the thing it was built to do.

In nineteen thirty-three more than four thousand banks suspended. In nineteen thirty-four only sixty-one failed, and just nine of those were FDIC-insured.

Deposits flowed back.

Coverage was made permanent in the Banking Act of nineteen thirty-five and raised to five thousand dollars.

For the next four decades the American banking system looked, from the sidewalk, almost boring. Failures were few.

Premiums were flat. Every insured bank paid the same rate regardless of how it lent, because the whole point was confidence, not a credit-rating agency with a government seal.

The ceiling crept up with inflation and politics.

Ten thousand dollars in nineteen fifty. Forty thousand dollars in nineteen seventy-four.

Then one hundred thousand dollars in nineteen eighty, under the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act.

Skeptics had never fully disappeared, including Roosevelt and the American Bankers Association.

Economists and some bankers kept repeating the nineteen thirty-three objection. If depositors do not care, someone else has to, and that someone is the supervisor, who is always late.

Those warnings sounded academic while the three-six-three world of savings and loans still held.

Pay three percent on passbook savings, lend at six percent on thirty-year mortgages, and be on the golf course by three.

Regulation boxed thrifts into home mortgages, and interest rates stayed tame enough that the moral hazard stayed mostly latent.

The insurance funds looked fat. The public remembered the breadlines, not the footnotes.

Proponents could point to a genuine, measured victory. The contagious bank run, as Americans had known it, was gone.

Then the nineteen seventies broke the box. The guarantee was still there. The watchfulness was not.

Inflation and Paul Volcker’s Federal Reserve pushed short-term rates toward twenty percent in nineteen eighty-one.

Savings and loans were still stuffed with old fixed-rate mortgages yielding six or eight percent, while they had to bid for deposits in a market Regulation Q could no longer contain.

On a market-value basis, large parts of the industry were insolvent by the early nineteen eighties.

Estimates of the hole ran well into the tens of billions, and some contemporary analyses put the industry’s aggregate net worth deeply negative.

The straightforward response would have been to close the dead institutions, pay the depositors, and charge the loss to FSLIC.

FSLIC did not have the money. Congress did not want the bill, and the industry had friends.

So policy ran the other way.

The Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act, signed on October fifteenth, nineteen eighty-two, let thrifts load up on commercial real estate, consumer loans, and other assets they had never been staffed to underwrite.

California’s Nolan Act and Texas’s liberal state charters went further.

Ronald Reagan called Garn-St. Germain the most important financial legislation in fifty years. It was meant as a life raft.

For a thrift that was already broke, it was a lottery ticket bought with insured cash.

The victory of nineteen thirty-three had a mechanism, and the mechanism had a shadow.

Once a deposit was insured, the depositor’s incentive to distinguish a careful thrift from a reckless one collapsed to a single number: the interest rate.

The bank’s incentive shifted too. Losses beyond a thin slice of capital belonged to the insurance fund, which is to say, eventually, to the Treasury.

What followed was not a mystery of markets so much as a predictable use of other people’s guarantees.

Insolvent zombie thrifts paid above-market rates to attract deposits, then threw the money at acquisition-development-construction loans, raw land, and, in the more adventurous shops, junk bonds.

Deposit brokers in New York and elsewhere sliced large sums into one hundred thousand dollar certificates of deposit — the insurance ceiling — and shopped the nation for the highest yield.

No one had to inspect the loan file in Dallas or Irvine. The government had already inspected the ceiling.

Vernon Savings and Loan in Texas, run by Don Dixon, became a grim mascot.

A French villa, a fleet of aircraft, and a fifty-five hundred dollar teapot.

When federal conservators finally walked in, they found a loan book that was almost entirely delinquent.

Lincoln Savings, under Charles Keating, grew from a sleepy Orange County thrift into a vehicle for junk bonds and desert real estate.

Lincoln Savings also sold uninsured bonds to thousands of mostly elderly customers in its branches.

Many of those customers believed the bonds were as safe as the insured certificates advertised a few feet away.

Lincoln’s failure cost the government on the order of three billion dollars.

Across Texas, California, Florida, and the Southwest, similar books piled up.

Accounting forbearance and regulatory accounting principles let institutions count supervisory goodwill and other fictions as capital, so the zombies could keep growing.

Edward Kane and other economists described the result as a put option.

Heads, the owners and managers won. Tails, FSLIC paid.

By nineteen eighty-seven the FSLIC fund itself was insolvent. In nineteen eighty-eight and nineteen eighty-nine the failures came in waves.

From the mid-nineteen eighties through the mid-nineteen nineties, more than a thousand thrifts were closed or resolved.

The Resolution Trust Corporation, the RTC, created in nineteen eighty-nine, would eventually take over hundreds of them and dispose of hundreds of billions of dollars in assets.

Much of it was distressed real estate dumped into a weak market.

Second-order damage spread through construction employment and local tax bases.

A political scandal, the Keating Five, showed how the same guarantee that calmed depositors could be used to buy time from senators.

Third-order, the cleanup itself tightened credit as surviving institutions and the RTC retrenched, adding weight to the early nineteen nineties downturn.

The FDIC later put the taxpayer cost of the savings-and-loan crisis at roughly one hundred twenty-four billion dollars.

Broader tallies that include industry assessments run higher, commonly around one hundred fifty billion dollars.

The runs of nineteen thirty-three had been stopped. The bill arrived, with interest, in nineteen eighty-nine.

Congress did not repeal deposit insurance. It tried, imperfectly, to put the fear back somewhere else.

The Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of nineteen eighty-nine, signed by George H. W. Bush, abolished FSLIC, shifted thrift insurance to the FDIC, and created the RTC.

It raised capital expectations and restricted some of the wilder asset powers.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation Improvement Act of nineteen ninety-one went further.

Risk-based premiums, so riskier banks would pay more. Prompt corrective action, a ladder of mandatory constraints as capital declined.

Least-cost resolution rules, and tighter limits on brokered deposits for weak institutions.

Those tools were a confession that the nineteen thirty-three design had been incomplete. They were also imperfect.

Premiums still lag the risk. Capital ratios can be gamed. Prompt corrective action is only as honest as the accounting.

The two thousand eight crisis revealed that the largest banks carried an implicit guarantee beyond the statutory cap — too big to fail.

Coverage was raised to two hundred fifty thousand dollars, first as emergency, then as law.

In March of two thousand twenty-three, when Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank collapsed, uninsured depositors were protected after all.

The theory was that a run on the uninsured would become a run on the system.

The safety net widened again, for reasons that would have sounded familiar to Steagall. Panic is expensive, and guarantees are fast.

The countermeasures remain. So does the original bargain.

A guarantee that removes fear also removes watchfulness, and the watchfulness has to live somewhere.

It has to live in premiums that bite, so a reckless book costs more to insure than a careful one.

It has to live in capital that is real, not supervisory goodwill and other fictions that let an insolvent firm keep growing.

And it has to live in supervisors who close the dead instead of hoping they earn their way out.

Incentive structures find their loopholes, especially when the loophole is legal and the loss is socialized.

Forbearance is not mercy. It is a loan of public money at a hidden interest rate, and the borrower who is already insolvent will not use it conservatively.

Deposit insurance remains one of the great stabilizing inventions of the twentieth century.

The original bargain is still on the table every time a new class of liabilities looks run-prone.

In March of two thousand twenty-three, uninsured depositors at Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank were protected to stop a wider run.

Coverage had already been lifted to two hundred fifty thousand dollars, and the largest banks already carried an implicit too-big-to-fail promise past the statutory cap.

Each widening had a reason that would have sounded familiar in nineteen thirty-three. Panic is expensive, and guarantees are fast.

That does not make the reason wrong. It makes the residual question sharper.

The next time a regional bank, or some new pool of money that people treat like a checking account, starts to look run-prone, the crowd will want to be calmed.

What would you watch for before that day arrives?

Not whether the guarantee gets written. Who, exactly, is still supposed to stay afraid — the customer who no longer inspects the books, the supervisor who arrives late, or the taxpayer who holds whatever risk is left.
