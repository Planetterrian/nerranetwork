> **Park managers exterminated Yellowstone’s wolves to protect elk and livestock, then watched the rivers, forests, and beavers collapse in their absence.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the winter of 1926, a government hunter tracked and shot the last known wolf pack inside Yellowstone National Park. The action fulfilled a policy that had begun two decades earlier with the explicit goal of increasing elk numbers for hunters and protecting livestock on surrounding ranches. Within a few years the elk population surged, yet the park’s willow stands, aspen groves, and river channels began to change in ways no one had predicted.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The decision emerged from the U.S. Biological Survey and the National Park Service during the Progressive Era, when scientific game management was still young. Rangers and biologists viewed wolves as competitors that reduced the supply of game animals valued by sportsmen and as threats to cattle and sheep on the edges of the park. At the time, Yellowstone’s elk herd was estimated at roughly 10,000 animals and appeared to be declining under predation pressure. Federal predator-control programs had already succeeded in reducing wolf numbers across the western states, and extending the same methods to the nation’s first national park seemed consistent with both economic and conservation priorities. The people implementing the policy were trained in the utilitarian conservation tradition of Gifford Pinchot; they believed active intervention would produce a more abundant and stable landscape for human use.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1914 and 1926, park rangers and hired hunters killed or trapped at least 136 wolves inside Yellowstone boundaries, with additional animals taken on adjacent national forests. The last resident pack was eliminated in 1926 near Soda Butte Creek. Early reports from park staff noted rising elk calf survival and larger winter herds, which were celebrated in annual superintendent reports. Ranchers bordering the park reported fewer livestock losses. Although a handful of biologists, including Adolph Murie, later questioned the completeness of the removal, the policy faced little organized opposition at the time. By the early 1930s the park had declared wolves “extirpated.”

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Without wolves, elk numbers climbed toward 20,000 by the late 1930s and remained high for decades. Heavy browsing prevented willow and aspen regeneration along the Lamar and Gallatin rivers; some stands that had persisted for centuries failed to recruit new trees. Reduced woody vegetation removed food and dam-building material for beavers, whose colonies on the northern range dropped from dozens to a handful. With fewer beaver ponds, water tables fell and side channels dried, simplifying the river system and reducing habitat for trout and waterfowl. Songbird species that nested in the multi-layered willow thickets declined measurably. Researchers later documented that the absence of wolves also allowed coyotes to increase, which in turn suppressed smaller prey populations. The causal chain ran from the removal of a single apex predator through successive layers of vegetation, hydrology, and animal communities—an effect biologists would later label a trophic cascade.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By the 1970s and 1980s, accumulating field evidence from ecologists such as William Ripple and Robert Beschta prompted the National Park Service and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to plan reintroduction. In 1995 and 1996, 31 wolves captured in Alberta and British Columbia were released in the park. Within ten years the northern-range elk herd fell below 10,000, willow and aspen began recruiting again in some drainages, and beaver colonies increased. The recovery has not been uniform; climate variation, grizzly bear numbers, and human hunting outside the park also influence vegetation. Still, the reintroduction remains the clearest large-scale demonstration that restoring a missing top predator can reverse measurable changes in a terrestrial ecosystem.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
The episode shows that removing a keystone species can trigger effects that travel far beyond the original target population and become visible only after many years. Managers who focus on a single desirable outcome—more elk, fewer livestock losses—risk overlooking the indirect connections that maintain the rest of the system. The same principle appears today whenever policies target one variable, whether that is a single pest species, a carbon-reduction metric, or an economic incentive, without mapping the wider network of relationships. Before acting on a clear, immediate benefit, it is worth asking what other species or processes might depend on the element being removed or added.
