From nineteen ninety-four, cheap diclofenac cut India's vultures to one-thousandth and added over one hundred thousand deaths yearly.

A painkiller meant for working cattle, a scavenger collapse nobody measured, a sanitation gap that reached people, a ban that leaked, and the test that was never required.

Diclofenac began as a human drug.

In the nineteen seventies it was developed as a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory for arthritis and for pain after surgery.

It did what that class of drugs is built to do. It eased swelling. It gave people back ordinary movement.

When the patent lapsed in the early nineteen nineties, Indian pharmaceutical firms made inexpensive versions.

Farmers could finally afford a dose.

Before that shift, livestock owners had few practical options for the pain and inflammation that cut milk yields and draft power in working animals.

A bullock that could not plow was not only an animal in distress. It was a season of field work at risk.

The drug therefore looked like a straightforward welfare gain and a productivity gain for millions of smallholders who had watched cattle suffer without treatment.

No one at the time tested the compound on the obligate scavengers that cleaned the countryside of dead livestock.

That omission was not a secret plot. It was a blank in the file.

In the absence of any published data on avian kidney physiology, the decision carried no visible downside for the people who made the drug or administered it.

Farmers in the Gangetic plain and in Rajasthan, where cattle densities were highest, saw immediate improvements in animal mobility after a single low-cost injection.

The same dose that eased joint swelling in a bullock also kept the animal productive for another season of plowing or hauling.

Because diclofenac was already approved for human use, regulators treated the extension to livestock as a minor administrative step rather than a new environmental release.

The economic logic was simple arithmetic.

One vial treated several animals at a fraction of the price of imported alternatives.

Milk output or field work could resume within days instead of weeks.

For a smallholder counting days until the next milking or the next planting, that arithmetic was hard to argue with.

The intended patient was the cow. The intended return was work and milk. Both of those showed up quickly, and both of those were easy to see.

By the mid-nineteen nineties, veterinary use had spread rapidly through the cattle belt of northern and western India.

Roadside pharmacies and livestock markets stocked the human formulation in vials labeled for cattle.

Farmers injected animals shortly before slaughter or natural death.

Early surveys by the Bombay Natural History Society recorded no obvious change in carcass availability.

Vultures still arrived in large numbers at known feeding sites.

If you were standing at one of those sites in those years, the old picture still held. Birds came. Carcasses went.

A few ornithologists noted occasional dead birds with white urate deposits around the vent.

Those incidents were treated as isolated disease events rather than a systemic threat.

That reading was available from the evidence then in hand. A handful of dead birds, against flocks that still filled the sky, did not yet look like a collapse.

The scale of adoption can be traced in pharmacy sales records.

Once the molecule entered the generic market, annual production volumes rose from laboratory quantities to hundreds of tonnes.

That was enough to dose millions of cattle each year across states where open grazing was the norm.

Because the human and veterinary supply chains were not yet separated by regulation, the same blister packs moved from urban hospitals to rural veterinary shops with minimal friction.

A drug approved for people did not have to change plants, or packaging logic, or much of its route, to reach a bullock.

Early monitoring focused on whether the drug reduced clinical signs in livestock.

No parallel program tracked residue levels in the carcasses those livestock left behind.

The first systematic necropsies that later linked the drug to vulture mortality were still years away.

So the absence of reported problems was taken as evidence of safety.

Silence in the data was read as a clean result. It was, more precisely, a result no one had yet gone looking for.

The causal chain began when a treated animal died and its carcass retained enough diclofenac residue to kill any vulture that fed on it.

The bird did not have to receive an injection. It only had to do the work it had always done.

In two thousand four, J. Lindsay Oaks and colleagues published in Nature the experimental proof.

Oral doses matching those found in treated livestock produced the visceral gout and kidney failure already seen in field carcasses.

The white deposits ornithologists had noted years earlier were not a scattered disease. They were a signature of the drug.

By two thousand seven the oriental white-backed vulture had declined to one-tenth of one percent of its early-nineteen-nineties numbers, according to repeated road-transect surveys reported in PLOS One.

A ninety-seven percent drop recorded between the early nineteen nineties and two thousand seven left too few birds to process the daily volume of livestock mortality across the subcontinent.

Three Gyps species that had once numbered in the tens of millions were, within a decade of the generic flood, a remnant.

The arithmetic of exposure is straightforward once the residue data are examined.

A single treated buffalo could contain several lethal doses for a vulture.

Gyps species feed in groups of dozens.

One contaminated carcass could remove multiple breeding adults in a single afternoon.

Without those adults returning to nests, recruitment collapsed.

The loss was not only of birds in the air. It was of the next generation that those birds would have raised.

With the primary scavengers gone, the carcasses that once disappeared within hours lay rotting for days at village dumps and urban waste sites.

That persistence was the first visible rearrangement of the countryside.

Feral dog populations rose sharply, because an abundant, previously contested food source was now uncontested.

Each additional dog raised the probability of a human bite.

India already accounted for roughly a third of global rabies deaths, so the bite risk landed on a country that was already carrying a heavy share of that disease.

At the same time, uneaten carcasses leached pathogens into shallow aquifers and streams used for washing and irrigation.

Surface water near carcass dumps showed elevated bacterial loads.

The effect concentrated in the same districts that had once supported the densest vulture colonies.

The sanitation gap therefore ran through two measurable pathways. More dogs, and dirtier water, in the places where the birds had been thickest.

Economists Eyal Frank and Anant Sudarshan used the natural experiment of districts that historically differed in vulture habitat suitability.

In the American Economic Review they estimated that all-cause human mortality rose four point seven percent in high-suitability districts between two thousand and two thousand five.

Districts that had supported the largest vulture populations recorded human death rates more than four percent higher than comparable districts that had never hosted many vultures.

The four point seven percent increase reflects both direct zoonotic risk and the broader degradation of local water quality once the keystone scavenger was removed.

A narrow approval for a cattle painkiller had, through residue, kidney failure, and an emptied sky, become a public-health number.

India banned veterinary diclofenac in two thousand six and strengthened the prohibition in two thousand eight.

The response was real. It was also late relative to the collapse already measured, and it had to work through supply chains that had already mixed.

Follow-up carcass surveys published in PLOS One showed the prevalence of diclofenac-contaminated livestock fell from roughly ten point eight percent before the ban to six point five percent several years later.

That fall slowed the declines. It did not stop them.

Necropsies of vultures found dead between two thousand eight and two thousand twelve, reported in Oryx, revealed that some birds still carried diclofenac.

Others showed nimesulide residues associated with the same gout pathology.

Human-formulation diclofenac continues to be diverted into veterinary channels.

Populations of the three Gyps species remain at a few percent of pre-nineteen-nineties levels, with only modest signs of recovery.

The persistence of the drug after the formal ban illustrates the difficulty of separating two parallel supply chains that share the same molecule and the same manufacturing plants.

Even after the two thousand eight tightening, which required colored dyes and larger vial sizes for human formulations, enforcement relied on state-level pharmacy inspections.

Those inspections could not monitor every rural transaction.

A dye and a bigger vial are signals. They are not a seal on every roadside shop.

Meanwhile, alternative non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as nimesulide entered the veterinary market without the same level of pre-release testing against vulture kidney tissue.

The original oversight was reproduced at a smaller scale.

The ban changed the label. It did not, by itself, change the question regulators had failed to ask in nineteen ninety-four.

When regulators approved diclofenac for livestock, they evaluated only the intended patient species and the immediate economic return to farmers.

The process rewarded any low-cost drug that cleared livestock quickly.

It did not require tests on the scavengers whose removal would later impose sanitation costs in human lives.

A second principle sits beside that one.

Once a compound enters widespread agricultural use, a later ban on the veterinary formulation can still be undermined by leakage from the parallel human supply chain that regulators continue to authorize.

The burden that was missing is a demonstration of safety for the entire local scavenger community before marketing approval.

That requirement would move the proof from post-release mortality counts to pre-release feeding trials.
