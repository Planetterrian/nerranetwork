> **Mexico City's 1989 Hoy No Circula program prompted 85,000 annual used-vehicle imports, shifting the fleet toward dirtier cars.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1989 Mexico City launched Hoy No Circula, banning most drivers from using their cars one weekday each week according to the last digit of the license plate. The rule aimed to cut vehicle emissions on high-smog days. Instead, households acquired additional older vehicles, and the city shifted from exporting 74,000 used cars a year to importing 85,000 annually between 1990 and 1993.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Mexico City officials introduced the program amid worsening ozone and particulate levels that triggered frequent air-quality emergencies. They drew on the simple observation that removing a share of cars from the road on any given day would lower total exhaust output. At the time, vehicle ownership was rising quickly, public transit capacity was limited, and no precedent existed for large-scale behavioral offsets. The policy treated the fleet as fixed and assumed drivers would simply stay home or switch modes on their restricted day. Rational planners therefore expected measurable daily reductions in carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons, and nitrogen oxides. They calculated that a one-in-five reduction in weekday driving would translate directly into lower concentrations at monitoring stations, because each vehicle removed meant one less set of tailpipe emissions during peak ozone formation hours. No one had yet measured how households might respond once the restriction became permanent rather than temporary.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The ban began on 20 November 1989 and applied to private cars during weekday hours inside the Federal District. Exemptions were later added for newer vehicles that passed emissions tests, a feature intended to accelerate fleet turnover. Early monitoring showed lower traffic counts on restricted days, and city statements described the measure as an immediate success in easing congestion. Skeptics noted that enforcement relied on visible plate checks rather than continuous verification, yet the program expanded in reach and remained popular with residents who saw clearer skies on their non-driving days. Officials tracked the first-year drop in observed vehicle volumes and concluded the arithmetic was working as planned: one-fifth fewer cars meant roughly one-fifth fewer grams of pollutant per monitored hour. The exemption for newer cars was added precisely because planners wanted to reward owners who upgraded, assuming the older vehicles would gradually leave the fleet rather than stay in use by new buyers.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Households that needed daily mobility purchased second cars whose plates allowed driving on the restricted day. Because newer vehicles cost more, many buyers chose older, higher-emitting models that were cheaper on the used market. A 2008 study in the Journal of Political Economy documented that total vehicles in circulation rose and that the average emissions rate of the fleet increased rather than declined. Remote-sensing data collected in 2022 later showed that model-year 1994–2005 passenger vehicles registered in the State of Mexico made up only 7 percent of the sampled fleet yet produced 25–42 percent of measured CO, HC, NOx, and smoke emissions. Saturday expansions of the rule in 2008 produced no detectable air-quality gains across eight pollutants, consistent with the same adaptation pattern. Lower-income owners who could not afford a second car faced greater travel burdens while wealthier households drove more often in exempt newer cars. Gasoline consumption rose and the growth rate of public-transit ridership slowed. The net result was that the daily emission reductions originally projected at 15 percent or more failed to materialize on a sustained basis. The causal chain ran through the fixed plate-digit rule itself: once permission to drive became tied to a permanent attribute of each vehicle rather than to its actual emissions or to total kilometers traveled, any household with sufficient cash could neutralize the restriction by adding one more car whose digit covered the banned day. Older vehicles entered the city in large numbers because their lower purchase price offset the cost of the second registration and insurance; the 74,000-to-85,000 reversal in used-vehicle trade flows shows the scale of that inflow. When exemptions for newer cars were introduced, the same logic rewarded replacement among higher-income owners while the displaced older cars remained available for lower-income buyers who still needed mobility on every weekday. The result was a fleet whose average age and emissions rate moved in the opposite direction from the planners’ forecast.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Subsequent analyses, including household surveys and remote-sensing campaigns, confirmed the fleet-composition shift and prompted discussion of low-emission zones and accelerated electric-vehicle incentives. The original weekday rule remains in force, now paired with verification programs that still exempt many post-2005 vehicles. National light-duty emission standards have not been tightened since 2005, leaving older high-emitting cars on the road longer. Mexico City continues to set targets for further reductions while the highest-emitting 7 percent of the fleet accounts for a disproportionate share of pollutants. Proposals for tighter age-based restrictions or higher verification fees have been discussed, yet each new layer must still contend with the same arithmetic: any rule that leaves one usable vehicle short on a given day creates an incentive to acquire another vehicle whose plate or exemption status fills the gap.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Mexico City tied driving permission to a fixed plate digit, the rule rewarded any household able to add a second vehicle whose plate avoided the ban. When exemptions were later granted to newer cars, the same rule rewarded higher-income owners who could replace vehicles while leaving older cars in circulation for other users. Policymakers who next consider plate-based restrictions can examine whether the chosen trigger—digit, age, or emissions test—creates an incentive to keep or acquire additional high-emitting units rather than reduce total travel. How might a city test a proposed restriction against the actual cost and availability of second vehicles before it is enacted?
