This is Lee Myung-bak's water-security choice, the rushed orders that fixed depth and storage, the still water and summer blooms, the audits that could not unwind the target, and a question about testing flow before a number is locked.

Lee Myung-bak had campaigned on water security and flood protection.

The country had lived through years of drought and typhoon damage, and those memories sat close to the surface of the election.

In June two thousand eight, two months after he abandoned a larger grand canal proposal amid public opposition, he turned to the four major rivers as a practical alternative.

He directed his land minister to add weirs and deepen dredging, rather than rely on dike reinforcement alone.

Government planners had initially proposed reinforcing existing dikes and limited dredging to manage peak flows.

Lee believed deeper channels and controllable storage would deliver both reliable supply and safety during the monsoon season.

At the time, that approach aligned with conventional engineering views.

Faster-moving, deeper water was understood to reduce overflow risks for downstream cities and farms.

The administration treated the work as a straightforward infrastructure upgrade.

It would also create jobs during the global financial crisis, which gave the timetable a second kind of urgency.

Planners calculated that added depth and weirs could meet irrigation needs and peak-flow storage without requiring new land acquisition on the scale of the rejected canal.

The canal had asked the public for a different river. This plan asked the same rivers to hold more water, in place.

Between late two thousand eight and June two thousand nine, Lee issued repeated design orders.

Those orders raised the minimum depth on the Nakdong River from four meters to six meters.

They also set a total storage target of eight hundred million tons.

Construction crews installed sixteen large concrete weirs and performed extensive dredging.

The work was complete by twenty twelve.

Early government statements highlighted reinforced banks and new water reserves as immediate gains.

Some local officials reported fewer minor flood events in the first year after completion.

Those reports mattered. A project sold on flood protection had, in places, something to point to.

Skeptics in environmental groups, and in parts of the Environment Ministry, warned that the weirs would reduce flow velocity.

Those concerns were set aside during the accelerated timetable.

The Board of Audit and Inspection later documented that ministry staff had raised the risk of green tides, yet were directed not to emphasize it.

The compressed schedule had another consequence for the science itself.

Hydraulic modeling of the new minimum depths was completed only after the presidential directives had already fixed the storage volumes.

The numbers were locked. The picture of how the water would actually move arrived afterward.

Once the weirs held water at the new depths, flow on the Nakdong dropped to roughly one-tenth of its prior speed in many reaches.

A river that used to turn over in a day could now linger.

Lower velocity allowed nutrients to remain in place.

Biochemical oxygen demand and phytoplankton levels rose above the five-year average recorded before two thousand nine.

The Board of Audit and Inspection's twenty thirteen and twenty eighteen reports recorded higher algae concentrations and chemical oxygen demand across every reservoir the project had created.

Visible blooms that people came to call a green latte appeared each summer.

The twenty fourteen Prime Minister's Office panel confirmed that stagnation from the structures and the dredging was the direct cause.

The same panel noted that flood protection had improved in ninety-three point seven percent of surveyed areas.

Both findings could be true at once. The water was safer from some peaks, and poorer as water.

Because the weirs could not be operated to restore natural flushing without losing the storage the president had ordered, operators faced a recurring choice between water quantity and water quality.

Open the gates, and the reserve the directive required began to drain. Keep them shut, and the stillness remained.

Downstream communities that had expected cleaner irrigation water instead dealt with treatment costs and periodic restrictions during bloom events.

The same slowed water that reduced some flood peaks also prevented the natural scouring that had once limited nutrient buildup.

What the current no longer carried away stayed, season after season, for the next warm stretch to feed on.

Over successive seasons, the ecological parks built beside the rivers required repeated interventions.

The altered hydrology no longer supported the vegetation planners had assumed would thrive.

The twenty fourteen panel further observed that water leaks at six Nakdong weirs, and faulty gates at twelve others, compounded the difficulty of any corrective flushing.

Repairs themselves demanded stable reservoir levels. To fix the hardware, operators had to keep the water sitting still.

Environmental groups documented fish kills and shifts in benthic invertebrate communities that had not appeared in pre-project monitoring.

Those effects traced to the sustained low-oxygen conditions, rather than to any single typhoon.

The harm was not a storm that came and went. It was a new normal in the channel.

Multiple audits by the Board of Audit and Inspection, and the twenty fourteen investigative commission, documented the water-quality decline and structural issues at fifteen of the sixteen weirs.

Successive administrations faced pressure from civic groups to open gates more frequently, or to remove structures.

Storage targets limited how far those measures could go.

Some weirs now operate under adjusted schedules that trade a portion of the original water reserve for modest flow increases.

The partial fixes have not restored pre-project oxygen levels, and they have not eliminated the seasonal blooms.

Public debate continues over whether further modification, or full removal, would be required to address the persistent eutrophication.

Proposals to dismantle individual weirs have been studied.

Each study must still reconcile the original storage commitment with the oxygen deficits the monitoring has recorded.

The political clock that hurried the build has a long echo. Every later government inherits a number it did not set, and a river that now behaves according to that number.

When a single leader sets numeric targets for depth and storage without an independent review of flow consequences, the resulting structures reward volume over circulation.

The metric that can be announced is the metric the concrete is built to serve.

When an environment ministry is instructed to suppress its own risk assessments, later audits must reconstruct the original warnings from internal records rather than act on them in real time.

The knowledge was in the building. It was not allowed to change the design.

When construction proceeds on a fixed political timetable, the completed system locks in operating rules that cannot easily adapt once monitoring data arrive.

By then the gates, the leaks, and the storage promise are all arguing with the oxygen readings.
