> **South Africa's 1970s four-year fire rotations eliminated protea populations that needed at least nine years to mature.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the early 1970s, managers at Bontebok National Park introduced rotational burning on a four-year cycle to promote grazing for the antelope the park was created to protect. The policy produced mean fire-return intervals of 5.8 to 8.0 years across management units. Several obligate-seeding protea species that require longer intervals to reach reproductive maturity and set seed declined sharply while the shrub layer stayed within target fuel limits. The rotation therefore met its immediate fuel-reduction target yet removed the multi-year window those species needed to rebuild a viable seed bank before the next fire arrived.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Fred Kruger and other researchers working in the Cape fynbos documented the role of fire in maintaining the shrublands and supported a shift from fire exclusion to active prescribed burning. The 1968 policy change established compartment-based burning on planned 12–15 year frequencies in conservation areas. Managers at Bontebok National Park faced a different local pressure: the need to maintain open grassland for the bontebok population whose numbers had fallen under earlier protection regimes. A four-year rotation appeared to keep fuel loads low near settlements and to favor the short-lived grasses the antelope preferred. Early research by P.G. Jordaan had already set a minimum safe interval of eight years for Protea repens, yet the grazing objective took precedence in the park. The decision rested on the information then available about fuel reduction and herbivore habitat rather than on detailed data for every reseeding shrub. Park staff therefore treated the four-year cycle as a practical compromise that simultaneously lowered wildfire risk and improved forage, without yet measuring how that same cycle would intersect with the seed-accumulation schedules of the slowest-maturing proteoids.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Park staff divided the lowland fynbos and renosterveld into burning compartments and applied fire on the four-year schedule beginning in the early 1970s. Records later showed that actual return periods averaged between 5.8 and 8.0 years because of operational constraints and occasional wildfires. Proponents noted reduced fuel accumulation and improved visibility for visitors. Skeptics within the emerging fynbos research community pointed to maturation data indicating that some proteoids needed longer intervals, but the grazing mandate remained the operational priority. Coordinated programmes under Fred Kruger continued to gather data on fire effects at sites such as Jonkershoek. The uniform rotation was adopted because it delivered measurable control over one variable—fuel height—while other outcomes remained unmonitored at the time. Because each compartment was treated on the same clock, managers could predict and document fuel loads with simple height measurements; the same predictability, however, removed any chance that a given patch would remain unburned long enough for slower species to complete their reproductive cycle.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Post-fire surveys at Bontebok recorded the progressive loss of slow-maturing seed-regenerating shrubs, including Protea repens, once the shortened cycle became established. Studies of eastern coastal fynbos in the Tsitsikamma and Outeniqua Mountains found juvenile periods of 4–9 years for serotinous Proteaceae, with near-zero recruitment after fires in five-year-old vegetation. Recruitment reached replacement levels only after fires in vegetation at least seven years old and peaked in stands that had remained unburned for 38 years. A 2021 analysis across multiple protea populations derived conservative minimum intervals of 11–14 years when the 50 percent flowering-for-three-seasons rule was applied to the slowest-maturing stands. The 2025 study of long-term records in the Outeniqua World Heritage Site confirmed that intervals shorter than 12–14 years, especially on drier slopes, produced recruitment failure. Because the rotation kept the shrub layer within fuel targets, managers saw no immediate problem in vegetation structure even as the seed bank of the dominant reseeders collapsed. The uniform schedule removed the variability that had previously allowed some patches to escape fire long enough for seed set. Second-order effects included reduced cover and biomass of longer-lived shrubs and increased dominance by short-lived graminoids at Jonkershoek comparison sites. Third-order effects appeared when the simplified community proved less resilient to subsequent droughts and alien-plant incursions. The arithmetic of the shortened cycle was straightforward: a plant that first flowers at year five or six could contribute seed in only one or two seasons before the next fire removed the adults; repeated over several rotations, the cumulative seed input fell below the threshold needed to replace the pre-fire population. In contrast, stands that reached 38 years accumulated multiple seasons of seed, so that even variable post-fire conditions still produced replacement-level or higher seedling counts. The policy therefore optimized the variable it measured—fuel height—while leaving the regeneration niche of the obligate reseeders outside any monitored threshold.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Later research programmes documented the mismatch between the four-year target and the maturation thresholds of proteoids and recommended raising minimum intervals to nine years or more. Bontebok managers adjusted burning plans to allow longer rotations in selected units, though operational and safety pressures continued to compress some intervals. The 2010 review of the park’s regime noted that repeated burning of immature vegetation had already removed several slow-maturing species. Subsequent policies in the broader fynbos region incorporated site-specific juvenile-period data rather than fixed rotations, yet wildfires still dominate actual fire occurrence. No single reversal restored the lost protea populations where local seed sources had been eliminated. Where seed banks had been exhausted, even extended intervals could not recreate the lost cohorts without active reintroduction.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a single measurable target such as fuel height or grazing sward dictates rotation length, the regeneration requirements of the slowest-maturing species fall outside the managed window. In this case the actor was the compartment schedule itself, which rewarded uniform fuel reduction while the rule for protea persistence—accumulation of seed over multiple post-fire years—remained unmeasured. Managers today who set intervals by one dominant variable can test whether the slowest life-history trait in the system still completes its cycle under that same interval. How many current hazard-reduction plans would still hold if the maturation time of the most sensitive native species were added as a binding constraint?
