> **The 2018 FOSTA-SESTA law shifted U.S. sex ads offshore without cutting trafficking arrests.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On April 11, 2018, President Donald Trump signed the Allow States and Victims to Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act, known as FOSTA-SESTA. The measure amended Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act to remove liability protections for platforms that knowingly facilitated sex trafficking or prostitution. Instead of shrinking the market, the law pushed commercial sex advertising to overseas servers and encrypted channels.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Representative Ann Wagner of Missouri introduced the Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act in the House in 2017, while Senator Rob Portman of Ohio sponsored the companion Stop Enabling Sex Traffickers Act in the Senate. Both lawmakers had examined Backpage.com’s operations and concluded that Section 230 was shielding a site that profited from ads linked to trafficking. They argued that platforms should face federal and state criminal and civil liability when they knowingly promoted or facilitated prostitution. At the time, Backpage generated substantial revenue from adult-services sections, and federal investigators had already documented cases in which traffickers used the site to advertise minors. Wagner and Portman presented the bills as a targeted update to existing law that would encourage platforms to remove illegal content without broadly censoring speech. The package passed the House 388–25 on February 27, 2018, and the Senate 97–2 on March 21, 2018. Supporters viewed the near-unanimous votes as evidence that the measure addressed a clear gap in accountability. They saw the change as restoring the original intent of Section 230 by carving out only the narrow set of claims involving knowing promotion of prostitution or trafficking, leaving the rest of the immunity framework intact.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Craigslist removed its entire personals section on March 23, 2018, citing the pending legislation. The bill became law on April 11, 2018, just five days after federal authorities seized Backpage.com. Major U.S. platforms responded by deleting or restricting adult-advertising categories to avoid new criminal exposure under 18 U.S.C. § 2421A. Proponents stated that the law would finally allow prosecutors and victims to reach the operators of sites that had previously claimed immunity. Within weeks, however, activity that had concentrated on a few large domestic platforms began appearing on smaller sites hosted outside the United States. The new criminal provision created penalties of up to ten years for operating a platform to promote prostitution and up to twenty-five years in aggravated cases involving five or more persons or reckless disregard of trafficking, which raised the stakes for any U.S.-based company that continued to host such material.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
A Government Accountability Office report covering 2014–2020 found that platform operators relocated servers and domains to countries including the Netherlands, Turkey, and Germany to reduce exposure to U.S. jurisdiction. The same report documented that the market fragmented across many more foreign sites, complicating evidence collection because investigators now needed mutual legal assistance treaties for each new jurisdiction. A Carnegie Mellon University study summarized by LegalClarity in 2026 showed that the volume of online commercial sex ads dipped immediately after FOSTA-SESTA but then returned to prior levels, distributed across a larger number of overseas platforms. The Hacking//Hustling participatory research project, published in 2020, surveyed online sex workers and reported that 72.5 percent experienced increased economic instability after the law took effect. The same survey found that 33.8 percent of respondents reported more violent encounters with clients once screening tools on domestic sites disappeared. Because platforms could no longer safely host screening information or review boards without risking liability, many workers moved to encrypted messaging apps and offshore boards that offered fewer safety features. The GAO noted that only one of the eleven federal criminal cases brought against platform operators between 2014 and 2020 actually relied on the new FOSTA provisions. Cross-border evidence requests lengthened investigations, as described in the 2021 Porn Studies article citing the GAO findings. The shift also reduced the visibility of transactions to U.S.-based researchers and service organizations that had previously monitored domestic platforms. Operators calculated that moving hosting overseas lowered the probability of U.S. criminal charges more than any incremental moderation effort would, since the new statute exposed them to both federal prosecution and state-level suits. Fragmentation multiplied the number of distinct legal systems investigators had to navigate, turning what had been a single-point request into a series of separate treaty processes, each with its own delays and disclosure limits. When domestic platforms removed review sections and verification tools to stay inside the safe harbor, workers lost the ability to check client histories that had previously reduced risk, pushing more transactions into channels where no such history existed. The Carnegie Mellon data indicated the total volume of ads recovered because demand did not disappear and new offshore sites filled the gap, showing that the liability rule altered only the location, not the underlying volume of activity. Enforcement agencies therefore faced a larger set of smaller targets, each requiring fresh evidence collection across borders, which stretched limited resources and reduced the number of cases that could be completed in the same time period.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Federal prosecutors have continued to rely primarily on pre-existing statutes rather than the new FOSTA tools, according to the GAO. No civil damages or restitution orders have been issued under the law’s private right of action provisions. Sex-worker organizations and civil-liberties groups have called for repeal or amendment, arguing that the liability change produced measurable safety harms without corresponding reductions in trafficking. Some platforms have maintained minimal U.S. presence while routing traffic through foreign entities, a pattern the Taylor & Francis analysis linked to increased enforcement delays. The market structure that emerged after 2018 remains in place, with activity spread across more jurisdictions and fewer centralized points of contact for law enforcement. Researchers continue to track the same offshore distribution pattern first measured in the GAO report, confirming that the relocation incentive has not reversed.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When lawmakers removed Section 230 immunity for a specific category of content, platform operators responded by moving servers beyond U.S. reach rather than eliminating the underlying transactions. The rule change therefore rewarded relocation over moderation and made evidence gathering slower for investigators who must now navigate foreign legal systems. Anyone drafting platform-liability statutes can examine whether the new obligations apply only to domestically hosted services and whether enforcement resources exist for cross-border cases before the shift occurs. The arithmetic of risk shows that a ten- or twenty-five-year penalty attached to U.S. servers will prompt operators to choose foreign servers whenever the cost of relocation is lower than the cost of compliance. How might similar geographic incentives appear in today’s debates over encryption or content-moderation rules?
