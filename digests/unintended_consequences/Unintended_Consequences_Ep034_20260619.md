> **Purdue Pharma promoted OxyContin as nearly addiction-proof, yet the drug helped ignite an opioid epidemic that has killed more than a million Americans.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1996 a primary-care physician in southwestern Virginia wrote a first prescription for OxyContin 10 mg twice daily, telling the patient the time-release formula would control chronic back pain without the dependence risks of shorter-acting opioids. Within eighteen months the same doctor had written several hundred such prescriptions, confident that the medical literature supported an addiction rate below one percent. The patient, however, soon found the tablets losing effect after four hours and began crushing them for faster relief, a pattern repeated in exam rooms across Appalachia and the Midwest.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Purdue Pharma, controlled by the Sackler family, developed OxyContin to solve a recognized clinical problem: millions of Americans suffered untreated or undertreated chronic non-cancer pain, yet physicians hesitated to prescribe morphine or other immediate-release opioids because of fears of addiction and regulatory scrutiny. The company’s chemists created a controlled-release oxycodone tablet intended to deliver steady analgesia over twelve hours, theoretically reducing the peaks and troughs that could reinforce drug-seeking behavior. At the time, prevailing medical opinion held that patients in pain rarely became addicted; a 1980 letter in the New England Journal of Medicine reporting only four cases of addiction among nearly 12,000 hospitalized patients was widely cited as reassurance. Pain specialists and primary-care leaders welcomed a longer-acting option that might let patients function at work and at home. Regulators at the FDA, operating under 1990s standards that accepted company-submitted studies of short duration, approved the drug in December 1995 for moderate-to-severe pain.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Purdue launched OxyContin in 1996 with an unusually large sales force that visited physicians’ offices more than 600,000 times in the first year alone. Detailers distributed charts and videos stating that addiction occurred in “less than one percent” of patients, language drawn from the 1980 letter rather than from any long-term trial of the new formulation. Early sales figures were encouraging: by 2000 annual prescriptions exceeded 6 million, and revenue reached $1.1 billion. State medical boards and the Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations simultaneously began emphasizing pain as a “fifth vital sign,” reinforcing the message that aggressive treatment was responsible medicine. A small number of pain specialists and pharmacists raised concerns about diversion and crushing of the tablets, but these warnings remained marginal against the prevailing view that under-treatment of pain was the larger public-health problem.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The twelve-hour duration proved shorter for many patients, prompting rapid dose escalation that Purdue’s own internal studies had not fully anticipated. Because the tablet contained far more oxycodone than previous immediate-release products, crushing or chewing it delivered a large bolus that produced euphoria and physical dependence. Sales representatives continued to repeat the “less than one percent” figure even after company pharmacovigilance reports documented hundreds of abuse cases by 1999. As prescriptions multiplied, so did diversion: pills moved from medicine cabinets into high-school parking lots and, later, into informal pill mills in Florida and Ohio. When state and federal authorities tightened prescribing rules after 2010, many dependent patients turned to cheaper heroin, and later to illicitly manufactured fentanyl. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention now attributes roughly 500,000 deaths directly to prescription opioids and their successors between 1999 and 2022, with total overdose mortality exceeding one million when synthetic opioids are included. Secondary effects included overwhelmed foster-care systems in rural counties, declining labor-force participation among prime-age men, and a lasting erosion of trust between patients with legitimate pain and the physicians who now fear regulatory sanction for writing any opioid prescription.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Purdue Pharma filed for bankruptcy in 2019 after agreeing to settlements exceeding $8 billion; members of the Sackler family contributed an additional $6 billion while retaining personal immunity from further civil suits. The FDA required abuse-deterrent reformulations, and most states enacted prescription-drug monitoring programs. Medical education shifted toward multimodal pain management and lower starting doses, yet many patients with severe chronic pain now report difficulty finding any prescriber willing to continue therapy. The abrupt reduction in legitimate supply also accelerated the transition to illicit fentanyl, illustrating how supply-side interventions can produce new, more dangerous markets. Pain medicine as a specialty continues to grapple with revised guidelines that still lack robust long-term data on functional outcomes.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a single, easily measured claim—such as an addiction rate below one percent—becomes the cornerstone of both marketing and clinical practice, complex biological and social feedback loops are easily overlooked. Incentive structures that reward rapid uptake of a new therapy while externalizing the costs of dependence will reliably discover any gap between trial conditions and real-world use. Finally, once a treatment becomes embedded in professional norms and institutional metrics, reversing course requires not only new evidence but also repair of the trust that was spent in the original rollout. How might current enthusiasm for rapid adoption of weight-loss drugs or AI diagnostic tools be stress-tested against similar second-order effects?
