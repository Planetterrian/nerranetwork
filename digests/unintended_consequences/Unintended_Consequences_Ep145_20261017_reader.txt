Thirty-two major dams on the Tigris and Euphrates reduced the flood pulses that fed Iraq's Mesopotamian marshes.

In the early two thousands, Landsat images of southern Iraq showed what those missing pulses had left behind.

Vast reed beds and shallow lakes had become cracked salt flats, dotted with dead fish and abandoned buffalo.

The change had not arrived in a single season.

It accumulated as each new reservoir filled and held back the high-water peaks that once spread across the floodplain.

Turkish, Syrian, and Iraqi engineers had built those dams to store water for hydropower and for year-round irrigation in upstream regions that had long suffered from unreliable rainfall.

What the structures removed, instead, was the annual spring flood that had kept the downstream marshes alive for millennia.

Without those pulses, the balance between inflow and evaporation that had sustained the marshes for thousands of years tipped toward steady decline.

To understand why the dams looked like the right answer, you have to stand where the planners stood, after the Second World War, looking at dry land and an erratic river.

Turkish planners launched the Southeastern Anatolia Project, known as GAP, to lift one of the country's poorest regions out of poverty.

Their calculation was straightforward.

Store winter and spring runoff behind large dams.

Use that stored water to generate electricity for factories, and to water cotton, wheat, and fruit on land that had been too dry to farm reliably.

Similar projects in Syria and Iraq followed the same logic.

Rivers that had flooded unpredictably could be turned into reliable assets for national development.

At the time, the marshes downstream appeared as remote wetlands whose seasonal rhythms would simply adjust to steadier, lower flows.

The engineers worked with the data they possessed.

River gauges. Reservoir models. National growth targets.

What those tools did not yet hold was a full picture of how tightly the marshes depended on the pulse of high water.

Their models treated the river system as a linear supply chain rather than a pulsing delta, where the timing of delivery mattered as much as the total volume.

That framing was rational given the questions they were asking.

How much water can we hold? How much power can we make? How many hectares can we irrigate in a dry year?

The marshes were not inside that question.

A wetland that flooded and receded on its own schedule did not look, from an upstream planning office, like a customer with a delivery date.

Construction accelerated through the nineteen seventies and the nineteen eighties.

Turkey completed the Keban Dam in nineteen seventy-four and the Atatürk Dam in nineteen ninety-two.

Syria added the Tabqa Dam.

Iraq built its own storage reservoirs.

By the early nineteen nineties, more than thirty large dams stood along the two rivers.

Their combined storage capacity exceeded three times the average annual flow, according to the two thousand one report from the United Nations Environment Programme.

Early results looked promising.

Turkish hydropower output rose sharply.

New irrigation districts opened in the Harran plain.

From the standpoint of the regions GAP was built to serve, the project was doing what it had been designed to do.

Water that used to rush past in a few violent weeks could now be metered out across a growing season.

Factories had power. Fields that had waited on rain had a schedule.

Downstream, the same success registered as a flattening.

The spring flood peaks that had once reached the marshes began to drop in height and to arrive less often.

Each new dam upstream altered the hydrograph in measurable ways.

Peak discharges fell.

Base flows became more constant.

The variability the marshes had evolved to exploit was the very thing the operating rules were written to remove.

Iraqi authorities, facing their own water shortages after the nineteen ninety-one Gulf War, started additional drainage works that accelerated the change.

Those works were a response to scarcity, not a celebration of it.

Less water was arriving. The remaining channels were cut, banked, and redirected toward uses the state could measure.

Upstream and downstream, the same idea was at work: hold what you can, send onward what you do not need.

Nobody in that chain was required to recreate a historical flood hundreds of kilometers away.

The marshes had once covered between fifteen thousand and twenty thousand square kilometers.

They shrank dramatically.

The two thousand one assessment from the United Nations Environment Programme, drawing on Landsat imagery, documented an eighty-five percent loss by two thousand.

The bulk of the rapid desiccation occurred between nineteen ninety-one and nineteen ninety-five, when Iraqi drainage canals and embankments severed remaining water sources.

The upstream dams had already cut the frequency and height of flood pulses over preceding decades, so less water reached the marshes even before that final drainage.

The two losses stacked.

First the pulse was shortened and lowered, year by year, as reservoirs filled.

Then the last connections were cut on purpose, in a few hard seasons, because what remained no longer looked like a living floodplain worth leaving open.

Without periodic flushing, salinity climbed.

Reed beds died.

The fishery that supported thousands of Marsh Arab families collapsed.

Those families had lived by fishing, by herding buffalo, and by harvesting reeds, for generations.

A reed bed is not only habitat. It is building material, fodder, and a way of moving through shallow water.

When the reeds died and the buffalo were abandoned on drying ground, the livelihood and the landscape failed together.

Dust storms increased as exposed lake beds dried.

What had been a shallow sea of green became a source of windblown salt and silt for people who had never set a net in those waters.

A paper published in twenty twenty-five in the journal MDPI Plants, on threatened aquatic species, records vegetation losses between eighty and ninety-nine percent across the major marsh units from nineteen eighty-two to twenty seventeen.

The paper attributes that long-term stress to reduced inflows from the dammed rivers, alongside the drainage of the nineteen nineties.

The electricity and the crops went somewhere else.

They benefited distant urban and agricultural centers, the places the dams had been built to serve.

The causal chain ran through storage rules.

Those rules maximized retention for power and irrigation.

They carried no requirement to release water at the precise volume and timing needed to recreate historical flood peaks hundreds of kilometers downstream.

Volume, averaged over a year, can look adequate on a ledger while the week that matters never comes.

A marsh does not drink an annual average.

It drinks a pulse: a rise high enough to push fresh water across a flat, to flush salt, to wet the roots of reeds, and then a fall that leaves the shallows productive.

Steady base flow, even when the yearly total is large, does not do that work.

Evaporation in southern Iraq is relentless.

Once the spring sheet of water stopped arriving, the balance tipped, and each dry year made the next one harder to reverse.

That is a second-order effect of a first-order success.

The dams did store water. The turbines did turn. The Harran plain did green.

The third-order effect was social and atmospheric at once.

Communities whose entire economy was timed to the flood lost the calendar they lived by, while dust from the emptied beds traveled beyond the marsh edge.

After two thousand three, some embankments were breached, and partial reflooding occurred.

Reporting in WIRED, drawing on data from the United Nations Environment Programme and from NASA, put the restoration at roughly fifty-eight percent of the marshes by two thousand six.

Water could be let back onto ground that drainage works had sealed.

Recovery remained limited by continued upstream withdrawals, by higher salinity, and by reduced overall flow.

Breaching a bank returns a path. It does not return a river that is being held upstream for someone else's season.

Turkey continued operating the GAP reservoirs for power and irrigation.

Downstream provinces faced recurring drought.

No single country reversed its dam operations.

Talks on minimum flow agreements began, and produced modest results.

A minimum flow is a floor. It keeps a channel from going dry.

It is not the same instrument as a spring peak timed to spread across a floodplain.

The marshes that remain are smaller, more saline, and still vulnerable to any further reduction in spring runoff.

Attempts to negotiate coordinated releases have repeatedly stalled.

Each nation's operating rules still prioritize its own storage targets over the downstream pulse that once connected the entire basin.

That stall is not mysterious once you look at what the rules reward.

A reservoir operator who releases a flood peak on a downstream wetland's schedule is giving away stored energy and stored irrigation days.

The benefit lands in another country, in a landscape that does not appear on the operator's production sheet.

The cost lands at home, in a missed generation target or a shortfall in a cotton district.

Under those incentives, modest minimum-flow talks are the agreement that can be signed.

A pulse large enough to matter is the agreement that keeps failing.

When Turkish, Syrian, and Iraqi planners sized reservoirs to capture three times the rivers' average volume, they rewarded steady extraction for upstream farms and turbines.

They did not write a corresponding rule that protected the downstream flood pulse the marshes required.

Later restoration showed the limit of a downstream-only fix.

Breaching drainage works could bring water back only if upstream releases still delivered enough seasonal volume.

The path and the pulse are different requirements. Restoring one without the other leaves a smaller, saltier wetland that can be lost again in a dry run of years.

Decision makers today, sizing new storage on transboundary rivers, can test whether their operating rules preserve at least the historical peak-flow events that sustain wetlands hundreds of kilometers downstream.

That test is specific. It asks not only how much water the reservoir will hold, but which week the water must still be allowed to rise, and who is accountable if that week never comes.

If you follow environmental systems the way we do on Environmental Intelligence, the marsh story is a reminder that a river's average can look healthy while the event the wetland actually lives on has already been engineered out.
