The Federal Reserve launched quantitative easing to rescue the economy from another depression,

On November 25, 2008, the Federal Reserve announced it would purchase up to $600 billion in
mortgage-backed securities and agency debt.
The goal was straightforward.
Push long-term interest rates down when short-term rates had already reached zero.
Within months, the program expanded.
Over the next six years, the Fed's balance sheet grew from roughly $900 billion to more
than $4.5 trillion.
The immediate crisis passed, yet the same purchases that stabilized banks and credit markets
also drove sustained increases in the prices of stocks, bonds, and real estate.
Assets overwhelmingly held by higher income households.
The architects were Federal Reserve officials led by Chair Ben Bernanke.
They drew on his own academic work on the Great Depression.
After Lehman Brothers collapsed in September 2008, credit markets froze and unemployment
climbed toward 10%.
With the Federal Fund's rate already at zero, conventional tools offered no further leverage.
Large-scale asset purchases modeled on earlier Bank of Japan experiments and on Bernanke's
own historical research appeared to be the remaining channel for lowering borrowing costs
across the economy.
Policy makers viewed the step as an emergency measure to prevent a second 1930s-style contraction,
not as a permanent shift in monetary operations.
QE1 began in late 2008 and was enlarged in March 2009.
QE2 followed in November 2010 with $600 billion in treasury purchases.
QE3, announced in September 2012, added $40 billion per month in mortgage-backed securities
and continued until tapering began in late 2013.
At each stage, Fed statements emphasized that the purchases would support employment and
price stability.
Early data showed falling mortgage rates, rising home sales, and a stock market recovery.
Critics inside and outside the Fed, including several regional bank presidents, warned that
sustained purchases could distort price signals and favor financial asset owners.
But the majority of the Federal Open Market Committee judged the risks manageable given
the depth of the downturn.
Because the purchases directly increased demand for longer-term bonds and mortgage securities,
their yields fell and prices rose.
Investors seeking higher returns shifted into equities and real estate.
Lifting the S&P 500 more than 200% between March 2009 and the end of 2015.
Home prices in many coastal markets also recovered and then exceeded pre-crisis peaks.
Ownership of these assets is heavily skewed.
The top 10% of households by wealth held roughly 80% of equities and 70% of real estate equity
during this period. Wage growth for the median worker remained near 2% annually for most of the
decade, while corporate profits and capital gains accrued disproportionately to asset owners.
The resulting increase in the wealth-to-income ratio for the top 1% was documented in subsequent
Federal Reserve distributional accounts. Lower borrowing costs also encouraged share buybacks
and dividend increases, rather than broad-based wage gains or new hiring in some sectors.
Because the transmission mechanism relied on portfolio rebalancing,
the policy had no direct lever to raise labor compensation once unemployment began to fall.
Second order effects included higher pension fund liabilities for underfunded public plans and
increased housing cost burdens for younger households entering markets later.
Third order effects appeared in political discourse, as the visible divergence between asset prices
and median wages reinforced perceptions that monetary policy primarily served financial markets.
By 2014, the Fed began tapering purchases and completed the wind down in 2015,
though the balance sheet remained elevated. Officials publicly acknowledged that the
distributional consequences had been larger than anticipated. Minutes from 2013 through 2015
record repeated discussions of wealth effects. No formal reversal occurred, because reversing the
purchases at scale risk renewed market stress. Instead, later frameworks incorporated explicit
attention to financial conditions and labor market outcomes. The balance sheet expanded again
in 2020 under different circumstances, but the post-2008 experience shaped internal debates about
the limits of asset purchases as a long-term tool. Today, the distributional footprint of large scale
purchases remains a standing research topic at the Fed and other central banks. When a policy
operates through asset prices, its benefits concentrate where asset ownership is already highest.

rather than along the channel's designer's most intent. Monitoring only headline employment
