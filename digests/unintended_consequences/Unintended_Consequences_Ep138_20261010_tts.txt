Compensation for ten thousand wolf-killed reindeer annually let Finnish herds grow and deplete winter lichens.

In the early 1990s Finnish officials began paying herders the full market value plus a 1.5 multiplier for every reindeer carcass they could locate after a wolf kill.

The payments were meant to end the cycle of retaliation shootings that had driven wolves to the edge of extinction in the north.

Instead the same rule removed any financial reason for herders to guard newborn calves.

Reindeer numbers on the winter lichen grounds climbed until the forage itself began to disappear.

Reindeer husbandry in Finland’s northernmost districts had long operated under the shadow of large carnivores.

By the late 1980s repeated wolf and bear attacks were pushing some cooperatives toward bankruptcy.

Herders responded by shooting predators on sight.

Parliament therefore turned to a straightforward remedy already used in other Nordic countries.

Direct monetary compensation for documented losses seemed the practical answer.

The policy rested on two assumptions that seemed reasonable at the time.

First, if the state reimbursed every verified kill, herders would stop killing wolves.

Second, the payments would be modest because most carcasses would never be found in the vast winter ranges.

Officials examined records showing that many herding districts lost calves during the snow-free months yet could rarely produce physical evidence.

They therefore built a second computational payment stream based on district-level averages for calving success and predation rates.

This two-part structure looked like a practical way to cover both visible and invisible losses without requiring herders to patrol every square kilometre of remote pasture.

The compensation system was rolled out through the Reindeer Herders’ Association cooperatives and administered by the Agency for Rural Affairs.

Under the 2009 Game Animal Damages Act a herder received 1.5 times the current market value of any carcass that could be produced.

A separate computational payment covered estimated calf losses between May and November.

Districts that suffered repeated heavy losses qualified for a doubled multiplier.

Early reports from the cooperatives noted a sharp drop in illegal wolf shootings.

The Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry described the scheme as a successful model of coexistence.

The European Union later imposed an annual ceiling of ten million euros on total payouts.

Because the calf-loss component relied on cooperative-wide estimates rather than individual counts, payments flowed even when a particular herder chose not to increase fencing or night checks.

The system therefore scaled quickly across the 20 northernmost districts without requiring new field staff or verification technology.

Because compensation depended on locating a carcass, herders had little incentive to invest time or money in calf protection once the payment rule was in place.

Calf-loss models built into the system assumed average predation rates and paid cooperatives regardless of whether individual herders had taken extra precautions.

With the economic pressure to guard calves reduced, more animals survived to winter pasture.

Studies of the Finnish–Norwegian border fence show that lichen biomass on the Finnish side, where year-round grazing is permitted, has fallen to roughly one-third the level recorded on the Norwegian side that enforces strict seasonal rotation.

The Ambio reconstruction of lichen cover from 1959 onward attributes the decline primarily to sustained trampling and foraging rather than simple consumption.

As winter forage thinned, reindeer ranged more widely in search of food.

This increased encounters with wolves that had begun to recolonize from the east.

The same compensation payments that had lowered direct persecution therefore contributed to larger reindeer populations, heavier pasture pressure, and renewed wolf presence.

Districts qualifying for the doubled multiplier kept receiving higher payments even as total lichen cover continued to drop.

This created a feedback loop in which poorer forage led to more visible losses and therefore more compensation.

The computational calf payments, calculated once every three years from district averages, did not adjust downward when actual survival improved.

So the financial signal to reduce herd size never appeared.

By the mid-2010s several cooperatives reported that lichen pastures were producing visibly poorer body condition in reindeer.

The Ministry began experimenting with supplemental feeding programs.

The E U spending cap remained in force, so shortfalls in compensation were prorated across claimants.

No fundamental redesign of the per-head, ex-post payment rule has occurred.

Herders continue to locate and report carcasses, yet the underlying dynamic of expanding herds on shrinking forage persists.

Recent vegetation inventories confirm that the contrast across the Norwegian border fence has only widened since the compensation regime matured.

Supplemental feeding has eased immediate weight loss for some animals but has not restored lichen biomass, because the added feed does not reverse the trampling damage already done to the ground layer.

When Finland tied compensation directly to the number of documented carcasses, the rule rewarded the reporting of losses rather than the prevention of losses.

This allowed calf survival to rise without corresponding increases in herding effort.

When the same rule was paired with computational calf payments calculated from district averages, it further decoupled individual herder actions from financial outcomes.

Total reindeer numbers were permitted to exceed the winter range’s long-term capacity.

Decision-makers who next design predator-compensation programs can test whether payments are conditioned on verifiable protective measures before the first reindeer are counted.
