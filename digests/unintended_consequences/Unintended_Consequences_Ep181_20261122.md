> **Protecting 2 million hectares of Indonesian peat domes failed when adjacent drainage lowered water tables and ignited fires.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In Ketapang Regency, West Kalimantan, national zoning rules distinguished Peat Ecosystems with Protection Function on domes deeper than three meters from Cultivation Function zones on the shallow edges of each Peatland Hydrological Unit. Drainage canals installed for oil palm on the permitted mineral and shallow-peat soils lowered the water table under the protected domes. This was designed to separate conservation from cultivation. Instead, it caused the protected peat to dry, subside, and burn.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
After the 2015 fires burned 2.6 million hectares across Indonesia, with one-third on peat, the government created the Peatland Restoration Agency, known as BRG, and appointed Nazir Foead as its chief. Foead and the agency worked from existing rules such as Ministry of Agriculture Decree No. 14/2009, which already banned new oil palm on peat deeper than three meters, and Government Regulation PP 71/2014, which capped drainage depth at 0.4 meters. The approach mapped 865 Peatland Hydrological Units and required at least 30 percent of each unit, including the central domes, to remain in protection function. Officials reasoned that confining plantations to the outer, shallower zones would keep the carbon-rich domes wet while still allowing economic use of the landscape. The 2011 peat-conversion moratorium and later BRG restoration targets of two million hectares reflected the same logic: draw a clear spatial line between protection and production. At the time, the maps and the hydrological-unit framework appeared precise enough to enforce that separation on the ground. One objection at the planning stage was whether water could move laterally across the boundary between shallow and deep zones; the prevailing view held that the depth-based rule plus the 0.4-meter cap would keep the domes saturated because the peat itself was treated as a stable barrier once the permitted areas stayed within legal limits.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Regency governments, including Ketapang, incorporated the national zoning into local spatial plans through multistakeholder forums that brought together plantation companies, smallholders, and forestry agencies. Early maps showed roughly two million hectares of domes, and roughly half remained unplanted, so BRG pushed to designate those unplanted areas as protected even inside existing concessions. Companies operating on the permitted shallow fringes installed drainage canals and planted oil palm, following the water-table limits set in PP 71/2014. Proponents pointed to the legal clarity of the protection-function category and the post-2015 commitment to restore two million hectares by 2020. Some local officials noted that the policy preserved space for both conservation and continued cultivation within the same hydrological unit. The rollout treated each parcel’s drainage as an independent engineering choice; canals were dug to the allowed depth on the cultivation side, and the assumption was that any seepage would be minimal because the deeper peat held more water naturally. Multistakeholder meetings recorded agreement on the maps, yet field verification of exact peat depths remained limited by the resolution of the available surveys.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Drainage on the cultivation zones did not stop at the mapped boundary. Water flowed outward through the porous peat, dropping the water table inside the protected domes as well. In the Ex-Mega Rice Project area, models showed that full drainage for oil palm would eventually place 67 percent of the peat surface below flood level after one hundred years because of subsidence. The arithmetic ran as follows: each centimeter of water-table drawdown allows oxygen to reach previously anaerobic peat; oxidation then releases carbon dioxide and reduces the volume of the peat matrix by roughly the same amount, so the surface lowers; repeated cycles compound until the dome itself begins to flood from below during wet seasons while remaining dry enough to burn in dry seasons. Dry peat in the domes ignited during the annual dry season, and fires crossed back into the very areas the zoning had intended to keep wet. Nazir Foead observed in 2016 that inaccurate maps still allowed licenses on deep peat, so drainage continued under the legal cover of adjacent concessions. The 2017 study in Wetlands Ecology and Management recorded 28 percent encroachment of plantations onto protected peat despite the three-meter rule. Carbon emissions from the drained and burned peat reached an estimated 350 to 400 million tonnes of CO2 per year. Smallholders and companies that had followed the zoning rules found their fields threatened by the same fires the policy was meant to prevent. Restoration efforts by BRG, which relied on canal blocking, could not keep pace with ongoing drainage from neighboring licensed areas. The boundary between protection and production proved hydro-logically porous, so the second-order effect of repeated fires erased the first-order gain of spatial separation. A further objection—that better enforcement of the 0.4-meter cap would solve the problem—ran into the fact that even legal drainage on one side of the line altered the hydraulic gradient across the entire unit, pulling water from the dome faster than natural recharge could replace it.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
After the pattern became clear, BRG called for full protection of all remaining peat domes and for companies to forgo replanting once the first cycle ended. Regency-level multistakeholder forums in Ketapang produced a local policy instrument aimed at reconciling protection and production, yet implementation stayed limited by mismatched rules across national, provincial, and district levels. Some plantations adopted zero-burning practices and tighter water management under RSPO guidance, but the underlying drainage continued wherever mineral-soil or shallow-peat permits remained valid. By most accounts the two-million-hectare restoration target for 2020 was not fully met. Current efforts focus on aligning economic incentives and enforcing hydrological-unit planning more strictly, yet the same tension between adjacent land uses persists. One practical adjustment under discussion is to treat the entire hydrological unit as the unit of permitting rather than individual concessions, so that any new canal on the shallow fringe would require offsetting water-table controls inside the dome.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Ketapang planners drew protection lines around domes deeper than three meters, they rewarded any drainage that stayed just outside those lines, so water tables fell inside the protected zones anyway. When BRG mapped 865 hydrological units and required 30 percent conservation, it rewarded continued planting on the remaining 70 percent without a mechanism to keep that planting from altering the whole unit’s hydrology. When the three-meter depth rule and the 0.4-meter drainage cap were applied separately to neighboring parcels, they rewarded short-term planting decisions whose long-term subsidence and fire costs crossed parcel boundaries. How might today’s land-use maps treat entire hydrological units as single decision spaces rather than collections of adjacent permits?
