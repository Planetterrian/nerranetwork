In the spring of two thousand eight, farmers across Iowa and Illinois prepared fields for record corn plantings.

Congress had passed the Energy Independence and Security Act the previous December.

Policymakers had meant the expanded Renewable Fuel Standard to cut oil imports and to lower tailpipe emissions.

The route they chose was blending more corn ethanol into gasoline.

The same policy increased synthetic nitrogen applications on Midwest cropland.

Additional nitrate loads moved down the Mississippi River toward the Gulf of Mexico.

President George W. Bush signed the Energy Independence and Security Act on December nineteenth, two thousand seven.

Both houses of Congress had approved the law with bipartisan support.

Title Two of the law expanded the Renewable Fuel Standard that Congress had created in two thousand five.

The new statute set rising annual volume requirements.

Those requirements reached thirty-six billion gallons of renewable fuel by two thousand twenty-two.

At the time, crude-oil prices were high and volatile.

The United States depended on imported petroleum.

Greenhouse-gas emissions from transportation were a standing concern.

Against that background, greater domestic biofuel production looked like a practical step.

Lawmakers and agency staff at the Environmental Protection Agency, the EPA, saw corn-starch ethanol as a fuel already in commercial use.

Corn-starch ethanol could scale quickly without new engine technology.

The policy therefore rewarded increased corn production as the most direct route to the statutory targets.

Between two thousand five and two thousand seven, oil repeatedly exceeded seventy dollars per barrel.

Supply disruptions in the Middle East were still fresh in legislative memory.

Substituting a portion of gasoline demand with a domestically grown liquid fuel lined up with security goals.

The same substitution lined up with environmental goals many legislators had already endorsed in the earlier Energy Policy Act.

The people writing the volumes were not guessing in the dark about engines or refineries.

They were extending a fuel pathway the country already knew how to make.

The statute took effect immediately.

The EPA began issuing annual renewable-volume obligations.

Those obligations required refiners to blend specified quantities of ethanol.

Corn prices rose sharply through two thousand twelve as demand for ethanol feedstock grew, then fell; by two thousand sixteen the season-average price was below its two thousand eight level.

In the first years after enactment, ethanol production capacity expanded rapidly in the Midwest.

By two thousand ten, the industry was on track to meet the law’s intermediate targets.

Early reports from the Department of Agriculture noted higher farm incomes and greater domestic fuel output.

For farm communities watching those reports, the policy looked like it was doing what Congress had asked.

A few hydrologists warned otherwise.

Researchers later cited in a two thousand eight paper by Donner and Kucharik, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, PNAS, said meeting the full ethanol volumes would require more corn acreage.

More corn acreage would require more fertilizer.

Those cautions received limited attention during the rollout.

Refiners met their obligations by purchasing Renewable Identification Numbers tied to each gallon of ethanol.

That purchase created a liquid market.

The market transmitted statutory demand directly into the corn futures price.

Because the volume schedule increased each year, the price signal stayed consistent rather than temporary.

A farmer planning the next season was not looking at a one-year spike.

The farmer was looking at a schedule written to keep rising.

Lark and colleagues, in a PNAS paper in two thousand twenty-two, combined econometric land-use models with biophysical data.

The study found that the Renewable Fuel Standard increased United States corn acreage by the amount named at the top of this episode, and increased total cropland by two point one million hectares, between two thousand eight and two thousand sixteen.

The same study estimated that nationwide synthetic nitrogen fertilizer use rose seven point five percent.

Nitrate leaching from agricultural soils increased five point three percent.

Most of the additional corn was grown in the Mississippi River basin.

A larger share of that reactive nitrogen moved downstream.

Donner and Kucharik’s earlier PNAS modeling had projected a further rise.

Satisfying the ethanol mandates, that modeling said, would raise dissolved inorganic nitrogen export by the Mississippi-Atchafalaya system between ten and thirty-four percent.

The extra nitrate reached the northern Gulf of Mexico each summer.

There, the nitrate fueled algal blooms.

The subsequent decay of those blooms depleted oxygen in bottom waters.

The seasonal hypoxic area was already large before two thousand seven.

The area therefore faced additional nutrient pressure that made reduction targets harder to reach.

Farmers responded to higher corn prices by planting continuous corn rather than rotating with soybeans.

That shift further raised nitrogen requirements.

Equipment dealers and fertilizer suppliers adjusted inventories accordingly.

Those inventory choices locked in the higher application rates for multiple seasons.

The policy’s price signal operated nationwide.

Even regions outside the traditional Corn Belt saw expanded corn plantings and associated fertilizer use.

One consequence of the continuous-corn shift is that soybean nitrogen credits disappear.

A legume leaves nitrogen in the soil for the next crop.

After soybeans, a following corn crop can often take a reduced nitrogen rate.

Each additional hectare in continuous corn receives a full recommended nitrogen rate instead.

That full rate amplified the modeled national increase in reactive nitrogen.

The Mississippi basin drains roughly forty percent of United States cropland.

Even modest percentage rises in application translate into thousands of additional tons of nitrogen reaching the river each year.

Picture the chain as a set of doors that all opened the same way.

A gallon target became a blending duty.

A blending duty became a tradable credit.

A tradable credit became a corn price.

A corn price became a planting decision.

A planting decision became fertilizer on a field that drains, eventually, to a summer dead zone.

None of those doors required a person to intend harm to the Gulf.

Each door only required someone to meet the metric in front of them.

By the mid-twenty-tens, the modeled water-quality effects had entered regulatory discussions.

The statutory volume mandates remained in place.

The EPA continued to adjust annual standards.

The agency did not alter the underlying corn-ethanol pathway.

Industry groups disputed the magnitude of the nitrogen increases reported by Lark and colleagues.

Those groups argued that yield improvements and improved nutrient management had offset much of the expansion.

The size of the effect is therefore not a settled public number in every forum.

What did not arrive was a comprehensive federal program reversing the land-use changes attributed to the Renewable Fuel Standard.

Some states have tightened nutrient-management rules for watersheds draining to the Gulf.

The federal biofuel requirements continue to shape planting decisions each spring.

Estimates of current nitrate loads still reflect the elevated baseline established after two thousand seven.

The persistence of the mandates reflects the statutory structure.

Once the volume schedule is written into law, only new legislation or a successful lawsuit can change the core requirement.

Later environmental programs have had to operate around an agricultural baseline that has already shifted.

Hypoxia-reduction work, in other words, inherited a farm map the fuel law had already redrawn.

A state rule on how fertilizer is applied can matter.

A state rule cannot, by itself, erase a federal reason to plant more corn.

When Congress tied renewable-fuel volumes directly to corn-starch ethanol in the two thousand seven Act, the price premium on corn rewarded any farmer who could plant more of it.

The reward did not depend on downstream nitrogen consequences.

The EPA’s annual blending obligations then transmitted that price signal year after year.

Those obligations carried no automatic adjustment for water-quality outcomes.

Later attempts to meet hypoxia-reduction goals therefore had to work against an agricultural system already reoriented by the earlier mandate.

The thing counted was gallons.
