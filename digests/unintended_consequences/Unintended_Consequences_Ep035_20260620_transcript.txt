Millions of women took hormone replacement therapy
to prevent heart disease and stay youthful
until one trial showed it raised their risks
of cancer, stroke, and heart attack instead.
In the summer of 2002, more than six million American women
were taking some form of hormone replacement therapy.
Many of them were using it for reasons
that had little to do with hot flashes.
Then on July 9th, the Data Safety Monitoring Board
halted the largest part of the Women's Health Initiative
trial three years early.
The reason was not ambiguous.
Combined estrogen and progestin
increased the incidence of invasive breast cancer,
coronary heart disease, stroke, and pulmonary embolism.
The same regimen that physicians had prescribed
to protect aging hearts had instead produced measurable harm.
The modern use of menopausal hormone therapy
began with a clear clinical problem.
The sudden loss of a variant estrogen
produced vasomotor symptoms, sleep disruption,
and accelerated bone loss.
Consider what that loss actually meant in practice
without the estrogen that the ovaries had supplied.
Many women experienced sudden temperature spikes
and night sweats that interrupted sleep night after night.
At the same time, bone mineral density began to decline
at a faster rate than before menopause,
raising the lifetime probability of fractures.
In 1966, Brooklyn gynecologist Robert A. Wilson
published Feminine Forever.
He argued that estrogen deficiency was a treatable disease state
rather than a natural transition.
Pharmaceutical companies developed
conjugated equine estrogens known as premerin
and later combined regimens that added
progestin to protect the uterus.
By the late 1980s, observational studies,
such as the nurse's health study suggested
that women who chose to take estrogen after menopause,
experienced roughly 40 to 50% fewer coronary events.
That figure, if taken at face value,
implied that for every hundred women
who would otherwise have had a heart attack
or angina episode, only 50 to 60 would experience one
while on therapy.
Medical societies therefore began to frame long-term
harmonies as a preventive strategy against osteoporosis
and cardiovascular disease.
These conditions together accounted for the majority
of deaths in older women.
At the time, no large randomized trial had tested that framing.
One obvious objection was whether healthier women
were simply more likely to start and continue therapy,
yet the size of the reported difference
made many clinicians set that concern aside for the moment.
Between 1982 and 1992, annual U.S. prescriptions
for non-contraceptive estrogens rose from roughly 15 million
to more than 30 million.
That doubling occurred over a single decade.
The Food and Drug Administration expanded the label
in 1992 to include osteoporosis prevention.
Many clinicians extended the indication further
to presumed cardio-protection.
Academic centers and industry-sponsored
continuing education programs presented hormone therapy
as standard care for healthy postmenopausal women.
A 1995 survey of U.S. physicians found that more than 80%
recommended estrogen to asymptomatic patients
with a family history of heart disease.
Skeptics existed.
Some epidemiologists noted that women who took hormones
also tended to have higher education
and better access to care.
Yet the prevailing view held that the observational benefit
was large enough to outweigh uncertain risks.
The arithmetic seemed straightforward at the time.
If coronary events dropped by roughly 40 to 50%
in the women who took estrogen,
and if cardiovascular disease caused most deaths
after age 65, then withholding therapy looked
like the greater harm.
The Women's Health Initiative enrolled 16,608 women,
aged 50 to 79 with an intact uterus.
Researchers randomized them to daily conjugated
equine estrogen, plus medroxy progesterone acetate,
or placebo.
After 5.2 years, the hazard ratio for coronary heart disease
was 1.29.
In plain terms, that meant the rate of new coronary events
was 29% higher in the hormone group than in the placebo group.
The hazard ratio for stroke was 1.41,
indicating a 41% increase.
The hazard ratio for pulmonary embolism was 2.13,
more than doubling the risk.
The hazard ratio for invasive breast cancer was 1.26,
a 26% rise.
Absolute excess risks were modest.
There were eight additional coronary events,
eight additional strokes, and eight additional breast cancers
per 10,000 women per year.
These risks appeared in a population
that had been told the therapy was protective.
Because most participants were more than a decade past
menopause, the trial could not distinguish
whether earlier initiation would have produced
different results.
One might ask whether the numbers would have looked better
if the average participant had begun therapy at age 51
instead of age 63.
The study design simply did not allow that comparison.
The sudden reversal triggered an estimated 50% drop
in US hormone prescriptions within 18 months.
Subsequent observational data suggested
that women who had started therapy near the time
of menopause experience different risk profiles.
Yet the blanket recommendation had already collapsed.
Many patients who might have benefited
for short-term symptom relief stop treatment.
Others continued without clear guidance
underration or formulation.
Professional societies quickly narrowed indications
to the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time.
They focused on vasomolder symptoms.
The Food and Drug Administration added black box warnings
in 2003.
Over the following decade,
re-analyses of the women's health initiative data
stratified by age and years since menopause
showed that women aged 50 to 59
who began therapy closer to menopause
had no excess coronary risk.
They showed possible benefit for all cause mortality.
European regulators reached similar conclusions.
Today, guidelines from the North American menopause society
and the endocrine society emphasize individualized decisions
rather than universal prevention.
The episode also prompted larger investments
in comparative effectiveness research
on different estrogen roots and doses.
This created an indirect but durable shift
in how menopausal care is studied.
Large-scale observational patterns can mislead
when healthier or wealthier patients
are more likely to receive the intervention under study.
Translating risk reduction observed in one demographic group
to an entire population can produce net harm
once absolute risks are measured in randomized settings.
A abrupt withdrawal of a widely used therapy
can itself leave patients without viable alternatives
until new evidence accumulates.
The question that remains is how medicine can design trials
early enough and stratify them finally enough
to separate the patients who gain from those who lose
before millions begin treatment.
