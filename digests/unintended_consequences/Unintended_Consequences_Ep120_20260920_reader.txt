State agricultural authorities championed the fence.

They faced a rapidly expanding rabbit population that had been introduced decades earlier for hunting and food.

By the early twentieth century those animals were stripping vegetation across vast stretches of the continent.

The spread threatened sheep stations and wheat farms that supported the young economy.

Officials reasoned that a continuous physical barrier, maintained by regular patrols, could halt further westward movement.

The barrier would buy time for other control measures to take hold.

The approach drew on earlier, smaller fences that had shown some success on individual properties.

At the time the engineering task seemed manageable.

A single long line appeared preferable to endless local enclosures.

Decision-makers worked with the tools and understanding available.

They viewed the fence as a practical, large-scale application of existing boundary technology.

Construction of the Western Australian barrier began in 1907 and continued for several years.

Government crews extended the fence across hundreds of kilometres of arid country.

Early reports noted that sections already in place appeared to slow rabbit movement in the short term.

Local landholders expressed relief that a coordinated effort was finally underway.

Proponents argued that consistent maintenance and dog patrols would keep the structure effective.

A few observers raised questions about whether the rabbits might already be present on both sides of the planned route.

The prevailing view held that the barrier would still limit further spread once finished.

The project proceeded on the assumption that the animals’ advance was a spatial problem that could be solved by blocking their path.

Even when construction crews encountered evidence of fresh diggings or tracks on the western side of the alignment, the interpretation remained the same.

These were treated as small incursions that the finished fence plus regular patrols could still contain.

Materials were chosen for durability in dry conditions.

The completed barrier therefore encountered populations already established on the western side.

Once the animals were present on both sides, the fence could no longer perform its intended function of containment.

The original physical fence, fixed in place and unable to adapt, offered no help against these changing biological dynamics.

Control efforts shifted almost entirely to ongoing biological and chemical interventions.

The costly barrier remained in the landscape with little ongoing purpose.

Each time a virus strain killed a large fraction of rabbits, the survivors carried genes that conferred partial immunity.

Their offspring inherited and amplified those genes.

Within a few breeding seasons the proportion of resistant animals rose enough that the same virus strain produced only modest mortality.

Field workers then faced the choice of accepting lower kill rates or investing in new strains.

The fence, built of wire and posts, could not be updated in any comparable way.

Its physical presence continued, yet it no longer intersected with the actual movement or survival patterns of the population it had been meant to restrain.

Once the limits of the fence became clear, authorities accepted that it would not serve as a permanent solution.

They gradually reduced emphasis on its maintenance for rabbit control.

Attention turned to successive refinements of the myxoma virus and, later, to additional agents such as rabbit haemorrhagic disease.

Each new biocontrol measure produced temporary reductions followed by the emergence of resistance.

The fence itself has remained a visible feature in parts of the landscape.

Its role in rabbit management is now minimal.

Modern efforts combine multiple evolving tools rather than relying on any single fixed barrier.

Interventions that assume a fixed problem can be solved by a fixed structure often meet systems capable of changing around them.
