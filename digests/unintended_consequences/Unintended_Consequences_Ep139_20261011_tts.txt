South Georgia toothfish licenses meant to stop illegal fishing instead cut local orca calf survival.

This is Unintended Consequences, episode one hundred thirty-nine.

In the cold waters off South Georgia, longline vessels began hauling empty hooks where Patagonian toothfish had once waited.

The Argentine government had issued a limited number of exclusive licenses in the 1990s precisely to keep foreign poachers out and give the stock room to recover.

Instead, the same rules concentrated the boats in the places where orcas had long hunted.

The irony sits in the geography itself.

The very shelf edges that made enforcement feasible also made the fishing pattern predictable enough for the whales to treat the lines as a new, reliable hunting ground rather than a passing disturbance.

Argentine fisheries managers and scientists working through the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources faced a clear problem in the early 1990s.

Unlicensed vessels were stripping toothfish stocks around South Georgia at rates no one could track.

The remedy they chose was straightforward.

By granting a small number of vessels the sole right to fish inside the zone, they hoped to create both accountability and economic incentive for those operators to protect the resource.

At the time the approach matched the best available thinking on limited-entry fisheries.

It had already shown results in other high-value whitefish grounds.

The managers were not trying to reshape marine mammal behavior.

They were trying to replace chaos with a known list of permitted boats.

They reasoned that if only a handful of operators held the rights, each would have a stake in keeping the total catch within sustainable bounds.

Their future income depended on the same stock remaining viable year after year.

The licenses took effect in 1993 and 1994.

They restricted the fishery to a handful of Argentine-flagged longliners that had to report catches and positions.

Early reports showed the hoped-for drop in unlicensed activity and a modest rebound in average fish size.

Proponents inside the Argentine fisheries secretariat pointed to rising catch-per-unit-effort figures in the first two seasons as proof the system worked.

A few observers noted that the permitted vessels now worked the same narrow shelf edges every year.

The concern stayed on paper because the immediate conservation target, fewer pirate boats, had been met.

The arithmetic looked favorable on the page.

Fewer vessels meant lower total effort.

The reported landings could be divided by the known number of hooks to produce a single number that appeared to rise.

That single number became the metric everyone watched, even as the spatial footprint of the effort stayed fixed on the same few square kilometers of seabed.

The repeated presence of the same vessels in the same orca feeding grounds changed the whales options.

Within a few seasons, local pods learned to follow the longlines and strip hooked toothfish before the gear could be retrieved.

Catch rates for the licensed boats fell sharply.

To reach fish the orcas could not easily take, skippers moved to deeper sets and switched to heavier lines and larger anchors.

Those changes increased the chance that an orca calf swimming near the surface would become entangled and drown.

Population surveys conducted in later years recorded fewer calves reaching independence in the affected groups.

The original policy had succeeded in keeping unlicensed vessels away.

Yet it had also turned a mobile, dispersed fishery into a fixed, predictable food source.

The license removed the option to fish elsewhere inside the zone.

So every boat returned to the same lines day after day.

The orcas, facing a repeatable signal of floats and bait, discovered they could reduce their own search time.

The fishers, seeing their daily yield drop, added weight and depth that the calves could not escape.

The second-order effect was therefore not simply lower orca numbers.

It was a change in the age structure of the local pods.

The animals most likely to investigate the new food source were the younger ones still learning to hunt.

The third-order effect appeared when the same pods began to associate all vessel noise with an easy meal.

This increased the frequency of interactions even on days when the gear had already been modified.

By the early 2000s, researchers and some of the licensed captains themselves began documenting the pattern of calf entanglements.

The Commission responded with seasonal area closures and requirements for acoustic deterrents.

Those measures reduced but did not eliminate the problem.

A later shift toward even deeper pot gear for toothfish removed the longline interaction for orcas.

Yet it introduced new questions about seafloor impact.

Today the South Georgia toothfish fishery operates under tighter spatial rules.

Local orca groups show signs of behavioral recovery, though full recruitment rates have not returned to pre-license levels.

The pot-gear transition itself illustrates how one fix can seed the next uncertainty.

While the surface lines disappeared, the heavier traps now sit on the same concentrated grounds.

Any future change in crab or other bycatch species could again alter the incentive structure for the remaining vessels.

When Argentina limited the number of vessels allowed near South Georgia, it rewarded predictable daily presence in the same feeding zones rather than movement across wider grounds.

That predictability gave orcas a reliable new foraging tactic and gave fishers a reason to adopt gear that increased calf mortality.

Anyone writing a new limited-entry rule today can test whether the permitted activity will become spatially fixed.

They can also test whether the target species or its predators can adapt faster than the managers can adjust the rules.

The question is whether the next license design will still assume that concentrating effort leaves the rest of the ecosystem unchanged.
