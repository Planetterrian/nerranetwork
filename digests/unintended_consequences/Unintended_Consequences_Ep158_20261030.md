> **After banning four breeds in 1991, UK dog bite hospital admissions rose 81 percent by 2017.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 2018 the House of Commons Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee examined hospital records and found admissions for dog bites had climbed from 4,110 in 2005 to 7,461 in 2017. The 1991 Dangerous Dogs Act had banned four types outright and placed strict controls on any others thought to be fighting dogs. The measure was written to remove dangerous animals from British streets; instead the stock of powerful dogs simply changed shape.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The Act was drafted in the spring of 1991 after a cluster of serious attacks, several involving American pit bull terriers, prompted urgent calls for action in Parliament. Ministers at the time faced public pressure to act quickly on what appeared to be a new and identifiable threat. They chose breed-specific prohibitions because the dogs involved in the worst incidents shared visible physical traits and because similar laws already existed in parts of the United States. The four types named—pit bull terrier type, Japanese Tosa, Dogo Argentino and Fila Brasileiro—were seen as the core of the problem. An exemption register was added so that existing, well-managed dogs could stay with their owners under muzzling, insurance and neutering rules. At the moment of passage the policy looked like a targeted, evidence-based response to a narrow set of animals rather than a broad attack on all large dogs. The arithmetic seemed straightforward: remove the animals whose appearance matched the recent fatalities, and the risk would fall in proportion. Officials could point to the physical markers—broad skulls, muscular frames, short coats—that distinguished the four listed types from other large breeds. Because those markers were observable on the street, enforcement could begin without waiting for behavioural records that many owners did not keep. The exemption scheme further softened the measure by allowing owners who could demonstrate responsible management to retain their dogs under a formal register, thereby avoiding the charge that every animal of a given appearance would be seized regardless of history.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Royal Assent came in August 1991. Police forces began seizing dogs that matched the banned types, and courts applied a “deeming” process that placed the burden on owners to prove their animal was not of a prohibited type. Early enforcement produced visible results: many pit bull type dogs were removed from the streets of London and other cities. Government statements at the time described the register as a practical compromise that balanced public safety with the rights of responsible owners. Within a few years, however, veterinary and legal observers began noting that the legal definition of “type” was difficult to apply consistently in court. The deeming clause required owners to produce expert testimony on skull measurements, ear set, and tail carriage—details that could vary between individual dogs of the same lineage. Courts therefore spent time weighing photographs and veterinary reports rather than examining how the animal was housed or exercised. Proponents pointed to the declining number of registered pit bull types as proof that the visible threat had been curtailed; critics noted that the same physical traits could appear in crosses that fell outside the statutory definitions.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The RSPCA later reviewed thirty fatal dog attacks that occurred after the Act and found twenty-one involved dogs that were never on the prohibited list. Hospital data collected by the 2018 EFRA Committee showed the upward trend in bite injuries continued without interruption. Owners who wanted a dog for status or protection simply turned to breeds that remained legal, most often Staffordshire bull terrier crosses and other mastiff-type mixes. These dogs were not subject to the muzzling or registration rules, yet many possessed comparable strength and bite force. Because the law focused on appearance rather than behaviour or owner history, enforcement resources stayed tied up in court cases about ear shape and skull measurements instead of assessing how dogs were actually kept. A 1996 prospective study in the journal Injury found no measurable drop in accident-and-emergency attendances for dog bites after the Act took effect. Over time the visible population of “dangerous” dogs shifted, but the underlying pattern—powerful animals in the hands of some irresponsible owners—remained intact. The Veterinary Record analysis of cases from 1992 to 2019 confirmed that prohibited types continued to appear on the Index of Exempted Dogs, showing the ban had not eliminated the animals, only re-categorised them. One objection sometimes raised is that the rise in admissions might reflect better reporting rather than more incidents; yet the EFRA Committee examined the same dataset across multiple years and found the increase persisted even after adjustments for population growth and changes in hospital coding. Another objection is that the banned breeds were simply replaced by animals of similar temperament; the data instead show that the replacement breeds were chosen precisely because they escaped the legal definition, not because they lacked physical capacity. The causal chain therefore runs from a rule keyed to appearance, through owners selecting unregulated but equally capable substitutes, to a sustained or rising rate of injury that the original prohibitions could not address.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
The 2018 EFRA report concluded there was no evidence that breed-specific legislation had reduced bite rates and recommended a move toward deed-not-breed approaches focused on owner behaviour. Successive governments have kept the 1991 framework in place while adding limited amendments, such as extending controls to private property in 2014. Calls for full repeal continue from veterinary bodies and welfare organisations, yet no replacement system has been enacted. The Index of Exempted Dogs still operates, and police time continues to be spent on identification disputes rather than preventive work with owners. The result is a statute that is both retained and widely regarded as ineffective by the very committees that have studied its outcomes. Later reviews have therefore inherited the same enforcement machinery while acknowledging that the machinery does not track the risk factors that actually produce serious bites.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When the 1991 Act tied enforcement to visible breed rather than to how an individual dog was acquired and kept, it rewarded owners who simply chose unregulated but equally capable animals. When the exemption scheme required owners to prove a negative in court, it concentrated legal effort on appearance disputes instead of risk assessment. When later reviews documented rising bite numbers yet left the original structure untouched, the policy became self-perpetuating. What single behaviour or ownership factor would you measure today if the goal were to reduce serious incidents rather than to name particular breeds?
