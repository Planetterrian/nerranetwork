In 1881, New Zealand's Rabbit Newsants Act gave ferrets official protection as natural
enemies of the rabbit and empowered inspectors to enforce control on landowners.
Pastoral runs in the southern South Island had already seen wool clip and laming rates
fall sharply from rabbit damage by the mid-1870s.
The act was designed to cut rabbit numbers on grazing land.
Pastoralist Cuthbert Cowan brought the first documented private shipment of ferrets to
his Southland property in 1876, after rabbits had begun to reduce sheep numbers on his runs.
Rabbits had arrived with whalers and early settlers from the 1820s through the 1850s,
and multiplied once pastoral farming expanded.
By the early 1870s, burrowing and pasture loss were cutting wool production and land values
across Otago and Southland. Government rental income from affected runs dropped from
2,288 pounds to 619 pounds by 1881. Superintending rabbit inspector Benjamin P. Bailey and local
runholders turned to ferrets as a cheaper alternative to repeated poisoning and trapping.
The axe shielded mustalids from harm and directed inspectors to promote their use.
The choice rested on ferrets killing rabbits efficiently in Britain,

Cowan had already shown on his own land that ferrets could be bred locally and released

Each ferret could in principle remove dozens of rabbits a season and spare the recurring
