> **Europe and the US mandated biofuels to cut transport emissions, then watched palm-oil plantations clear rainforests faster than before.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 2007, Indonesian officials approved new palm-oil concessions across Central Kalimantan that would eventually cover more than 2 million hectares. European refineries had already signed long-term supply contracts for the crude palm oil those plantations would produce, because EU rules counted the resulting biodiesel as a low-carbon fuel. Within five years, satellite records showed that roughly one-third of the new plantings sat on land that had been primary forest only months earlier.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
In the mid-2000s, both the European Commission and the United States Congress faced rising oil prices and mounting evidence that transport-sector carbon dioxide was still climbing. Officials looked for a drop-in substitute that could use existing engines and pipelines. Biofuels made from crops appeared to offer a closed carbon cycle: plants absorbed CO2 while growing, then released it when burned. The 2005 U.S. Energy Policy Act and the 2009 EU Renewable Energy Directive therefore set binding volume targets—36 billion gallons of renewable fuel in the United States by 2022 and 10 percent renewable energy in transport across the EU by 2020. At the time, lifecycle models used by both governments treated land-use change as a minor or zero factor, so the arithmetic showed large net savings.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The U.S. Renewable Fuel Standard required refiners to blend increasing shares of corn ethanol and advanced biofuels, backed by tax credits of 51 cents per gallon. In Europe, the Fuel Quality Directive and national blending mandates created similar demand for fatty-acid methyl esters derived from vegetable oils. By 2010, U.S. corn acreage had risen by roughly 10 million acres compared with 2005 levels, while Indonesia’s palm-oil output doubled between 2005 and 2012. Trade data from that period show that more than half of Europe’s palm-oil imports were ultimately routed to biodiesel plants in Rotterdam and Antwerp. Proponents cited falling tailpipe emissions in regulatory filings; early government reports recorded compliance without yet measuring distant land conversion.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Lifecycle studies published after 2008, beginning with Searchinger’s Science paper and followed by multiple EU-commissioned reviews, recalculated emissions once peat-soil drainage and forest clearing were included. On tropical peatlands, the release of stored carbon turned a nominal 30–50 percent saving into a net increase that could exceed 100 percent relative to gasoline over a 30-year horizon. In Indonesia and Malaysia, the area of industrial palm-oil concessions expanded by an estimated 6 million hectares between 2000 and 2010; roughly 20 percent of that expansion occurred on previously forested land according to satellite analyses. The resulting smoke from peat fires in 2015 alone produced more CO2 in a single season than Germany’s annual industrial emissions. Corn ethanol expansion in the Midwest, meanwhile, displaced soybean acreage, which in turn encouraged additional soy planting in Brazil’s cerrado and Amazon margins. These indirect effects were not captured in the original regulatory models, so the very policies meant to reduce atmospheric carbon added measurable increments instead.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
By 2015 the EU had introduced an indirect-land-use-change factor into its accounting rules and capped the contribution of food-based biofuels at 7 percent of transport energy. The United States Environmental Protection Agency adjusted its greenhouse-gas thresholds for new biofuel pathways, effectively slowing approvals for additional corn ethanol capacity. Palm-oil biodiesel lost its “advanced” status in some member states, and several large European buyers shifted contracts toward certified sustainable sources or waste oils. Even so, existing plantations continued to operate, and enforcement of zero-deforestation commitments remained uneven. Current EU targets now emphasize waste-based and advanced fuels, yet legacy demand from earlier mandates still shapes global vegetable-oil markets.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Incentive structures that reward a single measured attribute—tailpipe carbon intensity—while leaving other variables unpriced will reliably shift pressure onto those variables. Complex ecological and economic systems respond through channels that are visible only after the policy is already locked in. When designing future climate or energy rules, therefore, it is worth asking which unmeasured margins the new incentive will enlarge and whether those margins can be monitored before the next planting season begins.
