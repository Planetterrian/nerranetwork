Every story we cover starts with someone who had a reasonable plan.

In the spring of nineteen sixty-eight, a Boston gynecologist examined a cluster of young women, some still in their teens. The patients arrived with a diagnosis of clear-cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina, a form of cancer that develops in the tissue lining the vaginal canal.

That particular cancer was so uncommon in their age group that the hospital’s pathology department had seen only one prior case in thirty years. Each of the patients’ mothers had taken the same medication during pregnancy. The medication was diethylstilbestrol, or DES. The drug had been introduced with the explicit goal of supporting pregnancies that might otherwise end in miscarriage.

Instead, it produced a cancer that would not appear until the exposed daughters reached adolescence or early adulthood.

The compound was first synthesized in nineteen thirty-eight by British biochemist Sir Charles Dodds and his team at the Courtauld Institute. Dodds was seeking an inexpensive, orally active estrogen that could treat menopausal symptoms without relying on scarce natural extracts from animal ovaries. Within a few years, American obstetricians began testing the same molecule for a different purpose.

They wanted to address recurrent miscarriage and preterm labor. At the time, miscarriage was understood largely as a failure of hormonal support. Estrogen levels were observed to drop before many spontaneous abortions.

Researchers at Harvard and the University of Chicago therefore reasoned that administering a potent synthetic estrogen might restore the hormonal environment required to maintain pregnancy. Early case series published in the American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology appeared to show reduced rates of loss.

The logic seemed sound given the limited tools available to physicians in the nineteen forties.

By nineteen forty-seven the F D A had already approved DES for menopausal symptoms. Its use in pregnancy spread rapidly through off-label prescribing. Pharmaceutical companies marketed it under more than two hundred brand names. By the mid-nineteen fifties an estimated five to ten million women worldwide had received the drug during gestation.

That range, five million on the lower end and ten million on the higher end, reflects the rough counts compiled later from prescription records and marketing data across multiple countries. Professional journals carried advertisements claiming it produced stronger, healthier babies.

A small number of researchers, including British pharmacologist Sir Robert Robinson, had noted in the early nineteen forties that high-dose estrogens could alter genital-tract development in animal embryos. Yet these findings received little clinical attention. Most practitioners viewed DES as a low-risk intervention that addressed a common and distressing problem.

One might ask why the animal data did not slow adoption, but at the time the prevailing view held that findings in non-human embryos did not necessarily translate to human pregnancy outcomes under medical supervision.

In nineteen seventy-one, physicians Arthur Herbst and Robert Scully published a case-control study in the New England Journal of Medicine. The study linked maternal DES use to the rare vaginal cancers appearing in their daughters. The median age at diagnosis was nineteen. Subsequent research established that DES crossed the placenta and interfered with the developing Müllerian ducts.

This interference produced permanent structural changes that later became malignant. The same exposure also increased risks of infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and breast cancer in the daughters. Later studies documented elevated rates of hypospadias and other genital anomalies in sons.

Because the drug had been given to healthy pregnant women rather than to patients already ill, the population exposed in utero was unusually large. The latency period of roughly twenty years meant that the connection between prescription and disease was invisible to the original prescribers and to the women who took the medication.

No short-term clinical trial could have detected an outcome that manifested only after the children reached adulthood.

On twenty-two November nineteen seventy-one the F D A withdrew approval of DES for pregnancy use. Cancer registries and specialized clinics were established to follow exposed individuals. Several large lawsuits reached settlement in the nineteen eighties. The drug remained available for other indications for a time, but its obstetric use ended abruptly.

Long-term follow-up has continued into the third generation. Some grandchildren of exposed women show subtle reproductive-tract changes, although cancer risk in that cohort remains under study. Modern endocrine-disruptor research now routinely examines multi-generational effects that were not considered in mid-century drug evaluation.

The DES story illustrates that interventions acting on developing physiology can produce effects whose full scope appears only after an entire generation has passed. It also shows that absence of observed harm in short-term studies does not equal proof of long-term safety when the endpoint lies decades ahead. Finally, the episode underscores the value of retaining detailed exposure records.

Without the maternal medication histories collected by Herbst’s team, the causal link would have remained invisible. When new medical or policy tools are introduced at scale, what mechanisms exist to detect consequences that surface only in the next generation?
