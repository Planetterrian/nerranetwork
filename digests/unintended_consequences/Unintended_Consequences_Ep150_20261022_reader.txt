India issued eighty million free gas connections, yet ninety percent of users still burned firewood.

This is Unintended Consequences, episode 150.

A health promise made in Ballia, a rollout built for speed, the refill math that never covered a year of cooking, an audit of what left the kitchen, and a metric that still cannot see the fire.

On May first, twenty sixteen, Prime Minister Narendra Modi stood in Ballia, in Uttar Pradesh, and launched the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana.

The promise was free liquefied petroleum gas connections, LPG, for fifty million poor households.

Women would no longer have to cook over wood and dung.

The problem the scheme was built to answer was not abstract.

Indoor air pollution from solid fuels was estimated to cause hundreds of thousands of premature deaths each year.

Women and children took the hardest hit, because the smoke lived in the room where the meal was made.

Modi's government, like earlier administrations, treated LPG as the practical clean fuel already sitting inside most districts.

Cylinders were already moving to rural dealers.

Distribution networks already reached most blocks.

What looked missing was the first payment, not a new market and not a new forest policy.

So the scheme aimed at that first barrier.

The connection, the regulator, and the first cylinder.

Cover up to one thousand six hundred rupees.

Register the connection in an adult woman's name.

Officials hoped that ownership would become daily use, and that daily use would show up as less smoke in the house.

At the time, that chain looked straightforward, and it looked measurable.

Earlier subsidy schemes had already taught a hard lesson about cash.

Households rarely bought the opening hardware when money was thin at the start of a season.

Paying for that first step was meant to clear the moment when a family was most likely to walk away.

Putting the asset in the woman's name was meant to give her leverage inside the household budget when the next refill decision arrived.

She would hold the papers.

The next cylinder would, in theory, be hers to claim.

That framing also fit how a government can run a large program.

Numeric targets could be tracked through oil-marketing-company records.

Nobody had to stand in a kitchen and count whether the fire that night was gas or wood.

The connection itself was treated as the decisive intervention.

Affordability of the first step stood in for a deeper change in fuel prices or in who could still walk into a forest and come home with fuel that carried no bill.

The people designing this were working a real death toll with a fuel the country already knew how to move.

The bet was that once the hardware crossed the threshold, the rest of the cooking year would follow.

Oil-marketing companies built the rollout around Ujjwala melas.

Applications moved through the Socio-Economic and Caste Census list.

The target did not stay at fifty million.

It rose to eighty million connections.

Press releases from the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas recorded the pace in public.

Twenty million connections in twenty sixteen to seventeen.

Fifteen point six million in twenty seventeen to eighteen.

Thirty-six point two million in twenty eighteen to nineteen.

By June twenty nineteen the cumulative total stood at seventy-two point three million.

Early coverage celebrated the speed.

The government described the program as a direct step toward smoke-free villages.

The melas made that claim visible.

Local officials and company representatives could hand over the connection documents, the stove, and the first cylinder in a single afternoon.

A photograph could hold the whole achievement.

A report could count it the same day.

The administrative load per household stayed low enough for the oil companies to meet monthly quotas.

What the file needed was the woman's name and an address check.

That same speed shaped what the file did not deeply test.

Whether the household already held an LPG connection somewhere else rested largely on Aadhaar matching.

An audit would later flag that step as incomplete.

For a program judged on connections delivered, incomplete matching was a cost of moving fast, not yet a crack in the story.

Proponents could point at villages receiving hardware that poor households had not been buying on their own.

Skeptics, even then, would have had to ask a question the photographs could not answer.

What happens on the afternoon after the mela, when the first cylinder is empty and the next one has a price?

The Comptroller and Auditor General later looked at what happened after the connection arrived.

Among beneficiaries who had held a connection for more than a year, average annual refills stood at three point six six cylinders as of March thirty-first, twenty eighteen.

By December thirty-first, twenty eighteen, that average had fallen to three point two one.

A refill lasts roughly a month for a typical household.

Three cylinders therefore covered only about three months of cooking.

The other nine months had to be fueled somehow.

A study of four thousand nine hundred ninety-four households across five hundred villages in the Central Indian Highlands Landscape, published in twenty twenty-one in Energy Research and Social Science, found LPG-owning families still gathering and burning firewood.

Researchers called the pattern fuel stacking.

Households kept the free connection.

Purchased LPG became a supplement, not a replacement.

Three pressures held the older fuel in daily rotation.

Refill costs.

Irregular cash income.

And wood nearby that carried a price of zero.

When a household's cash arrives in one or two harvest sales, several hundred rupees for a cylinder in a lean month sits next to school fees and medical costs.

Those bills do not wait for the next crop.

Wood that can be gathered in an afternoon has no cash price.

On most days, the wood wins.

The survey also showed that households with more forest within easy walking distance were more likely to keep collecting every day, even after the LPG connection arrived.

The marginal cost of another trip remained zero.

Distance to trees mattered as much as the paper in the woman's name.

That is a second-order effect the connection count could not see.

The scheme had prioritized poor and forest-fringe households because the smoke burden was heaviest there.

Those were also the places where free wood was closest, and where cash was most seasonal.

The groups named first for the hardware were the groups for whom daily replacement was hardest.

There was a partial gain, and it should be said plainly.

Longer ownership correlated with fewer collection hours.

For some families, total firewood volume did fall.

The drop was gradual.

It never reached full displacement for the great majority.

A connection could shave hours off a wood trip and still leave a wood fire as the ordinary way to cook.

Health gains that depend on the fire going out do not arrive on the same schedule as a handover.

The audit found another break in the chain.

One hundred ninety-eight thousand beneficiaries averaged more than twelve cylinders a year.

That volume is improbable for a domestic kitchen.

It suggested some cylinders were being diverted to commercial kitchens.

Multiple daily refills at single-bottle connections pointed the same way.

Some subsidized fuel left the domestic system entirely.

That is a third-order effect.

Cylinders diverted to commercial use are cylinders no longer available for the households the scheme named.

Any simple count of health gains from connections delivered becomes harder to read, because some of the subsidized fuel never stayed in the room where the smoke was supposed to fall.

None of this required bad faith at the launch in Ballia.

It required a price, a season, and a forest that the connection record did not contain.

The audit prompted tighter checks on refill patterns.

Emphasis shifted, at least in the language of review, toward sustained use rather than connection counts alone.

Later government updates still reported cumulative connections past the original eighty-million target.

Independent surveys kept finding the same stacking in forest-adjacent districts.

No large-scale reversal took the hardware back.

State programs began experimenting instead.

Targeted subsidies for additional cylinders.

Community-level alternatives to firewood.

The core design stayed as it was.

Pay once for access.

Then charge a market price for the fuel.

Follow-up surveys in the same Central Indian Highlands Landscape kept recording the same split.

The probability of daily LPG use stayed lowest among the poorest households and the most forest-adjacent households.

Those were exactly the groups the scheme had put first in line for the initial connection.

The fix did not undo the metric that had made the program look finished.

Tighter checks can catch a suspicious refill pattern.

They do not, by themselves, put cash in a lean month or put a price on an afternoon in the forest.

Experiments with extra cylinder subsidies and community firewood alternatives were an admission that the second cylinder was the real test.

They were also still small next to a national design that treated the first handover as the intervention.

Where things stood after the audit was not a cancelled scheme.

It was a scheme still adding names, beside surveys that still found wood in daily use, in the places the names had been meant to protect.

A connection is hardware.

A kitchen without wood smoke is a choice that has to win every afternoon against a fuel that costs time and nothing else.

When success is the number of connections transferred, oil-marketing-company records can close the file.

Months of cooking without firewood never enter that file, so the cheaper or free alternative stays in the pot.

Registering ownership in a woman's name was meant to move the next refill decision.

The refill cost still landed on a budget that rose and fell with agricultural seasons.

The connection became an occasional convenience.

When diversion controls are weak, some of the subsidized fuel leaves the domestic system, and the health arithmetic attached to the original list gets harder to trust.

The same split is live in any clean-fuel effort that scores itself on kits delivered.

Induction sets counted at the warehouse.

Pellet stoves logged as distributed while the next bag of fuel waits on a harvest.

Household electric connections celebrated before anyone asks whether the monthly bill can survive a lean season.
