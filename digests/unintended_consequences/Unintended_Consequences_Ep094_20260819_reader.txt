California tried to stop couches from burning — and quietly filled a continent's bloodstreams with flame retardants.

In a Sacramento testing room in the mid-nineteen seventies, a technician held a small open flame to a cube of polyurethane foam and watched the clock.

Twelve seconds.

If the foam did not keep burning, it passed, and a couch built with that foam could be sold in California.

The test was meant to stop a dropped match or a smoldering cushion from turning a living room into a funeral.

It instead sent a generation of chemical flame retardants into household dust, into wildlife, and into the blood of children who had never seen the inside of a burn unit.

This is how a decent fire rule became a continent's chemistry.

The rule had a bureaucratic name, Technical Bulletin 117, or TB 117.

Its origin was very human.

TB 117 was written by the California Bureau of Home Furnishings, part of the state's Department of Consumer Affairs.

It was championed by fire-safety officials who had spent the previous decade watching a new kind of furniture kill people.

Polyurethane foam had swept the industry in the nineteen sixties and early nineteen seventies because it was cheap, light, and comfortable.

It was also, once ignited, closer to solid gasoline than to the cotton and horsehair it replaced.

The foam melted, pooled, and released heat so fast that a chair could drive a room toward flashover in minutes.

Smoking rates were still high.

Cigarettes left on sofas were among the leading causes of fatal residential fires.

In nineteen seventy-three, the federal report America Burning, issued by the National Commission on Fire Prevention and Control, described a country with one of the worst fire-death records in the industrialized world.

The United States was losing many thousands of people a year.

Upholstered furniture accounted for a share of those deaths out of all proportion to how often it ignited, because when that furniture went, it went badly.

Gordon Damant, the bureau's longtime flammability chief, and the colleagues around him were not theorizing about a distant risk.

Damant and those colleagues were looking at coroners' reports and burned living rooms, and at children who had been asleep on the couch.

A standard that forced the filling material to resist a small open flame looked, with the information on the desk in nineteen seventy-five, like a direct and decent intervention.

The chemistry that would be used to meet TB 117 was not yet a public controversy.

The science of what those chemicals would do once they left the foam was still thin.

California made TB 117 mandatory in nineteen seventy-five.

Foam had to endure that twelve-second open-flame exposure, along with a smoldering-cigarette test on the filling.

The cheapest reliable way to pass was not a better foam.

It was to load the foam, while it was still being mixed, with additive flame retardants.

The first wave was commercial mixtures of polybrominated diphenyl ethers, or PBDEs, especially a formulation called pentaBDE.

Later came a rotating cast of chlorinated and organophosphate substitutes.

Researchers have estimated that a single full-size sofa could carry on the order of a pound of these chemicals.

A pound of additive, sitting in the cushions of one couch.

No factory was going to run a separate foam line for one state.

California was already one of the largest furniture markets on earth, and national retailers wanted one stock-keeping unit, one couch that could ship anywhere.

Factories from North Carolina to the Pacific Rim treated California's bulletin as the spec sheet for the continent.

By the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties, the foam in an Ohio apartment or a Vancouver starter sofa was, more often than not, California foam.

Officials could point to a real decline in fire deaths in the years that followed, and those officials did.

Proponents described the standard as a quiet engineering fix, the sort of thing a competent state does after it has counted the bodies.

There was no loud opposition at the start.

The closest early warning sat in a different closet.

In nineteen seventy-seven, after the chemist Arlene Blum and the biochemist Bruce Ames showed that chlorinated Tris — then used in children's sleepwear — was mutagenic, that chemical was pulled from pajamas.

The lesson did not travel to the living room.

For decades, skeptics of TB 117 were scarce, and the ones who existed were mostly arguing about test method, not about bloodstreams.

The standard simply became furniture.

The causal chain was physical before it was political.

The flame retardants were mixed into the foam, not chemically locked to it.

Those additives were semi-volatile.

As cushions flexed and crumbled, the compounds migrated into air and, especially, into household dust.

The fine gray film on baseboards, on remote controls, on the hands of toddlers who lived on the floor.

Dust is how a couch becomes a diet.

Young children ingest far more of that dust, relative to their body weight, than adults do, because those children crawl, they mouth their fingers, they live closer to the carpet.

Biomonitoring through the two thousands found Americans carrying PBDE body burdens many times higher than people in Europe, where furniture chemistry had taken a different path.

California sat at the high end of that American range.

A two thousand eight paper by Ami Zota and colleagues in Environmental Science and Technology put the irony right in the question.

Were California's elevated levels in house dust and in blood the fallout of its own furniture fire rules?

Nursing mothers passed the compounds along in breast milk.

The same chemical family turned up in harbor seals in San Francisco Bay and in killer whales along the Pacific coast — animals that had never sat down on a sofa.

PBDEs are structurally cousins to polychlorinated biphenyls, or PCBs, and to thyroid hormones, which is why toxicologists worried once the measurements came in.

These compounds persist.

Some of them linger in people for years.

Epidemiological studies have associated higher prenatal and childhood PBDE exposures with attention problems and modest differences in IQ scores.

Isolating one chemical's effect in a real household is difficult, and the honest version of that literature is association, not a courtroom verdict.

Then the chemistry changed without the exposure story changing.

Under pressure from negotiations with the Environmental Protection Agency, or EPA, manufacturers phased out pentaBDE and octaBDE in the mid-two thousands.

The foam still had to pass TB 117.

The replacements included a mixture called Firemaster 550 and, in a twist that still startles people who remember the pajama fight, chlorinated Tris.

That compound, also called TDCPP, was the one withdrawn from children's sleepwear a generation earlier.

A two thousand twelve study of American couches by Heather Stapleton and colleagues found flame-retardant chemicals in the great majority of samples.

In furniture made after the pentaBDE phase-out, Tris was among the most common.

The standard had not specified a molecule.

It had specified a twelve-second performance.

Industry met the number with whatever additive was legal, cheap, and effective in a lab dish of foam.

Meanwhile the original promise — that the treated foam would buy time in a real fire — came under serious challenge.

Vytenis Babrauskas, a fire scientist formerly of the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and others argued that the bulletin's small flame on bare foam was a poor stand-in for a living room.

In a real chair the fabric ignites first.

Once the cover is involved, treated foam still burns, still drips, still drives heat.

Full-scale tests did not, in this reading, show a reliable gift of extra escape time.

The chemical industry and some fire-safety traditionalists disputed that conclusion, and those critics had a powerful background fact.

United States fire deaths fell dramatically from the nineteen seventies to the two thousands.

Those deaths did fall.

They also fell in a world that had filled with smoke alarms, stubbed out a large share of its cigarettes, tightened electrical and heating codes, and sped up emergency response.

How much of the saving belonged to the foam is still honestly contested.

What was less contested in firehouses was the smoke.

Halogenated foam does not go quietly.

Burning that foam can produce a thicker, more corrosive plume — dioxins, furans, hydrogen cyanide, the black stew that modern furniture makes when it burns.

Firefighters already faced elevated cancer rates for many reasons.

By the two thousands, many in the profession had come to see the living-room fire as a toxic event in a way their predecessors' wood-and-cotton fires had not been.

The International Association of Fire Fighters eventually supported getting the chemicals out.

The people TB 117 had been written to protect from flames were now asking to be protected from the remedy.

The geography of all this was almost accidental.

A parent in Ohio, or here in British Columbia, never voted on TB 117.

That parent bought a couch.

Because California's market was large enough to set the mold, the bulletin became a continental materials policy without ever being named as one.

Second-order effects stacked on third.

Dust became a pathway.

Breast milk became a pathway.

The secondhand sofa market became a long tail of old foam that will sit in student apartments and basement suites for another decade.

Wildlife at the top of Pacific food webs became an archive of living-room chemistry.

And because the first generation of chemicals was withdrawn for their persistence, a second generation occupied the same couches, the same dust, the same blood tests.

A substitution that solved a regulatory problem without solving the exposure.

The turn came in public, and late.

In May of two thousand twelve, the Chicago Tribune published Playing with Fire, a series by Patricia Callahan, Sam Roe, and Michael Hawthorne.

The series pulled together the exposure science, the doubts about the test, and the political hardball that had kept the status quo in place.

That included a chemical-industry group, Citizens for Fire Safety, that presented itself as a grassroots firefighters' voice.

That same spring and summer, scientists and advocates who had been working the issue for years, among them Arlene Blum's Green Science Policy Institute in Berkeley, had a hearing they had not had in nineteen seventy-five.

In June of two thousand twelve, Governor Jerry Brown directed the state to revise the bulletin.

The rewrite, TB 117-2013, took effect in two thousand fourteen.

It shifted the test from an open flame on bare foam to a smoldering-cigarette test on the cover fabric.

That change could be met with smolder-resistant textiles and barrier layers and did not require dosing the foam at all.

It was not a ban.

It was a door left open.

Some manufacturers walked through that door.

Others, out of habit or supply-chain inertia, kept using retardants.

Senator Mark Leno's Senate Bill 1019, also in two thousand fourteen, at least made those manufacturers say so on a label.

The cleaner break came later.

Assembly Bill 2998, signed by Brown in two thousand eighteen and effective in two thousand twenty, barred a broad class of flame retardants from new upholstered furniture, mattresses, and children's products sold in California.

Other states have since written related limits.

New sofas can be, and often are, built without the old additives.

That is the repair.

The meta-irony is the lag.

A standard that made the chemicals optional did not make them disappear, and a ban that applies to new products does not empty the apartments already furnished.

PBDE levels in people have declined in the biomonitoring record since pentaBDE left the market, which is genuine good news.

Organophosphate flame retardants rose as the PBDEs left.

The stock of TB 117 couches is still out there, slowly aging into dust.

A standard will be optimized for the test, not for the world.

If you measure only one number, you will get that number — and whatever chemistry, whatever shortcut, whatever side effect that number requires, wherever those side effects later go.

Materials that are not locked in place will move.

An additive in a product people live against becomes an exposure pathway, and children, who live on the floor, will find it first.

Large-market rules travel farther than their authors' jurisdiction.

Write them as if they will govern more bodies than the statute names.

The deeper habit to watch is older than any one product.

Acute, visible, countable deaths crowd the desk, and they should.

The officials of nineteen seventy-five were not reckless.

Those officials were looking at fire.

We are still writing tests like that.

A food wrapper that must resist grease.

A phone or a laptop that must not ignite on a lab bench.

Building insulation that must hit a fire rating.

Each of those rules is aimed at a real harm.

Each one can be met with an additive that was never asked to stay put, or with a substitute that was never asked what it does in dust, in milk, in the animals downstream.

The question is not whether to write the standard.

The question is what you would watch for after the number is met.

Who lives closest to the product.

Whether the chemical is locked or free to migrate.

Whether one big market is about to set the recipe for everyone else.

And whether, in the same breath as the hazard we can see, we are willing to ask what slower hazard we might be installing in the dust.
