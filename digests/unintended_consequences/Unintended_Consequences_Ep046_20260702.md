> **The 1978 Airline Deregulation Act promised cheaper flights for everyone — and produced an industry where four carriers now control roughly 80 percent of the market.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
On October 24, 1978, President Jimmy Carter signed the Airline Deregulation Act in the White House Rose Garden, surrounded by economists who had spent years arguing that regulated fares kept ordinary Americans on the ground. The law removed the Civil Aeronautics Board’s power to set prices and approve routes, with the explicit goal of letting competition drive costs down. Within two decades the average fare had fallen sharply, yet dozens of smaller cities had lost all scheduled service and the workforce that once flew those planes saw real wages decline for the first time since the jet age began.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The regulated system that existed from 1938 to 1978 had produced stable but expensive service. The Civil Aeronautics Board treated airlines like public utilities, approving fares that guaranteed carriers a reasonable return while preventing price wars that might bankrupt any of them. By the mid-1970s, however, inflation had pushed coach fares on many routes above what middle-income families could easily pay. Alfred E. Kahn, an economist appointed to lead the CAB in 1977, and Senator Edward Kennedy, who held hearings the same year, believed that removing government price controls would allow new entrants to compete on cost and route structure. Their reasoning rested on straightforward microeconomics: if carriers could charge whatever the market would bear and fly whatever cities proved profitable, fares would fall and capacity would rise. At the time, no one had seen a fully deregulated national passenger network in the jet era, so the experiment felt like a low-risk correction of an obvious inefficiency.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
The Airline Deregulation Act phased out the CAB’s authority over domestic fares and routes by 1983 and dissolved the agency itself in 1985. Early results appeared encouraging. Between 1978 and 1985, real average fares dropped roughly 20 percent while passenger enplanements rose from 240 million to more than 340 million. Low-cost carriers such as People Express and New York Air entered the market with dramatically lower prices on high-density corridors. Proponents pointed to these numbers as proof that the policy was working as intended. A few skeptics, including some airline executives and labor leaders, warned that smaller communities would lose service once carriers were free to abandon unprofitable routes, but those concerns received limited attention during the first years of expansion.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The same freedom that lowered fares on busy routes also rewarded carriers that could spread fixed costs across larger networks. Airlines quickly adopted the hub-and-spoke model, concentrating flights at a handful of major airports where connecting traffic justified frequent service. This structure produced powerful economies of scale that favored the largest carriers. Over the next three decades more than 100 airlines entered and exited the industry through bankruptcy or merger. By the late 2010s, American, Delta, United, and Southwest accounted for approximately 80 percent of domestic passengers. Because the new competitive environment placed relentless pressure on costs, carriers negotiated wage concessions and shifted work to regional partners that paid pilots and flight attendants substantially less than legacy carriers had under regulation. At the same time, service to communities generating fewer than 100 passengers a day largely disappeared once federal subsidies were not in place; Congress later created the Essential Air Service program to restore some of those flights, but the program now supports only a fraction of the routes that existed in 1978. The combination of consolidation and hub concentration also produced the familiar experience of long layovers, cramped cabins, and frequent schedule changes that became the norm for most travelers.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Lawmakers responded with targeted interventions rather than re-regulation. The Essential Air Service subsidy, created in 1978 and still funded today, preserves service to about 60 small airports at an annual cost of roughly $300 million. Antitrust authorities blocked some proposed mergers in the 1980s and 1990s but approved the largest combinations after 2008, including the United-Continental and American-US Airways deals. No serious legislative effort has emerged to restore broad fare or route regulation. The current industry structure reflects four decades of adaptation to the incentives created in 1978: lower prices on the routes that support high volume, thinner service everywhere else, and compensation levels that have not kept pace with productivity gains in other sectors of the economy.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When policymakers remove constraints on pricing and entry, the resulting market tends to reward scale and network effects more quickly than regulators anticipate. Incentive structures that appear neutral on paper often amplify existing differences in cost structure and geographic position. Before changing the rules that govern an entire industry, it is useful to model not only the first-round price effects but also the second-round consolidation that can follow once firms are free to choose which customers they will serve. The airline case continues to shape debates over whether similar deregulation in other network industries will produce comparable concentration.
