In the summer of nineteen twenty-eight, more than a thousand adult Large Coppers flew across the sedge and pools of Woodwalton Fen.

That flight looked like a restoration. The year before, Captain E.B. Purefoy had brought Dutch butterflies to a small National Trust reserve and planted their foodplant, great water dock, in advance.

The insects answered. Eggs and larvae appeared on the dock leaves that same season. Everyone involved took the count as confirmation that a lost butterfly could be returned to a fragment of English fen.

The colony did not hold on its own. Wardens kept breeding fresh adults and releasing them every season, while the farmland around the reserve kept draining the fen drier.

What began as a careful act of repair became a population that existed only because people kept putting it back.

The English race of the Large Copper, Lycaena dispar dispar, had disappeared by eighteen fifty-one.

The cause was not a mystery to the people who came after. Centuries of fen drainage for agriculture had taken the wetlands the butterfly needed.

By the early twentieth century only fragments of wetland remained in eastern England. The original race was gone. The landscape that had held it was mostly fields.

Purefoy was an experienced lepidopterist. He looked at those fragments and saw a second chance in a newly discovered Dutch race, Lycaena dispar batavus.

The Dutch insect was not the lost English one. It was close enough, in his judgment, to stand in for a butterfly England no longer had.

Woodwalton Fen, in Huntingdonshire, still held pockets of suitable vegetation. It was small. It was also protected, which mattered. A National Trust boundary meant the ground inside it would not be ploughed.

Purefoy and the reserve managers believed that preparing the site with the larval foodplant would let a self-sustaining colony establish itself inside that boundary.

The reasoning followed the best available knowledge. The butterfly’s extinction had been caused by habitat loss. Targeted habitat restoration, plus fresh stock, should reverse it.

That logic is worth sitting with. If the plant is missing, put the plant back. If the insect is missing, bring the insect back. If the ground is protected, the work has a place to stand.

The drainage that removed the original English population had begun in the seventeenth century. Large straight channels were cut. Wind-powered pumps were installed.

By the nineteenth century steam engines accelerated the lowering of water levels across hundreds of square miles. Peat soils became arable fields.

Purefoy was therefore working with a landscape whose hydrology had already been altered for more than two hundred years.

He could see that the remaining fragments were small. He also knew that the Dutch race had evolved in similar, if not identical, wetland conditions.

The decision to proceed rested on an assumption that still sounds reasonable. A carefully chosen reserve could be made sufficient if the missing plant was restored.

The reserve was the unit he could act on. The water under it was not a separate problem in the plan. It was treated as part of the ground the boundary already held.

In nineteen twenty-seven Purefoy released twenty-five males and thirteen females of the Dutch race at Woodwalton Fen.

The reserve had already been planted with great water dock. The planting set out hundreds of young Rumex hydrolapathum along the wetter margins of the reserve’s internal waterways.

Purefoy chose locations where the peat remained saturated for most of the year. Those spots matched the conditions he had observed in Dutch colonies.

The following year more than one thousand adults were counted on the wing. Learn Butterflies records that the early numbers encouraged everyone involved.

The count represented roughly twenty-seven times the number of founders. An apparent multiplication of that size suggested that the restored foodplant and the protected status of the fen were together sufficient.

Wardens recorded eggs and larvae on the dock leaves that season. The sense, on the evidence in front of them, was that the cycle had closed.

Reserve staff continued modest management. They kept the foodplant abundant inside the fen’s existing ditches and pools.

No one at the time expected that the colony would require repeated human intervention simply to persist.

The encouragement was not vanity. A small release had produced a large flight, and the life stages were visible on the plant that had been put there for them. A lepidopterist and a set of wardens could look at that summer and believe the hard part was done.

The land beyond Woodwalton Fen’s boundaries had been progressively drained for arable farming since the seventeenth century.

Each new pump and each new ditch lowered the regional water table. Moisture was pulled out of the reserve through the peat, whether or not anyone inside the fence intended it.

Great water dock needs consistently wet conditions. As the table fell, the plant became harder to maintain in sufficient density.

Duffey’s studies and the records kept at the reserve show that natural recruitment soon fell below replacement levels.

The butterflies were still there. The next generation, on its own, was not replacing the one before it.

Wardens responded by rearing butterflies in a greenhouse and releasing batches of adults each summer. The practice continued for decades.

Larvae were also caged on selected plants, to protect them from flooding or from drought.

These measures kept a visible population alive. The colony never became independent of the annual supplements.

By the late nineteen sixties a major flood further damaged the foodplant stands. That damage prompted another round of captive rearing after nineteen sixty-nine.

The surrounding agricultural drainage continued unchanged. The hydrological deficit grew rather than shrank.

The second-order effect was quiet and cumulative. Each year of a lowered water table reduced the area of suitable dock.

The third-order effect followed from that shrinkage. A smaller stand of dock increased the proportion of the population that had to be artificially supported.

Without that support the numbers would have collapsed far earlier than the early nineteen nineties, when the last supplemented adults were seen.

The peat itself compounded the difficulty. Once drained from the outside, the organic soil began to oxidise and subside.

Subsidence lowered the surface of the reserve relative to the surrounding fields. Water became even harder to hold at the level the dock required.

Every additional pump installed on neighbouring farms increased the gradient. Water was drawn through the peat more rapidly.

The wardens’ greenhouse rearing addressed the shortage of adult butterflies. The underlying shortage of suitable larval habitat continued to widen.

Caging larvae protected individuals from immediate weather extremes. Caging could not expand the total area of dock that stayed wet long enough for the caterpillars to complete their development.

The arithmetic of the intervention shifted year by year. More adults had to be reared to compensate for fewer naturally occurring plants.

Picture the work from the warden’s side of the ditch. A greenhouse, a batch of adults, a cage over a plant that might otherwise be drowned or dried. Each of those acts kept something alive that the fen, as it then stood, could not keep alive alone.

None of those acts raised the water table on the farms next door. The pumps kept running. The peat kept losing height. The wet margin kept narrowing.

A visitor in a good summer could still see Large Coppers on the wing and conclude that the nineteen twenty-seven release had worked. The books in the greenhouse told a different story. The flight was being restocked.

That gap between what the eye saw and what the rearing records required is the heart of the unintended consequence. Protection of the fragment produced a population. It did not produce a population that could replace itself.

The good intention had been precise. Restore the plant. Release the insect. Stay inside a reserve that would not be farmed. The water that the plant required was still being removed by a drainage regime older than the reserve and larger than its boundary.

After the final sightings in nineteen ninety-four and nineteen ninety-five, the formal reintroduction programme at Woodwalton Fen ended.

The site remained a National Nature Reserve. Managers accepted that a viable Large Copper population could not be sustained without restoring the wider fen hydrology.

That acceptance is a change in the unit of the problem. The reserve was no longer treated as a sufficient container. The water regime around it had to move with it.

The Great Fen project, now under way, aims to re-wet large areas of former farmland between Woodwalton and Holme Fen.

The tools are clay bunds and controlled water levels, meant to recreate the conditions the butterfly once required.

No new releases have occurred while the landscape-scale work continues.

The original nineteen twenty-seven effort therefore stands as a sixty-year demonstration that protecting a single fragment is not enough when the surrounding drainage regime remains unaltered.

The project’s current phase involves purchasing arable land and installing impermeable barriers, so that winter rainfall can be retained rather than pumped away.

Those measures address the same water-table gradient that had steadily reduced the area of great water dock inside the original reserve.

Until the restored blocks reach a sufficient size and wetness, any reintroduced butterflies would face the same constraints that forced the earlier programme into perpetual supplementation.

The irony is not that the first plan was careless. The irony is that the rescue became the evidence. Sixty years of greenhouse rearing showed, more clearly than a failed first summer would have, that the boundary was the wrong edge.

A collapse in nineteen twenty-nine might have been read as a bad stock or a bad season. A colony that could be seen, year after year, only because it was put back, pointed at the water leaving under the fence.

Purefoy and the Woodwalton wardens treated the reserve boundary as the decisive limit of their intervention. The result was a population that existed only inside annual budgets for captive rearing.

The principle that follows is narrower than a slogan about thinking bigger. Any plan that sets a fixed geographical perimeter must also secure the water regime across that perimeter, or the foodplant itself will disappear.

What was measured, season after season, was adults on the wing and larvae on selected plants. What was not secured was the wetness those plants needed once the regional table fell and the peat subsided.

Reward the visible insect, and you will get a greenhouse. Secure the gradient that feeds the plant, and the insect has a chance to stop being a budget line.

When the next reintroduction is considered, the test will be whether the Great Fen’s restored water table can support great water dock without constant human rescue.

The same question faces every recovery project that begins inside a remnant habitat ringed by intensified land use.

Environmental Intelligence has been tracking how restoration targets get drawn against working farmland, and the Woodwalton record is a long case of what those lines leave out.
