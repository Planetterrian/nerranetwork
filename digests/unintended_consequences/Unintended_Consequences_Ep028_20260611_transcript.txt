The first nationwide adoption occurred in Germany and Austria-Hungary on 30 April, 1916.
The Imperial German government faced an allied blockade that had cut off overseas coal and food,
leaving domestic reserves under intense pressure.
Every ton of fuel burned for lighting was therefore a ton unavailable for the war economy.
Specifically for the blast furnaces that produced steel,

Officials calculated that shifting clocks forward one hour would reduce evening demand for artificial

simple observation that most lighting loads occurred after sunset.
The same logic reappeared in the United States during the 1973 oil embargo.
President Nixon asked Congress for emergency authority to extend daylight saving time year-round,
arguing that an extra hour of evening daylight would cut national petroleum consumption by roughly
1%. Lawmakers facing gas lines and rationing plans passed the emergency daylight saving time
Energy Conservation Act in December 1973, with broad bipartisan support because the projected
saving, though modest, looked achievable without new infrastructure.
Germany's 1916 decree took effect in a single night, clocks advanced at 11 pm on 30 April,
and the change was presented as a patriotic duty that every household could perform by moving
the hands on its own timepieces. The United States followed in March 1918 under the Standard Time Act,
though the measure was repealed after the armistice, once the wartime pressure eased.
During the 1973 crisis, the new federal rule began on 6 January 1974, and was scheduled to last
until the end of the embargo, relying on the precedent that British gas lighting loads had fallen
measurably during both world wars when clocks were advanced. Skeptics in Congress, including

One might ask whether the extra evening light would simply offset any morning losses,
yet the objection centered on the fact that heating systems often run on different schedules than

Large-scale studies later quantified the health effects, a 2008 Swedish registry study

Traffic data show a parallel pattern. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration

Complex physiological and behavioral systems often absorb a simple schedule change by shifting risk
rather than eliminating cost. When an intervention is justified solely by an assumed first order
resource saving, any second order affects on sleep, commuting or temperature control can erase the
intended benefit. Because the original wartime justification no longer applies, the policy now persists
largely through institutional inertia. The practical question for any proposed clock change is therefore
whether the measurable health and safety externalities have been priced into the decision at all.
