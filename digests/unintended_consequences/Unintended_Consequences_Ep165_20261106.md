> **Red-light cameras cut right-angle crashes 24.6 percent yet raised rear-end crashes 14.9 percent across 132 intersections.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 2005 the Federal Highway Administration examined 132 camera-equipped intersections in seven U.S. jurisdictions and recorded a 24.6 percent drop in right-angle crashes alongside a 14.9 percent rise in rear-end collisions. The same report calculated a modest net economic benefit once crash costs were tallied. This safety device, installed to prevent deadly side-impact crashes, produced a measurable increase in another crash type at many of the same sites.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Red-light running killed more than 800 people and injured roughly 200,000 others each year in the United States by the late 1990s, according to figures cited in the 2007 Virginia Transportation Research Council report. Transportation engineers and public-safety officials in several states viewed automated cameras as a practical way to enforce signals without placing officers at every intersection. Jurisdictions such as Alexandria, Arlington, Fairfax County, Falls Church, and Vienna in Northern Virginia began installing cameras after the state legislature authorized their use. The technology appeared to offer continuous enforcement at lower staffing cost while targeting the specific violation—entering an intersection after the light turned red—that produced the most severe angle collisions. At the time, early studies that did not adjust for regression to the mean suggested large safety gains, reinforcing the decision to expand the programs. Officials reasoned that if the camera could change driver behavior at the exact moment the light changed, the most destructive crashes would decline even if total crash volume stayed the same or rose slightly.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1998 and 2004 the six Northern Virginia jurisdictions operated red-light cameras at selected intersections and collected seven years of crash data for later analysis. Proponents pointed to immediate drops in red-light violations captured on video. The Federal Highway Administration launched a larger multijurisdictional evaluation that applied empirical Bayes methods to 132 sites, seeking to isolate the cameras’ effect from background trends. Early press coverage and agency statements emphasized the reduction in angle crashes as evidence that the cameras were working as intended. A smaller number of engineers and statisticians warned that prior evaluations had not controlled for regression to the mean, a statistical artifact that can exaggerate apparent safety improvements when sites are chosen because they recently experienced high crash counts. The Virginia program therefore became one of the first large-scale tests in which both the intended angle-crash reduction and any offsetting rear-end increase could be measured against the same baseline data set.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The 2005 Federal Highway Administration study documented the 24.6 percent decline in right-angle crashes and the simultaneous 14.9 percent increase in rear-end crashes. Drivers approaching camera-equipped intersections braked more abruptly to avoid a citation, and following vehicles sometimes could not stop in time. The Virginia Transportation Research Council’s 2007 analysis of the same Northern Virginia corridors found rear-end crashes rose between 27 and 42 percent depending on the statistical method, with substantial variation across jurisdictions. In Arlington, every crash category studied increased; in Vienna, most categories declined. When comprehensive crash costs were calculated, the aggregate result across the six jurisdictions showed a net increase in total crash costs, although injury-only costs fell modestly once three fatal angle crashes were set aside. The Campbell Collaboration’s 2020 systematic review of 38 studies confirmed an average 19 percent rise in rear-end collisions once regression-to-the-mean adjustments were applied. The causal chain ran directly from the enforcement incentive: the camera punished red-light entry but rewarded sudden stops, shifting crash type rather than eliminating harm. Second-order effects included higher repair costs for property-damage crashes and occasional disputes over camera calibration and yellow-interval timing. Because rear-end collisions at these sites were more often low-speed events involving two vehicles already in the same lane, their average injury severity remained lower than the angle crashes they replaced; yet the sheer increase in frequency still raised total repair bills and insurance claims in several jurisdictions. One jurisdiction recorded an annual increase of $140,883 in comprehensive crash costs while another recorded an annual reduction of $92,367, illustrating that the same camera technology produced opposite net outcomes depending on local traffic volume, signal timing, and approach geometry.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
The Virginia Transportation Research Council concluded that cameras should be evaluated intersection by intersection rather than adopted as a blanket policy. Some jurisdictions reduced or removed cameras where rear-end increases outweighed angle-crash reductions; others retained them at high-risk angle-crash locations. Later national guidance incorporated site-specific crash-type trade-offs into camera-program design. The 2020 Campbell review noted that effects continued to vary with study quality and local conditions, leaving program managers to weigh local geometry, signal timing, and traffic volume before deciding on new installations. Over time, several agencies began publishing the full before-and-after crash matrices for each candidate site so that decision makers could see both the angle-crash savings and the rear-end penalty in the same table before voting on installation.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Northern Virginia officials selected intersections for cameras based on recent red-light crash counts, they triggered both the intended drop in angle collisions and the unintended rise in rear-end collisions at those same sites. When the Federal Highway Administration monetized all crash types together, the net benefit appeared only after the rear-end increase was subtracted from the angle-crash savings. Engineers who later required site-by-site before-and-after data avoided committing resources to locations where the rear-end penalty exceeded the angle-crash gain. How might a city today test a new enforcement tool at a handful of intersections before scaling it to every corridor that shows the original problem?
