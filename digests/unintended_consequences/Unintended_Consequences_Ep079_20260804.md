> **Cheap flights and Instagram made the world's fragile wonders accessible to millions — and then the crowds began to erode what they came to see.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the summer of 2018, Thai authorities closed Maya Bay, the small cove on Ko Phi Phi Leh that had drawn more than 5,000 visitors a day after appearing in the 2000 film *The Beach*. The coral had been trampled, the water clouded by sunscreen and boat fuel, and the once-white sand was compacted into hardpan. Officials had not set out to damage the bay; they had welcomed the tourism that arrived after the movie as a straightforward economic gain for southern Thailand. The closure lasted four years because the daily volume had crossed the threshold at which the physical processes maintaining the bay—live coral growth, sediment turnover, and water exchange—could no longer keep pace with the rate of disturbance. Instead, the very accessibility that spread the joy of the place had removed its capacity to remain itself.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
After the Second World War, governments and airlines worked to make long-distance travel available beyond the wealthy. Jet aircraft, introduced in the late 1950s, lowered costs per passenger; deregulation in the United States in 1978 and in Europe in the 1990s allowed new low-cost carriers to enter the market. Ryanair began operations in 1985 and EasyJet in 1995, offering fares that sometimes fell below the price of a train ticket between the same cities. At the same time, social-media platforms after 2010 let travelers share precise locations with followers, turning once-obscure waterfalls in Iceland or temples in Peru into recognizable destinations. Policymakers and industry leaders viewed these developments as extensions of earlier public-health and leisure policies: wider access to nature and culture would improve lives and spread economic benefits to host communities. The assumption was that more visitors would simply scale the existing benefits without changing the underlying resource, because each additional arrival was treated as adding only marginal demand on infrastructure already sized for smaller numbers.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Budget carriers expanded routes rapidly through the 2000s. By 2019, European low-cost flights exceeded 1.2 billion seats annually. Cruise lines increased capacity, with vessels carrying more than 4,000 passengers docking at Venice’s historic center several times a week. Instagram’s location-tagging feature, rolled out in 2010, made it simple for users to mark exact coordinates; by 2017, posts tagged at Iceland’s Seljalandsfoss waterfall had risen into the hundreds of thousands. Local governments initially celebrated the revenue. Maya Bay saw visitor numbers climb from a few hundred a day in the 1990s to over 5,000 after the film. Venice’s tourism office promoted day trips as a way to spread spending. Early data showed hotel occupancy and restaurant receipts rising; few formal caps existed because the prevailing model treated visitor growth as an unqualified good, and the arithmetic of rising receipts appeared to confirm that the system was working as intended.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The same mechanisms that increased access removed any practical limit on daily volume. In Venice, day-trippers reached an estimated 20–25 million annually by the mid-2010s, while the resident population fell below 50,000. Housing converted to short-term rentals raised rents beyond the reach of many local families. Wastewater systems designed for a smaller permanent population overflowed during peak summer days, and the wakes of large cruise ships accelerated erosion along the lagoon’s foundations. At Machu Picchu, daily visitors climbed past 5,000, compacting soil on the Inca trails and increasing landslide risk during rainy seasons. In Iceland, roadside pullouts at popular sites were widened repeatedly, yet parking still overflowed onto fragile moss, and search-and-rescue calls rose with the number of unprepared hikers. Maya Bay’s coral cover declined by roughly 80 percent in the decade after the film, according to surveys by Thailand’s Department of National Parks. The causal chain was straightforward: zero or near-zero marginal cost for each additional visitor, combined with visible social proof on social media, produced daily loads that exceeded the ecological and infrastructural thresholds of the sites. Consider the arithmetic at Maya Bay alone: an increase from a few hundred visitors to 5,000 represents roughly a tenfold to twentyfold rise in daily foot traffic, boat moorings, and chemical inputs, all concentrated on a cove whose coral and sediment systems had evolved under far lower disturbance rates. Second-order effects followed. Residents left historic centers, removing the very local life that many tourists had hoped to encounter. Emergency closures then shifted pressure to neighboring sites that lacked even basic management plans. One might ask whether the revenue offset these losses; in practice the revenue accrued to short-term operators and platforms while the costs of erosion control, rescue services, and habitat repair remained with public agencies and the remaining residents.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Thailand closed Maya Bay in June 2018, reopening it in January 2022 with visitor numbers capped at roughly 380 per hour and a requirement that boats moor offshore. Venice introduced a five-euro day-tripper fee for peak periods beginning in 2024, paired with a reservation system, though enforcement remains limited to a few entry points. Iceland has added parking fees and timed entry at several highlands sites and has funded trail hardening with a portion of the revenue. Peru now requires advance tickets for Machu Picchu and has capped daily entries near 4,000. These measures have reduced peak loads at the most stressed locations, yet they have also concentrated demand on still-open alternatives and created administrative costs that smaller municipalities struggle to meet. Some residents note that the fees generate revenue without restoring the quieter character of the places before mass visitation began.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Shared scenic or cultural resources without effective gatekeeping tend toward overuse because the cost of each additional visitor falls almost entirely on the resource itself rather than on the visitor or the platform that directs them there. Pricing, quotas, and physical limits can restore balance, but only when they are set below the visible degradation threshold and are enforced consistently. The pattern appears again with any open-access digital or physical commons whose popularity grows faster than its carrying capacity. How might current platforms and transport systems be designed so that the next wave of newly accessible places does not repeat the same sequence?
