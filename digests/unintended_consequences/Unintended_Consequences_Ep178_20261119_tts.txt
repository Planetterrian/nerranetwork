Over seventy-five thousand ferrets released to control rabbits instead reduced native ground-nesting birds.

This is Unintended Consequences, episode one hundred seventy-eight.

In eighteen eighty-one New Zealand's Rabbit Nuisance Act gave ferrets official protection as natural enemies of the rabbit and empowered inspectors to enforce control on landowners. Pastoral runs in the southern South Island had already seen wool clip and lambing rates fall sharply from rabbit damage by the mid eighteen seventies. The Act was designed to cut rabbit numbers on grazing land.

Pastoralist Cuthbert Cowan brought the first documented private shipment of ferrets to his Southland property in eighteen seventy-six, after rabbits had begun to reduce sheep numbers on his runs. Rabbits had arrived with whalers and early settlers from the eighteen twenties through the eighteen fifties and multiplied once pastoral farming expanded.

By the early eighteen seventies, burrowing and pasture loss were cutting wool production and land values across Otago and Southland. Government rental income from affected runs dropped from two thousand two hundred eighty-eight pounds to six hundred nineteen pounds by eighteen eighty-one.

Superintending Rabbit Inspector Benjamin P. Bayly and local runholders turned to ferrets as a cheaper alternative to repeated poisoning and trapping. The Act shielded mustelids from harm and directed inspectors to promote their use. The choice rested on ferrets killing rabbits efficiently in Britain, and on the urgent need to protect the colony's dominant export income.

Cowan had already shown, on his own land, that ferrets could be bred locally and released without waiting for slow, expensive imports from England, which often arrived weakened after months at sea. The arithmetic looked straightforward. Each ferret could in principle remove dozens of rabbits a season and spare the recurring labour of trappers covering thousands of hectares of open tussock.

Fines of ten pounds on anyone who killed a protected mustelid gave landowners a legal reason to tolerate the new arrivals rather than treat the ferrets as another pest. Officials viewed the programme as an extension of familiar British game-keeping practice.

The Department of Agriculture, formed in eighteen ninety-two, placed rabbit control under its own inspectors and spent roughly a quarter of its eighteen ninety-five budget on the work. Government contracts purchased twenty-one thousand seven hundred sixty ferrets in eighteen eighty-seven to eighty-eight alone. Otago stations recorded more than seven thousand releases in eighteen ninety.

The Amuri Rabbit Board liberated one thousand ferrets plus stoats, weasels and cats in eighteen eighty-eight to eighty-nine. Private breeding stations supplied additional animals at up to ten shillings each for release on Crown pastoral leases. Historian R. M. Burdon later estimated the liberations running from eighteen seventy to nineteen twenty.

Early reports noted local drops in rabbit numbers on some runs, which encouraged further shipments even as stoats and weasels were added in at least twenty-five separate consignments between eighteen eighty-three and eighteen ninety-two.

Inspectors tracked the numbers released per station and recorded survival rates on the open plains, where cover was scarce compared with English hedgerows. The inspectors responded by increasing the scale of releases rather than questioning the method.

When initial ferret stocks proved insufficient, local breeding colonies on several South Island runs produced young animals on site for release the same season, cutting both transport losses and costs. The same records show that stoats and weasels were added once ferret numbers alone appeared inadequate to match rabbit breeding on the vast leasehold blocks.

Ferrets proved less specialised than hoped and readily took ground-nesting birds whose populations were already thinned by ship rats and cats. University of Waikato researcher Carolyn King's review of eighteen eighties newspapers and committee evidence shows that mustelids preyed on kiwi, weka and blue duck chicks as readily as on rabbits.

The same eighteen eighty-four Joint Committee testimony that recorded Cowan's twenty-six thousand rabbit kill also documented continued pasture damage once rabbit numbers rebounded. Because ferrets could not keep pace with rabbit breeding rates on open tussock country, landowners continued to press for more releases while native bird populations declined without similar public notice.

At least seven thousand eight hundred thirty-eight stoats and weasels arrived in the same period, adding further predation pressure. By nineteen hundred the combined mustelid population was established across the South Island, contributing to the mainland extinction of kakapo.

Rabbit boards formed after nineteen forty-seven still inherited a landscape where the original biological-control programme had failed to prevent either the target pest or the non-target losses.

On the open Canterbury and Otago plains, rabbits could retreat into burrows or disperse across hundreds of hectares, while ground-nesting birds had no such refuge once a ferret located a nest. The eighteen eighty-one Act's protection clause prevented any systematic removal of the mustelids even after early signs of bird losses appeared in station diaries.

Because the inspectors' performance was measured by rabbit counts on pastoral leases rather than by counts of surviving native species, the broader ecological cost remained outside the formal accounting.

Official government purchases of ferrets for release ended in eighteen eighty-nine, though Rabbit Boards and private landowners continued liberations into the twentieth century. Later legislation shifted emphasis to organised poisoning and, after nineteen eighty-nine, to regional councils that inherited both rabbit and predator problems.

Ferrets remain widespread and continue to limit recovery of several endemic birds. The Department of Conservation lists ferrets among the introduced predators whose presence compounds the effects of earlier rat and cat introductions. No subsequent programme has fully reversed the combined impacts on ground-nesting species.

The shift to poisoning reduced some rabbit pressure but left the established mustelid populations untouched, so the secondary predation on birds persisted even as rabbit boards were later disbanded.

When Benjamin Bayly's inspectors protected and released ferrets under the eighteen eighty-one Act, the rule rewarded any animal that killed rabbits on pastoral leases without measuring effects on other species. Later Rabbit Board funding formulas continued to reward rapid visible kills over long-term ecosystem accounting.

A control agent can look cheap and familiar, borrowed from a practice that already works somewhere else, and still be scored only on the pest the budget was written to shrink. Diet and range then sit outside the ledger until the losses are already widespread.

Decision-makers today can test whether a proposed control agent's diet and habitat range have been measured against all resident species before a large-scale release. The same test applies when modern agencies consider a new biological agent for any single pest.
