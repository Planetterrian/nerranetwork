> **Millions of women took hormone replacement therapy to prevent heart disease and stay youthful, until one trial showed it raised their risks of cancer, stroke, and heart attack instead.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the summer of 2002, more than six million American women were taking some form of hormone replacement therapy, many of them for reasons that had little to do with hot flashes. Then, on 9 July, the data safety monitoring board halted the largest part of the Women’s Health Initiative trial three years early. The reason was not ambiguous: combined estrogen and progestin increased the incidence of invasive breast cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke, and pulmonary embolism. The same regimen that physicians had prescribed to protect aging hearts had instead produced measurable harm.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
The modern use of menopausal hormone therapy began with a clear clinical problem: the sudden loss of ovarian estrogen produced vasomotor symptoms, sleep disruption, and accelerated bone loss. In 1966, Brooklyn gynecologist Robert A. Wilson published *Feminine Forever*, arguing that estrogen deficiency was a treatable disease state rather than a natural transition. Pharmaceutical companies developed conjugated equine estrogens (Premarin) and, later, combined regimens that added progestin to protect the uterus. By the late 1980s, observational studies such as the Nurses’ Health Study suggested that women who chose to take estrogen after menopause experienced roughly 40–50 percent fewer coronary events. Medical societies therefore began to frame long-term hormone use as a preventive strategy against osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease, conditions that together accounted for the majority of deaths in older women. At the time, no large randomized trial had tested that framing.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 1982 and 1992, annual U.S. prescriptions for non-contraceptive estrogens rose from roughly 15 million to more than 30 million. The Food and Drug Administration expanded the label in 1992 to include osteoporosis prevention; many clinicians extended the indication further to presumed cardioprotection. Academic centers and industry-sponsored continuing-education programs presented hormone therapy as standard care for healthy postmenopausal women. A 1995 survey of U.S. physicians found that more than 80 percent recommended estrogen to asymptomatic patients with a family history of heart disease. Skeptics existed—some epidemiologists noted that women who took hormones also tended to have higher education and better access to care—but the prevailing view held that the observational benefit was large enough to outweigh uncertain risks.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The Women’s Health Initiative enrolled 16,608 women aged 50–79 with an intact uterus and randomized them to daily conjugated equine estrogen plus medroxyprogesterone acetate or placebo. After 5.2 years the hazard ratio for coronary heart disease was 1.29, for stroke 1.41, for pulmonary embolism 2.13, and for invasive breast cancer 1.26. Absolute excess risks were modest—eight additional coronary events, eight additional strokes, and eight additional breast cancers per 10,000 women per year—but they appeared in a population that had been told the therapy was protective. Because most participants were more than a decade past menopause, the trial could not distinguish whether earlier initiation would have produced different results. The sudden reversal triggered an estimated 50 percent drop in U.S. hormone prescriptions within 18 months. Subsequent observational data suggested that women who had started therapy near the time of menopause experienced different risk profiles, yet the blanket recommendation had already collapsed. Many patients who might have benefited for short-term symptom relief stopped treatment, while others continued without clear guidance on duration or formulation.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Professional societies quickly narrowed indications to the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time, focused on vasomotor symptoms. The FDA added black-box warnings in 2003. Over the following decade, re-analyses of the Women’s Health Initiative data stratified by age and years since menopause showed that women aged 50–59 who began therapy closer to menopause had no excess coronary risk and possible benefit for all-cause mortality. European regulators reached similar conclusions. Today, guidelines from the North American Menopause Society and the Endocrine Society emphasize individualized decisions rather than universal prevention. The episode also prompted larger investments in comparative-effectiveness research on different estrogen routes and doses, an indirect but durable shift in how menopausal care is studied.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
Large-scale observational patterns can mislead when healthier or wealthier patients are more likely to receive the intervention under study. Second, translating risk reduction observed in one demographic group to an entire population can produce net harm once absolute risks are measured in randomized settings. Finally, abrupt withdrawal of a widely used therapy can itself leave patients without viable alternatives until new evidence accumulates. The question that remains is how medicine can design trials early enough, and stratify them finely enough, to separate the patients who gain from those who lose before millions begin treatment.
