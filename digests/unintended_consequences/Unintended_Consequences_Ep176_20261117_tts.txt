On April eleventh, twenty eighteen, President Donald Trump signed the Allow States and Victims to Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act, known as FOSTA-SESTA. The measure amended Section two hundred thirty of the Communications Decency Act. It removed liability protections for platforms that knowingly facilitated sex trafficking or prostitution.

Commercial sex advertising moved onto overseas servers and into encrypted channels.

Representative Ann Wagner of Missouri had introduced the Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act in the House in twenty seventeen. Senator Rob Portman of Ohio sponsored the companion bill in the Senate, the Stop Enabling Sex Traffickers Act. Both lawmakers had examined Backpage dot com. They concluded that Section two hundred thirty was shielding a site that profited from ads linked to trafficking.

They argued that platforms should face federal and state criminal and civil liability when they knowingly promoted or facilitated prostitution.

At the time, Backpage generated substantial revenue from adult-services sections. Federal investigators had already documented cases in which traffickers used the site to advertise minors. Wagner and Portman presented the bills as a targeted update. The change would encourage platforms to remove illegal content without broadly censoring speech.

The package passed the House three hundred eighty-eight to twenty-five on February twenty-seventh, twenty eighteen, and the Senate ninety-seven to two on March twenty-first, twenty eighteen. Supporters took those near-unanimous votes as evidence of a clear gap in accountability.

They saw the carve-out as restoring the original intent of Section two hundred thirty, limited to knowing promotion of prostitution or trafficking, with the rest of the immunity framework left intact.

Craigslist removed its entire personals section on March twenty-third, twenty eighteen, citing the pending legislation. The bill became law on April eleventh, just five days after federal authorities seized Backpage dot com.

Major United States platforms deleted or restricted adult-advertising categories to avoid new criminal exposure under Title Eighteen of the United States Code, section two thousand four hundred twenty-one A. Proponents said prosecutors and victims could finally reach operators who had claimed immunity.

Within weeks, activity that had sat on a few large domestic platforms began appearing on smaller sites hosted outside the United States. The new provision set penalties of up to ten years for operating a platform to promote prostitution, and up to twenty-five years in aggravated cases involving five or more persons or reckless disregard of trafficking.

A Government Accountability Office report covering twenty fourteen through twenty twenty found that platform operators relocated servers and domains to countries including the Netherlands, Turkey, and Germany to reduce exposure to United States jurisdiction. The market fragmented across many more foreign sites. Investigators needed a mutual legal assistance treaty for each new jurisdiction.

A Carnegie Mellon University study, summarized by LegalClarity in twenty twenty-six, showed that the volume of online commercial sex ads dipped right after FOSTA-SESTA, then returned to prior levels across a larger number of overseas platforms. Demand had not disappeared. New offshore sites filled the gap. The liability rule changed the location of the ads, not the underlying volume.

The Hacking//Hustling participatory research project, published in twenty twenty, surveyed online sex workers. Seventy-two point five percent reported increased economic instability after the law took effect. Thirty-three point eight percent reported more violent encounters with clients once screening tools on domestic sites disappeared.

Platforms could no longer safely host screening information or review boards without risking liability. Many workers moved to encrypted messaging apps and offshore boards with fewer safety features, and lost the client histories that had previously reduced risk.

The Government Accountability Office noted that only one of the eleven federal criminal cases brought against platform operators between twenty fourteen and twenty twenty actually relied on the new FOSTA provisions. A twenty twenty-one article in Porn Studies, citing those findings, described how cross-border evidence requests lengthened investigations.

What had been a single-point request became a series of separate treaty processes, each with its own delays and disclosure limits. Agencies faced a larger set of smaller targets. Fresh evidence collection across borders stretched limited resources and reduced the number of cases that could be finished in the same period.

The shift also reduced the visibility of transactions to United States researchers and service organizations that had monitored domestic platforms. Operators had calculated that moving hosting overseas lowered the probability of United States criminal charges more than any incremental moderation would, because the statute exposed them to federal prosecution and state-level suits.

Federal prosecutors have continued to rely primarily on pre-existing statutes rather than the new FOSTA tools, according to the Government Accountability Office. No civil damages or restitution orders have been issued under the law's private right of action.

Sex-worker organizations and civil-liberties groups have called for repeal or amendment, arguing that the liability change produced measurable safety harms without corresponding reductions in trafficking. A Taylor and Francis analysis linked a further pattern to longer enforcement delays: some platforms kept a minimal United States presence while routing traffic through foreign entities.

Researchers still track the offshore distribution first measured in the Government Accountability Office report. The relocation incentive has not reversed, and law enforcement has fewer centralized points of contact.

Strip immunity from one category of content and an operator who can leave will price staying against leaving. If the duty bites only where the server sits, geography becomes the cheaper form of compliance, and moderation never has to win that comparison.

Anyone drafting a platform-liability statute can ask, before the shift, whether the new duty reaches only domestically hosted services, and whether investigators already have the treaties and the staff for a market that splits. A vote can look like accountability while the measurable work moves into legal systems the statute does not control.

Those same incentives sit inside today's arguments over encryption and over content-moderation rules, wherever a duty is costly only inside one country's servers or only on platforms that keep readable records.
