> **Philippine officials banned logging across 3.76 million hectares in 1992, pushing cutters into remote watersheds that then flooded more often.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In May 1991 the Department of Environment and Natural Resources issued DAO 24, which prohibited logging in old-growth forests beginning January 1992. The measure was meant to halt commercial harvest on public lands and protect remaining primary forest. Instead, former concession workers and new migrants carried chainsaws into headwater areas where enforcement was weak, fragmenting watersheds and raising downstream flood risk without any drop in national wood demand.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
President Corazon Aquino faced accelerating forest loss that had already reduced cover to roughly 18 percent of the national land area. In October 1988 she imposed a tax on loggers to fund replanting. In March 1989 she banned log exports. In May 1991 her administration issued DAO 24, shifting all commercial cutting out of old-growth stands and into residual or plantation forests. The June 1992 National Integrated Protected Areas System Act further locked selected primary forests inside reserves. These steps followed the visible damage from Typhoon Uring in November 1991, when logs and debris from cleared slopes contributed to the deaths of at least 4,000 people in Ormoc. Aquino and her advisers treated the removal of large-scale operators as the direct way to keep soil on slopes and water in channels. They had watched decades of export-driven cutting reduce the country’s once-abundant primary hardwood stands and reasoned that ending legal access to those stands would let forests recover while shifting any remaining harvest to already-disturbed second-growth areas. The sequence of measures therefore aimed at both immediate protection of virgin timber and long-term watershed stability.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
DAO 24 took effect on schedule in January 1992. By 1996 the number of Timber License Agreements had fallen from 97 concessions covering 3.76 million hectares to 36 concessions covering 1.56 million hectares. The government cancelled several large operations, including the ACME Plywood concession in the Sierra Madre after documented illegal cutting. Proponents pointed to the drop in official log production as evidence that pressure on virgin stands was easing. Skeptics inside the Forest Management Bureau noted that cancelled concessions were not being placed under effective protection and that roads built for the former operations remained open. The reduction removed roughly 2.2 million hectares from licensed commercial use within six years, yet the same road networks that had once carried company trucks now stood available for any smaller operator who could reach them. Because the policy focused on cancelling licenses rather than closing or patrolling the access routes, the physical infrastructure of extraction stayed in place even as the legal operators departed.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Ernesto S. Guiang’s FAO review later documented that the policy displaced rather than reduced harvest pressure. Lowland families followed the same logging roads into the uplands, where more than 20 million people eventually lived. In the Northern Sierra Madre, Yoshiki Seki recorded that ex-logging workers and migrants from Ifugao turned former concession land into open-access zones. Small-scale operators with chainsaws moved into residual forests that the 1992 rules had left outside protected boundaries. Commercial logging had accounted for roughly 10 percent of annual forest loss; the remaining 90 percent came from agricultural expansion and slash-and-burn once the large firms departed. Because total demand for timber and fuelwood stayed constant, the new cutters simply worked smaller patches across more watersheds. Headwater soils eroded faster, increasing silt loads that reached lowland rice fields and coastal fisheries. The same pattern repeated after each new provincial moratorium: legal operators left, illegal chainsaw crews stayed, and the forest edge continued to retreat. One might ask whether stricter enforcement could have prevented the shift, yet the very roads and markets created under the earlier concession system continued to lower the cost of reaching remote stands for anyone with portable equipment. When large firms exited, they left behind both cleared patches ready for planting and an established network of buyers who still needed wood, so the economic incentive simply moved to the next available actor. The result was a wider scattering of cuts rather than a smaller total volume, with each new site contributing incremental erosion that accumulated in downstream channels. Over time the open-access condition meant that even community members who had once supported the ban on outsiders found themselves drawn into the same small-scale cutting once the corporate presence disappeared.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Subsequent administrations added further restrictions, including the 1995 Executive Order 263 on community-based forest management, yet annual forest loss remained near 47,000 hectares according to Forest Management Bureau data. Later presidents, including Rodrigo Duterte in 2018, issued fresh regional bans after typhoon-driven landslides. The underlying shift from corporate concessions to dispersed small-scale cutting has never been reversed. Enforcement remains weakest precisely where the original policy opened access roads and left residual stands without clear tenure. Each new ban therefore encountered the same geography of fragmented holdings and mobile cutters that the 1992 measures had helped create.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a national rule removes large, visible operators from an open resource, it rewards whoever can reach the same resource with smaller, harder-to-detect equipment. In the Philippine case the 1992 ban on old-growth concessions lowered recorded production while increasing the number of entry points into headwaters. Any later restriction that leaves roads, markets, and wood demand unchanged will again favor the most mobile cutters over the most regulated ones. The pattern suggests that future forest rules must address both the legal status of the land and the daily economics of the people already living on its edge. How might a similar policy change today alter the daily choices of the people already positioned along those same roads?
