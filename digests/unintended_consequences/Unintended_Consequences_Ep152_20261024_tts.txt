Ban-the-box laws widened the Black-White job callback gap from seven to forty-three percent in New Jersey and New York City.

This is Unintended Consequences, episode one hundred fifty-two.

This is the hope that hid the record, the staggered rollout that made a test possible, the substitution employers made once the question was gone, the later rules layered on top, and the trait a designer still has to name.

In twenty eighteen the Quarterly Journal of Economics published a field experiment built for that exact change.

Researchers sent roughly fifteen thousand fictitious job applications in New Jersey and New York City, before local ban-the-box rules took effect and after.

The applications were not a survey of opinions.

They were an attempt to watch what a real employer did with a real inbox once a familiar question disappeared.

Criminal records had cut callbacks sharply at employers that still asked the question.

The policy had been written to give people with convictions a fairer first look.

Instead it enlarged the racial disparity that remained after the record was hidden.

Amanda Agan and Sonja Starr designed the correspondence study so the applications could be compared cleanly.

The fictitious applicants were young men.

Their names signaled race.

Education, experience, and address were otherwise identical.

The only variable that changed systematically after the policy was the absence of the conviction question.

That design matters, because a widening gap in callbacks could not be laid on differences in applicant quality.

The paper showed the effect concentrated among employers that had previously asked the question.

Those were the firms whose information environment actually changed.

Because the study tracked the same set of employers across the policy change, it ruled out a simpler story.

Firms newly entering the market, or leaving it, were not what drove the result.

Hold the labor market constant, hold the employer constant, remove one line from the form, and the callback pattern still moved.

The researchers read that movement as statistical discrimination.

Without the record, some employers used race as a proxy for the likelihood of a criminal history.

A separate analysis, by Jennifer Doleac and Benjamin Hansen, looked past the callback and into employment itself.

Using Current Population Survey data from two thousand four to two thousand fourteen, they found ban-the-box policies reduced employment probabilities by three point four percentage points for young Black men without college degrees.

The same analysis found a drop of two point three percentage points for young Hispanic men in that group.

Those drops were largest precisely where criminal-record prevalence differs most across racial lines in national data.

That group is young men without college degrees.

The pattern is consistent with employers updating their beliefs about the probability that an applicant in that group has a record, once the direct information is withheld.

None of that was the future the first legislators were trying to build.

The first ban-the-box law appeared in Hawaii in nineteen ninety-eight.

Legislators there wanted to reduce the automatic exclusion of applicants who had served their sentences and could not get past the initial form.

Picture the moment they were trying to interrupt.

A person has finished a sentence.

A job is open.

The application arrives, and a checkbox ends the conversation before a human being has heard a word about the work.

That is a hard problem to look at and do nothing.

People returning from prison faced unemployment rates several times the national average.

Lawmakers connected that fact to a second risk.

Lack of steady work raised the chance that someone would reoffend, not because a checkbox predicted character, but because idleness and poverty make a stable life harder to hold.

The reform was aimed at that chain.

Get the person to a conversation.

Let the record be explained.

Give work a chance to do what punishment already finished had not done alone.

By the mid-twenty tens, more than thirty states and the District of Columbia had passed versions of the idea.

Some versions applied to public employers.

Some reached government contractors.

Some covered all private firms.

The details differed, and that difference will matter in a moment, but the shared bet was the same.

Hide the conviction at the front of the process, and a fairer hearing becomes more likely.

In twenty fifteen the federal government removed the question from its own job applications.

Proponents reasoned from a simple sequence.

If employers never saw the conviction until an interview had already begun, qualified candidates would have a chance to explain their record in person.

They would not be filtered out by a checkbox.

The logic rested on an observation that many people who had hired, or tried to get hired, already recognized.

Many employers used the box as a quick screen even when the offense bore no relation to the job duties.

A conviction for something unrelated to the work could function like a closed door.

Time is scarce in a hiring office.

A box is fast.

A conversation is slow.

The reformers were not imagining cruel people so much as busy people using the cheapest signal on the page.

Their answer was to take that cheap signal away, at least until a person was already in the room.

It is worth sitting with how reasonable that answer was on the information they had.

They could see the screen.

They could see the unemployment.

They could see the path from joblessness back toward harm.

What they could not yet see, in nineteen ninety-eight or even in the early wave of state laws, was the signal an employer would reach for once the box was gone.

Cities and states adopted the rules at different times.

That stagger created natural experiments.

New Jersey and New York City both changed their requirements in the years covered by the Agan and Starr study.

An employer in one place could be observed before the rule and after it, while the surrounding labor market was held as constant as a real city allows.

That is why a field experiment could isolate the effect of removing the box, rather than blaming the difference on a boom or a slump.

Early reports from advocates noted something the reformers had hoped to see.

More applicants with records reached the interview stage.

On its own terms, that is not a small thing.

The checkbox had been the wall.

For some people with convictions, the wall moved.

Supporters pointed to the growing number of jurisdictions that had acted.

They also pointed to the Obama administration's decision as evidence that the approach was workable and humane.

A policy that starts in one state, spreads past thirty, and reaches federal applications looks, from the inside, like a success gathering proof.

Few contemporaneous accounts examined the other half of the employer's problem.

If the conviction question is gone, what does a risk-averse hirer use instead?

The advocates were watching the applicants the law was written for.

The applicants without records, whose names still signaled race, were easier to leave out of that first round of stories.

Both things can be true in the same season.

More people with records can reach an interview.

And employers who used to sort on a verified fact can start sorting on a proxy.

The staggered map made that second movement measurable.

It did not, at the time, make it obvious to the people passing the laws.

Before the laws, the employers that used the box were not ignoring race, and they were not ignoring records either.

Those employers called back white applicants with records at lower rates than Black applicants without records.

Read that slowly, because it is the world the checkbox was actually producing.

A record could outweigh the advantage that whiteness otherwise carried in that callback pool.

The box was a blunt instrument, and it was also a piece of information that cut across the racial signal.

After the box was removed, the Black-White callback gap among applicants without records widened dramatically at those same employers.

These were not different firms with different cultures.

These were the firms that had asked, and then were told not to ask.

The information left on the application was what a name, a school, an address, and a work history could still convey.

Race remained visible in the way the experiment constructed it, through the name.

The conviction did not.

Statistical discrimination is a cold phrase for a human guess.

An employer wants to avoid a hire they believe is more likely to carry a record, and they no longer have the record.

They reach for the group trait that, in the data they carry in their head, correlates with that record.

Young Black men, in the national pattern the later employment study leaned on, sit in the demographic where that correlation is widest.

The guess does not require the employer to announce a theory.

It requires only that the callback button get pressed less often when the name fits the proxy.

The experiment's young men without records absorbed that guess.

Their applications matched on education, experience, and address.

What differed was the name, and the fact that the conviction question was no longer there to contradict the guess.

Second-order, the people the law meant to lift were not the only people in the file.

Applicants with records could gain a first look they had been denied.

Applicants without records, if they belonged to the group employers treated as higher risk, could lose callbacks they would have received when the box let an employer see a clean history directly.

Third-order, the employment numbers moved in the same direction as that guess.

Doleac and Hansen were not counting fictitious emails.

They were looking at whether young men without college degrees were working, in the years and places the policies arrived.

A callback gap is an insult you can measure in a lab-like field test.

An employment drop is rent, transit, and the next application, paid in the actual labor market.

The researchers' interpretation ties those drops to the withheld fact.

Where record prevalence differs most by race, hiding the record gives race more work to do as a stand-in.

That is not a claim that every employer did this, or that every jurisdiction saw the same size of effect.

It is a claim that the average pattern, in the Current Population Survey window from two thousand four to two thousand fourteen, moved against the groups the proxy would be expected to burden.

And it is a claim that the Quarterly Journal of Economics experiment locates the change in the firms that lost the question, not in the firms that never asked it.

The policy changed the information environment in which hiring decisions were made.

Employers did not stop deciding.

They decided with a poorer file and a remaining signal.

Sonja Starr told Jezebel in twenty sixteen that the findings were not necessarily an indictment of the policy.

The reason she gave is worth keeping, because it refuses a simple victory lap in either direction.

Removing the box still helped some applicants with records.

Addressing racial bias directly remained difficult.

A result can be real and still not tell a legislature to pretend the original problem has vanished.

No large-scale repeal followed.

The checkbox did not sweep back across the map.

Some jurisdictions later added requirements that employers consider the relevance and age of convictions.

Some required that the question wait until a conditional offer.

Those additions are an attempt to keep the humane aim and narrow the harm.

Relevance is a sensible word if the offense has nothing to do with the job.

Age of the conviction is a sensible word if a person is being judged forever for a finished sentence.

A delay until a conditional offer tries to preserve the interview the Hawaii legislators wanted in nineteen ninety-eight.

The core mechanism, though, remains in place in dozens of cities and states.

Hiding one verifiable fact still leaves employers to rely on other signals.

Later versions of the policy layered additional rules on top of the original disclosure change rather than restoring the box.

That choice preserved the information asymmetry.

It tried, at the same time, to constrain how employers could act on the remaining signals.

Whether those constraints bind is a harder thing to see from a statute than from a callback log.

A rule can tell an employer to weigh relevance.

A rule cannot, by itself, stop a tired reader from letting a name do the work a missing line used to do.

The aftermath is not a clean reversal and not a clean vindication.

It is a policy that still helps some of the people it named, still shifts burden onto people it did not mean to burden, and still governs hiring forms in a great many places.

When a disclosure rule withholds one fact, the next decision does not become neutral.

It becomes a search for the next most available signal, and in this case that signal was race, read off a name, in a pool of otherwise identical young men.

The substitution showed up among the employers who had used the box, which is the footprint you would expect if the policy itself changed the file, not if the economy merely changed clothes.

The same substitution showed up again as lower employment probabilities for young Black men without college degrees, and for young Hispanic men in that group, in the years the policies spread.

A designer who wants to know whether a disclosure rule is working cannot stop at the applicants the rule was written to release from a screen.

The data that later judge the rule are the same data that can show who absorbed the proxy.

Track the group whose observable trait now stands in for the hidden fact, and track them in the same places, before and after the form changes.
