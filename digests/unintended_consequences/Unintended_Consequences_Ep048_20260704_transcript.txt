Mao Zedong launched this 1958 drive to transform China from an agrarian country into an
industrial power in just a few years. Agricultural surpluses were expected to feed city workers
and pay for imported machinery. Sparrows appeared on the pest list because they consumed grain seeds
and ripening crops. The other three targets were rats, flies, and mosquitoes.
Party planners drew on both traditional pest control practices and recent public health successes
against mosquitoes. They treated the birds as a straightforward production constraint, eliminating
them looked like an inexpensive way to raise output at a moment when every kilogram of grain
mattered for steel quotas and urban rations. Local cadres who met or exceeded pest killing targets
earned political credit. This reinforced the sense that the policy aligned incentives with national
goals. The campaign began in earnest in April 1958 with mass rallies and school drills.
Families were told to destroy nests, smash eggs, and shoot or net adult birds. In cities,
factory whistles, and household utensils created continuous noise barrages. In the countryside,
villagers formed sparrow-killing brigades that patrolled fields at dawn and dusk.
Official reports from late 1958 and 1959 claimed hundreds of millions of birds killed in a single
province. Newspapers printed photographs of children proudly displaying slingshots and piles of
dead sparrows. Some agronomists inside the Chinese Academy of Sciences quietly noted that
sparrows also ate large numbers of locusts and other insects. Their cautions were sidelined amid
the political pressure to demonstrate revolutionary zeal. By the end of 1959, the Eurasian tree sparrow
had become rare in many eastern provinces. With sparrow populations collapsed, locust and other insect
numbers rose sharply in 1959 and 1960. Fields already stressed by communal dining halls,
exaggerated harvest reports, and diverted labor for backyard steel furnaces now faced unchecked
insect damage. In parts of Hainan and Anhui, locustswarms stripped entire districts of foliage within days.
The loss of an avian predator was only one factor among many that produced the 1959 to 1961
famine. Yet it amplified the effect of already reduced planting and harvesting capacity.
Provincial Health Reports from 1960 described sudden spikes in crop loss attributed to insects that
had previously been kept in check. Rural families who had participated enthusiastically in the killing
drives watched seedlings vanish. Some later recalled the eerie silence in fields where bird
song had once been constant. The ecological chain ran from policy directive to local mobilization
to near total removal of one species to rapid growth in others, to measurable declines in grain
that reached the state granaries. Because the same political system discouraged honest reporting
of shortfalls, the insect damage remained invisible in official statistics until the crisis was already
widespread. In March 1960, the central government quietly removed sparrows from the four pests list
and substituted bedbugs. Some provinces began importing sparrows from the Soviet Union and Mongolia
to restore populations. Though numbers recovered only slowly, the policy reversal did not halt the famine,
which continued until agricultural reforms and grain imports eased conditions in 1962.
In later decades, Chinese ecologists and historians documented the episode as a textbook case of
single species intervention. Today, the Eurasian tree sparrow is again common across China.
The four pests campaign is taught in some university envy studies as an early warning about the risks
of treating living systems as simple machines. Grain loss to birds was the narrow variable here.
