> **Thirty-two major dams on the Tigris and Euphrates reduced the flood pulses that fed Iraq's Mesopotamian marshes.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the early 2000s, Landsat images showed the vast reed beds and shallow lakes of southern Iraq reduced to cracked salt flats dotted with dead fish and abandoned buffalo. Turkish, Syrian, and Iraqi engineers had built the dams to store water for hydropower and year-round irrigation in upstream regions that had long suffered from unreliable rainfall. Instead, the structures removed the annual spring floods that had kept the downstream marshes alive for millennia. The change was not instantaneous; it accumulated as each new reservoir filled and held back the high-water peaks that once spread across the floodplain. Without those pulses, the balance between inflow and evaporation that had sustained the marshes for thousands of years tipped toward steady decline.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
After World War II, Turkish planners launched the Southeastern Anatolia Project, known as GAP, to lift one of the country’s poorest regions out of poverty. They calculated that storing winter and spring runoff behind large dams would generate electricity for factories and provide controlled water for cotton, wheat, and fruit on previously dry land. Similar projects in Syria and Iraq followed the same logic: rivers that had flooded unpredictably could be turned into reliable assets for national development. At the time, the marshes downstream appeared as remote wetlands whose seasonal rhythms would simply adjust to steadier, lower flows. The engineers worked with the data they possessed—river gauges, reservoir models, and national growth targets—without a full picture of how tightly the marshes depended on the pulse of high water. Their models treated the river system as a linear supply chain rather than a pulsing delta where timing of delivery mattered as much as total volume.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Construction accelerated through the 1970s and 1980s. Turkey completed the Keban Dam in 1974 and the Atatürk Dam in 1992; Syria added the Tabqa Dam; Iraq built its own storage reservoirs. By the early 1990s, more than thirty large dams stood along the two rivers, their combined storage capacity exceeding three times the average annual flow, according to the 2001 UNEP report. Early results looked promising: Turkish hydropower output rose sharply and new irrigation districts opened in the Harran plain. Downstream, however, the spring flood peaks that had once reached the marshes began to flatten. Iraqi authorities, facing their own water shortages after the 1991 Gulf War, started additional drainage works that accelerated the change. Each new dam upstream altered the hydrograph in measurable ways: peak discharges dropped while base flows became more constant, removing the very variability the marshes had evolved to exploit.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
The marshes, which once covered between 15,000 and 20,000 square kilometers, shrank dramatically. UNEP’s 2001 assessment, drawing on Landsat imagery, documented an 85 percent loss by 2000, with the bulk of the rapid desiccation occurring between 1991 and 1995 when Iraqi drainage canals and embankments severed remaining water sources. The upstream dams had already cut the frequency and height of flood pulses over preceding decades, so less water reached the marshes even before the final drainage. Without periodic flushing, salinity climbed, reed beds died, and the fishery that supported thousands of Marsh Arab families collapsed. Dust storms increased as exposed lake beds dried. The 2025 MDPI Plants paper on threatened aquatic species records vegetation losses between 80 and 99 percent across the major marsh units from 1982 to 2017, attributing the long-term stress to reduced inflows from the dammed rivers alongside the 1990s drainage. Communities that had lived by fishing, buffalo herding, and reed harvesting for generations lost their livelihoods while the electricity and crops benefited distant urban and agricultural centers. The causal chain ran through storage rules that maximized retention for power and irrigation without any requirement to release water at the precise volume and timing needed to recreate historical flood peaks hundreds of kilometers downstream.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
After 2003, some embankments were breached and partial reflooding occurred, restoring roughly 58 percent of the marshes by 2006 according to WIRED reporting on UNEP and NASA data. Recovery remained limited by continued upstream withdrawals, higher salinity, and reduced overall flow. Turkey continued operating the GAP reservoirs for power and irrigation, while downstream provinces faced recurring drought. No single country reversed its dam operations; instead, talks on minimum flow agreements began but produced modest results. The marshes that remain are smaller, more saline, and still vulnerable to any further reduction in spring runoff. Attempts to negotiate coordinated releases have repeatedly stalled because each nation’s operating rules still prioritize its own storage targets over the downstream pulse that once connected the entire basin.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When Turkish, Syrian, and Iraqi planners sized reservoirs to capture three times the rivers’ average volume, they rewarded steady extraction for upstream farms and turbines without a corresponding rule that protected the downstream flood pulse the marshes required. Later restoration efforts showed that breaching drainage works could bring water back only if upstream releases still delivered enough seasonal volume. Decision makers today sizing new storage on transboundary rivers can test whether their operating rules preserve at least the historical peak-flow events that sustain wetlands hundreds of kilometers downstream. How might current basin models change if they treated the marsh flood pulse as a measurable delivery target rather than leftover water?
