Residents in Hackney and Islington reported longer waits at local junctions and in some cases
rerouted school runs that added minutes to already tight schedules. The causal chain was straight
forward once the numbers are examined step by step. A cordon price creates a sharp discontinuity
at its edge. Inside the line, the cost is 5 pounds, outside it is zero. Drivers rationally seek
the lowest cost path that avoids the priced area, which means any trip whose origin and destination
can be connected without crossing the cordon will shift to the unpriced boundary roads.
Because the scheme did not price or restrict the boundary roads themselves,
those roads became the new equilibrium points for marginal trips that could no longer use the
interior as a shortcut. An obvious objection is that many drivers might simply cancel trips or
switch to transit. While some did, the monitoring data showed that enough marginal traffic remained on
the perimeter to produce measurable increases. Second order effects included pressure on parking in
adjacent neighborhoods and complaints from bus operators whose cross boundary services encountered new
delays. Third order effects appeared in local planning debates as several burrows began considering

No wholesale reversal occurred. The core scheme remained in place and was later cited in the design
of clean air zones in other British cities. The lasting adjustment has been a broader recognition that
cordon systems require explicit attention to the ring of streets immediately outside the priced area.
Any boundary drawn for pricing or regulation will shift activity to the spaces just beyond it unless
those spaces are also managed. Systems that treat the edge as neutral ground reliably discover that
