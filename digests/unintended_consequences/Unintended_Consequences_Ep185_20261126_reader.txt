Subsidies covering five hundred fifty thousand hectares increased Morocco's groundwater use instead of conserving it.

In two thousand seven Morocco launched the National Program of Water Savings in Irrigation under the Green Morocco Plan, supported by World Bank financing, and claimed savings exceeding one billion cubic meters.

Marcel Kuper and colleagues at CIRAD traced the policy roots to the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties, when state planners in large-scale schemes such as Tadla sought to stabilize medium-sized farms through surface-water delivery and industrial crops.

By the mid-two thousands, declining rainfall and growing demand had produced visible groundwater depletion, and officials viewed drip conversion as the logical next step.

Subsidies climbed from earlier levels of sixty percent to eighty to one hundred percent for micro-irrigation equipment, wells, and reservoirs.

Policymakers reasoned that higher application efficiency would reduce the volume of water needed per hectare while raising yields and rural incomes, in line with the Green Morocco Plan's emphasis on agricultural intensification and export competitiveness.

At the time, remote-sensing data and field studies had not yet quantified how farmers would respond, so the decision rested on the premise that technical efficiency gains would translate directly into lower basin-wide withdrawals.

Officials had watched surface-water quotas shrink in dry years and concluded that replacing open-channel flooding with pressurized pipes, emitters, and on-farm reservoirs would stretch every cubic meter further without new dams or transfers.

The capital grants were also expected to lift small and medium holdings into higher-value fruit and vegetable production, improving food security and foreign-exchange earnings from exports.

Between two thousand seven and two thousand eight the government scaled subsidy rates sharply and streamlined approvals for turnkey projects that included ponds and private wells.

By the end of two thousand eight roughly one hundred sixty-three thousand hectares had been equipped, concentrated in fruit-tree zones of the Tadla perimeter and the El Haouz plain.

Early monitoring recorded higher biomass per hectare and lower reported water deliveries at the scheme level, and program documents presented those figures as evidence of conservation.

The World Bank's Large-Scale Irrigation Modernization Project, backed by a loan of one hundred fifty million dollars, conditioned further support on collective conversion to drip.

Local agricultural offices promoted drip as a way for farmers to maintain production even when surface-water quotas fell short.

A two thousand nine Irrigation Association paper noted that average installation costs reached about five thousand seven hundred twenty-five dollars per hectare, with seventy percent of projects also building on-farm reservoirs.

Proponents highlighted the prospect of twenty to fifty percent water savings.

Few contemporaneous reports tracked whether the freed water stayed in the aquifer or was applied elsewhere.

Application forms required only proof of equipment purchase and basic soil tests, and no clause limited how many additional hectares a recipient could bring under irrigation once the system was installed.

An Agricultural Water Management paper from two thousand seventeen reported that aquifer overdraft in the affected basins reached one billion cubic meters per year even as drip coverage expanded.

In the El Haouz perimeter, remote-sensing analysis funded by the Global Water Security and Sanitation Partnership, GWSP, showed that water consumption per hectare did not decline once drip systems were installed, as farmers increased crop density and planted more water-intensive varieties.

Marcel Kuper's two thousand twelve analysis of forty years in the Tadla scheme found that larger and medium-sized holdings, which captured thirty-six percent of farms, used new access to groundwater to lease additional land and diversify into higher-value orchards.

Because drip reduced surface-water losses that had previously recharged shallow aquifers, net recharge fell while private tubewell pumping rose.

The two thousand seventeen CIRAD book chapter on North African cases traced how the same efficiency narrative persisted in policy documents despite the absence of basin-scale metering that would have revealed the expansion effect.

Farmers who received one hundred percent subsidies for wells and ponds faced no volumetric limit on extraction, so the marginal cost of adding irrigated area dropped.

Informal land markets accelerated, smallholders without capital for wells rented out parcels, and deeper aquifers began to be tapped as shallow tables dropped.

By the time the two thousand seventeen Elsevier study quantified the paradox, the pattern had already locked in across multiple overexploited basins.

If a hectare previously received eight thousand cubic meters under flood irrigation and now required only five thousand under drip, the three thousand cubic meters saved could irrigate another six-tenths of a hectare of the same crop, or support a shift to citrus or vegetables that demanded nine thousand cubic meters per hectare.

Without an overall extraction cap, that extra hectare or that extra crop was planted.

Larger operators captured most of the subsidy because those operators could finance the remaining twenty percent or the reservoir component, while smaller farms stayed on surface water or rented land out, concentrating groundwater rights.

As recharge from surface losses declined, the same volume of pumping lowered water tables faster, forcing some farmers to deepen wells and raising energy costs that higher-value output partly offset.

Subsequent World Bank and GWSP evaluations in the twenty twenties acknowledged that measured water consumption had not fallen in line with earlier projections.

The Large-Scale Irrigation Modernization Project continued, yet added components for monitoring actual evapotranspiration rather than relying on theoretical savings.

No nationwide rollback of the subsidy structure occurred, and newer guidance emphasized pairing drip conversion with enforceable extraction permits.

In the Tadla and El Haouz zones, some collective schemes began experimenting with collective well management, though enforcement remained uneven.

Aquifer levels in the most intensively converted perimeters continued to decline through the early twenty twenties, prompting calls for volumetric pricing that had not been part of the original program design.

Pilot programs now require satellite verification of cropped area alongside drip installation, but uptake of these paired rules has been slower than the original equipment grants.

Any new efficiency program can be tested, before funds are disbursed, for a binding limit on aggregate extraction rather than a promise of savings per hectare.
