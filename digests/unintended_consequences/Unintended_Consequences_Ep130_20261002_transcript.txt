In the late 1990s, European policy makers faced rising concern over greenhouse gas emissions
from road transport.
They viewed diesel engines as a ready technical solution because those engines delivered more
kilometers per liter of fuel, and therefore emitted less CO2 per kilometer than comparable
petrol engines. The arithmetic was straightforward. A vehicle that travels the same distance on less
fuel releases fewer carbon atoms into the atmosphere. The United Kingdom's 2002 company car tax reform
formalized this logic by tying the tax rate directly to official CO2 grams per kilometer figures.
At the time, regulators treated the lower CO2 number as the decisive environmental metric
and did not assign equal weight to other tailpipe pollutants.
Most Western European countries reduced excise duty on diesel relative to petrol and adjusted
annual circulation taxes to favor diesel models.
In the United Kingdom, the 2002 company car tax change accelerated the shift among business
fleets, which in turn influenced the wider used car market.
Early data showed the intended result.
Diesel's share of new car registrations in Western Europe climbed from under 25% to
more than 50% by 2011.
Proponents pointed to measurable reductions in fleet average CO2 and welcomed the contribution
to national climate targets.
A few air quality specialists noted that diesel combustion produced substantially more nitrogen
oxygen oxides, and particulate matter, yet these warnings received limited policy attention
while the CO2 metric remained the dominant target.
Diesel engines emit several times more nitrogen oxides under real-world driving conditions
than the laboratory test cycles used for type approval.
Because the tax incentives rapidly increased the number of diesel vehicles on the road, total
NOX output from passenger cars rose even as CO2 declined.
When diesel's share moved from roughly 25% to over 50%, the proportion of higher NOX vehicles in the fleet approximately doubled.
Each additional diesel car replaced a lower NOX petrol car, so the aggregate emissions of nitrogen oxides increased,
even though individual vehicles met the CO2-based tax criteria.
Roadside monitoring stations in London, Paris, Berlin, and other major cities began recording persistent breaches
of the European Union nitrogen dioxide limit values.
The 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal later revealed that many diesel cars had been equipped
with software that reduced NOX only during official tests, confirming that real-world emissions
were often several times higher than certified figures.
