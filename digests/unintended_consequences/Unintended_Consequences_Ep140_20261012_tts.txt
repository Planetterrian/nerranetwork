In the central United States, late in the nineteen eighties, a federal contract paid a farmer to stop planting one field.

The soil on that field was supposed to rest. Erosion was supposed to slow.

On a neighboring parcel that same farmer still owned, new ground was cleared and planted instead.

The land taken out of production was measured and celebrated.

The land brought into production was not.

That gap had a name among the economists who later measured it. Slippage.

It did not begin as a trick. It began as a contract that looked, on the drafting table, almost too clean to fail.

The Conservation Reserve Program, CRP, was written into the nineteen eighty-five Food Security Act.

The years before that bill had been full of visible soil loss across the Great Plains and the Midwest.

Sheet erosion cut fields. Wind lifted topsoil off summer-fallowed ground and carried it for miles.

Lawmakers and officials at the United States Department of Agriculture, the USDA, wanted three things at once.

They wanted less water erosion and less wind erosion.

They wanted smaller federal grain surpluses.

And they wanted farmers to have a reliable rental payment if those farmers would retire highly erodible acres for ten-year periods.

At the time, the policy looked straightforward.

Pay landowners to take marginal cropland out of annual tillage, and the environmental gains would follow directly.

JunJie Wu later documented how widely that expectation was shared among the people who designed the program.

The contracts were voluntary.

The payments were set by competitive bids.

Early enrollment targets were met quickly, because many operators already knew which fields were losing topsoil fastest.

No one at the drafting table treated every other acre on a farm as a fixed quantity.

The assumption was that retired acres would stay retired.

The arithmetic seemed simple enough on paper.

If an operator idled one hundred acres that had been losing eight tons of soil per acre each year to sheet erosion, the gross saving would be eight hundred tons a year.

Picture that. Eight hundred tons of soil that would not leave those one hundred acres.

The same kind of calculation applied to wind erosion on the Northern Plains.

There, blowing dust from summer-fallowed fields could remove several inches of topsoil in a single dry season.

Several inches. Not a film of dust. A depth you could measure with a ruler after one bad year.

Because the program used a bid system, the agency could select the most erosive parcels first.

The rental rate was calibrated to the local cash-rent market, so participation would be attractive without overpaying.

Designers therefore expected the measured reductions on contracted acres to translate almost one for one into watershed-level improvements.

Less sediment in the water. Better downstream water quality.

The logic was not careless. It was linear.

Retire the worst field. Count the soil that field no longer loses. Treat that count as the gain for the watershed.

What the contract did not do was ask what the rental check might finance on land the contract never touched.

By nineteen ninety, more than thirty million acres had been accepted into CRP contracts across the central United States.

Thirty million acres is a landscape, not a pilot plot.

The Department of Agriculture published annual reports showing large measured drops in sheet-and-rill erosion on the enrolled parcels.

Proponents pointed to those numbers as proof the rental payments were working.

And on the enrolled parcels, the numbers were real.

Soil that had been leaving those fields was leaving more slowly, or not at all, while the contracts held.

At the same time, a few agricultural economists began asking a different question.

Were the acres that remained in production changing their own use?

Shinsuke Uchida would later use farm-level census records to test exactly that.

The early success stories focused on the contracted fields.

The data that could reveal substitution across other land on the same farms were not yet assembled.

Early implementation reports therefore recorded only the acres inside contract boundaries.

When a county reported fifty thousand acres enrolled, the erosion-reduction tables subtracted the historic erosion rates for those fifty thousand acres and stopped there.

The possibility that the same operator might now plant an additional ten thousand acres on other ground under the same ownership sat outside the accounting frame.

That was not a hidden footnote. It was the frame itself.

If the contract boundary was the unit of measurement, anything beyond that boundary did not enter the subtraction.

Satellite imagery from the period later showed something the tables had not been built to see.

Some of the newly cultivated parcels had been hay meadows or woodlots.

Converting them required clearing, drainage tiling, or breaking sod.

Those operations themselves generated short-term erosion spikes before the new cropland stabilized.

So the program could be reducing erosion on a contracted field while a clearing crew, paid in part by the rental income, opened a meadow next door.

Both things could be true in the same season, on the same ownership.

The reports that reached Washington counted only one of them.

JunJie Wu's two thousand study in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics examined county-level data across the central United States.

Wu found that for every one hundred acres enrolled in CRP, roughly twenty acres of non-cropland were converted to crops.

Those conversions landed on land that had previously been pasture, hay, or woodland.

Twenty acres for every one hundred retired. Not a rounding error at the edge of a spreadsheet.

A fifth of the retired area, showing up again as new cropland.

And those new acres were often more erosive than the average retired parcel.

That detail changes the arithmetic.

The program had been designed to retire the most erosive ground first, through competitive bids.

The ground coming into crops was not a random swap of like for like.

Because the new acres were often more erosive, the net reduction in water erosion was about nine percent smaller than the gross figure implied.

The net reduction in wind erosion was about fourteen percent smaller.

Nine percent and fourteen percent are not the whole story disappearing.

They are the story shrinking, in a way the annual reports had not been written to show.

A watershed that was promised the full gross saving received something thinner.

Shinsuke Uchida's twenty eleven analysis of Agricultural Census longitudinal records reached a similar conclusion at the individual-farm level.

Partial participants converted an average of twenty-five percent of their remaining non-cropland to cropping after enrollment.

Uchida's twenty fourteen working paper refined the estimate to fourteen percent, using fixed-effects methods that controlled for time-invariant farm characteristics.

Fourteen percent in one specification. Twenty-five percent in another. Roughly twenty acres per one hundred in Wu's county data.

The studies do not land on a single integer. They land on a range, and the range points the same direction.

Enrollment was followed by conversion.

The mechanism was simple.

A farmer who received CRP rental income on part of the operation could use that income to cover the costs of clearing or draining another part.

The program rule paid only for the acres inside the contract boundary.

It placed no restriction on the acres outside it.

The result was slippage that appeared in satellite imagery as well as in census tabulations.

David Fleming's twenty ten study documented that pattern across more than a thousand Northern Plains and Corn Belt counties.

More than a thousand counties. Not one unusual township.

The second-order effect followed from the first.

Total cultivated area declined less than program administrators expected.

So some of the sediment and nutrient reductions that had been forecast never materialized at the watershed scale.

The field-level celebration and the watershed-level outcome were not the same number.

One objection sometimes raised is that the converted acres were marginal and would have been cropped eventually anyway.

That objection deserves a straight look, because timing matters if you are trying to know what a payment caused.

The longitudinal farm records show the timing of conversion clustered immediately after CRP enrollment on the same ownership.

And the magnitude of conversion scaled with the share of non-cropland the farm held at the moment of enrollment.

Farms that entered the program with large reserves of convertible pasture or woodland showed the largest subsequent increases in cropped area.

If conversion had been a slow, independent drift toward more crops, you would not expect it to bunch right after the contract, or to scale with how much convertible land the farm already held.

The cash-flow effect is straightforward.

The annual CRP rental check arrived regardless of commodity prices.

That lowered the risk of investing in new drainage or clearing equipment on the unconstrained acres.

A farmer staring at a volatile grain market might hesitate to tile a wet pasture.

A farmer staring at that same market, with a ten-year rental check already in the plan, had a cushion.

The payment did not require the operator to keep non-enrolled land in its pre-enrollment state.

So the payment effectively subsidized the expansion of the cultivated footprint, rather than simply shrinking it.

That is the causal chain, stated without drama.

Retire a field. Receive rent. Use the rent to open ground the contract never mentioned. Watch the net erosion gain come in smaller than the gross count.

Then watch the watershed miss part of the sediment reduction that the gross count had been used to promise.

Third-order, the political success of the program rested on the gross numbers.

Those numbers were easier to publish, because they lived inside the contract boundary, where measurement was required.

The slippage lived outside that boundary, where measurement was not.

A program can be popular, and even genuinely helpful on the acres it pays for, while still overstating what the landscape gained.

Later CRP rules added sodbuster and swampbuster compliance requirements.

Those rules tied benefits to the treatment of non-enrolled land.

Sodbuster spoke to newly broken sod. Swampbuster spoke to wetlands.

They were an attempt to say that a payment on one parcel could not be entirely silent about what happened on other parcels under the same operation.

Yet the core rental contract still covered only the acres bid into the program.

Researchers continued to publish slippage estimates.

Some auction designs began to incorporate points that penalized farms with large reserves of convertible non-cropland.

If a farm held a big stock of pasture or woodland that could be brought into crops, the scoring could treat that stock as a reason to bid more carefully, or not to select that bid at all.

No large-scale reversal of existing contracts occurred.

The acres already under ten-year agreements stayed under those agreements.

The program itself was reauthorized in subsequent farm bills with tighter environmental criteria.

The basic tension remained.

Pay for retired acres. Leave other land on the same ownership free to change use.

Compliance conditions and auction points narrowed that tension. They did not erase the contract structure that had produced it.

It is worth sitting with what the redesign did and did not do.

It did not treat the original designers as fools.

It treated the original measurement frame as incomplete, and tried to add rules around a contract that had already been written.

Adding a compliance condition after enrollment has begun is a different political act from writing that condition into the first bid.

Farmers had already planned around the rental check.

Counties had already reported the gross erosion drops.

The later rules had to live beside contracts that still paid only inside the boundary.

That is why the slippage estimates kept coming, even after sodbuster and swampbuster were on the books.

The fix addressed treatment of non-enrolled land. It did not rewrite the unit the program was built to count.

When a payment rule rewards the retirement of one parcel, and leaves every other parcel on the same ownership free to change use, the measured gain on the paid parcel will overstate the net change across the whole farm.

Wu's county figures and Uchida's farm records are what that overstatement looked like in numbers: a gross saving, then a net saving nine to fourteen percent smaller, with fourteen to twenty-five acres of new crops appearing against every one hundred retired.

A second principle sits inside the same records.

Slippage grew larger when participants held more convertible non-cropland at the moment they enrolled, because that capacity is what the payment freed them to exercise.

The check did not create pasture out of nothing. It lowered the risk of converting pasture the farm already had.

A third principle is about sequence.

Adding compliance conditions on non-enrolled land after enrollment began was an attempt to close a gap the original contracts had left open.

The attempt was real. The gap was structural. Closing it halfway, after the checks were already flowing, is not the same as deciding, at the bid, which acres outside the boundary a program is willing to watch.
