German lawmakers passed the Renewable Energy Sources Act in two thousand to accelerate wind, solar, and biomass after years of slow progress under earlier support schemes. They had watched Denmark's early wind programs and wanted a reliable mechanism that would guarantee investors a fixed price for every kilowatt-hour fed into the grid.

The goal was straightforward. Replace fossil and nuclear generation with renewables as quickly as possible while keeping the lights on. Wholesale electricity prices were high enough, at the time, that adding power with zero marginal cost was expected to lower consumer bills over the long run.

Policymakers also believed the nuclear phase-out agreed after Fukushima would be covered by the growing renewable fleet.

Claudia Kemfert, then at the German Institute for Economic Research, later noted that the missing piece was an adequate price on carbon dioxide. The architects focused on deployment volume rather than on the relative operating costs of the remaining thermal plants.

An objection often raised later is whether those lawmakers should have foreseen the interaction with the European Union Emissions Trading System, the ETS. The ETS price had been designed separately and was expected to tighten on its own schedule. In practice the two instruments operated on different timelines and different price signals.

The feed-in tariff system expanded rapidly after two thousand and again after two thousand nine, when solar tariffs were still generous. By twenty thirteen renewables supplied one hundred fifty-two point four billion kilowatt-hours, up from far lower figures a decade earlier, and twenty-four point one percent of gross electricity.

Proponents pointed to falling technology costs and rising public acceptance. Skeptics inside the utility sector warned that the merit-order effect would eventually push conventional plants out of the money, but few predicted which plants would be displaced first.

The ETS price had already fallen below ten euros per tonne by twenty eleven and hovered near seven euros in twenty thirteen. At the same time, cheap United States coal displaced by domestic shale gas flooded European markets. These price signals were visible in day-ahead markets but were not part of the original renewable-support design.

A further complication was that lignite plants, once built, had very low variable costs because the fuel sat in open-pit mines next to the stations. Even modest wholesale price drops left those stations profitable while gas plants with pipeline fuel costs did not.

Agora Energiewende's April twenty fourteen report documented what followed. A fuel switch from gas to coal inside Germany and in neighboring countries. Renewables have zero marginal cost, so renewables set the clearing price in more hours and drove wholesale prices down. Gas plants, with higher fuel costs, became unprofitable first and reduced output.

Lignite and hard-coal plants, whose fuel and carbon costs were lower, remained in the money and increased generation.

Patrick Graichen of Agora Energiewende told reporters that German coal plants were displacing gas plants not only domestically but also in the Netherlands. Carbon-dioxide emissions rose in both twenty twelve and twenty thirteen despite the renewable growth. The nuclear phase-out itself did not drive the increase.

Agora's analysis showed that renewable additions had fully offset the lost nuclear generation. The second-order effect was record electricity exports, much of that power coal-fired, which further raised domestic emissions. Economists noted that the ETS price was simply too low to change the relative cost of lignite versus gas.

The nuclear phase-out gap does not, on the numbers, explain the coal surge. Renewables covered that gap. The additional coal burn came from the changed dispatch order among the remaining thermal fleet.

The third-order effect was that utilities kept older lignite units online longer because those units still cleared the market, delaying any early retirement that higher carbon prices might have triggered.

By late twenty fourteen the picture began to shift. Renewables reached twenty-five point eight percent of gross generation and became the single largest source for the first time, ahead of lignite at twenty-five point six percent. Total emissions fell roughly five percent that year as milder weather and further renewable growth reduced coal and gas burn.

Policymakers responded with the twenty fourteen reform of the Renewable Energy Sources Act, which slowed tariff digression and introduced auctions. Patrick Graichen argued that a tighter emissions cap or a national carbon price floor would still be needed to force coal reductions. No immediate reversal of the feed-in tariff occurred.

The system evolved toward market-based procurement while the underlying merit-order dynamic remained. The reform addressed deployment speed but left the carbon-price gap intact, so the same relative-cost logic continued to favor lignite whenever gas prices stayed high.

When a support rule rewards only the volume of zero-marginal-cost generation without adjusting the carbon price that governs dispatch, the plants with the lowest running costs among the remaining fleet keep operating. The same price drop that makes renewables competitive can also extend the life of the cheapest fossil plants if their carbon cost stays low.

Any new clean-energy mandate should therefore be paired with an explicit adjustment to the relative cost of the dirtiest remaining resources, or the displacement will favor those resources over cleaner but more expensive ones.

Capacity markets pay for availability, and clean-energy standards pay for clean megawatt-hours, yet neither instrument automatically checks which remaining plant becomes more profitable once zero-marginal-cost power floods the stack.
