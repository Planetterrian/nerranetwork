Federal agencies and regional boosters in the 1930s saw untamed rivers as both threat and
wasted resource. The Bureau of Reclamation, working under the new deal, set out to build storage
reservoirs that would irrigate arid land, generate cheap hydroelectricity, and reduce the devastating
floods that had repeatedly inundated the Columbia and Klamath Basins. Engineers calculated that holding
back spring runoff in large reservoirs would let water be released steadily through turbines during
peak demand periods, turning seasonal river flow into a reliable supply of power measured in megawatts.
President Franklin Roosevelt personally championed Grand Cooley Dam on the Columbia. He argued that public
power would modernize rural economies and lift families out of poverty. At the time, salmon runs
appeared inexhaustible. State and federal fisheries managers believed fish ladders or hatcheries
could offset any losses from new barriers. The overriding calculation was straightforward.
Stored water and falling water could be converted into measurable economic growth for a region still
recovering from the depression. Construction began on Bonneville Dam in 1933 and on Grand Cooley in 1933.
Both were completed before the end of World War II. By 1942, Grand Cooley alone was producing
more electricity than any other single installation in the United States, and irrigating more than
half a million acres of the Columbia Basin. Similar projects followed on the Klamath River.
The Bureau of Reclamation completed Copco No. 1 in 1918 and later added JC Boyle,
Copco No. 2 and Iron Gate. Early reports from the 1940s and 1950s documented rising farm output
and falling flood damages. Local newspapers hailed the dams as permanent monuments to progress.
A handful of fisheries biologists noted declining upstream passage. Their concerns were treated
as secondary to national defense and agricultural expansion during and after the war.
Once the dams were in place, an adromous fish lost access to the cold, gravel bed tributaries where
they had historically spawned. On the Columbia System, estimates suggest that roughly 40% of
historic salmon and steelhead habitat was blocked by the mid-1960s. Adult fish that reached the base
of Grand Cooley or the L-Wah dams either turned back or exhausted themselves attempting to surmount
concrete faces never designed for passage. Juvenile out migrants faced reservoirs that slowed their
journey to the sea and increased predation. Over successive decades, commercial and tribal
harvests on the lower rivers fell sharply. By the 1990s, several Columbia River stocks were listed
under the Endangered Species Act. The causal chain ran through simple biology. Without access to
natal streams, each generation produced fewer returning adults. This collapsed the nutrient cycle
that had once carried marine derived nitrogen into mountain forests. Tribal communities whose seasonal
economies and ceremonial life had revolved around predictable salmon runs experienced, cultural as well
as economic loss that compounded across generations. By the late 1980s, mounting legal pressure
from tribes and environmental groups, combined with the high cost of maintaining aging structures and
operating fish hatcheries with limited success, shifted the policy conversation. Congress authorized
removal of the two L-Wah River dams in 1992. Deconstruction began in 2011 and was largely complete by
2014. On the climate, a 2016 agreement led to the drawdown of the four lower dams beginning in 2023.
This became the largest river restoration project attempted in the United States.
Early monitoring shows increased spawning activity above the former dam sites.
Full recovery of historic run sizes will take decades and remains uncertain.
The financial cost of removal, more than $300 million for the L-Wah project alone,
now appears on federal and state balance sheets that once recorded only construction and power
revenues. Infrastructure decisions embed ecological debts that surface only after the original
builders have left office, and the surrounding economy has reorganized around the new river regime.
When the full chain of effects, from blocked migration to lost nutrients to cultural disruption,
finally becomes visible, reversal can require resources comparable to the original construction.
The practical implication is that any large intervention should carry an explicit
funded mechanism for later reassessment, rather than assuming benefits will remain net positive
indefinitely. The question for today's planners is which current systems built with equally
