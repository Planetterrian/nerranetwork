The Royal Navy cured scurvy with lemon juice in seventeen ninety-five, then quietly lost the remedy as steamships and limes replaced the original practice.

In April nineteen twelve, on the Ross Ice Shelf, the last supporting party turned back from Robert Falcon Scott’s polar party. They watched the men continue south already showing the tell-tale stiffness and bleeding gums of scurvy. The expedition carried standardized lime juice issued by the Royal Navy, yet the disease still appeared.

A cure that had once made long voyages safe had become unreliable without anyone noticing the change. What stands out here is how the symptoms emerged on a journey whose planners had every reason to believe the official ration would prevent them.

The stiffness in the joints and the bleeding gums were the same signs that had once emptied decks on sailing ships, yet the men had followed the standing orders without deviation.

In seventeen forty-seven, Scottish naval surgeon James Lind conducted his classic trial aboard HMS Salisbury. He gave sailors suffering from scurvy either seawater, vinegar, or citrus fruit. The two men who received oranges and lemons recovered within days while the others did not. Lind’s seventeen fifty-three Treatise on the Scurvy recommended fresh citrus as the most effective remedy then known.

By the seventeen eighties, physician Gilbert Blane, serving as commissioner to the Sick and Hurt Board, pressed the Admiralty to adopt lemon juice as standard issue. The decision addressed a problem that had killed more sailors than combat. On some eighteenth-century fleets, scurvy claimed one-third of a crew before a voyage ended.

If a ship sailed with three hundred men, that meant roughly one hundred lives lost to the disease alone, before any shot was fired. Blane and his colleagues were working with the evidence available—repeated shipboard demonstrations that citrus worked—rather than any understanding of vitamins.

They could point to the men who had walked again after a few days of oranges and lemons, and that observable recovery was enough to justify changing the supply lists.

In seventeen ninety-five the Admiralty ordered lemon juice issued daily to all Royal Navy ships on foreign stations. By eighteen hundred the order applied fleet-wide. Surgeons recorded the juice in their logs as anti-scorbutic, and the incidence of scurvy dropped sharply on long deployments.

The measure coincided with Britain’s growing naval dominance during the Napoleonic Wars, reinforcing the perception that the policy was sound. Early success was measured simply by the absence of the disease on vessels that previously lost dozens of men. No laboratory existed to test the active principle, so the Navy preserved the practice as a standing order rather than a scientific theory.

One way to see the strength of that decision is to note how the daily ration became part of the normal routine: each man received his portion at the same time the water was issued, and the logs simply stopped listing scurvy cases where they had once filled pages.

Steam propulsion shortened typical voyage lengths after the eighteen forties, and scurvy became rare enough that routine observation of its prevention faded. The Navy shifted its citrus supply from Sicilian lemons to cheaper West Indian limes, whose juice contained roughly one-third the vitamin C of lemons.

If a daily lemon ration had once delivered on the order of thirty to forty milligrams of the active compound, the lime ration now supplied perhaps ten to fifteen milligrams under the same volume. The juice was stored for months in copper vats that further reduced potency through oxidation and metal contamination.

Copper ions speed the breakdown of the compound when air is present, and the vats were opened and resealed repeatedly during long storage. Because no sailor developed obvious scurvy on the shorter routes, the weaker preparation appeared equally effective.

The absence of new cases on the steam-powered runs served as confirmation that the system still worked, even though the material inside the casks had changed.

When longer expeditions resumed in the polar regions, the same lime juice failed to protect crews. Scott’s nineteen oh one to nineteen oh four and nineteen ten to nineteen twelve expeditions both recorded scorbutic symptoms despite following the official ration. Medical officers attributed the cases to tainted tinned meat or ptomaine poisoning rather than insufficient vitamin C.

Without knowledge of the underlying mechanism, the Navy could not recognize that its institutional memory had preserved a procedure whose active ingredient had quietly disappeared.

The men on the ice followed every step that had been written down, yet the chain that once connected the fruit on the dock to the health of the crew had been altered at two separate points: the choice of fruit and the method of storage.

Vitamin C was isolated in nineteen thirty-two, allowing researchers to confirm that the original lemon juice had supplied an essential nutrient the later lime juice did not. The Admiralty revised its anti-scorbutic provisions in the nineteen thirties, but the episode left no formal inquiry into how a working remedy had been degraded.

Modern navies and Antarctic programs now test ascorbic-acid levels directly. The episode remains a reference point in discussions of institutional knowledge loss whenever procedures outlive the conditions that made them reliable.

When a remedy succeeds without an explanation of why it works, any later change in materials, storage, or operating conditions can erode its effectiveness without immediate detection. Organizations therefore need mechanisms that preserve the underlying mechanism, not merely the checklist.

The same risk appears today whenever automated systems or regulatory routines replace earlier practices whose original rationale has been forgotten. How many current safety or health protocols rest on results that would no longer hold if the hidden variables shifted?
