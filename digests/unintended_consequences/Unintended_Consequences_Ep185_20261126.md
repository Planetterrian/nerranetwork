> **Morocco's drip irrigation subsidies for 550,000 hectares increased groundwater use instead of conserving it.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 2007 Morocco launched the National Program of Water Savings in Irrigation under the Green Morocco Plan, targeting modernization of roughly 550,000 hectares with drip systems. The program, supported by World Bank financing, sought to align water service with efficient technologies and claimed savings exceeding one billion cubic meters. Instead, total withdrawals from aquifers continued to rise as farmers expanded irrigated area and shifted to thirstier crops.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Marcel Kuper and colleagues at CIRAD traced the policy roots to the 1980s and 1990s, when state planners in large-scale schemes such as Tadla sought to stabilize medium-sized farms through surface-water delivery and industrial crops. By the mid-2000s, declining rainfall and growing demand had produced visible groundwater depletion, prompting officials to view drip conversion as the logical next step. The National Program of Water Savings in Irrigation, rolled out from 2007, offered subsidies that climbed from earlier levels of 60 percent to 80–100 percent for micro-irrigation equipment, wells, and reservoirs. Policymakers reasoned that higher application efficiency would reduce the volume of water needed per hectare while raising yields and rural incomes. The approach aligned with the broader Green Morocco Plan’s emphasis on agricultural intensification and export competitiveness. At the time, remote-sensing data and field studies had not yet quantified how farmers would respond once the new systems were in place. The decision therefore rested on the straightforward premise that technical efficiency gains would translate directly into lower basin-wide withdrawals. Officials had watched surface-water quotas shrink in dry years and concluded that replacing open-channel flooding with pressurized pipes, emitters, and on-farm reservoirs would stretch every cubic meter further without requiring new dams or transfers. They also expected the same capital grants to lift small and medium holdings into higher-value fruit and vegetable production, improving both food security and foreign-exchange earnings from exports.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Between 2007 and 2008 the government scaled subsidy rates sharply and streamlined approvals for turnkey projects that included ponds and private wells. By the end of 2008 roughly 163,000 hectares had been equipped, concentrated in fruit-tree zones of the Tadla perimeter and the El Haouz plain. Early monitoring recorded higher biomass per hectare and lower reported water deliveries at the scheme level, figures that program documents presented as evidence of conservation. The World Bank’s Large-Scale Irrigation Modernization Project, backed by a $150 million loan, reinforced the rollout by conditioning further support on collective conversion to drip. Local agricultural offices promoted the technology as a way for farmers to maintain production even when surface-water quotas fell short. A 2009 Irrigation Association paper noted that average installation costs reached about $5,725 per hectare, with 70 percent of projects also building on-farm reservoirs. Proponents highlighted the prospect of 20–50 percent water savings across the 550,000-hectare target. Few contemporaneous reports tracked whether the freed water stayed in the aquifer or was applied elsewhere. Application forms required only proof of equipment purchase and basic soil tests; no clause limited the number of additional hectares a recipient could bring under irrigation once the system was installed.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Studies published after the initial conversions documented the opposite outcome. An Agricultural Water Management paper from 2017 reported that aquifer overdraft in the affected basins reached one billion cubic meters per year even as drip coverage expanded. In the El Haouz perimeter, remote-sensing analysis funded by the Global Water Security and Sanitation Partnership showed that water consumption per hectare did not decline once drip systems were installed; instead, farmers increased crop density and planted more water-intensive varieties. Marcel Kuper’s 2012 analysis of forty years in the Tadla scheme found that larger and medium-sized holdings, which captured 36 percent of farms, used new access to groundwater to lease additional land and diversify into higher-value orchards. Because drip reduced surface-water losses that had previously recharged shallow aquifers, net recharge fell while private tubewell pumping rose. The 2017 CIRAD book chapter on North African cases traced how the same efficiency narrative persisted in policy documents despite the absence of basin-scale metering that would have revealed the expansion effect. Farmers who received 100 percent subsidies for wells and ponds faced no volumetric limit on extraction, so the marginal cost of adding irrigated area dropped. Second-order effects followed: informal land markets accelerated, smallholders without capital for wells rented out parcels, and deeper aquifers began to be tapped as shallow tables dropped. By the time the 2017 Elsevier study quantified the paradox, the pattern had already locked in across multiple overexploited basins. The arithmetic was straightforward once measured: if a hectare previously received 8,000 cubic meters under flood irrigation and now required only 5,000 under drip, the 3,000 cubic meters “saved” could irrigate another 0.6 hectares of the same crop or support a shift to citrus or vegetables that demanded 9,000 cubic meters per hectare. Without an overall extraction cap, that extra hectare or extra crop was planted. Larger operators captured most of the subsidy because they could finance the remaining 20 percent or the reservoir component; smaller farms stayed on surface water or rented land out, concentrating groundwater rights. As recharge from surface losses declined, the same volume of pumping lowered water tables faster, forcing some farmers to deepen wells and increasing energy costs that were partly offset by higher-value output. The policy therefore rewarded the visible metric—drip equipment installed—while leaving the unmeasured metric—total aquifer balance—unconstrained.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Subsequent World Bank and GWSP-supported evaluations in the 2020s acknowledged that measured water consumption had not fallen in line with earlier projections. The Large-Scale Irrigation Modernization Project continued, yet added components for monitoring actual evapotranspiration rather than relying on theoretical savings. No nationwide rollback of the subsidy structure occurred; instead, newer guidance emphasized pairing drip conversion with enforceable extraction permits. In the Tadla and El Haouz zones, some collective schemes began experimenting with collective well management, though enforcement remained uneven. Aquifer levels in the most intensively converted perimeters continued to decline through the early 2020s, prompting calls for volumetric pricing that had not been part of the original program design. Pilot programs now require satellite verification of cropped area alongside drip installation, but uptake of these paired rules has been slower than the original equipment grants.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When subsidies reward only the installation of efficient hardware without capping total withdrawals, the saved water tends to fund expansion rather than rest in the ground. In this case the measurable variable was equipment uptake, not aquifer balance, so the rule rewarded the appearance of conservation. Policymakers today can test whether any new efficiency program includes binding limits on aggregate extraction before funds are disbursed. How might current water-saving incentives in other arid regions avoid repeating that measurement gap?
