> **The Royal Navy cured scurvy with lemon juice in 1795, then quietly lost the remedy as steamships and limes replaced the original practice.**
---
### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In January 1912, high on the Antarctic plateau, the last supporting party turned back from Robert Falcon Scott’s polar party; on the long march home its leader was brought down by the tell-tale stiffness and bleeding gums of scurvy. The expedition carried standardized lime juice issued by the Royal Navy, yet the disease still appeared. A cure that had once made long voyages safe had become unreliable without anyone noticing the change.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
In 1747, Scottish naval surgeon James Lind conducted his classic trial aboard HMS Salisbury, giving sailors suffering from scurvy either seawater, vinegar, or citrus fruit. The two men who received oranges and lemons recovered within days while the others did not. Lind’s 1753 Treatise on the Scurvy recommended fresh citrus as the most effective remedy then known. By the 1780s, physician Gilbert Blane, serving as commissioner to the Sick and Hurt Board, pressed the Admiralty to adopt lemon juice as standard issue. The decision addressed a problem that had killed more sailors than combat: on some long eighteenth-century voyages, scurvy claimed a large fraction of a crew before the voyage ended. Blane and his colleagues were working with the evidence available—repeated shipboard demonstrations that citrus worked—rather than any understanding of vitamins.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
In 1795 the Admiralty ordered lemon juice issued daily to all Royal Navy ships on foreign stations; by 1800 the order applied fleet-wide. Surgeons recorded the juice in their logs as “anti-scorbutic,” and the incidence of scurvy dropped sharply on long deployments. The measure coincided with Britain’s growing naval dominance during the Napoleonic Wars, reinforcing the perception that the policy was sound. Early success was measured simply by the absence of the disease on vessels that previously lost dozens of men. No laboratory existed to test the active principle, so the Navy preserved the practice as a standing order rather than a scientific theory.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Steam propulsion shortened typical voyage lengths after the 1840s, and scurvy became rare enough that routine observation of its prevention faded. The Navy shifted its citrus supply from Mediterranean lemons to cheaper West Indian limes, whose juice contained substantially less vitamin C than the lemons it replaced. The juice was stored for long periods and passed through copper equipment, which further reduced its potency through oxidation and metal contamination. Because no sailor developed obvious scurvy on the shorter routes, the weaker preparation appeared equally effective. When longer expeditions resumed in the polar regions, the same lime juice failed to protect crews. Scott’s 1901–1904 and 1910–1912 expeditions both recorded scorbutic symptoms despite following the official ration; medical officers attributed the cases to tainted tinned meat or ptomaine poisoning rather than insufficient vitamin C. Without knowledge of the underlying mechanism, the Navy could not recognize that its institutional memory had preserved a procedure whose active ingredient had quietly disappeared.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Vitamin C was identified as the anti-scorbutic factor in 1932, allowing researchers to confirm that the original lemon juice had supplied an essential nutrient the later lime juice did not. The Admiralty revised its anti-scorbutic provisions in the 1930s, but the episode left no formal inquiry into how a working remedy had been degraded. Modern navies and Antarctic programs now test ascorbic-acid levels directly. The episode remains a reference point in discussions of institutional knowledge loss whenever procedures outlive the conditions that made them reliable.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a remedy succeeds without an explanation of why it works, any later change in materials, storage, or operating conditions can erode its effectiveness without immediate detection. Organizations therefore need mechanisms that preserve the underlying mechanism, not merely the checklist. The same risk appears today whenever automated systems or regulatory routines replace earlier practices whose original rationale has been forgotten. How many current safety or health protocols rest on results that would no longer hold if the hidden variables shifted?
