Finland's 2017 basic income trial raised part-time work without lifting full-time employment because the flat payment shranked the extra reward for earning more. In early 2017, 2000 unemployed Finns began receiving an unconditional monthly payment of 560 euros from the Social Insurance Institution of Finland, known as Kella. The designers had hoped the removal of means-testing and reporting requirements would free people to accept any work that appeared.
Instead, the same payment structure that eliminated penalties for small earnings also reduced the net financial gain from moving into higher paying or full-time roles.
Consider a recipient who finds a part-time position paying a few hundred euros above the flat amount. Under the old tapered system, the benefit would have declined gradually, preserving some incremental reward for each additional hour.
Yet the new flat line meant every extra euro earned stayed fully with the recipient only after the original benefit had already been replaced entirely by wages.
That arithmetic quietly reversed the incentive gradient once earnings crossed a modest threshold, leaving recipients with a stable but lower-powered reason to push further.
The result was visible in administrative records as more part-time days worked and no measurable lift in full-time transitions.

Policy makers at KELA and in the center-right coalition believed that the existing unemployment benefit system created high effective marginal tax rates whenever a recipient took even modest part-time work.
An unconditional payment, they reasoned, would remove that disincentive, simplified administration, and let people test jobs without fear of losing all support.
The approach drew on earlier Nordic discussions about basic income as a way to adapt welfare to a more fluid labor market while preserving dignity and reducing bureaucracy.
Those discussions had emphasized that lengthy claim forms and abrupt benefit cliffs often discourage small steps back into employment.
By replacing the whole apparatus with a single monthly transfer that continued regardless of earnings, the designers expected individuals to weigh job offers on their own merits rather than on how the offer would interact with benefit withdrawal rules.
They also anticipated lower administrative costs and less stigma. Parliament approved a two-year randomized trial that ran from January 2017 through December 2018.
A randomly selected group of 2,000 people aged 25 to 58 who had been receiving unemployment benefits at the end of 2016 were moved onto the flat 560-year-op payment.
A matched control group remained on the conventional system.
Early administrative data showed that recipients reported less stress about paperwork and were quicker to accept short-term or part-time assignments.
Proponents in the Ministry of Social Affairs highlighted these early signs as evidence that the simplified structure was working as intended.
The random assignment itself was meant to isolate the effect of the payment rule from other labor market variables.
Because the payment arrived automatically and without conditions, case workers spent less time verifying hours or recalculating entitlements.
At the same time, the trial's limited scale meant it did not alter wage levels, vacancy postings or regional job availability.
When researchers later compared the two groups using administrative records, the treatment group showed a clear increase in part-time employment days.
At the same time, the difference in transitions to full-time work was statistically insignificant.
The flat payment meant that any additional euro earned above the benefit level produced a smaller net gain than under the tapered unemployment system.
Recipients therefore had less financial reason to move from stable part-time roles into positions that demanded more commitment or carried greater risk of failure.
Imagine two otherwise identical workers.
One still on the old system faces a benefit reduction that offsets only part of each new wage euro.
The other keeps the full 560 euros on top of whatever wages arrive.
Yet once wages exceed that fixed amount, the marginal reward per extra hour is simply the wage itself.
That difference in slope matters most precisely when a worker is deciding whether to add another shift or pursue a more demanding full-time opening.
Second order affects followed. Some participants remained attached to low intensity work that fit around the fixed payment.
Employers and sectors that rely on part-time labor found a steadier pool of workers less motivated to seek full-time alternatives.
The design had removed one barrier, the fear of losing benefits, yet left untouched others such as skill mismatches, regional job availability and childcare constraints.
After the trial concluded, the center-right government did not extend the unconditional model nationwide.
Subsequent administrations shifted focus toward activation measures that combined modest supplements with stronger job search requirements.
The finish experiment nevertheless influenced later European discussions, prompting designers and other pilots to test tapered or earnings disregard versions rather than purely flat payments.
No large-scale replication of the original flat structure has been attempted in Finland since.
The decision to move away from the flat design reflected the evidence that part-time attachment had risen while full-time movement had not.
Later pilots elsewhere incorporated earnings disregard that preserved some taper above a threshold.
Those adjustments themselves introduced new administrative layers.
Incentive structures always reveal their marginal effects once the payment rule is fixed.
When a transfer is truly unconditional, it stabilizes whatever level of work effort recipients find easiest to maintain.
Anyone designing income support today therefore needs to model not only the removal of penalties but also the altered rewards that remain once the new baseline is in place.
The finished case shows that the arithmetic of marginal gain operates even when the payment itself is simple.
