We're looking at a case today where the people involved were doing their best with the information they had.
D-A-R-E placed uniformed officers in classrooms to teach millions of children to refuse drugs,
yet repeated evaluations found the program produced little measurable change in behavior.
In the spring of 1984, Los Angeles Police Department officers stood at the front
of 5th-grade classrooms holding plastic bags of marijuana and cocaine while students recited pledges
to stay drug-free. The officers were not simply displaying the substances,
they were walking through the physical characteristics of each one,
naming the plants and chemicals involved, and describing how the materials were packaged and used.
Students then repeated a pledge that framed refusal as a personal commitment made in front of an
authority figure. The scene felt concrete and memorable because it replaced abstract warnings
with visible objects and spoken words repeated in unison. The program, called Drug Abuse Resistance
Education, had been launched the previous year by LAPD Chief Darrell Gates in partnership with the
Los Angeles Unified School District. It was designed to reduce youth drug use through direct
authoritative instruction. Instead, the same approach that felt intuitively right to parents,
and police would later be shown by multiple studies to leave actual drug use rates essentially unchanged.
D-A-R-E emerged in 1983 amid the crack cocaine surge and the broader federal war on drugs.
Gates and school officials believed that placing trained officers in elementary and middle schools
would give children credible first-hand information about the dangers of substances before peer pressure
took hold. At the time, most anti-drug efforts relied on occasional assemblies or pamphlets.
Embedding officers for 17 weekly sessions seemed like a logical escalation that combined law
enforcement authority with classroom access. Policymakers viewed the program as a preventative
public health measure rather than simple enforcement. Early anecdotal reports from participating
schools suggested students were engaged and parents felt reassured. The decision reflected the
prevailing belief that clear, repeated messaging from respected adults could alter risk behavior
in much the same way safety campaigns had reduced drunk driving. Within two years, the curriculum had
spread beyond Los Angeles to dozens of California districts. By 1988, it operated in more than
1,000 US communities. Federal and state grants covered officer training and materials, while local
police departments supplied personnel on duty time. That funding structure meant school districts
did not have to reallocate their own classroom budgets to bring officers in, which removed a common
financial objection that might otherwise have slowed adoption. Proponents pointed to high
enrollment numbers, reaching an estimated 26 million students by the mid-1990s, and to visible symbols
such as graduation certificates and red t-shirts that signaled community commitment. A few early
internal reviews noted short-term gains in students stated intentions to avoid drugs, which program
advocates cited as proof of concept. Skeptics inside public health circles warned that the curriculum's
heavy emphasis on drug identification and refusal scripts lacked interactive skill practice.
Those cautions received little traction while political support remained strong. Large-scale
evaluations beginning in the early 1990s consistently found no statistically significant reduction in
students' later drug use. A 1994 meta-analysis by Ennit and colleagues published in the American
Journal of Public Health, reviewed eight methodologically sound studies and concluded that D-A-R-E's
effects on behavior were negligible. Some smaller studies even recorded modest increases in curiosity
about substances after students received detailed descriptions of drug effects and paraphernalia.
The causal pathway appears straightforward. The curriculum relied primarily on
didactic lectures and fear-based messaging, rather than on practicing refusal skills under realistic
social pressure, an approach already known to be less effective in behavior change research.
Because the program consumed classroom time and local budgets, schools sometimes deferred other
prevention activities that later proved more promising. Politically, the program's popularity among
parents and elected officials created a feedback loop in which cancellation carried higher immediate
costs than continuation, even after negative findings accumulated. Over time, the gap between measured
outcomes and public perception widened. Many communities retained D-A-R-E long after federal agencies
had quietly stopped listing it as an evidence-based model. By the early 2000s, mounting evidence prompted
several districts to drop the original curriculum. In 2009, the D-A-R-E organization itself adopted a
new evidence-informed program called Keeping It Real, developed at Pennsylvania State University and
built around interactive storytelling and peer-resistant strategies rather than officer lectures.
The transition preserved the visible police school partnership while altering the pedagogical core.
Some communities that had already invested in training continued the older format for several
additional years. Today, Keeping It Real operates in a smaller number of sites and rigorous evaluations
of the revised program show modest positive effects on decision-making skills, though at a far
smaller scale than the original D-A-R-E reached. An intervention's visibility and political durability
can remain high, even when its measured impact on the target behavior is low. Early adoption of
rigorous, independent outcome evaluation, rather than reliance on enrollment numbers or participant
satisfaction, helps surface ineffective designs before they become entrenched. When an activity
produces clear organizational or symbolic benefits for the adults involved, those benefits can
decouple from results for the intended beneficiaries. The D-A-R-E story therefore raises a practical
question for any new prevention or education effort. What independent data would be required to continue,
modify, or retire the program, and who is positioned to act on that data before sunk costs and community
