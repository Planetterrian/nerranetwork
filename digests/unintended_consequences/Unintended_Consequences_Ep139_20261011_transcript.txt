Fisheries managers for South Georgia and scientists working through the commission for the conservation
of Antarctic marine living resources faced a clear problem in the early 1990s.
Unlicensed vessels were stripping toothfish stocks around South Georgia at rates no one
could track.
The remedy they chose was straightforward.
By granting a small number of vessels the sole right to fish inside the zone, they
hoped to create both accountability and economic incentive for those operators to protect
the resource.
At the time the approach matched the best available thinking on limited entry fisheries.
It had already shown results in other high-value whitefish grounds.
The managers were not trying to reshape marine mammal behavior.
They were trying to replace chaos with a known list of permitted boats.
They reasoned that if only a handful of operators held the rights, each would have a stake
in keeping the total catch within sustainable bounds.
Their future income depended on the same stock remaining viable year after year.
The licenses took effect in 1993 and 1994.
They restricted the fishery to a handful of licensed longliners that had to report
catches and positions. Early reports showed the hoped-for drop in unlicensed activity and a modest
rebound in average fish size. Proponents inside the South Georgia fisheries authority pointed to rising
catch per unit effort figures in the first two seasons as proof the system worked. A few observers
noted that the permitted vessels now work the same narrow shelf edges every year. The concern stayed
on paper because the immediate conservation target fewer pirate boats had been met. The arithmetic
take looked favorable on the page. Fewer vessels meant lower total effort. The reported
landings could be divided by the known number of hooks to produce a single number that appeared
to rise. That single number became the metric everyone watched, even as the spatial footprint
of the effort stayed fixed on the same few square kilometers of seabed. The repeated presence
of the same vessels in the same Orca feeding grounds changed the whales options. Within a few
Few seasons, local pods learned to follow the long lines and strip hook toothfish before
the gear could be retrieved.
Catch rates for the license boats fell sharply.
To reach fish, the orcas could not easily take, skippers moved to deeper sets and switch
to heavier lines and larger anchors.
Those changes increased the chance that an orca calf swimming near the surface would become
entangled and drowned.
Population surveys conducted in later years recorded fewer calves reaching independence
in the affected groups.
The original policy had succeeded in keeping unlicensed vessels away, yet it had also turned
a mobile dispersed fishery into a fixed, predictable food source.
The license removed the option to fish elsewhere inside the zone, so every boat returned to
the same lines day after day.
The orcas, facing a repeatable signal of floats and bait, discovered they could reduce
their own search time.
The fishers, seeing their daily yield drop, added weight and depth that the calves could
not escape.
The second order effect was therefore not simply lower orca numbers.
It was a change in the age structure of the local pods.
The animals most likely to investigate the new food source where the younger ones
still learning to hunt.
The third order effect appeared when the same pods began to associate all vessel noise
with an easy meal.
This increased the frequency of interactions even on days when the gear had already
been modified.
By the early 2000s, researchers and some of the licensed captains themselves began documenting
the pattern of calf entanglements. The commission responded with seasonal area closures and requirements
for acoustic deterrence. Those measures reduced but did not eliminate the problem. A later shift toward
even deeper pot gear for toothfish removed the long line interaction for orcas, yet it introduced
new questions about seafloor impact. Today the South Georgia toothfish fishery operates under
tighter spatial rules. Local Orca groups show signs of behavioral recovery, though full recruitment
rates have not returned to pre-license levels. The pot gear transition itself illustrates how one
fix can see the next uncertainty. While the surface lines disappeared, the heavier traps now sit on
the same concentrated grounds. Any future change in crab or other bycatch species could again alter
the incentive structure for the remaining vessels. When Argentina limited the number of vessels
allowed near South Georgia, it rewarded predictable daily presence in the same feeding zones rather
than movement across wider grounds. That predictability gave Orca's a reliable new foraging
tactic and gave fishers a reason to adopt gear that increased calf mortality. Anyone writing
a new limited entry rule today can test whether the permitted activity will become spatially fixed.
They can also test whether the target species or its predators can adapt faster than the managers
can adjust the rules. The question is whether the next license design will still assume that
that concentrating effort leaves the rest of the ecosystem unchanged.
