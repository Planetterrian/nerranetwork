> **Spain built high-speed AVE rail to replace short-haul flights between its biggest cities, only to watch the connecting traffic that sustained dozens of smaller regional airports disappear.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the 2000s, the first full AVE corridors between Madrid and Barcelona and Madrid and Seville began carrying passengers who had previously flown those routes. Airlines responded by discontinuing the flights. With the connecting traffic gone, smaller regional airports that had relied on those hub connections saw passenger numbers fall sharply enough that several closed, leaving cities without direct air service to the rest of the country.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
Spanish planners and successive governments turned to high-speed rail because the country’s geography and growing economy made the existing mix of roads and conventional rail feel increasingly inadequate for moving people between major centers. The goal was to create fast, reliable links that would reduce reliance on domestic aviation for the busiest corridors while lowering overall travel times and emissions. At the time, Madrid–Barcelona and Madrid–Seville were among Europe’s busiest short-haul air routes, and shifting those passengers onto trains appeared to be a straightforward substitution that would free airport capacity for longer international flights. Decision-makers worked with traffic forecasts that treated the two modes as direct competitors rather than parts of an interconnected network. The investment also aligned with broader European Union priorities that favored rail for distances under roughly 500 kilometers. In that context, building the AVE lines looked like a rational, forward-looking infrastructure choice.

Planners could point to the arithmetic of passenger volumes on those two dense corridors and see clear room for a faster surface alternative that would also cut fuel burn per trip. They could also see that conventional rail and road links already existed but were too slow to capture the time-sensitive business traveler. Adding high-speed track therefore read as an incremental upgrade that would improve the existing system rather than replace it entirely. The assumption that airlines would simply redeploy aircraft to other routes or international services followed logically from the view that the removed flights were self-contained city-pair operations. No one needed to imagine that the same success would ripple outward and change the viability calculations for dozens of thinner routes that depended on feed through the major hubs.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
Construction proceeded corridor by corridor, with the Madrid–Seville line opening first and the Madrid–Barcelona line following later in the decade. Early ridership on the new services exceeded many projections, and airlines quickly removed aircraft from those city pairs. Proponents pointed to the trains’ punctuality and city-center-to-city-center times as clear evidence that the policy was working. A few observers noted that the rail lines would change how airlines scheduled their remaining flights, but those warnings received limited attention amid the visible success on the main routes. Regional airports continued to publish traffic statistics that still included feeder traffic from the soon-to-be-discontinued flights.

The Madrid–Seville AVE began service in 1992, and the Madrid–Barcelona line followed in 2008 after years of construction that included new tunnels and viaducts engineered for 300 km/h operation. Passenger counts on the Seville corridor rose steadily once the trains offered three-hour journeys that competed directly with the one-hour flight plus airport procedures. Airlines responded by cutting frequencies and then withdrawing aircraft types that had been dedicated to the route, freeing them for other markets or retirement. Because the trains ran from city-center stations, the time advantage was even larger than the raw speed suggested, reinforcing the perception that the substitution was clean and complete.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
Once the airlines withdrew from the Madrid–Barcelona and Madrid–Seville air routes, the network effects spread outward. Regional airports that had depended on passengers changing planes in Madrid or Barcelona for onward connections lost that traffic almost overnight. Without enough remaining demand, carriers reduced or eliminated service to those smaller fields, and several facilities recorded sustained drops in movements that eventually led to closure. Cities without an AVE station found themselves farther from the national transport spine than before, because the air connections that had previously compensated for their location were no longer viable. The infrastructure had not simply substituted one mode for another; it had reconfigured the entire topology of air service by removing the economic justification for thin routes that depended on hub feed. Second-order effects included reduced frequency on other domestic legs and fewer options for travelers who needed to reach secondary cities on the same day. Third-order effects appeared in local economies that had counted on airport-related employment and visitor access. The causal chain began with the success of the AVE in capturing the densest markets, which in turn made those markets unavailable to airlines, which in turn removed the traffic that kept marginal airports operational.

Consider the arithmetic at a typical regional airport: if 40 percent of its departures had been feeder flights whose passengers connected onward from Madrid, the sudden disappearance of those connecting passengers could drop total movements below the level needed to cover fixed costs for ground handling, security staffing, and runway maintenance. Airlines, facing their own yield calculations, then pruned the remaining direct flights because load factors fell. The result was not a gradual decline but a step-change loss that made continued operation uneconomic for both the airport operator and the carrier. Cities that had never been on the AVE map experienced this contraction even though they had never been promised high-speed rail; their previous connectivity had rested on the very short-haul flights the AVE had rendered unprofitable.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
Once the pattern of airport closures and lost connectivity became visible in traffic data, regional governments and transport authorities began examining ways to restore links through subsidized routes or improved bus and conventional rail feeders. Some airports received public-service obligation support to maintain minimal service, yet these measures could not fully recreate the previous network density. In places where new AVE extensions later reached additional cities, the same substitution dynamic repeated on a smaller scale. The overall system stabilized with a smaller number of airports handling most domestic traffic and a rail network that serves the largest corridors efficiently, though cities off the high-speed map continue to experience thinner connectivity than existed before the AVE era.

Public-service obligation contracts have kept a handful of routes alive, but they require ongoing budget allocations that were not part of the original AVE business case. Where conventional rail or bus connections were strengthened, travel times lengthened again for passengers who once made same-day connections through Madrid. The pattern suggests that once the hub-and-spoke structure of air service contracts, restoring equivalent reach demands either sustained subsidy or acceptance of lower frequency and convenience.

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
When a new infrastructure mode captures the highest-volume routes, it can remove the economic foundation that supports thinner connecting services across an entire network. Treating one mode as a simple replacement for another overlooks how carriers and passengers actually combine modes to reach secondary destinations. Planners who model only the direct corridor risk underestimating the system-wide topology changes that follow. How might today’s proposals for new rail or air capacity account for the feeder and connecting traffic that currently keeps smaller facilities viable?
