### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Strength decline starts at age 35 in exercisers — ScienceDaily**
   Researchers tracked fitness and strength metrics across decades in a cohort followed for 47 years. The data showed the downturn begins around age 35 and accelerates later in life. Starting exercise in adulthood still produced measurable gains in physical performance despite the overall trajectory. The study measured objective markers of strength and endurance rather than self-reports. Researchers found that this decline accelerates as people get older, but starting to exercise in adulthood can still improve physical performance. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261014000912.htm)

2. **Ascension Island planting turned volcanic rock into rainforest — Phys.org**
   Two British scientists organized seed plantings from empire botanical gardens starting in the 19th century. Five decades of deliberate planting converted a sun-scorched site with only a few fern species into a humid cloud forest. Researchers from Pompeu Fabra University spent a month on the island collecting historical records, ecological data, and soil microbiome samples. The team is now building models of the reverse process to test whether other degraded ecosystems can be restored. Víctor Maull led the work at the Complex Systems Laboratory under Ricard Solé, with Nastassia Knödlseder handling post-doctoral contributions on microbiology. Historical nautical charts, logbooks and letters documented the original engineering steps. Soil microbiome analysis is being completed with the BioFun Lab in Seville to identify rapid nutrient-driven changes. The island now shows sharp transitions from rainforest to desert conditions within a 10-minute walk. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-ascension-island-lush-cloud-forest.html)

3. **Microplastics generate hydroxyl radicals at water interfaces — Nature**
   Commodity microplastics spontaneously produce reactive oxygen species in water, with output rising alongside interfacial hydrophobicity. Ab initio calculations link the effect to partial solvation and electrostatic forces that favor hydroxide oxidation at the plastic-water boundary. The process occurs across common polymers including polyethylene and polypropylene. The findings indicate direct implications for biomolecular adsorption and cellular damage. The study examined six widely used plastics and found the radical generation scales with surface properties rather than bulk chemistry. Hydroxide oxidation becomes energetically more favourable at increasingly hydrophobic interfaces through combined partial solvation and electrostatic effects. Source: [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11141-y?error=cookies_not_supported&code=b0ecb827-28b7-40e9-a8d3-60ef01f3cd44)

4. **Consumers seek clearer information on precision fermentation foods — Phys.org**
   Qualitative research from the EIT Food Consumer Observatory found many people remain curious about precision fermentation products but need specific answers before trying them. The study was released ahead of World Food Day and focused on questions around safety, taste, and production methods. Companies that address these points directly may accelerate adoption. The work examined consumer responses to novel food technologies and identified clear information gaps that currently limit willingness to taste results. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-consumers-clearer-precision-fermentation.html)

5. **Gene therapy restores hearing in children born deaf — WLWT**
   Cincinnati Children's doctors used a new gene therapy approach that enabled some children born deaf to regain hearing. The treatment targets the underlying genetic cause rather than providing external amplification. Early results show functional improvement in auditory responses. The work focuses on restoring natural hearing pathways in patients with specific genetic forms of deafness. Source: [wlwt.com](https://www.wlwt.com/article/new-gene-therapy-cincinnati-childrens-restore-hearing-deaf/74139390)

6. **Report outlines options to cut on-farm emissions in Australia — Phys.org**
   CSIRO identified a growing set of practical measures already available to Australian producers for lowering agricultural greenhouse gas output. The analysis also flags additional research opportunities for further reductions. The report emphasizes solutions that fit existing farm operations. The findings highlight both immediate actions and longer-term research needs for emissions reduction across Australian agriculture. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-investment-priorities-emissions-agriculture.html)

7. **Aircraft exhaust particles gain oxidative potential over time — Phys.org**
   Laboratory tests indicate fine particulate matter from aircraft combustion increases its ability to trigger oxidative stress after aging in the atmosphere. The particles penetrate deep into the respiratory tract and promote inflammation. Researchers measured the change in oxidative potential under controlled conditions. The work examined how atmospheric aging alters the harmful properties of these combustion-derived particles. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-fine-particles-aircraft-exhaust-lab.html)

8. **Urbanization weakens latitudinal diversity gradient in birds — Phys.org**
   McGill University researchers documented that city development reduces the usual increase in bird species richness toward the tropics. The study compared urban and non-urban sites across multiple latitudes. The effect narrows the classic biodiversity pattern observed in natural habitats. The research demonstrates that urbanization disrupts one of Earth's most consistent ecological gradients. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-urbanization-bigger-toll-bird-diversity.html)

### Planetterrian Spotlight
A 47-year longitudinal study tracked objective strength and fitness measures and located the first clear downturn at age 35. The decline continued even in participants who maintained regular exercise, yet those who began activity as adults still recorded gains relative to their prior baseline. Because the inflection appears earlier than many expect, routine strength assessments in the 30s could flag opportunities for targeted activity adjustments before losses compound. The data also show the rate of decline itself speeds up with advancing age, underscoring the value of sustained loading. One open question is whether specific training variables, such as resistance volume or recovery intervals, alter the slope after 35. The study design followed the same individuals across nearly five decades, providing rare within-person evidence rather than cross-sectional snapshots.

### Science Deep Dive: How microplastics generate reactive oxygen species
Most people assume everyday plastics remain chemically inert once they enter the environment or the body. Commodity microplastics instead drive spontaneous hydroxyl radical formation at their surfaces when suspended in water. The reaction scales with the hydrophobicity of the polymer-water interface and tracks closely with measured electrostatic properties. Hydroxide oxidation becomes energetically favored under partial solvation conditions, according to the ab initio calculations reported in the study. This interfacial chemistry occurs across widely used polymers and supplies a direct route to oxidative stress on nearby biomolecules. The same process can promote protein aggregation and membrane damage in exposed cells. Future material design that reduces surface hydrophobicity or alters interfacial charge may lower this unintended reactivity. The research examined polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, poly(methyl methacrylate) and polyethylene terephthalate, all common in consumer products. Radical output increased as the plastic surface became more hydrophobic, consistent with stronger electrostatic effects at the boundary. Partial solvation of hydroxide ions at these interfaces lowers the energy barrier for electron transfer, enabling the spontaneous formation of hydroxyl radicals without external energy input. Because microplastics are now detected in human tissues and most ecosystems, the surface chemistry identified here offers one mechanistic link between plastic exposure and observed cellular stress responses. The study used a combination of spectroscopic measurements and computational modeling to establish the structure-activity relationship, ruling out bulk polymer composition as the primary driver. Researchers note that the same interfacial properties also influence how proteins adsorb and aggregate on plastic surfaces, extending the implications beyond radical generation alone. Altering surface charge or hydrophobicity during plastic manufacturing could therefore reduce both radical production and downstream biological effects without changing the bulk material properties that make plastics useful.
