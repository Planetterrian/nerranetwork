University of St Andrews researchers working with the University of Copenhagen, the North Coast Cetacean Society, and Fisheries and Oceans Canada recovered, as detailed by Phys.org, both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes from the exhaled breath of North Pacific humpbacks.

About eighty-four percent of the genetic material in a given sample belonged to the whale. Airway microbes rode along in those same collections and accounted for roughly sixteen percent of the DNA.

When the same whales had been biopsied, those biopsy sequences agreed with the blow-derived genomes. Blow taken more than once across two years gave the same genetic picture.

Diversity estimates lined up with the biopsy numbers, and the population showed little recent inbreeding. Three previously undescribed mitochondrial variants appeared in the set.

Every collection ran under Gitga’at First Nation research agreements and a Fisheries and Oceans Canada permit.

In British Columbia’s Kitimat Fjord System, sterile petri dishes flew on commercial drones through the breath of individual whales.

Those dishes returned low-coverage nuclear genomes and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes, gathering host DNA and airway microbes in one pass. More than one third of the flights launched directly from land.

The resulting data set is the first population-level whole-genome resource for North Pacific humpbacks.

Novel sequencing of scorpion venom at Eastern Connecticut State University identified, in a Hartford Courant report on a study titled Microbiota Discovered in Scorpion Venom, previously unknown microbial communities.

Venom from multiple species showed bacterial diversity that changed from animal to animal and from species to species.

A distinctive group called Scorpion Group 1 turned up in the eastern swollen-stinger scorpion and widened that lineage’s known evolutionary range.

Peptides already isolated from scorpion venom have shown activity against Staphylococcus and drug-resistant tuberculosis strains in lab and animal tests.

Most people assume a scorpion’s venom gland stays sterile, because an infected gland would harm the animal that produced it.

That belief treated the gland as a sealed toxin factory. The fluid those microbes occupy is loaded with protein and harsh in its salt and ion chemistry, a mix long treated as too hostile for life.

This is the first time venom from these animals has been sequenced for microbiota in a systematic way. That sweep pulled up a bacterial lineage once treated as rare and now recovered across further species.

The gland is not only packaging toxins for injection. It is also maintaining a filtered habitat that lets some bacteria persist and keeps others out.

An infection after a sting may therefore start with bacteria that already lived in the venom, not only with microbes picked up from skin or air.

Follow-up tests can ask whether those resident microbes themselves make compounds with antibiotic activity.

A thirteen-protein panel plus clinical variables predicted, in a Nature Metabolism study, insulin sensitivity measured by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp with an R squared of zero point six seven in one hundred sixty-one people.

In roughly twenty thousand UK Biobank participants, that model outperformed the triglyceride-to-high-density-lipoprotein ratio for type two diabetes prediction.

It also forecasted hepatic, renal, cardiovascular and respiratory disease in cases where hemoglobin A1c did not.

The proteins reflect contributions from liver, adipose tissue and immune cells. A subset of the signature shifted toward an insulin-sensitive profile after exercise.

The signature was validated in an independent cohort of one thousand seven hundred people.
