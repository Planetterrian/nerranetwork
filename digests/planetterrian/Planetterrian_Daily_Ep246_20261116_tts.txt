Salk Institute scientists developed Superb-seq, in work covered by Phys dot org, and ran it on thirty-four thousand cells from three cell lines.

The method combines Cas9 editing, a short edit-reporting D N A sequence, and single-cell R N A sequencing, so each cell carries both a record of where D N A was cut and a readout of which genes were then active.

In one group of ten thousand human leukemia cells, Superb-seq identified forty-three edit sites and detected five thousand seven hundred sixty-one edited cells.

Thirty-six of those forty-three sites were off-target, and the same assay distinguished edits likely to be benign from those that disrupt genes protecting against cancer or other disorders.
