A University of Bristol–led group, in a Phys.org account of a Nature Ecology and Evolution paper, spent five years covering more than five point five million hectares with airplanes, helicopters and drones.

Those flights produced matched maps of canopy height and terrain elevation rather than a patchwork of incompatible surveys.

Almost twenty percent of the surveyed area carries repeated measurements, so the same stands can be compared across time.

Researchers at more than one hundred institutions contributed scenes spanning two hundred twenty-four ecological regions, and a public website and archive let users find flights by location, date and collector.

The maps feed carbon-stock estimates, biodiversity monitoring, and checks on satellite systems including the European Space Agency’s BIOMASS mission.

Defective HTRA2 protein impairs mitochondrial Complex 1 and lets oxygen build to toxic levels in brain tissue, according to ScienceDaily coverage of Gladstone Institutes work.

Mice lacking that protein that breathed reduced-oxygen air lived three times longer than littermates in normal air and showed less striatal inflammation.

The team traced HTRA2 to a clean-up partnership with CLPB that keeps misfolded proteins from clogging Complex 1.

The same genetic screen flagged seventy-five other disease-linked genes that might respond to hypoxia.

Postdoctoral fellow Ankur Garg led the mouse experiments.

Natan Feter at the University of Southern California led an analysis of how different kinds of physical activity track with later dementia.

Seventy-four studies covering more than four point two million adults linked recreational exercise to twenty-four percent lower all-cause dementia risk.

Physically demanding work correlated with twenty percent higher risk after a median ten-year follow-up.

Household chores showed benefit in the single study that measured them.

The pattern held for Alzheimer’s and vascular dementia and was non-linear: risk fell as leisure activity rose, and it rose with more occupational activity before plateauing.

Feter pointed to social and environmental factors around work as the likely drivers of that split.

Something most people get wrong is the belief that the brain treats every kind of physical movement as the same protective dose.

Chosen recreational effort usually arrives at an intensity the person picks, with rest built into the week and with other people around the activity.

The body works, then it recovers, and that cycle is part of the session rather than an accident.

Occupational labor often repeats the same strain, cuts rest short, and stacks workplace stressors across years instead of hours.

Those conditions do not cancel the fact of movement; they change what the movement does while it is happening.

Social contact around leisure can sit beside the effort, whereas job demands can isolate the same muscles inside noise, heat, time pressure or limited control.

Chronic strain without recovery is a different exposure from a hike that ends.

Scheduled, self-paced activity is the form the review ties to brain-health gains, rather than assuming job demands will do that work on their own.

The next decisive measurement is whether adding protected recovery time to physically demanding jobs narrows the gap already observed between leisure and labor.

Chemists at Virginia Tech placed common polyvinyl chloride, or PVC, from pipes, frames and cards into solvent with aluminum trichloride and alpha-olefins.

The mixture heated to one hundred fifty-eight degrees Fahrenheit for three hours and yielded polyalphaolefin, a stable synthetic lubricant base stock.

The route upcycles material that resists ordinary recycling because of its chlorine content and additives.

Earlier work from the same lab turned other plastics into surfactants used in soaps.

The resulting oil belongs to a family valued for stability across wide temperature ranges.

Tahoura Samad and senior author Sangeeta Bhatia at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, or MIT, attached the cancer-approved antibiotic calicheamicin to albumin and paired it with a circulating antidote protein, work described by MIT Health.

The conjugate is cleaved only by neutrophil elastase at infection sites, and the antidote stays in circulation to further shield the liver.

In mice the modified compound cleared bacterial pneumonia with lower liver damage than the free drug.

Re-using a molecule whose oncology safety data already exist shortens the path toward antibiotic development.

The compound is already sold as a dietary supplement and is being examined for muscle loss tied to type two diabetes.
