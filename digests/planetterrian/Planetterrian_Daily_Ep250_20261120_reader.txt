Wernicke’s area, in findings Science News draws from a paper published today in Science, occupies a temporal-lobe patch above the left ear.

Direct neural recordings captured activity there while people attempted to speak.

The measurements were taken during speech attempts, not during passive listening.

Researchers note the map could guide future brain-implant designs for restoring speech after injury.

Something most people get wrong is treating that patch as a purely receptive zone that only decodes incoming language.

The cortex there is assembling the motor commands for speech.

The activity is not a reaction to sound already in the air; it is the brain putting those commands in order while the next word is still being formed.

That sequence matches a familiar bodily fact: you often know the shape of a sentence before your mouth moves.

Future speech-restoration implants may read those planning signals from this location rather than waiting for downstream motor areas.
