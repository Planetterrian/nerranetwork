Karolinska Institutet researchers, in a Nature Communications paper described by Phys.org, showed that TACO1 assists mitochondrial ribosomes when translation stalls.

The stalls fall on difficult amino acid stretches, where a ribosome can otherwise let go of an unfinished chain.

TACO1 helps the ribosome complete that chain instead of releasing a fragment.

Full proteins in those energy-producing organelles are the set mitochondria need for energy generation.

Disruption of the assist appears in certain mitochondrial diseases.

Compact laser-plasma accelerators, in simulations, generated wavelengths suited to imaging live cells in water.

Panos Panagiotopoulos of Wageningen University, in a Phys.org account of his Ph.D. work, showed something most people get wrong about river barriers: a fish pass on a dam or weir is not enough for migrants to reach spawning water.

Isolated ladders often fail when maintenance lapses, and they fail again when water flows are not coordinated across several sites on the same route.

Fish moving through those streams depend on consistent water levels and on passages that stay clear as operators change daily settings.

Tracking across Dutch waterways found that fixes aimed at one structure at a time left many fish unable to finish their routes.

Making each barrier passable on paper still left the run incomplete when the structures were operated as separate machines.

Maintenance crews have to clear debris on a regular cycle, and they have to adjust gate settings so the passes still work during peak migration.

Passage rates improved measurably when water plans treated the whole river as one network instead of a string of independent weirs.

Monitoring that pairs fish counters with real-time flow data is the next tool named for spotting which of those adjustments produce the largest gains in completed migrations.
