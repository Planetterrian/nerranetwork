### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Plasma protein clocks flag organ aging and disease risk — Nature Aging**
   Researchers analyzed roughly 7,000 proteins in plasma samples to build organ-specific proteomic clocks. The clocks tracked risk for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular events, dementia, and multiple cancers. Lifestyle factors such as physical inactivity, smoking, and heavy alcohol use shifted the clocks in measurable ways. This work replicates earlier proteomic aging signatures with an independent platform. The approach adds a practical layer to ongoing biomarker efforts in aging biology by showing which organs drive individual risk trajectories.

2. **Experiment-guided AlphaFold3 captures protein shape ensembles — Nature Biotechnology**
   AlphaFold3 typically outputs one dominant conformation even when proteins sample multiple states. Researchers at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria combined the model with experimental data to generate ensembles that match measured conditions. The method corrects for local structural changes that depend on environment or ligands. It improves accuracy for flexible regions without retraining the underlying network. Follow-up work will test whether the same guidance improves predictions for membrane proteins and large complexes.

3. **Mummified dogs show Tiwanaku people buried pets beside homes — Phys.org**
   Two intentionally mummified dogs from southern Peru date to roughly 1,100 years ago. One young animal was placed on a woven mat in a small pit, positioned as if sleeping. The burials predate the later use of dogs as status symbols in Andean societies. The arid environment preserved soft tissue and wrapping materials that reveal deliberate funerary treatment. The finds expand the timeline for companion-animal relationships in pre-Inca cultures.

4. **Two-year trial finds no cognitive benefit from omega-3 supplements — Science Daily**
   Fish oil capsules raised brain omega-3 levels yet produced no measurable change in memory, cognition, or Alzheimer’s-related imaging markers. The study followed participants for two years and directly compared supplemented and control groups. Results shift focus from isolated supplements toward broader dietary patterns that deliver the same fatty acids. The null finding aligns with earlier trials that also failed to show prevention effects in cognitively healthy adults.

5. **Foamy immune cells packed with myelin lipids drive MS progression — Science Daily**
   Brain tissue from patients with aggressive multiple sclerosis contained abundant “foamy” macrophages filled with fat droplets after they engulfed damaged myelin. These lipid-overloaded cells switched from repair functions to sustaining inflammation. The observation links myelin debris clearance failure to faster disease worsening. Researchers are now examining whether clearing the lipid load can restore protective activity.

6. **Order of species loss changes grassland stability under stress — Phys.org**
   Grasslands covering 40 percent of land surface were studied to test how the sequence of extinctions affects ecosystem resilience. Removing certain species first reduced resistance to drought and nutrient shifts more than random loss. The work shows that functional traits of the first species to disappear determine how much productivity and carbon storage remain. Models that ignore loss order therefore underestimate future risks to food production and biodiversity.

7. **Anesthetized brains still parse grammar and predict words — Science Daily**
   Patients under general anesthesia processed nouns, verbs, and adjectives while hearing stories, with neural signals showing anticipation of upcoming words. The findings indicate that language networks remain active even when consciousness is suppressed. Researchers recorded activity from implanted electrodes during routine surgical anesthesia. The data challenge the assumption that unconscious states eliminate complex linguistic computation.

8. **Soil bacteria strengthen roots with lignin to tolerate salt — Science Daily**
   Beneficial microbes stimulated lignin production in plant roots rather than blocking salt uptake. Greenhouse and field trials showed improved growth and yield on saline soils after bacterial inoculation. The mechanism gives crops a structural buffer against osmotic stress. The approach offers a biological alternative to chemical amendments for reclaiming salt-affected farmland.

9. **Rediscovered megalodon vertebrae confirm 80-foot length — Phys.org**
   An associated set of fossil vertebrae from Otodus megalodon, missing since the 1980s, was re-examined by Danish and international researchers. The specimen supports earlier size estimates reaching approximately 80 feet. Bone proportions also suggest lifestyle details about swimming efficiency and prey size. The find closes a long-standing gap in the megalodon fossil record.

10. **Retargeted Bxb1 recombinase enables large DNA insertion — Nature Biotechnology**
    A modified large serine recombinase now directs precise integration of sizable DNA payloads at chosen genomic sites. The retargeting method avoids random insertion and reduces off-target events. Tests in human cells demonstrated one-step placement of multi-kilobase sequences. The tool expands options for stable gene addition beyond CRISPR cut-and-paste approaches.

11. **HIIT preserves muscle while reducing fat in adults over 70 — Science Daily**
    A six-month randomized trial of more than 120 participants in their seventies compared high-intensity interval training with moderate and low-intensity exercise. Only the HIIT group lost fat mass while maintaining lean muscle. Moderate and low-intensity programs produced smaller fat losses accompanied by muscle decline. The differential effect highlights intensity as a variable that protects sarcopenia risk during weight reduction.

12. **James Webb detects salty clouds on the pink exoplanet — Science Daily**
    The telescope identified water vapor, methane, carbon dioxide, ammonia, and previously unconfirmed salt clouds in the atmosphere of a 57-light-year world. The salt aerosols help explain the planet’s persistent pink hue. Spectral data now allow direct comparison of cloud chemistry across the growing sample of directly characterized exoplanets.

13. **Long-term chlorpyrifos exposure doubles Parkinson’s risk — Science Daily**
    UCLA researchers linked residential proximity to the pesticide with more than twice the likelihood of developing Parkinson’s disease. Laboratory experiments showed the compound damages dopamine neurons and impairs clearance of toxic proteins. The population-level association strengthens the case for examining pesticide residues in Parkinson’s epidemiology.

14. **Ancient alga’s UV protection system aided land colonization — Phys.org**
    A living relative of the earliest land plants revealed a dynamic sunscreen mechanism that adjusts to ultraviolet exposure. The system likely protected the first terrestrial flora more than 500 million years ago. The study traces how this biochemical toolkit was retained and modified in modern plant lineages.

15. **Solar storms briefly suppress precipitation across North America — Phys.org**
    University of New Hampshire analysis of solar events showed measurable drops in rain and snow in the hours and days afterward. Stronger storms produced larger reductions. The link operates through atmospheric ionization changes that alter cloud microphysics. The pattern offers a new variable for short-term weather and climate modeling.

### Planetterrian Spotlight
The HIIT finding in adults over seventy adds a concrete variable to exercise prescriptions aimed at preserving muscle during fat loss. Because only the high-intensity arm maintained lean mass while lowering adiposity, the result sharpens questions about intensity thresholds that protect against sarcopenia. It also fits ongoing work on whether brief vigorous bouts can be scaled safely in older populations. What intensity range would you test next in similar age groups?

### Science Deep Dive: What Anesthesia Leaves Intact
Most people picture general anesthesia as a complete shutdown of brain activity, like flipping a master switch. In reality the brain continues sophisticated processing even while the person remains unresponsive. Right now, as you listen, your own language networks are predicting the next word in this sentence; similar predictive signals appear in anesthetized patients hearing stories. One study recorded electrical activity showing that noun-verb-adjective distinctions and upcoming-word forecasts persist under surgical anesthesia. The practical takeaway is that unconscious states are not uniform blanks; future brain-computer interfaces may exploit these preserved pathways for communication when patients cannot move or speak.
