Colorado State University researchers developed an open-source smoke logger that attaches to animal collars and records hazardous particle concentrations.

The device pairs with existing wildlife tracking systems for location and physiological data.

Initial results appear in Methods in Ecology and Evolution.

Professor George Wittemyer led the project with U.S. Forest Service ecologist Mark Ditmer and postdoctoral researcher Adam Parlin.

The logger addresses the absence of air-quality monitoring in remote wilderness areas where smoke plumes now persist for weeks.

Researchers designed the unit to be small, affordable, and freely available for other field teams.

The collar-mounted smoke logger gives researchers the first direct view of particle concentrations animals experience during prolonged wildfire events.

Previous studies relied on distant ground stations or satellite estimates that missed fine-scale exposure.

By pairing the logger with GPS and heart-rate collars, teams can now match specific smoke levels to changes in movement, foraging, or stress markers.

The open-source design means any wildlife group can build and deploy units without new hardware purchases.

Most people assume bacteria simply grab whatever iron they find and keep growing.

In reality Pseudomonas aeruginosa runs a two-protein switch that checks iron both outside and inside the cell before rewriting large sections of its own metabolism.

The system lets the pathogen adjust energy production and virulence factors to match the iron-poor environment inside a human host.

Once the switch flips, the bacterium rewrites large portions of its metabolic network in a single coordinated response.

This matters for anyone who has taken antibiotics, because iron availability inside tissues can change how effectively the drug clears the infection.

The next result that would settle remaining questions is a direct test of whether blocking the two-protein sensor reduces bacterial survival in animal models of lung infection.
