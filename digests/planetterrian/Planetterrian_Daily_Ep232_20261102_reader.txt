McCormick Professor Julius Lucks leads the DREAM Cloud Lab at Northwestern after the National Science Foundation awarded the Center for Synthetic Biology twenty million dollars over four years.

The Daily Northwestern describes a facility that pairs computing hardware with robots that carry out much of the synthesis in cell-free lysate on plates.

Models trained on large data sets take over much of the guessing that used to dominate protein-sequence testing.

Co-Principal Investigator Michael Jewett at Stanford notes that the order of amino acids sets a protein’s shape, and that shape decides whether it works as an antibody, a detergent, or a detector of contaminants.

The DREAM Brain chatbot will let remote users specify desired protein parameters and receive a data package after synthesis and characterization.

Four initial science drivers focus on metal recovery, plastic degradation, biosensing, and protein-based materials.

Lucks assembled a partnership between Northwestern scientists, Stanford University, and the Chicago Biomedical Consortium.

Childhood measles climbed again after pandemic disruptions to routine immunization, according to a global study in Nature Medicine.

The GBD twenty twenty-three Measles Collaborators tracked both infections and mortality across regions where routine immunization schedules had been interrupted.

Nearly sixteen million children under age five contracted measles in twenty twenty-three, with one hundred eighteen thousand deaths.

Vaccination rates fell after public-health programs and funding were disrupted, reversing a decades-long decline.

The compilation drew on surveillance systems that captured confirmed cases and modeled estimates for areas with incomplete reporting.

Authors noted that the reversal affected the previous trajectory of steady reductions achieved over prior decades.

A University of Bath-led analysis of two hundred sixteen thousand five hundred two people estimated bodily carbon dioxide output across three decades, according to Phys.org.

Annual output reached two point zero five billion metric tons.

That figure was seventy-four percent higher in twenty fifteen than in nineteen eighty-five.

Investigators calculated individual metabolic rates from demographic and dietary data before scaling to national and global populations.

The rise reflects both population growth and shifts in average body mass and activity patterns over that span.

The resulting quantity is a measurable flux within the terrestrial carbon cycle that had previously received limited quantification.

Older adults who ate unsalted skin-roasted peanuts daily showed stronger insulin signaling in memory-related brain regions.

ScienceDaily reported that participants received two servings each day for sixteen weeks.

Imaging and metabolic assessments before and after the period showed measurable shifts in regional insulin signaling.

The peanuts were a single addition to usual diets, with no other prescribed changes.

The work focused on brain regions where age-related insulin resistance has been associated with later cognitive decline.

That insulin responsiveness typically declines with age and links to Alzheimer’s risk.

Remote users of DREAM can receive the physical protein itself after the robots finish characterization.

The interface interprets a natural-language request, then robotic liquid handlers assemble DNA constructs in cell-free lysate reactions and run functional assays.

Senior personnel Ashty Karim explained that proteins will be assembled reproducibly at small volumes so robots can pipette and move trays through measurement and analysis steps.

Executive director Michelle Hoffmann of the Chicago Biomedical Consortium described the chatbot as accepting natural-language requests and then returning job status updates plus final data packages.

Hoffmann noted that after the initial prompt the chatbot will present menus of parameters, guiding non-specialists through feasible design choices before synthesis begins.

The biofoundry already owned by the Center for Synthetic Biology will be augmented with new computational infrastructure, robotic liquid handlers, and staffing.

Karim highlighted reproducible small-volume reactions that robots can handle consistently across thousands of variants.

Most people assume a new protein still has to be built by testing amino-acid strings one at a time over years of bench work.

Models can predict folding patterns and functional properties from sequence data alone, then pass the strongest candidates to automated assembly.

Machine-learning algorithms identify statistical patterns that link sequence to shape and activity from large collections of known protein structures.

The model then proposes variants likely to perform a requested task.

Plate readers record whether the resulting proteins bind their targets or catalyze the desired reaction.

Antibodies that recognize pathogens and enzymes that process nutrients are already doing this work inside the body.

New versions of those molecules are created for medicines, industrial cleaners, and environmental sensors.

The AI handles the initial exploration of sequence space while the hardware carries out construction and testing.

In October twenty twenty-five, one hundred thirty-five letters of interest arrived from AI companies, research groups, and academic institutions across forty-eight states within forty-eight hours.

Centralizing the biofoundry and the computing stack removes the requirement that every research team maintain its own expensive automation suite.

Lucks emphasized that the goal is to solve the protein-engineering bottleneck once so multiple downstream biotechnology applications can proceed without repeating the same manual optimization work.

One open question is how broadly the models will generalize once they move past the first validation cases.

Performance data from those cases will show whether the predictions hold across diverse functional requirements and whether assembly stays consistent at the scale needed for broader adoption.

The first drivers were chosen to represent common protein-engineering goals, a practical test of whether the end-to-end pipeline delivers usable molecules rather than only faster calculations.

The lab aims to serve as a Midwest engine for biotechnology and to ease a bottleneck many developers still face.

UCLA investigators linked long-term exposure to one widely used pesticide with more than two point five times higher Parkinson’s disease risk.

The work combined epidemiological records with cellular assays to map both the elevated disease odds and the intracellular damage mechanism.

Investigators traced how the chemical harms brain cells and identified a potential protective route.

They also tested interventions that preserved cell viability despite the exposure.

The findings add a specific environmental factor to the set of known contributors to Parkinson’s incidence.

Crown-of-thorns starfish on Australia’s Great Barrier Reef often fed without destroying whole colonies.

Nearly seventy-five percent of observed feeding events left living coral tissue intact.

By consuming fast-growing dominant corals, the predators open space for slower-growing species.

The observations covered multiple reef sites where starfish densities remained below outbreak thresholds.

Partial feeding events allowed surviving coral fragments to recover and permitted recruitment of additional species.

Habitual tea consumption was tracked against heart and blood vessel disease in a large adult cohort.

Incidence was eleven percent lower among people who drank tea regularly.

The cohort numbered nearly four hundred eighty-six thousand adults.

Risk reached its lowest point at roughly six to eight cups daily, with black tea showing steadier benefits than green tea.

Data collection captured self-reported intake frequency alongside verified medical outcomes over an extended follow-up period.

The dose-response curve indicated a plateau beyond the six-to-eight-cup range, and black tea held the association across subgroups while green tea benefits appeared more variable.

A specimen recovered in Egypt challenges the long-standing view that East Africa was the sole cradle of ape evolution.

The fossil is seventeen million years old, from the early Miocene, and preserves dental and skeletal features that align with later ape lineages.

Researchers now suggest North Africa and the Middle East may have played earlier roles.

Its location extends the geographic range previously considered for the earliest diversification events.

The find prompts re-examination of migration and speciation models that had centered exclusively on East African sites.

Southern Hemisphere plantings of conifers outperformed their Northern Hemisphere source populations, according to Phys.org.

Across one hundred ninety-two sites on four continents, trees grown far from their evolutionary origins put on biomass up to four times faster.

Drought resistance remained intact despite that accelerated accumulation.

The study aggregated long-term forestry trial data to compare performance across matched genetic provenances.

Faster growth arrived without increased mortality under variable rainfall.

Researchers at the Radcliffe Department of Medicine developed a new approach to liver testing that avoids invasive procedures.

The technique aims to provide equivalent diagnostic information through imaging or biomarker combinations that do not require tissue sampling.

Early validation focused on accuracy relative to established biopsy standards.

The advance addresses patient burden and procedural risks associated with conventional liver evaluation.

Details of the method and validation data appear on the department site.
