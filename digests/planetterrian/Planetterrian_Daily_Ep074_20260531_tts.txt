In a mouse model the brain's glymphatic network removed beta-amyloid through a fast surface pathway between the skull and meninges. A second deeper pathway relied on diffusion and operated more than thirty times slower.

The dual-route finding explains how metabolic waste exits both superficial and parenchymal regions under varying conditions. The study published in Science Advances maps clearance kinetics across different physiological states, which matters because it separates rapid surface flow from slower internal diffusion rather than treating clearance as a single uniform process.

Researchers plan to test whether similar routes appear in human imaging, and that next step will determine whether the mouse kinetics translate to measurable differences in people at risk for protein accumulation.

Shifting to vascular responses that intersect with the oral microbiome, older adults who drank concentrated nitrate-rich beetroot juice twice daily for two weeks showed measurable reductions in blood pressure. The same regimen produced no comparable change in younger participants.

The effect coincided with shifts in the composition of bacteria living in the mouth, and researchers link the nitrate-to-nitrite conversion performed by these microbes to the observed vascular response.

This contributes to the microbiome program by addressing the causation-versus-correlation question through a short, controlled intervention that ties microbial activity directly to a physiological readout.

The next step is testing whether the microbiome changes are necessary for the benefit, which would clarify if altering those bacteria could become a practical lever for blood-pressure management in older adults.

McGill University scientists temporarily inhibited a pair of surface proteins on natural killer cells. In-vivo safety readouts will be watched next because any clinical translation will require confirming that the temporary inhibition does not create lasting changes in NK-cell behavior outside the tumor setting.
