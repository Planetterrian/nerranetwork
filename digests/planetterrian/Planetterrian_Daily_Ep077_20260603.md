### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Genetically modified hookworms produce and deliver therapeutics — Phys.org**
   Researchers at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis engineered hookworms to secrete therapeutic molecules while living inside the host gut. The parasites already survive for years by releasing molecules that allow coexistence with human hosts. The team redirected this secretion system so the worms manufacture and release a chosen drug. This approach exploits the worm’s long-term persistence rather than relying on repeated dosing. The work targets potential applications in under-resourced regions where hookworm infections are common. Next steps include testing safety and dosing control in animal models. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-genetically-hookworms-therapeutics.html)

2. **Wading bird populations declining in New York–New Jersey Harbor — Phys.org**
   Researchers tracked trends in the largest northeastern U.S. breeding population of colonial nesting wading birds. Urban estuaries still support these species, yet counts show measurable declines over recent monitoring periods. Bird populations serve as indicators of overall environmental health in busy harbor systems. The study examined both suburban and more developed sites within the same estuary. Habitat quality and food availability appear linked to the observed drops. Continued surveys will clarify whether local conditions or broader regional pressures drive the changes. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-wading-bird-populations-yorknew-jersey.html)

3. **Wetland loss raised U.S. flood insurance claims by over $10 billion — Phys.org**
   A study in Nature Water quantified residential flood insurance payouts linked to wetland conversion since 1985. Losses exceeded $10 billion across the country as natural flood buffers disappeared. Riverine flooding accounts for the measured increase in claims. The analysis matched historical wetland maps with insurance records at fine spatial scales. Wetlands reduce peak flows by storing and slowing water during storms. Future work will examine how restoration projects alter these cost trajectories. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-wetlands-loss-residential-payments-billion.html)

4. **Terahertz imaging maps material chirality at 100-micrometer resolution — Phys.org**
   A new terahertz technique distinguishes mirror-image molecular structures without physical contact. Chiral molecules show different behaviors despite identical composition, a property central to drug design and biology. The method achieves spatial maps at 100-micrometer resolution across material samples. It reveals handedness patterns that conventional imaging misses at this scale. DNA and many pharmaceuticals are chiral, making the distinction biologically relevant. Researchers aim to extend the approach to dynamic samples and thicker specimens. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-terahertz-imaging-spatial-chirality-materials.html)

5. **Red-shouldered hawk parents favor female offspring in good territories — Phys.org**
   A Journal of Raptor Research study analyzed nestling sex ratios across suburban and rural sites from 2004 to 2016. Parents produced more females when occupying higher-quality territories. Female offspring benefit more from abundant resources during development. The pattern offers a biological signal of habitat condition for the species. Urban adaptation may shift these ratios as hawks increasingly nest near people. Longer-term monitoring will test whether the bias persists across generations. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-girl-power-red-shouldered-hawk.html)

6. **Leaf-mimicking katydids use camouflage to boost mating calls — Phys.org**
   University of St Andrews researchers found that katydids with strong leaf mimicry also reshape their songs. The camouflage alters sound transmission so calls reach females more effectively. This is the first demonstration that visual mimicry can enhance an acoustic mating signal. Males with better leaf resemblance produced more attractive songs in playback tests. The dual function improves reproductive success in dense vegetation. Follow-up experiments will measure how habitat structure modifies the advantage. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-leafy-camouflage-reshapes-katydid-songs.html)

7. **Ötzi the Iceman’s microbiome reveals 5,300-year-old microbial community — Phys.org**
   Eurac Research scientists reconstructed the microbial profile associated with Europe’s oldest natural mummy. The analysis captured gut flora, skin microbes, and cold-adapted yeasts preserved with the body. Some yeasts remained viable when cultured in the lab. The findings show a complex microbiome shaped by Copper Age diet and environment. Comparisons with modern samples highlight shifts in microbial diversity over millennia. Further sequencing will clarify which taxa persisted through the mummification process. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-tzi-iceman-microbiome-year-relationship.html)

8. **Caribbean reef sharks prefer prey-rich locations in protected waters — Phys.org**
   FIU researchers tracked shark movements in Bahamian waters closed to fishing. Sharks consistently selected sites with highest prey abundance rather than areas where food was scarce. The preference persisted even though overall population levels were stable. The study used acoustic tags and prey surveys across multiple reefs. Results support managing entire habitat mosaics instead of isolated no-take zones. Next observations will test whether the pattern holds in fished regions. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-sharks-hotspots-prey-underlining-holistic.html)

9. **Limited evidence that AI improves seasonal flu vaccine strain selection — Nature**
   Two linked papers in Nature Medicine examine claims that artificial intelligence outperforms standard methods for choosing influenza vaccine strains. Current evidence does not show consistent superiority across multiple seasons or metrics. The analyses compared AI predictions against historical strain selection outcomes. Authors note that larger, prospective datasets are still needed. Standard antigenic cartography and ferret models remain the regulatory baseline. Ongoing surveillance will determine whether refined models close the gap. Source: [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04461-z)

10. **Childbirth care quality remains substandard in three Exemplar countries — Nature**
    A Nature Medicine study assessed care provided by skilled health personnel in Nepal, Senegal, and Zambia. High coverage of facility births did not translate into recommended clinical practices. Direct observations revealed gaps in monitoring, infection prevention, and immediate newborn care. The findings come from standardized assessments across multiple facilities in each country. Quality shortfalls persist even where access has expanded rapidly. The authors call for targeted training and supervision focused on process measures.

11. **Single Cas13 effector controls entire bacterial operons — Nature**
    The SONAR system uses one engineered Cas13 protein to regulate multiple genes in an operon. Simple changes to the crRNA sequence switch the effector between transcript degradation, translation inhibition, or activation. The approach works in living bacteria without requiring multiple distinct proteins. It provides programmable, RNA-level control at the operon scale. Tests confirmed orthogonality across different targets. Future applications include metabolic engineering and antimicrobial strategies.

12. **Spatial proteomics reveals stromal barriers to antibody delivery in tumors — Nature**
    Researchers imaged interactions between therapeutic antibodies and the tumor microenvironment using spatial proteomics. Stromal components consistently limited antibody penetration into solid tumor regions. The barriers appear conserved across multiple human tumor types examined. Mapping these physical and molecular obstacles identifies where delivery fails. The technique combines high-plex protein detection with spatial coordinates. Follow-on studies will test whether disrupting specific stromal elements improves distribution.

13. **Large collaborative studies aim to improve psychology reproducibility — Nature**
    Multiple labs are launching coordinated projects that recruit babies and dogs as participants. These populations allow bigger sample sizes while testing core cognitive mechanisms. The effort targets long-standing replication concerns in cognitive science. Standardized protocols across sites reduce lab-specific variability. Early data collection focuses on attention, memory, and social cognition tasks. Results will establish benchmarks for future multi-lab cognitive research.

14. **Viable yeasts recovered from Ötzi the Iceman’s remains — Science News**
    Scientists cultured cold-loving yeasts directly from the 5,300-year-old mummy’s preserved tissues. The isolates suggest the Iceman’s microbiome was not entirely static after death. Some strains grew under laboratory conditions mimicking ancient gut temperatures. The work appears in the journal Microbiome. Researchers baked test sourdough with the revived yeasts to confirm activity. Additional genomic sequencing will trace lineage continuity with modern strains. Source: [sciencenews.org](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/otzi-iceman-viable-yeasts-microbiome)

15. **Europe’s agrifood system faces simultaneous climate, health, and biodiversity pressures — Phys.org**
    A review outlines how climate-driven droughts and floods compound existing stresses on European agriculture. Diet-related diseases, biodiversity loss, and greenhouse-gas emissions all trace partly to current production and consumption patterns. The system must deliver affordable food while meeting climate and nature targets. Policy lock-ins limit rapid shifts toward lower-impact practices. Integrated approaches that address production, diet, and land use together show the greatest potential. Modeling efforts will quantify trade-offs across these goals. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-food-europe-green-shift-article.html)

### Science Deep Dive: Wetlands Are Not Wasted Land
Most people assume wetlands are low-value areas that can be drained or filled with little consequence. In reality they actively store floodwater and reduce peak river flows during storms. Right now, as heavy rain falls on developed landscapes, the absence of those natural sponges is increasing insurance payouts and property damage. Wetland loss since 1985 has already added more than $10 billion to U.S. residential flood claims. The next time local planning discussions turn to “unused” land near rivers or coasts, the measurable cost of removing those buffers is worth tracking in future conservation or restoration decisions.
