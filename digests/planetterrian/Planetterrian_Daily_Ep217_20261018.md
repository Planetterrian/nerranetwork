### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **World's oldest shipwreck found off Turkey coast — Phys.org**
   A research team led by Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University dated the wreck to around 2700–2400 B.C. using pottery vessels recovered from shallow waters near Kekova on the southern Turkish coast. The cargo included large ovoid amphorae with two handles and raised horizontal clay belts around their widest parts, some bearing residues from eastern Mediterranean production regions. The vessel appears to have sought shelter during a storm before striking rocks, after which its timbers disappeared while the pottery remained embedded in the rocky underwater slope. This find predates the previous record holder, a wreck off Dokos in Greece dated to about 4200 years ago, by roughly 300 years. Pottery shapes and manufacturing techniques supplied the primary dating evidence, and divers spent four months extracting the vessels while working around barnacles on the slope. The discovery offers a time capsule of Early Bronze Age trade networks that connected the Levant with Egypt and other societies during the pyramid age. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-world-oldest-shipwreck-coast-turkey.html)

2. **Worm brain study reveals sex differences in nervous systems — Phys.org**
   Northeastern University researchers mapped neural circuits in the roundworm C. elegans to compare how males and hermaphrodites process sensory input and generate behavioral responses. The work focused on regions tied to mate detection and environmental navigation, where distinct wiring patterns between the sexes became evident. C. elegans serves as a model organism because its entire nervous system consists of exactly 302 neurons whose connections have already been fully charted. Experiments exposed each sex variant to identical stimuli and recorded divergent response patterns in sensory processing circuits. The findings highlight how even compact nervous systems can produce sex-specific information handling without requiring large differences in neuron count. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-worm-brain-sex-differences-nervous.html)

3. **Oligodendrocytes shift from protective to harmful role in aging brains — ScienceDaily**
   University of Edinburgh researchers followed the Lothian Birth Cohort 1936, collecting cognitive scores from 866 participants between ages 70 and 82 on tests of memory, processing speed, and spatial understanding. Post-mortem brain tissue from individuals with faster decline contained fewer large nerve fibers and an abnormal buildup of unhealthy myelin around remaining fibers. Lower levels of the protective protein NRF2 inside oligodendrocytes correlated with both the myelin excess and the fiber loss. When researchers experimentally lowered NRF2 in mouse oligodendrocytes, the animals developed the same myelin overproduction and large-fiber reduction seen in the human samples. The NRF2 pathway is already targeted by an approved multiple sclerosis drug, raising the possibility of repurposing existing compounds for age-related cognitive protection. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261015073222.htm)

4. **Mantis shrimp red-light pigments function as reversible switches — Phys.org**
   Researchers at ULiège and Humboldt University isolated individual visual pigments from mantis shrimp eyes and measured their responses to red wavelengths that few other animals can detect. Certain pigments returned to their original state after light exposure ended, confirming reversible switching through direct spectroscopic measurements on purified proteins. The study, published in Science Advances, examined roughly 30 visual pigments in total, far exceeding the three found in humans. The reversible property emerged only after the team analyzed each pigment separately rather than in mixtures. These findings open a route toward new optogenetic tools that could exploit the pigments' light-controlled state changes. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-red-sensitive-pigments-mantis-shrimp.html)

5. **Electrical sliding alters graphene magnetic states persistently — Phys.org**
   Cornell researchers applied voltage to slide layers within ultrathin graphene sheets and observed switches between distinct magnetic states that persisted after the voltage was removed. The transitions arise from altered interlayer coupling that modifies electron interactions within the two-dimensional lattice. Transport and spectroscopic measurements confirmed the magnetic changes without requiring continuous external input. The effect demonstrates a method for electrically controlling quantum properties in layered materials that remains stable once set. The work suggests potential applications in devices that store magnetic information through mechanical layer positioning. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-electrical-graphene-magnetic-states.html)

6. **Forest soils increase methane absorption as climate warms — ScienceDaily**
   A 24-year monitoring program across multiple forest sites recorded an average 3 percent annual rise in methane uptake under conditions of rising temperatures and declining rainfall. Researchers tracked gas fluxes at the soil surface and linked the upward trend to microbial communities that oxidize atmospheric methane more effectively as soils dry. The result runs counter to earlier models that predicted reduced methane removal from warming soils. Methane-consuming bacteria appear to gain advantage under the drier, warmer regimes now observed in many temperate and boreal forests. Continued measurements at additional sites will determine whether the absorption rate keeps climbing or eventually reaches a plateau.

7. **Webb telescope maps recurring weather states on nearby exoplanet — ScienceDaily**
   NASA's James Webb Space Telescope observed a turbulent world 20 light-years away and identified three repeating atmospheric states shaped by temperature shifts and cloud redistribution. Repeated observations revealed an unexpectedly orderly pattern despite the planet's overall turbulence. Specific temperature changes consistently triggered measurable movements in cloud cover that defined each state. The data set supplies a concrete template for modeling weather dynamics on other distant planets where direct measurements remain limited. Further telescope time on similar targets could test whether the three-state cycle appears more widely.

8. **Global survey ties financial hardship to reduced trust in science — Phys.org**
   A worldwide trust survey connected poorer access to health care and unequal distribution of research benefits with measurably lower public confidence in science. Respondents in regions where local communities hold limited influence over research priorities reported the widest trust gaps. The results indicate that perceptions of fairness in who gains from scientific advances shape overall attitudes toward the enterprise. Experts suggest that improving access and participation could narrow those gaps without requiring changes to the underlying research itself. The survey covered multiple continents and highlighted consistent patterns across differing economic contexts.

9. **Song lyrics grow less emotionally explicit across decades — Phys.org**
   Michigan State University researchers applied natural language processing to more than 38,000 popular songs and documented a gradual decline in direct references to emotions and relationships. The downward trend appeared both across successive generations of artists and within the careers of individual performers. Quantification of emotional language showed the shift occurred steadily rather than in abrupt jumps tied to single events. The pattern suggests evolving cultural norms around how popular music conveys personal experience. Further analysis could test whether the same trajectory holds in non-English language music markets. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-popular-song-lyrics-emotion-analysis.html)

10. **Landegren traces gene-variant method to multiple companies — www.lifesciencefocus.com**
    Ulf Landegren developed a technique for distinguishing between different gene variants during his postdoctoral work at Caltech in the 1980s. That invention generated patents and directly seeded a series of companies focused on genetic analysis tools. Subsequent innovations in detection methods built on the original approach for identifying genetic differences at scale. The foundational technique continues to underpin diagnostic and research applications still in routine laboratory use. Landegren has described how many later projects trace their conceptual roots to the early gene-variant work. Source: [lifesciencefocus.com](https://www.lifesciencefocus.com/article/view/1251667/ulf_landegren_from_scientific_discovery_to_successful_companies)

### Science Deep Dive: How forest soils remove methane
Most people assume that warming soils will release more methane into the atmosphere and accelerate climate change. In reality, certain forest soils become stronger sinks for atmospheric methane as temperatures rise and rainfall declines. The mechanism involves shifts in microbial communities that oxidize methane before it escapes the soil layer. A 24-year monitoring program recorded an average 3 percent annual increase in methane uptake across the studied sites. This absorption occurs through methane-consuming bacteria that thrive under the drier, warmer conditions now common in many forests. The process begins when methane diffuses into the soil pore spaces where specialized microbes use it as an energy source, converting it into carbon dioxide and water. Over the multi-year observation window, researchers documented consistent upward trends in uptake rates at multiple temperate and boreal locations, even as air temperatures climbed and precipitation patterns shifted. The bacteria responsible appear to gain a competitive edge when soil moisture drops, allowing them to process larger volumes of atmospheric methane that would otherwise remain in the air. Listeners can support continued long-term soil gas monitoring programs in their regions to track whether the pattern holds across more forest types. The next key measurement will come from expanded sites that test whether the uptake rate continues rising or eventually plateaus. Understanding this soil-microbe interaction matters because forests already cover roughly 30 percent of Earth's land surface, meaning even modest per-area increases in methane removal could influence global atmospheric budgets over decades. The 24-year data set provides one of the longest continuous records available for testing climate models that previously assumed net methane release from warming soils. Additional measurements at sites with different tree species and soil types will show how broadly the pattern applies. If the trend persists, forest management decisions that preserve soil structure and microbial diversity could become part of broader strategies for atmospheric methane control. The study also highlights the value of maintaining long-term environmental monitoring networks that capture slow changes invisible in shorter experiments. Future work could examine whether similar uptake increases occur in agricultural or grassland soils under comparable climate shifts. The concrete number of a 3 percent yearly rise offers a benchmark against which new observations can be compared directly.

Today's findings show steady progress in mapping both ancient trade routes and modern biological mechanisms.
