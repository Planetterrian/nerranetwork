### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Japan and South Korea outpace Western nations in journal output growth — Fierce Biotech**
   Nature Index data show China leading with a 22% rise in contributions from 2024 to 2025. Japan and South Korea each posted nearly 10% growth, exceeding gains recorded by the United States, Germany and the United Kingdom. The rankings track author affiliations in high-quality natural-science journals. Continued tracking will show whether the trend alters global collaboration patterns.

2. **Mosquito-borne virus reaches Scotland for the first time — Phys.org**
   Infectious-disease researchers recorded an African-origin virus transmitted by mosquitoes in Scottish samples. Heather Ferguson described the detection as unexpected within her career. The finding expands the known geographic range of the pathogen. Surveillance programs will monitor further northward spread.

3. **Dwarf mongooses adjust behaviour ahead of rival encounters — Phys.org**
   University of Bristol researchers observed groups altering movement, communication and resource defence in high-risk zones even without immediate rivals present. Adjustments scaled with the likelihood of conflict. The study appears in Nature Ecology & Evolution. Future work will test whether similar anticipation occurs in additional social species.

4. **Mekong basin faces accumulating pollution from upstream activity — Phys.org**
   Fishermen report declining catches alongside visible contamination in the river system. The pollution affects both human health and long-term livelihoods across multiple countries. Local communities continue daily use despite documented risks. Ongoing monitoring will quantify cumulative exposure levels.

5. **Performance-based funds proposed for EU external climate targets — Phys.org**
   The Potsdam Institute model allocates 5 billion euros annually to reward verified emission reductions outside EU borders. The approach meets the 5-percentage-point external component of the 2040 target without creating perverse incentives. It links payments directly to jurisdictional outcomes. Pilot implementations will test verification methods.

6. **Autonomy and social features raise MOOC completion and depth — Phys.org**
   Xiaomei Wei’s Leiden University analysis examined learner engagement across massive open online courses. Courses combining learner choice, peer interaction and demanding tasks produced stronger content mastery. The dissertation defence is scheduled for June 25. Platform designers can apply the identified factors to future iterations.

7. **Armed conflict sharply reduces school attendance in affected regions — Phys.org**
   UNESCO data indicate more than 50% of primary-age children out of school in the Central African Republic, South Sudan and Eritrea. The study links direct targeting of children to the steepest enrolment drops. Quantifying exact numbers remains difficult in active zones. Additional field surveys will refine estimates.

8. **High-velocity impactor likely explains Venus’s slow retrograde spin — Phys.org**
   European Geosciences Union models point to a moon-sized body striking at high angle within Venus’s first 50 million years. The event accounts for the planet’s 248-day rotation period. Earlier explanations lacked matching dynamical signatures. Refined simulations will incorporate additional early-solar-system constraints.

9. **Electron-beam curing speeds production of durable coatings — Phys.org**
   The method cross-links polymers without solvents or heat, yielding tougher films for packaging and automotive uses. Layers protect against moisture, ultraviolet light and corrosion. Industrial trials are expanding to additional substrates. Adoption rates will depend on equipment cost reductions.

10. **Rigid planning laws hinder rapid climate adaptation — Phys.org**
    Courts and local authorities operate under fixed rules that slow responses to changing environmental conditions. Proposed reforms would allow dynamic adjustments while retaining core protections. The study highlights gaps between legal frameworks and observed climate impacts. Legislative reviews are expected in multiple jurisdictions.

11. **Underground fungal networks span vast distances yet remain largely unmapped — Phys.org**
    Fungi constitute some of Earth’s largest organisms and support nutrient cycling for crops and wild plants. The networks stay invisible to surface observers. Improved imaging will clarify their role in ecosystem stability. Land-management plans may incorporate fungal mapping once data improve.

12. **Tech executives test multiple interventions aimed at extending lifespan — Nature**
    Influencers and high-net-worth individuals share protocols ranging from supplements to experimental procedures. The article examines the evidence base behind common practices. Limited human data constrain claims of efficacy. Longer-term cohort studies will clarify which approaches produce measurable effects.

13. **Climate change projected to affect Amazon and Cerrado erosion differently — Phys.org**
    Catchment modelling for two Brazilian sites shows opposing soil-loss trends under future rainfall patterns. The Amazon basin faces one trajectory while the Cerrado faces another. Land-management recommendations differ by biome. Follow-up measurements will validate model outputs.

14. **Plants actively redirect sugars to wound sites for repair — Phys.org**
    Fluorescent sensors tracked glucose movement in living specimens after injury. Concentration increased locally to supply energy for regeneration. The work appears in PNAS. Crop researchers will test whether enhancing this response improves resilience.

15. **Newly identified protein anchors gram-negative outer membrane to cell wall — Phys.org**
    University of Notre Dame scientists characterised the molecule that secures the outer membrane in antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Disruption of this attachment could weaken resistance mechanisms. The finding advances understanding of envelope architecture. Structural studies will guide potential inhibitor design.

### Science Deep Dive: How Plants Heal Wounds
Most people picture plants as passive after damage, simply waiting for new growth. In reality, injured tissues trigger an active rerouting of sugars that concentrates glucose at the wound site to power repair. Right now, as a leaf on a nearby tree encounters an insect bite or tear, transport proteins shift resources within minutes to support local cell division and wall reinforcement. Researchers using fluorescent sensors recorded this localised spike, showing the response scales with injury severity. The finding carries a practical takeaway: selecting crop varieties that maintain strong sugar-transport responses under stress could reduce losses from physical damage or storms. Watch for field trials that test whether boosting this pathway improves yield stability.
