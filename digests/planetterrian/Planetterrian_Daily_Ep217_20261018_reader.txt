Archaeologists have dated the world's oldest known shipwreck to two thousand seven hundred B.C. from pottery cargo found off Turkey.

Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University led the recovery from shallow waters near Kekova on the southern Turkish coast, according to Phys.org.

The cargo included large ovoid amphorae with two handles and raised horizontal clay belts around their widest parts.

Some of those vessels still carry residues from eastern Mediterranean production regions.

The ship appears to have sought shelter during a storm before striking rocks.

Its timbers then disappeared, while the ceramics stayed embedded in the rocky underwater slope.

Pottery shapes and manufacturing techniques supplied the primary dating evidence, spanning two thousand seven hundred to two thousand four hundred B.C.

That age predates the previous record holder, a wreck off Dokos in Greece about four thousand two hundred years ago, by roughly three hundred years.

Divers spent four months extracting the vessels while working around barnacles on the slope.

Manufacturing details on the Kekova jars still point toward specific eastern Mediterranean production regions.

The vessel's coastal path lines up with Early Bronze Age exchange that moved cedar, metals, and resins toward Egypt and neighboring societies.

Those routes also tied the Levant into the same networks during the pyramid age.

Continued excavation is aimed at showing how often such voyages actually ran.

Durable ceramics are reconstructing those maritime connections even though the wooden hull is gone.

Northeastern University researchers mapped neural circuits in the roundworm C. elegans to compare how males and hermaphrodites process sensory input, according to Phys.org.

The entire nervous system of this worm consists of exactly three hundred two neurons whose connections have already been fully charted.

The comparison used that complete connectome to set male circuits beside hermaphrodite circuits directly.

Distinct wiring patterns appeared in sensory regions tied to mate detection and environmental navigation.

Experiments exposed each sex variant to identical stimuli and recorded divergent response patterns in those sensory processing circuits.

Compact nervous systems produced sex-specific information handling without large differences in neuron count.

University of Edinburgh researchers followed the Lothian Birth Cohort nineteen thirty-six and collected cognitive scores from eight hundred sixty-six participants between ages seventy and eighty-two.

The tests covered memory, processing speed, and spatial understanding, according to ScienceDaily.

Post-mortem brain tissue from people with faster decline contained fewer large nerve fibers.

The same tissue showed an abnormal buildup of unhealthy myelin around remaining fibers.

Lower levels of the protective protein NRF2 inside oligodendrocytes correlated with both the myelin excess and the fiber loss.

When researchers experimentally lowered NRF2 in mouse oligodendrocytes, the animals developed the same myelin overproduction and large-fiber reduction seen in the human samples.

The NRF2 pathway is already targeted by an approved multiple sclerosis drug.

A twenty-four year monitoring program across multiple forest sites recorded an average three percent annual rise in methane uptake under rising temperatures and declining rainfall.

ScienceDaily describes researchers tracking gas fluxes at the soil surface and linking the upward trend to microbial communities that oxidize atmospheric methane more effectively as soils dry.

The result runs counter to earlier models that predicted reduced methane removal from warming soils.

Methane-consuming bacteria appear to gain advantage under the drier, warmer regimes now observed in many temperate and boreal forests.

Most people assume warming forest ground will pour extra methane into the air as the climate heats up.

The soil is doing something else: methane first diffuses into pore spaces, where specialized microbes take it as an energy source.

Those microbes convert the gas into carbon dioxide and water.

When soil moisture drops, the same bacteria can process larger volumes of atmospheric methane that would otherwise remain in the air.

Forests already cover roughly thirty percent of Earth's land surface.

Even modest per-area increases in methane removal could influence global atmospheric budgets over decades.

The record is one of the longest continuous soil-gas series available for testing climate models that had assumed net methane release from warming ground.

Additional measurements at sites with different tree species and soil types will show how broadly the pattern applies.

Forest management that preserves soil structure and microbial diversity is being discussed as one way to keep that microbial work intact.

Future work could examine whether similar uptake increases occur in agricultural or grassland soils under comparable climate shifts.

Long-term environmental monitoring networks are what make these slow soil changes visible at all, because shorter experiments miss them.

Researchers at ULiège and Humboldt University isolated individual visual pigments from mantis shrimp eyes and measured their responses to red wavelengths that few other animals can detect.

The study, published in Science Advances, examined roughly thirty visual pigments in total, far exceeding the three found in humans.

Certain pigments returned to their original state after red-light exposure ended.

Direct spectroscopy on purified proteins, not mixtures, confirmed that reversible switching.

The reversible property emerged only after the team analyzed each pigment separately.

Phys.org notes that this light-controlled behavior is being considered as a path toward new optogenetic tools.

Cornell researchers applied voltage to slide layers within ultrathin graphene sheets and watched magnetic states switch and then stay switched after the voltage came off.

Phys.org reports that the transitions arise from altered interlayer coupling that modifies electron interactions within the two-dimensional lattice.

Transport measurements and spectroscopic measurements both confirmed the magnetic changes without continuous external input.

Once set, those quantum properties in the layered material remained stable.

Mechanical positioning of the layers is the lever that stores the magnetic information.

The National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, used the James Webb Space Telescope to observe a turbulent world twenty light-years away.

The telescope identified three repeating atmospheric states shaped by temperature shifts and cloud redistribution, according to ScienceDaily.

Repeated observations revealed an unexpectedly orderly pattern despite the planet's overall turbulence.

Specific temperature changes consistently triggered measurable movements in cloud cover that defined each state.

The data set supplies a concrete template for modeling weather dynamics on other distant planets where direct measurements remain limited.

Further telescope time on similar targets could test whether the three-state cycle appears more widely.

The survey covered multiple continents and found consistent patterns across differing economic contexts.

Further analysis could test whether the same trajectory holds in non-English language music markets.
