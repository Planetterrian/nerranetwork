Most people assume menopause is a gradual hormone fade that stretches across a decade.

Three hundred million laboratory tests from more than one million women show multiple organ systems flipping at the final menstrual period itself.

The analysis, discussed on r/science, tracks bone turnover markers, liver enzymes, kidney filtration measures and blood lipid profiles.

Those four sets of markers all register the change inside the same narrow window.

The timing lines up with ovarian estrogen production dropping below a functional threshold.

Until that drop, estrogen had buffered bone, liver, kidney and lipid systems with a steady regulatory signal.

Hormone shifts that predict the transition can begin up to fifteen years earlier.

The discontinuity at the last period is still abrupt.

Men of the same age, tracked in the same datasets, show no comparable jump.

The sample is large enough to separate that menopause-linked discontinuity from ordinary age-related drift.

Targeted panels of bone density, liver enzymes and lipid profiles can be timed to the months around a patient's reported final period.

That is a tighter window than spreading the same checks evenly across the preceding decade.

Columbia scientists identified a bacterial antiviral pathway that protects genomes from viral attack.

Phys.org reports the system shares molecular features with processes that safeguard eukaryotic chromosomes.

The same mechanism illuminates an early evolutionary event that contributed to the emergence of higher organisms.

The finding connects microbial immunity to genome-stability machinery across domains of life.

Researchers suggest the pathway could be repurposed for targeted DNA editing in human cells.

They also describe a possible adaptation into a gene editor aimed at slowing cellular aging and improving cancer therapies.

A geostatistical model now estimates snake envenoming and fatality rates across seventy-nine countries.

Researchers, in work covered by Phys.org, reviewed studies through March twenty twenty-five and folded in climate, snake species richness and socioeconomic data.

Sub-Saharan Africa carries the heaviest estimated toll.

More than half of at-risk nations lack systematic case recording.

Many victims live hours from clinics or see traditional healers first, so those cases never enter national statistics.

The framework combines real observations with environmental signals to produce country-level estimates.

Elephant megacarcasses leave nutrient legacies that create coldspots of biodiversity beside hotspots of ecosystem processes.

A study summarized on r/science maps how large-animal decomposition alters soil chemistry and plant communities over time in savanna landscapes.

As the body breaks down, nitrogen, phosphorus and other minerals collect in tight soil patches and stay there for years.

Nearby, some microbes and plants speed up, while the count of species in that immediate zone falls.

A single carcass site can reorganize food webs and nutrient cycling long after the animal is gone.

Persistent nutrient gradients can suppress some species while speeding decomposition and nutrient cycling in others.

Similar inputs likely occurred across continents before megafauna declines cut how often they arrived.

The study leaves open what scale of carcass input would shift biodiversity metrics in a given savanna patch, and whether restoring large-herbivore populations could recreate these process hotspots.

Dr. Ruth Thurstan at the University of Exeter read one thousand one hundred ninety-two seafood recipes in Good Housekeeping, starting in nineteen twenty-three.

Phys.org reports that before the nineteen seventies the pages featured diverse species, with flatfish, herring and oysters prominent.

After the nineteen seventies, five species — cod, haddock, tuna, salmon and shrimp — rose to roughly eighty percent of recipes, and other species dropped.

The shift lined up with falling United Kingdom catches, rising imports, supermarket growth and frozen standardized products.

Early recipes stressed domestic skills and whole-fish preparation.

Later ones emphasized convenience and status.

Herring, oyster and lobster remain available, yet they receive far less attention in published guidance.

Incorrect outputs from medical chatbots can prompt patient decisions whose origins rarely show up in clinical records.

An analysis discussed on r/science follows how those errors move through self-directed actions that patients may not report as coming from automated advice.

Standard medical documentation rarely records the source of information that led to a particular choice.

Attributing harm is hard when the first recommendation leaves no clear audit trail.

The gap complicates individual case review and broader safety monitoring of consumer-facing tools.

Dr. Seamus O'Mahony, a gastroenterologist with over thirty years in National Health Service, or NHS, hospitals, argues that drugs, stem cells and epigenetic reprogramming have not delayed aging in humans.

The Telegraph reports his view that diet and exercise reduce disease risk and extend healthy years, and that the distinction matters for claims about outwitting aging.

His new book examines the cultural and financial momentum around longevity summits and biotech investment.

He first encountered the movement at the twenty twenty-four Longevity Summit in Dublin, where Aubrey de Grey presented ideas about avoiding age-related death through technology.

O'Mahony observes that older people were once valued for knowledge, yet now often live in nursing homes where death is feared more intensely.

The book interviews leading figures and asks whether the billion-dollar industry rests on demonstrated human results.

Dr. David Sinclair notes that nearly every adult cell still holds the genetic instructions used to build an arm before birth.

Lifespan.com describes how those instructions produced bones of the right length, movable joints, muscles, tendons, blood vessels, skin and nerves connected to the spine.

The body ran that construction twice during embryonic development, without external scaffolds or models.

In adults the same instructions stay silent after limb loss, and wounds close through scar tissue instead.

Adult cells retain the full genome yet do not restart that morphogenetic sequence after amputation.

Sinclair's work tests whether partial cellular rejuvenation can reactivate those developmental programs in mature tissues.
