### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Gene therapy via AAV eases lipodystrophy symptoms in mice — University of Aberdeen**
   Researchers at the Rowett Institute tested an adeno-associated virus carrying leptin instructions in mice with generalised lipodystrophy. A single injection produced significant health gains that lasted at least two months, including reduced fatty liver, lower diabetes markers, and decreased hunger. The disorder limits the body’s ability to store fat and causes a rare form of diabetes, fatty liver disease, persistent hunger and chronic pain. The only current therapy requires lifelong daily leptin injections that are painful for patients lacking subcutaneous fat, costly, and must be refrigerated. Lead researcher Dr Nadine Sommer noted the effects match those of daily leptin injections in patients. Lower doses are now under test before human translation, which would require a different AAV vector. The work received support from the East of Scotland Bioscience Doctoral Training Partnership, BBSRC and Diabetes UK. Patient groups Lipodystrophy UK and Lipodystrophy United welcomed the findings as a step toward reducing treatment burden. Source: [abdn.ac.uk](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/news/26573/)

2. **Genetic mosaic explains varied beak shapes in Darwin's finches — Phys.org**
   Researchers examined insect-foraging finches in forested Galápagos areas rather than the more-studied seed-eating ground finches. They found genetic mosaics that help account for the range of beak forms suited to different food sources. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-genetic-mosaic-diverse-darwin-finches.html)

3. **Tobacco plants engineered to produce Taxol precursor — Phys.org**
   Scientists mapped the biosynthetic steps for paclitaxel, a widely used cancer drug, with the goal of shifting production from slow yew trees to faster-growing tobacco. The work targets cheaper and more scalable manufacturing as cancer diagnoses rise globally. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-recipe-world-important-cancer-drugs.html)

4. **Nanoparticles restore mitochondrial function to aid bone repair in diabetic rats — Phys.org**
   A research team developed nanoparticles that repair damaged mitochondria in bone tissue. In diabetic rat models, the particles improved regeneration where standard healing is impaired by the disease. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-nanoparticles-mitochondria-boost-bone-regeneration.html)

5. **Prickly pear cactus switches water-use strategies with rainfall pulses — Phys.org**
   Ben-Gurion University and Volcani Institute researchers showed the cactus activates distinct physiological modes to survive long droughts and rapidly exploit brief rains. The dual strategy allows efficient carbon gain during wet periods while conserving resources in dry spells. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-cactus-gears-pear.html)

6. **Single gene links petal color and scent production in petunias — Phys.org**
   Disrupting one gene altered both pigment pathways and the blend of volatile scent compounds. Some vanilla- and clove-associated volatiles increased, showing the same cellular machinery influences both traits. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-hidden-link-scent-revealed.html)

7. **Electron microscopy maps poxvirus replication core — Phys.org**
   Institute of Science and Technology Austria scientists imaged the internal structure of poxvirus cores. Gene-copying enzymes sit in dormant pairs beside export portals in the shell, ready to transcribe viral genes once the virus enters a host cell. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-electron-microscopy-reveals-headquarters-poxviruses.html)

8. **Physics-guided AI fills gaps in fluid-flow imaging data — Phys.org**
   University of California, Irvine engineers combined fundamental fluid equations with machine learning to reconstruct complete flow fields from sparse or noisy measurements. The method improves accuracy for medical imaging of blood movement where sensors capture only partial data. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-physics-ai-reconstructs-fluid-sparse.html)

9. **Music therapy lowers blood pressure during angiography — Eric Topol**
   A randomized trial assigned 100 patients undergoing coronary angiography with or without PCI to music before and during the procedure. The music group showed a 13 mm lower post-procedure systolic blood pressure and reduced heart rate. Source: [t.co](https://t.co/OAAwILbZ0x)

10. **Scotland’s food SMEs face barriers to cold-chain decarbonisation funding — Phys.org**
    Heriot-Watt University researchers interviewed Scottish food producers and wholesalers and conducted six case studies ranging from family-run producers to island wholesalers. SMEs make up around 95 percent of Scotland’s food and drink sector, and the study found a mismatch between available grants and the upfront investment needs of small firms. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-funding-smes-tackle-energy.html)

**Genetic immune susceptibility model for Epstein-Barr virus and multiple sclerosis: PNAS**
   A new experimental model shows that specific immune-system gene variants determine why only a small fraction of the 90 percent of people infected with Epstein-Barr virus develop multiple sclerosis. The study identifies genetic factors that allow the virus to trigger autoimmune damage in susceptible individuals. Source: [pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2606486123)

### Science Deep Dive: Why Playing a Violin Does Not Physically Improve Its Sound
Most players assume regular use gradually loosens wood fibers or alters resonances so an instrument literally opens up and sounds better over months or years. A controlled six-month trial led by a CNRS researcher tested that belief with a new violin played daily by a professional soloist. At the end the player reported the instrument felt easier to play and its tone had improved. Yet repeated physical and acoustic measurements detected no measurable change in the wood, varnish or vibrational properties that could explain the perceived difference. The result points instead to the player’s own adaptation in touch, bow pressure and auditory expectation. One concrete implication is that claims of “breaking in” an instrument rest on subjective experience rather than detectable material shifts. Future work could test whether longer periods or different playing styles produce measurable effects, or whether listener panels blind to playing history reach the same judgments. Players might therefore focus practice time on their own technique rather than expecting the wood itself to change.
