### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Insect-borne diseases cluster by land use in Amazon** — Phys.org
   Researchers from Oxford’s Environmental Change Institute mapped disease patterns across the Brazilian Amazon and found they align with specific combinations of deforestation, agriculture, and rural economies rather than occurring randomly. The study shows distinct regional signatures for different insect vectors tied to how people modify landscapes. These patterns emerge from interactions between environmental change and local economic activities. Follow-up work will test whether targeted land-management changes can reduce transmission hotspots.

2. **Calls grow to integrate Indigenous knowledge into conservation genomics** — Phys.org
   Genomic tools now allow direct influence over species and ecosystems, prompting researchers to examine how Indigenous data rights and ecological knowledge should shape their application. The discussion highlights historical human roles in ecosystems and questions who controls genomic data derived from Indigenous lands. Current frameworks often overlook these governance issues. Next steps include developing protocols that embed Indigenous oversight into project design from the outset.

3. **Room-temperature single-photon source fits in standard rack** — Phys.org
   KRISS engineers built a compact, plug-and-play device that generates single photons without cryogenic cooling and operates once powered on. The 19-inch rack format removes the need for specialized lab infrastructure. This moves quantum light sources toward practical deployment outside research settings. Further engineering will focus on integrating the source with existing quantum communication hardware.

4. **Rediscovered fossil clarifies megalodon life history** — Phys.org
   A long-missing specimen allowed researchers to reconstruct growth rates and life stages of the extinct shark across its range. The analysis shows how the animal’s size and habitat use changed through ontogeny. Museum collections can still yield new data when specimens resurface. Additional fossils will help refine estimates of population structure before extinction.

5. **Small-molecule switches control CRISPR editing in tissues** — Phys.org
   Shenzhen Institutes researchers created PRINCE and Little Prince systems that activate CRISPR only in the presence of specific drug inducers while remaining silent otherwise. The dual-control design was tested in living tissues to limit off-target activity. This approach addresses safety concerns in therapeutic genome editing. Ongoing work will optimize delivery and test longer-term stability in disease models.

6. **Combined posters raise share of higher-welfare groceries** — Phys.org
   University of Bonn researchers tested two animal-welfare label posters in a virtual supermarket and found the largest shift toward higher-standard products when both posters appeared together. Each poster alone produced smaller but measurable changes in participant choices. The effect was tracked through actual items placed in the virtual cart. Real-world trials are planned to check whether the pattern holds outside the lab.

7. **Free-text descriptions plus LLMs expose decision drivers** — Phys.org
   Teams from TU Dresden, Max Planck, and Basel combined observed choices with participants’ own written explanations of their reasoning. The method reveals motivations that choice data alone cannot capture. Machine-learning analysis of the text identifies recurring patterns across individuals. Future studies will apply the approach to larger and more diverse decision contexts.

8. **Engineered wheat produces giant starch granules** — Phys.org
   Scientists modified wheat to form supersized starch granules inside the grain. The change opens possibilities for altered digestibility and new industrial uses of the starch. The work demonstrates precise control over a key storage compound through targeted biological engineering. Yield and processing trials will determine commercial viability.

9. **Ancient DNA links Scythian elites across steppe sites** — Phys.org
   A Science Advances study combined genetics, archaeology, and anthropology to show that political power among Scythian elites passed through extended family lineages spanning multiple burial grounds. The data indicate inherited authority rather than purely local status. This revises earlier views of nomadic social structure. Additional genomes from under-sampled regions will test how widespread the pattern was.

10. **Mice and humans share core olfactory sampling strategy** — Phys.org
    Northwestern researchers found that mice, like humans, use single deliberate sniffs to probe their surroundings rather than only rapid sampling. The finding overturns the assumption that rodents rely exclusively on high-frequency sniffing. Electrophysiological recordings confirmed the controlled single-sniff behavior. The shared mechanism suggests conserved neural circuits for active sensing across mammals.

11. **Device keeps donor eyeballs viable for potential transplants** — MIT Technology Review
    A perfusion system maintains metabolic activity in whole eyes retrieved from deceased donors, slowing the rapid degeneration that previously blocked transplantation. Earlier attempts failed because the eye lost function before surgery could succeed. The approach supplies oxygen and nutrients directly to retinal tissue. Human trials will assess whether restored eyes can transmit visual signals after grafting.

12. **Eight-hour feeding window extends male mouse lifespan** — Lifespan.io
    Restricting food access to an 8-hour period increased median lifespan 12% in male mice. The gain appears linked to voluntary reduction in total calories rather than timing alone. Female mice showed smaller or no effects under the same protocol. Researchers are now testing whether the benefit persists when calories are matched across groups.

13. **Marine animals have shrunk during past warming episodes** — Phys.org
    A multi-university study of the fossil record documents consistent body-size reduction in mussels, crustaceans, and fish during intervals of strong global temperature rise. The “Lilliput effect” appears across hundreds of millions of years and is most pronounced in the warmest phases. The pattern offers a long-term biological signal of climate stress. Modern monitoring programs will check whether contemporary warming is producing similar size shifts.

14. **Pangenome methods boost gene-expression trait mapping** — bioRxiv
    Analysis of 430 samples with deep RNA-seq showed that graph-based methods detect expression quantitative trait loci involving structural variants and multiallelic indels missed by linear reference approaches. Power improved at 7.9% of genes, with 1.8% showing at least 50% gains in significance. The work also lifted median variance explained in expression prediction models from 10.1% to 12.5%. Larger cohorts will test translation to complex disease traits.

15. **Effective population size estimates biased in clonal fungi** — bioRxiv
    Review of 19 populations across eight genetic datasets revealed that large clones and unclear population boundaries systematically distort Ne estimates in ectomycorrhizal fungi. Both linkage-disequilibrium and sibship-frequency methods proved sensitive to these factors. The findings question current use of Ne as a genetic indicator for conservation in species with partial clonality. Refined sampling designs are needed before the metric can reliably inform policy.

### Planetterrian Spotlight
The perfusion device that sustains whole donor eyes moves regenerative ophthalmology past the viability barrier that halted earlier transplant attempts. By preserving retinal metabolism after retrieval, it creates a practical window for surgical reconnection and functional testing. If vision can be restored, the approach would address irreversible corneal and retinal blindness in patients lacking other options. The next milestone is a controlled human pilot measuring signal transmission from the grafted retina.

### Science Deep Dive: How Marine Life Responds to Warming
Most people assume that warmer conditions favor larger body sizes in animals, yet the fossil record shows the opposite pattern during past warming events. Right now, as ocean temperatures continue to rise, many marine species are already expressing the same size reduction seen in ancient warming intervals. A single memorable number: body size in some mollusk lineages dropped by up to 20% during the strongest warming pulses. The Lilliput effect appears tied to metabolic stress and oxygen limitation rather than simple temperature rules. One practical takeaway is that fisheries and conservation models should incorporate expected size declines when projecting future yields and ecosystem roles.
