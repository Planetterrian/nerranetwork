Tryptophan takes up an allosteric pocket on the enzyme TDO2, according to Phys.org coverage of a Molecular Cell paper from the Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry.

Occupying that pocket keeps tryptophan breakdown at the right level so the cell does not burn through its own substrate.

Groups at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas and the de Duve Institute in Brussels joined that mapping.

Something most people get wrong is the idea that enzymes simply bind a metabolite and convert it until the substrate runs low, with little internal feedback of their own.

TDO2 in tryptophan metabolism faces exactly that risk, because the molecule it consumes is also the cue that should keep the pathway in range.

When tryptophan sits in the unexpected second location, it blocks access by the kinase CK2 and the ubiquitin ligase CUL1-beta-TrCP.

That blockade stops a two-step mark-up: phosphorylation first, then ubiquitination, the sequence that would tag TDO2 for destruction.

The protective contact holds catabolism in balance without any outside signal arriving at the cell.

Break the allosteric contact and steady-state levels of downstream metabolites that influence inflammation and neurological function could shift.

Mutating the second site while leaving the catalytic pocket intact is the experiment that would show how much of ordinary metabolic control hangs on that single step.

Preclinical results show tighter targeting of immune cells in the dermis than standard injection routes achieve.

Fungal volatile organic compounds, catalogued over twenty years in a Bioengineer.org review, can push plant growth, suppress pathogens, and serve as starting points for industrial chemicals.

Species-specific volatile profiles now cover hundreds of isolates.

Biofumigants and growth-promoting agents built from those profiles are meant to cut the need for synthetic pesticides.

The same body of work moves fungi out of the narrow roles of decomposer or pathogen and treats them as chemical signaling agents.

Francis Crick Institute scientists found a specialized lipid layer in the fruit-fly pupal case that limits water loss when temperature is high and humidity is low, in a study covered by Phys.org.

The barrier only holds so far; past its limit, survival falls off sharply.

Those limits mean some insect populations may struggle to keep up with the hotter, drier conditions climate projections describe.
