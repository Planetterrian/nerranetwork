A record-breaking hot drought from 2018 to 2019 caused widespread plant mortality in Uluru, Katachuta National Park, according to work covered by Fizz.org.
Researchers surveyed nine species over a mild spring and an extreme summer, measuring
leaf traits, water content, and carbon nitrogen ratios.
Anangu traditional owners co-developed the research aims, selected the species, conducted

Something most people get wrong is the idea that desert plants have full-proof defenses
against heat and drought, simply because they live in arid zones.
Plants that keep growing during dry spells maintain carbon uptake, but risk running out of water
when conditions intensify.
Plants that shut down growth conserve water, yet they can exhaust stored reserves if the
drought last too long. The team found that maintaining stable water levels inside leaves
was the single best predictor of survival after comparing multiple physiological traits,
including carbon nitrogen ratios and overall leaf morphology.
Leaf relative water content emerged as that strongest statistical predictor. Plants that
experience large reductions in leaf water content suffered the most severe dieback, regardless
of their primary drought strategy. Species that lost this internal water balance suffered
the highest dieback once temperatures and water deficits reached the levels recorded in
2018 to 2019.
Neither drought tolerance nor drought avoidance conferred reliable protection at those combined
extremes.
One practical step that follows is supporting community nurseries that propagate the more
resilient drought avoiding species, while also advancing cultural burning practices that
reduce fuel loads and competition.
Future work will test whether restoring traditional fire regimes can reduce competition and improve
water availability for those drought-avoiding species identified in the survey.
Continued monitoring will show whether these local actions can buffer the next extreme drought,
