Researchers at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology ran molecular simulations in the STEPS program and mapped BK channel evolution across species.

Each channel needs four calcium ions to open fully, and the models built that binding step in from the start.

Lower-conductance versions force more channels to switch on to reach the same total current, so they compete for a limited pool of free calcium.

That contest raises variability in when a neuron fires, which the team examined in Purkinje cells that need submillisecond precision for motor control and sensory processing.

Evolutionary mapping showed higher conductance tracking phylogenetic proximity to mammals.

The study in Communications Biology, covered by Phys.org, simplifies the cell to one neuron type.

Erik De Schutter noted that BK channels regulate firing across neurons and that the models offer a possible answer to why the high-conductance form developed.

Uppsala University adapted Cell Painting to transparent three-dimensional minispheres of bowel cancer cells and screened fifty-two oncology drugs.

Two of those agents, five-fluorouracil and olaparib, produced nearly identical profiles in conventional two-dimensional layers.

Imaged cell by cell inside the spheres, they triggered distinct morphological changes, in work published in Nature Communications and reported by Phys.org.

The approach keeps single-cell resolution instead of averaging an entire sample into one reading.

Collaboration with AstraZeneca confirmed the method transfers to other laboratories, and the team released all images, data, and code.

Something most people get wrong is the idea that a flat layer of cancer cells on plastic is a faithful preview of how a drug will act inside living tissue.

Cells in the body sit in three-dimensional clusters, where neighbors, nutrient gradients, and mechanical forces differ sharply from those on a dish.

The minispheres were made optically clear with a sugar and glycerol solution so individual cells deep inside each cluster stayed visible under the microscope.

That clearing step let the team apply Cell Painting, which labels nuclei, mitochondria, and internal scaffolding, across thousands of cells per sphere.

Microscopes then captured thousands of shape and structure measurements from cells that would otherwise have been hidden in the core.

Follow-up gene-expression data pointed to stronger activation of the p53 stress-response pathway by five-fluorouracil even though it produced less detectable DNA damage than olaparib.

That split in pathway engagement was invisible when the same agents were scored on a flat layer.

Preserving spatial context therefore changes which cellular features a compound appears to alter.

The workflow runs in standard plates, so other groups can now test whether additional drug pairs separate once cells are read one by one inside a cluster.

Every image, measurement, and analysis script has been released so the comparisons can be repeated or extended.

Screening pipelines that still rely on two-dimensional readouts may advance or discard compounds on incomplete cellular information.
