Most people assume a meteor simply burns up or evaporates in a bright streak of heat and light.

What the rock is actually doing, in video and photographs of seventy-five documented falls, is melting and breaking apart.

Straightforward evaporation is not what decides how much mass makes it to the ground.

A shock wave forms as the body first hits the upper atmosphere, and the surface starts to glow.

Mass loss then shifts to melting, and the airflow strips molten droplets off the rock.

Up to forty percent of the original mass can leave that way once the fireball steadies around sixty kilometers altitude. 

Pieces start to break away at air pressures only about one-fifth the compressive strength measured later in recovered meteorites.

Heating on the way in, plus collisions the rock already took in space, leave weaknesses, so fragments come off well before peak dynamic stress.

An intact rear face holds together long enough to throw a low-pressure wake behind it.

Smaller shards get funneled in that wake and reach the ground in narrow strips instead of a wide scatter.
