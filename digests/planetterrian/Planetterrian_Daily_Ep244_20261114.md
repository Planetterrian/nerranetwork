### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

1. **Yale analysis finds drugs and exercise lower epigenetic age markers — Science Daily**
   Researchers at Yale School of Medicine pooled data from 51 clinical studies that tracked more than 110 DNA methylation biomarkers, including 16 major epigenetic clocks. Prescription medications and structured lifestyle interventions produced consistent reductions in these biological-age estimates. Over-the-counter supplements showed no reliable effect across the same set of clocks. Lead author Raghav Sehgal noted that the work marks the first demonstration that certain therapies produce measurable shifts in these markers. The study drew on trials that measured methylation patterns before and after each intervention. All data came from blood samples analyzed with established epigenetic-clock algorithms. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261112230452.htm)

2. **University of Florida study shows repeated forced smiles worsen mood — ScienceDaily**
   More than 1,100 participants completed tasks that required them to smile either once or repeatedly under controlled conditions. A single artificial smile produced a small positive shift in self-reported mood. Repeating the action erased the benefit or reversed it, leaving participants feeling worse than at baseline. The effect held after researchers controlled for participants’ initial emotional state. The work was conducted at the University of Florida.

3. **T-cell enzyme discovery separates organ aging from immune aging in mice — [@EricTopol](https://x.com/EricTopol)**
   Researchers identified a T-cell enzyme whose absence slowed aging across multiple organs while leaving immune function intact. The finding emerged from mouse models in which the enzyme was selectively deleted. Organs in the deficient animals showed reduced age-related decline compared with controls. The study appears in Immunity. Source: [cell.com](https://www.cell.com/immunity/fulltext/S1074-7613%2826%2900440-1?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS1074761326004401%3Fshowall%3Dtrue)

4. **Frontiers study finds social ties rarely tracked in agricultural scaling research — Phys.org**
   A review of climate-oriented agricultural papers showed that partnerships and institutional links appear often in project descriptions yet are seldom measured against actual scaling outcomes. The authors examined how social relationships influence whether innovations spread beyond pilot sites. They conclude that explicit documentation of these relationships would improve monitoring and evaluation of scaling efforts. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-role-social-relationships-scaling-agricultural.html)

5. **Penn researchers uncover bacterial mechanism that breaches host barriers — Phys.org**
   Scientists at Penn Dental Medicine mapped a previously unknown molecular switch that lets bacteria rapidly compromise epithelial defenses. The mechanism allows pathogens to alter host-cell junctions without triggering immediate immune detection. The work identifies a potential target for interventions that preserve barrier integrity during early infection. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-hidden-master-bacteria-breach-body.html)

6. **FIU trial finds standard ADHD classroom supports show limited academic benefit — Phys.org**
   Researchers at Florida International University tested common accommodations such as extended time, frequent breaks, and quiet workspaces in students with ADHD. None of the supports produced measurable gains in task completion or accuracy relative to control conditions. The study tracked performance on standardized academic measures across multiple sessions. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-common-adhd-classroom-accommodations-students.html)

7. **Microbe-derived molecule raises NAD and reverses gut damage in lab models — Medical Xpress**
   A compound produced by intestinal bacteria increased NAD levels and restored tissue integrity in models of gut injury. The effect occurred through direct metabolic support rather than immune modulation. Researchers traced the molecule’s activity to a specific bacterial biosynthetic pathway. Source: [medicalxpress.com](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-microbe-molecule-reverse-gut-boosting.html)

8. **Tsukuba team isolates antimicrobial peptides from fruit-fly sleep protein — Phys.org**
   Short peptides derived from the Drosophila Nemuri protein killed drug-resistant bacteria in culture. The peptides retained activity against strains that had developed resistance to conventional antibiotics. The work was published in Nature Communications. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-fruit-fly-protein-yields-peptides.html)

9. **Large cohort study links blood NAD levels to heart disease rather than chronological age — Medical Xpress**
   Measurements from more than 1,000 adults showed that circulating NAD tracked cardiovascular disease status more closely than age itself. Participants with established heart disease displayed lower NAD independent of their birth year. The association persisted after statistical adjustment for common risk factors. Source: [medicalxpress.com](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-blood-nad-track-heart-disease.html)

10. **Lifespan.io reports serotonin neurons curb physical activity in aged mice — Lifespan.io**
    Elevated serotonin output from a defined brain region suppressed dopamine release and reduced voluntary movement in older mice. Selective ablation of the serotonin-producing cells restored activity levels. The study identifies a neural circuit that contributes to age-related inactivity. Source: [lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/serotonin-signals-suppress-physical-activity-in-aging-mice/)

11. **Study identifies alternative genetic route to larger primate brains — Phys.org**
    Comparative genomic analysis revealed sequence changes that increase progenitor-cell proliferation in the developing primate cortex. The alterations differ from previously known human-specific variants yet produce comparable expansion of cortical layers. The work examined multiple primate species to trace the evolutionary timing of the changes.

12. **Science Advances paper ties mountain growth pulses to rises in biological diversity — Phys.org**
    Two distinct episodes of rapid orogeny across Earth’s history coincided with major increases in fossil diversity. The authors link heightened erosion and nutrient runoff from the new ranges to expanded marine and terrestrial habitats. The correlation spans the Proterozoic and Phanerozoic eons. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-mountain-growth-spurts-complex-life.html)

### Planetterrian Spotlight
**Hidden 'master switch' allows bacteria to breach the body's defenses — Phys.org**
Researchers at Penn Dental Medicine mapped a molecular mechanism that enables bacteria to rapidly compromise epithelial barriers without immediate immune activation. The switch alters host-cell junctions through a previously unrecognized pathway. Experiments showed that blocking the mechanism preserved barrier function in cell and tissue models. The finding points to a possible new class of anti-infective strategies focused on host-side protection rather than direct bacterial killing. One open question is whether the same pathway operates across different epithelial surfaces in living animals.

### Science Deep Dive: How forcing a smile affects mood
Most people assume that deliberately smiling will lift their mood, yet a University of Florida experiment found the opposite when the action is repeated. In daily life this matters because many of us smile through meetings, commutes, or family photos without realizing the cumulative effect. The study of more than 1,100 adults showed that a single forced smile produced a small mood increase while repeated smiles erased the gain or left participants feeling worse than before. The mechanism appears tied to the brain registering the mismatch between the expression and the underlying emotional state, which grows more salient with repetition. When the action is performed only once, the simple motor feedback can still nudge affect upward; when it continues, the repeated mismatch registers as inauthentic and subtracts from mood. Listeners who notice themselves smiling repeatedly on difficult days might experiment with pausing the expression for a minute or two to test whether the pattern holds in their own routine. Further trials that vary the duration and social context of forced smiling would clarify how widely the reversal applies.
