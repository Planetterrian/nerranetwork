Washington State University researchers, in a Phys.org report on work published in Scientific Reports, exposed newly emerged honey bees from six colonies to three temperature regimes in enclosures.

Bees held under continuous elevated temperature adapted with lower damage than the shock groups.

The team tested both optimal and supplemented diets across groups of one hundred fifty bees each.

Hormesis appears to explain why rapid temperature change produced greater effects than steady stress.

Something most people get wrong is the idea that a dog's gut microbes are an isolated pet-health matter that stops at the bowl.

A study of hundreds of dogs across four nations found that pet owners' daily habits influence the microbial communities inside their dogs.

Yesterday it was wastewater bacteria from eight Ohio cities serving one point three million people, with associations to local rates of obesity, high blood pressure, asthma, and depression; today's canine work adds that owner routines shape the microbes living in animals that share those surroundings.

The research underscores how shared environments link human and canine health rather than treating those ecosystems as separate.

Everyday surroundings and rapid change both quietly reshape biology across species, from a kennel to a hive.

Talon punctures on skulls and characteristic shoulder-bone marks turned up on adult male baboons in Chad's Ennedi plateau, documented by University of St Andrews researchers in a Phys.org report.

A United Nations Environment Programme and World Health Organization report estimates annual health and environmental costs from endocrine-disrupting chemical, or EDC, exposure exceed one trillion dollars.

The assessment reviewed thousands of studies on bisphenols, phthalates, and parabens and found the strongest links to neurological conditions and reproductive problems, including sperm decline.

Evidence also connects EDCs to rising rates of type two diabetes and asthma.

Workers in chemicals and recycling industries face the highest exposure, with one two thousand twenty-four wristband study detecting over twenty hazardous chemicals in Thai and Kenyan participants.

Roughly one thousand EDCs have been identified, though many remain untested.
