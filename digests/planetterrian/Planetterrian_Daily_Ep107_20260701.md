### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Antihypertensive benefits do not compound with longer use — Nature Medicine**
   An individual participant data meta-analysis pooled 51 randomized trials to examine cardiovascular outcomes under prolonged blood-pressure lowering. The results challenge the prior assumption that extended treatment duration produces progressively larger risk reductions. The analysis focused on major cardiovascular disease events rather than surrogate markers. No additional benefit accrued beyond the initial treatment period captured in the trials. Future work will examine whether specific patient subgroups show different duration patterns.

2. **34 blood circular RNAs predict Alzheimer’s conversion — Nature Medicine**
   Researchers tested a model built on 34 circular RNAs measured in blood across large cohorts. The panel predicted progression to symptomatic Alzheimer’s disease and outperformed both pTau217 assays and amyloid-PET imaging. The work adds a blood-based option for early identification of individuals at risk. Because the signature is detectable before clinical symptoms, it could support earlier enrollment in prevention studies. Next steps include validation in independent longitudinal cohorts.

3. **Innate cell responsiveness linked to symptomatic influenza — Nature Medicine**
   A controlled human influenza challenge study profiled local and systemic immune responses in participants. Individuals with heightened innate cell responsiveness before infection were more likely to develop symptoms once exposed. The finding separates pre-existing immune tone from the adaptive response that follows infection. It suggests that baseline innate reactivity may shape clinical outcome more than previously modeled. Follow-up studies will test whether modulating this tone alters infection severity.

4. **CRISPRi atlas maps transcriptional control in human stem cells — Nature Biotechnology**
   A genome-scale CRISPR interference screen generated a regulatory map across human induced pluripotent stem cells. The atlas identifies functional relationships between transcription factors and target genes at pluripotent state. It supplies a reference dataset for dissecting how gene networks maintain or exit pluripotency. The screen covered thousands of genes and produced quantitative perturbation effects. Researchers can now use the map to prioritize candidates for directed differentiation protocols.

5. **AI model helps prove decade-old physics conjecture — Phys.org**
   Two theoretical physicists collaborated with the Claude AI system to resolve a mathematical relation in the physics of complex systems. The 10-year-old jamming conjecture had resisted prior analytic attempts. The published proof appears in the Journal of Statistical Mechanics: Theory and Experiment. The work demonstrates how large language models can contribute to formal derivations when guided by domain experts. Further applications in statistical mechanics are now being explored.

6. **Three Oxa1 paralogs divide labor in trypanosome mitochondria — bioRxiv**
   Trypanosoma brucei encodes three Oxa1 insertases instead of the one or two found in most eukaryotes. Knock-out of each paralog followed by proteomics showed distinct effects on respiratory-chain complexes. TbOxa1-1 primarily affects complexes I and IV, while TbOxa1-2 is required for complex III and ATP synthase. TbOxa1-3 produces only minor phenotypes. The division of labor matches the highly divergent mitochondrial membrane proteome of this parasite.

7. **ConfDock adds atom-level uncertainty estimates to docking — bioRxiv**
   A conformal prediction framework called ConfDock produces prediction intervals for ligand poses at individual atom resolution. The method combines graph neural network quantile estimation with split conformal calibration across 238 protein-ligand complexes. It reduced mean interval width by 57.2 percent on average compared with standard split conformal prediction while preserving coverage guarantees. The approach works across four different docking algorithms. It supplies a practical way to flag poses whose reliability varies with local protein-ligand context.

8. **Sortase method enriches ubiquitinated proteins for proteomics — bioRxiv**
   A bacterial transpeptidase was used to attach biotin tags selectively to N-terminal diglycine remnants after protein digestion. The approach captured previously ubiquitinated proteins, including an autoubiquitination site on the E2 enzyme Ubc13. Elution can be performed with guanidinium or by exploiting sortase reversibility. A bifunctional peptide version allows trypsin-compatible release for downstream mass spectrometry. The technique targets non-canonical ubiquitination sites that antibody-based methods may miss.

9. **High-affinity nanobody turns mNeonGreen into multifunctional handle — bioRxiv**
   The single-domain antibody sdAb(mNG) binds mNeonGreen with 0.39 nM affinity, revealed by a 1.26-angstrom crystal structure. One reagent supports immunoprecipitation, direct immunofluorescence, super-resolution imaging, and intracellular relocalization when expressed as an intrabody. In primary neurons it visualizes synaptic vesicle transport; in zebrafish it triggers cell-autonomous depletion of an mNG-tagged transcription factor. The nanobody therefore converts a passive fluorescent tag into a programmable affinity module.

10. **Submillimeter Array captures earliest GRB observations at mm wavelengths — Phys.org**
    The Submillimeter Array on Maunakea demonstrated rapid-response capability by observing a gamma-ray burst within minutes of its discovery on January 26, 2026. These are the earliest millimeter and submillimeter data ever recorded for such an event. The observations were enabled by a new fastest-response system at the facility. Results are published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. The demonstration opens time-domain astronomy at these frequencies to routine follow-up.

11. **Earth-abundant MoS2 catalyst converts methane at low temperature — Phys.org**
    Molybdenum disulfide was shown to produce methyl peroxide and other liquid oxygenates from methane below 100 °C. The work, published in Advanced Functional Materials, used minimal modification of an industrial catalyst. Methyl peroxide serves as a precursor for methanol, an energy-dense liquid fuel. The process avoids high-temperature routes that currently dominate methane upgrading. Scale-up and selectivity improvements are the next reported targets.

12. **Future drought will cut grassland carbon uptake more than expected — Phys.org**
    Combined warming, elevated CO2, and drought were tested together rather than in isolation on grassland plots. The study, published in Science Advances, found drought effects on carbon uptake were amplified under future climate conditions. Earlier single-factor experiments had underestimated the interaction. The Innsbruck team quantified the additional loss across multiple climate drivers. Longer-term field trials will test whether plant community shifts alter the magnitude.

13. **DNA protector gene loss reveals cancer cell weaknesses — Phys.org**
    Molecular biologist Simon Boulton’s group studies how cells detect and repair DNA exposed during replication. Mutation or loss of key protector genes leaves portions of the genome vulnerable to damage. The work maps repair pathways that become essential once protector function is removed. These synthetic-lethal relationships are being tested as potential therapeutic entry points. Ongoing screens aim to identify additional context-specific vulnerabilities.

14. **Quantum computer reproduces string breaking with 104 qubits — Phys.org**
    A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory scientist used a remote IBM quantum processor to simulate hadronization in a simplified model. The calculation reproduced string breaking, a key step in forming hadrons from quarks. Results appear in Physical Review D. The demonstration shows quantum hardware can already tackle selected particle-physics processes beyond classical reach. Larger-scale simulations remain the stated next milestone.

15. **Black locust peptides reprogram symbiotic bacteria for nitrogen fixation — Phys.org**
    An international team identified a novel family of small proteins in Robinia pseudoacacia that direct its bacterial partners to fix nitrogen without killing the symbionts. Published in Science Advances, the mechanism differs from known legume strategies. The peptides keep the bacteria alive while shifting metabolism toward fixation. The finding expands understanding of how perennial plants sustain long-term symbioses. Researchers will test whether similar peptides exist in other woody species.

### Science Deep Dive: Mitochondrial Division of Labor
Most people picture mitochondria as having one standard protein-insertion machine that handles every membrane complex the same way. In reality, some organisms run multiple specialized versions of that machine, each tuned to different complexes. Right now, inside your own cells, the Oxa1 insertase is threading newly made proteins into the inner mitochondrial membrane to keep oxidative phosphorylation running. Trypanosomes carry three distinct Oxa1 paralogs; losing just one selectively dismantles either complex I and IV or complex III and ATP synthase. One memorable detail is that a single parasite species evolved this three-way split to manage a membrane proteome far more divergent than the one in human mitochondria. The practical takeaway is that future therapies aimed at mitochondrial disorders may need to target specific insertase paralogs rather than assuming a single pathway can be modulated uniformly.
