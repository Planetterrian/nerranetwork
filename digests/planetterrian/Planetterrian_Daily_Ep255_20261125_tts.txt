Most people assume that molecular clusters grow steadily more stable as extra units join, yet glycine does the opposite and locks in at a single size.

Clusters of the amino acid reach a sharp peak of stability at precisely six molecules.

Each glycine switches from its neutral form to a charged zwitterion once that six-member cluster assembles.

The charged form lets neighbors hold one another with stronger electrostatic bonds.

That internal rearrangement happens only at that specific size.

Smaller groupings and larger ones fall apart more readily because they never make the same switch.

Controlled cluster experiments isolated different numbers of glycine units and compared them side by side.

The same structural flip may operate in other small peptides, which would change how chemists predict which assemblies persist in solution or on surfaces.

Knowing the exact size of the switch lets them design more durable molecular scaffolds without adding extra stabilizing groups.
