### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Smallpox outbreak killed up to 220,000 Indigenous Australians in 1789 — Phys.org**
   Groundbreaking modeling traced the epidemic to the First Fleet arrival in Sydney. The outbreak swept through First Nations communities shortly after British ships landed. Researchers documented a devastating demographic impact that altered resistance to colonization and land management capacity. The effects remain measurable in descendant populations today. The study reconstructs transmission patterns from historical records and population estimates.

2. **Teens in US and UK grew more ambitious about careers over two decades — Phys.org**
   Researchers at Penn State tracked shifts in occupational expectations from 2000 onward. Family socioeconomic background influenced choices differently across job types. American students aiming for the highest-status careers showed relatively small socioeconomic gaps. The patterns held across both countries despite differing education systems. The work highlights how aspirations have decoupled from some traditional background constraints.

3. **Isotopic analysis reveals diet, gender roles and migration in 2,700-year-old Italian community — Phys.org**
   Teams from the Universities of Córdoba and Pisa examined human remains from a necropolis. Dietary traces showed clear differences by gender and social status. Mobility patterns emerged from isotopic signatures in the bones. Funerary practices reflected status through food-related symbolism. The study reconstructs daily life patterns that parallel recurring social structures across time.

4. **Seawater around reefs carries early chemical and microbial signals of ecosystem change — Phys.org**
   Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution researchers applied a medical-style diagnostic approach to coral systems. The surrounding water contains detectable markers before visible damage appears. The method parallels blood or urine tests used in human medicine. Early detection could allow intervention before bleaching or collapse becomes severe. The work demonstrates minimally invasive monitoring of reef health trajectories.

5. **Water's local asymmetry governs proton hopping between molecules — Phys.org**
   An international team led by Heidelberg University ran the most complex quantum simulations to date. Protons move by hopping across clusters of six water molecules rather than drifting as isolated particles. The modeling incorporated sites in Cambridge, Bochum, Dijon and Heidelberg. The asymmetry creates directional preferences that standard diffusion models miss. The framework explains transport efficiency in aqueous environments at atomic resolution.

6. **Archaeologists vary widely in how they document and preserve excavation data — Phys.org**
   A qualitative study of Catalan researchers found inconsistent practices across document types and formats. Excavations produce large volumes of mixed records that hold scientific value. Current methods leave much of this heritage poorly organized or at risk. The Journal of Documentation paper calls for standardized approaches suited to digital workflows. The findings reflect broader challenges as archaeology shifts toward larger digital datasets.

7. **New hybrid indicators achieve 55-fold calcium-dependent signal increase in red optical window — bioRxiv**
   Researchers developed HyBRIC and HyBRIC2 by combining luciferase activation with bioluminescence resonance energy transfer. The sensors were tested in HEK293T and HeLa cells with pyCTZ luciferin. HyBRIC2 improved brightness and responsiveness over the first version. The design enables excitation-free imaging with reduced tissue absorption in the red window. The approach offers a modular route to higher-contrast bioluminescent calcium reporters.

8. **Deep learning and evolutionary models generate selective antimicrobial peptide variants — bioRxiv**
   The team applied Evolutionary-Scale Cambrian embeddings to pardaxin and produced single- and multi-mutant analogues. Principal component analysis combined with conventional design rules yielded candidates active against either E. coli or S. aureus. Molecular dynamics simulations linked alpha-helical stability to selective inner-membrane activity. Experimental validation confirmed the predicted specificity patterns. The work demonstrates how evolutionary machine learning can expand the pool of narrow-spectrum agents.

9. **Aid fragmentation increases administrative corruption in developing regions — Phys.org**
   An SKKU-led team with collaborators from Chicago and Gothenburg used micro-level data across multiple agencies. The study is the first to quantify how simultaneous operation of many aid organizations correlates with higher corruption. Fragmentation creates overlapping oversight gaps that local actors can exploit. The findings point to coordination mechanisms as a potential mitigation lever. Data covered specific regions rather than national aggregates.

10. **Fatty acids and hydroxy acids together form more stable primitive membrane structures — Phys.org**
    Hebrew University researchers showed that mixtures of the two molecule types outperform either alone. The resulting assemblies resisted disruption better under early-Earth conditions. The work addresses how simple prebiotic chemistry could produce cell-like compartments. Dr. Moran Frenkel-Pinter and Dr. Rotem Edri led the experiments. The findings supply a cooperative mechanism for membrane emergence without modern lipids.

11. **Tropical intertidal snails already live near thermal limits under current conditions — Phys.org**
    Lingnan University and international collaborators tested tolerance in species exposed to direct sunlight. Warming further compresses the safety margin between current maxima and lethal thresholds. The experiments quantified how little additional temperature rise the animals can accommodate. Results highlight vulnerability in ecosystems already at the edge of habitable ranges. The study focused on species that cannot behaviorally escape peak heat.

12. **Alternating nutrient and starvation cycles stabilize self-destructive yeast survival strategy — Phys.org**
    Earth-Life Science Institute researchers combined population modeling with comparative experiments. The unusual behavior persists over evolutionary timescales only when environments fluctuate. Constant conditions allow the strategy to be outcompeted. The Journal of the Royal Society Interface paper explains long-term maintenance of an apparently costly trait. The mechanism relies on temporal variation rather than spatial structure.

13. **Seabirds that expend more energy breeding later migrate farther and raise more chicks — Phys.org**
    Nagoya University-led tracking showed increased reproductive output the following season. The same birds paid a survival cost. Researchers experimentally raised flight costs during breeding and followed individuals through migration and the next cycle. The tradeoff appears only after high-demand seasons. The pattern links current-year effort directly to future performance metrics.

14. **Statistical model predicts which Red Sea fish species will next reach the Mediterranean — Phys.org**
    Tel Aviv University researchers built the tool from traits of the more than 120 species already recorded since the Suez Canal opened. The model ranks likelihood based on biological and ecological characteristics. It identifies candidates for future invasion without waiting for first sightings. The approach offers an early-warning system for managers. More than 150 years of canal-mediated movement provide the training data.

15. **Children who own cellphones show lower reading comprehension regardless of school use — Phys.org**
    UCLA psychologists found ownership itself correlated with poorer scores on standardized measures. Bringing the device to school did not change the association. The study examined elementary-age students and controlled for other variables. Results suggest the presence of the device at home may affect reading habits or attention allocation. The work supports examining device access patterns beyond classroom policies.

### Planetterrian Spotlight
HyBRIC2 pairs luciferase activation with BRET inside one protein to deliver a 55-fold red-window calcium signal increase. The sensor resolves induced dynamics in mammalian cells without external excitation, removing photobleaching and autofluorescence limits that constrain fluorescent indicators. Because tissue absorption drops sharply in the red, the design opens longer-duration imaging in thicker samples or deeper structures. The modular architecture lets future variants tune brightness or kinetics while keeping the multiplicative sensing principle. Next steps include testing in intact neural circuits and comparing performance against existing red fluorescent probes in the same preparations.

### Science Deep Dive: Proton Transport in Water
Most people picture protons moving through water the way sodium ions drift in solution, carried along by bulk flow or simple diffusion. Right now, inside every cell and extracellular fluid in your body, that picture is wrong. Protons instead hop from one water molecule to the next across small clusters, and the direction and speed of each hop depend on fleeting local asymmetries in how the surrounding molecules are arranged. The latest quantum simulations show these hops occur within groups of six water molecules whose geometry creates a preferred path rather than random motion. One memorable detail: the process completes in timescales far shorter than typical molecular diffusion, allowing rapid pH buffering across membranes. The practical takeaway is that any model of acid-base balance, enzyme kinetics, or mitochondrial proton gradients must account for this hopping mechanism rather than treating protons as conventional solutes; future biophysical studies will likely refine how crowding or specific ions alter the asymmetry that makes hopping efficient.
