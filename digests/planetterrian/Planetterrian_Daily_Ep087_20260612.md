### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Electric fish pinpoint single timing hub for sensory predictions — Phys.org**
   Biologists at Washington University in St. Louis examined corollary discharge in electric fish, a copy of motor commands sent to sensory areas. The study, published in Current Biology, shows this signal routes through one central hub rather than distributed pathways. Fish use the mechanism to distinguish self-produced electric signals from external ones in milliseconds. The work clarifies how nervous systems solve the agency problem across vertebrates. Follow-up experiments will test whether the hub generalizes to mammalian auditory and visual systems.

2. **Single-photon catalyst reduces CO₂ while oxidizing biowaste — Phys.org**
   Researchers engineered a solar-driven material that uses one photon to drive two linked reactions. The catalyst simultaneously reduces carbon dioxide and oxidizes organic waste, yielding useful chemicals from both streams. The approach avoids separate energy inputs for each half-reaction. Performance data show stable operation under visible light without precious-metal co-catalysts. Next steps include scaling the material for continuous-flow reactors.

3. **Framework integrates local and global data for urban policy — Phys.org**
   The perspective outlines methods to combine fine-grained city measurements with Earth-system models. Authors emphasize translating interdisciplinary datasets into equitable policy without losing local context. The approach addresses gaps between climate projections and actionable urban interventions. Case examples focus on emissions, heat islands, and infrastructure resilience. Implementation will require new data-sharing standards across municipal agencies.

4. **Reprogramming trial delivers first glaucoma volunteer dosing — MIT Technology Review**
   Life Biosciences injected an experimental reprogramming therapy into the eye of a patient with glaucoma. The treatment aims to regenerate retinal ganglion cells by resetting epigenetic age in nerve tissue. Partial reprogramming avoids full induced pluripotent stem cell conversion to limit tumor risk. Early safety monitoring will track intraocular pressure and visual field changes. Additional cohorts are planned for other age-related eye conditions.

5. **Interoception maps the brain’s continuous internal sensing — MIT Technology Review**
   The hidden sense tracks heartbeat, gut tension, and respiratory effort without conscious attention. Signals travel via vagal and spinal pathways to insular and somatosensory cortices. Disruptions in interoceptive accuracy appear in anxiety, eating disorders, and certain chronic pain states. Training protocols that improve heartbeat detection have shown modest symptom reduction in small trials. Larger studies are testing whether targeted interoceptive feedback improves emotion regulation.

6. **Religious leader engagement improves Ebola outbreak control — STAT News**
   Prior outbreaks suffered when burial and mourning practices were ignored by response teams. The commentary details how traditional rites can be adapted to reduce transmission while respecting community needs. Successful models from earlier epidemics incorporated local leaders in safe body-handling protocols. Authors argue that exclusion of religious networks prolonged chains of infection. Current DRC and Uganda responses are incorporating these lessons in real time.

7. **Strength training of 90–120 minutes weekly linked to lower mortality — Science Daily**
   A 30-year follow-up of more than 147,000 adults found that this range of resistance exercise correlated with reduced death from cardiovascular and neurological causes. Adding aerobic activity further lowered risk beyond strength training alone. The observational design tracked self-reported activity and verified outcomes through national registries. Dose-response curves plateaued above 120 minutes per week. Replication in randomized trials is now being planned.

8. **High-performance materials designed for reduced critical mineral dependence — Phys.org**
   Researchers at Germany’s Federal Institute for Materials Research and Testing propose redesigning battery and turbine components from the outset. The perspective argues that early choices in chemistry and architecture improve recyclability and cut reliance on scarce elements. Modeling shows performance retention can be maintained while lowering raw-material intensity. Pilot projects will test these principles in hydrogen and wind technologies.

9. **Climate change projected to worsen ozone pollution in Barcelona — Phys.org**
   High-resolution simulations from ICTA-UAB forecast altered sea-land breeze patterns by 2050 and 2100. Weaker nighttime land breezes are expected to trap pollutants over the metropolitan area. The study links these circulation shifts to increased surface ozone concentrations. Health-risk estimates include additional respiratory and cardiovascular burden for millions of residents. Adaptation strategies under discussion include expanded urban greening and traffic controls.

10. **Potato leaves detect recent airborne PFAS missed by soil tests — Phys.org**
    Sampling near conflict zones in southern Israel showed substantially higher PFAS concentrations in leaves than in surrounding soils. The pattern points to atmospheric deposition rather than root uptake as the dominant recent exposure route. Researchers suggest plants can serve as sensitive sentinels for ongoing contamination. Expanded surveys will test the approach across additional crop species and regions.

11. **Fish evolution reshapes river morphology in human-altered systems — Phys.org**
    The international study demonstrates that fish are not passive responders but modify flow and sediment dynamics through their behavior. Human-made channels select for different swimming and foraging traits that in turn alter erosion patterns. Feedback loops between evolved fish populations and physical river structure are now documented. Management plans that ignore these biological feedbacks risk repeated restoration failures.

12. **Pramipexole reduces anhedonia in major depressive and bipolar disorders — Nature**
    A randomized placebo-controlled trial tested the dopamine agonist in patients meeting criteria for major depressive disorder, dysthymia, or bipolar depression. Pramipexole produced statistically significant drops on the Snaith–Hamilton Pleasure Scale relative to placebo. Target engagement was confirmed through changes in reward-related neural responses. The study enrolled participants across multiple sites with standardized diagnostic assessments. Larger phase 3 trials will examine durability and long-term safety.

13. **Digital twin brain model simulates individualized neural dynamics — PLOS Digital Health**
    The open-access platform constructs subject-specific brain models from multimodal imaging and electrophysiological data. Simulations allow testing of stimulation protocols or disease progression without invasive procedures. Validation against held-out recordings shows accurate reproduction of resting-state networks. The resource is intended for both research and eventual clinical planning applications.

14. **Atlas of circulating senescent proteins offers prognostic markers — ScienceDirect**
    Proteomic profiling identified proteins shed by senescent cells that circulate and correlate with future health outcomes. The accompanying cell atlas maps which tissues contribute most to the circulating pool. Several candidates improved prediction of mortality and multimorbidity beyond standard clinical scores. Independent cohorts are being assembled to confirm generalizability.

15. **Epigenetic drift and cell stress converge on senescence to drive aging — Cell**
    The review synthesizes evidence that stochastic epigenetic changes plus chronic stress push cells into irreversible senescence. Senescent cells then secrete factors that propagate dysfunction to neighboring tissues. Interventions that clear or reprogram these cells are discussed as upstream approaches to multiple age-related diseases. The authors highlight remaining gaps in human biomarker validation and tissue-specific timing.

### Science Deep Dive: How the brain predicts its own actions
Most people assume the brain simply reacts to whatever arrives through the senses. In reality, every movement you make generates an internal copy of the motor command that reaches sensory areas milliseconds ahead of the actual feedback. Right now, as you shift posture or turn a page, that copy is already adjusting auditory and visual cortices so the resulting sensations are tagged as self-produced rather than surprising. The electric fish work shows this predictive signal funnels through one compact timing hub rather than many parallel routes. A memorable demonstration: when the mechanism is experimentally disrupted, animals treat their own actions as external events and generate unnecessary responses. The practical takeaway is that many sensory and motor symptoms may trace to timing mismatches in this prediction system rather than outright sensory loss; future clinical assessments may therefore include targeted tests of corollary discharge integrity instead of relying solely on standard sensory thresholds.
