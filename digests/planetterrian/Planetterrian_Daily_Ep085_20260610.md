### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Marathon running raises cardiac biomarkers without clear harm** — BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine
   A review examined effects of marathon running on the heart. It documented release of cardiac troponin and NT-proBNP after races. These elevations occur without established clinical significance in most runners. The analysis draws on existing studies of endurance exercise physiology. Future work may clarify whether repeated biomarker spikes influence long-term cardiac structure.

2. **Microglia mitochondria show epigenetic control in Alzheimer's model** — Cell Press
   Researchers identified an epigenetic regulator acting on microglial mitochondria. The pro-inflammatory state of microglia drives disease progression in experimental models. Targeting this regulator could alter mitochondrial function inside these immune cells. The finding opens a potential route for therapy or prevention. Work remains at the preclinical stage.

3. **AI scores on sequential mammograms predict later cancer** — retrospective study
   Scores rose in women later diagnosed with breast cancer. Elevation appeared up to 10 years before clinical detection in the retrospective analysis. A separate prospective study supports the pattern over a 3-to-5-year window. The approach uses standard mammogram reads already performed by radiologists. Validation in larger cohorts is the next reported step.

4. **Ancestral sequence reconstruction revives ancient enzymes** — r/science
   The method infers protein sequences by tracing evolutionary history backward. It targets enzymes that may have performed optimally millions of years ago. Researchers apply the technique when modern versions fall short for biotechnology needs. The approach reconstructs sequences computationally before laboratory testing. No specific performance metrics appear in the post.

5. **Pedestrians show strong counterclockwise turning bias** — Phys.org
   Researchers in Spain and Japan tested people walking in varied group sizes. The vast majority preferred counterclockwise turns regardless of culture or gender. Age produced a small difference, with younger participants showing the pattern more consistently. The study measured turning behaviour directly during natural movement. No external factors overrode the directional preference.

6. **Soft bioelectronics advance wearable and implantable sensors** — Phys.org
   Wei Gao’s lab at Caltech develops stretchable, tissue-integrated devices. The sensors enable continuous monitoring and adaptive therapy. Materials combine softness and adhesion for long-term body contact. The work targets diagnosis and treatment across multiple conditions. No quantitative performance data are provided in the summary.

7. **Augmented reality ultrasound generates real-time 3D views** — MIT News
   MIT researchers created a system that renders live three-dimensional ultrasound images. The display overlays the volumetric data onto the patient’s body. The design aims to simplify interpretation during scanning procedures. No patient numbers or accuracy metrics are stated. Clinical testing steps are not described.

8. **Neolithic burials reveal dozens of headless skeletons** — r/science
   Archaeologists uncovered more than 30 headless human remains in southwestern Slovakia. The skeletons date to approximately 7,000 years ago. Evidence indicates deliberate burial after death. The pattern may reflect a specific Neolithic mortuary practice. Further analysis of the site continues.

9. **Breast milk fatty acids match newborn brain needs by species** — r/science
   A multi-university study compared fatty acid profiles across mammals. Humans showed the strongest alignment with prefrontal cortex development. The composition varies systematically between species. Findings support efforts to engineer infant formulas closer to human milk. No sample sizes are listed in the summary.

10. **Glucosamine use tied to faster cognitive decline** — Science Daily
    A large observational study linked the supplement to accelerated progression from mild impairment to Alzheimer’s. Users showed a reported 25 percent higher likelihood of developing dementia. Biological measurements provided possible mechanistic clues. The association does not establish causation. Replication in additional cohorts is needed.

11. **Asexual reproduction slowed early animal evolution** — Science Daily
    Fossil evidence indicates Earth’s first animals reproduced asexually. Low-competition communities limited morphological change over long periods. Environmental pressures later favoured sexual reproduction. Biodiversity and evolutionary rates increased sharply afterward. The study draws on paleontological records.

12. **Home exercise preserves cognition during chemotherapy** — Science Daily
    Cancer patients following a structured home program maintained better attention scores. The trial compared the intervention against a placebo condition. Low-dose ibuprofen produced smaller and less consistent cognitive benefits. Up to 80 percent of chemotherapy recipients experience “chemo brain.” The exercise protocol required no clinic visits.

13. **Theoretical models predict beta-decay rates in neutron-rich nuclei** — Phys.org
    TU Darmstadt researchers developed predictions for extremely neutron-rich isotopes. The rates influence models of heavy-element formation in the universe. Calculations were compared with available experimental data. Results appear in Physical Review Letters. Further measurements will test additional nuclei.

14. **Global commission targets brain health as economic driver** — Nature Medicine
    The Brain Health for Economic Resilience Commission will define and measure cognitive capacity. It frames brain health as a foundation for economic resilience. The effort is transdisciplinary and international. Nature Medicine announced the initiative in a recent comment. Specific metrics and timelines are not yet detailed.

15. **Development displaces millions of amphibians without follow-up** — Phys.org
    UBC researchers documented more than five million amphibians and reptiles moved in British Columbia over four years. No legal requirement exists to track post-relocation survival. The study is the first to quantify the scale of such relocations in the province. Data come from development permits. Long-term outcomes remain unknown.

One open question is whether measured differences in fatty acid profiles translate directly into measurable cognitive outcomes across feeding methods.

### Science Deep Dive: Turning Preferences in Everyday Movement
Most people assume their direction choices while walking are random or shaped mainly by culture and habit. In reality, humans carry an inherent bias toward counterclockwise turns that appears across tested populations. Right now, as you navigate a hallway or sidewalk, that same directional preference is likely influencing which way you veer when space opens up. The studies found the pattern held in groups of different sizes and showed only a modest weakening with age. One memorable detail is that the vast majority of participants followed the counterclockwise rule regardless of gender or background. The next time you observe crowd flow in a public space, notice whether the collective drift matches this built-in tendency. Researchers can now design clearer experiments on navigation aids or building layouts that either work with or against the bias.
