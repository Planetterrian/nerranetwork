### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **NASA prepares robotic mission to extend Swift telescope life — Phys.org**
   NASA will launch a robot on Tuesday to dock with the aging Swift telescope and prevent its uncontrolled re-entry. The spacecraft has operated for two decades and risks burning up without intervention. Engineers designed the rescue as a long-shot attempt to preserve an instrument still returning data on gamma-ray bursts and other high-energy events. Success would demonstrate in-orbit servicing techniques for other aging observatories.

2. **Single-sensor device detects hidden structural damage via wind vibrations — Phys.org**
   Researchers at CTU developed a low-cost tool that monitors natural vibrations caused by wind to reveal internal structural weaknesses. The system uses one sensor rather than arrays of traditional instruments. It identifies changes in a building or bridge’s resonant frequencies that signal damage before visible cracks appear. The approach aims to reduce inspection costs for infrastructure.

3. **Brain protein identified as potential vector for Alzheimer’s spread — Science Daily**
   A common protein appears to package and transport toxic tau between neurons, allowing Alzheimer’s pathology to propagate. Blocking formation of these packages could interrupt the process. The finding comes from laboratory models showing the protein’s role in moving tau from damaged to healthy cells. Researchers plan to test targeted interventions that prevent package assembly.

4. **Creatine shows mixed results as depression adjunct in review of trials — Science Daily**
   A new analysis of five randomized trials with 238 participants found creatine improved symptoms in two studies of women with major depressive disorder when added to standard treatment. The other three trials reported no clear benefit. Creatine is thought to support brain energy metabolism. Larger trials focused on specific patient subgroups are needed next.

5. **Dopamine signaling linked to lasting cognitive changes after pregnancy — r/science**
   Research on “mommy brain” shows dopamine pathways active during motherhood can produce enduring shifts in cognitive processing. These changes appear to enhance certain forms of attention and memory that persist beyond the postpartum period. The work draws on animal models and human imaging to trace the mechanism. Follow-up studies aim to determine how long the adaptations last across different life stages.

6. **Access to data and institutions outweighs idea quality for health startups — Phys.org**
   A Kaunas University of Technology study found that digital health ventures succeed more often when founders secure connections to healthcare systems, datasets, and funding than when they begin with a novel concept alone. The researchers examined factors that separate funded companies from those that stall. Practical infrastructure proved decisive in early survival. The findings apply to teams seeking to commercialize diagnostic or monitoring tools.

7. **Detection limits may hide evidence of extraterrestrial life — Science Daily**
   Scientists warn that current instruments and assumptions about biosignatures could produce false negatives even when life is present. Hidden metabolic byproducts, unfamiliar chemical patterns, and technology gaps all contribute to missed signals. The team recommends future missions prioritize tests that rule out overlooked signatures rather than only confirming expected ones. Revised search strategies could change how data from Mars and icy moons are interpreted.

8. **AI method evaluates student learning processes rather than final outputs — Phys.org**
   Researchers created an AI approach that tracks how students arrive at answers instead of scoring only the finished product. The system analyzes intermediate steps in problem-solving to distinguish genuine skill gains from AI-assisted polishing. Early tests suggest it can flag cases where tools mask incomplete understanding. Educators are exploring integration into existing assessment platforms.

9. **Space-based AI may help explain the Fermi paradox — Phys.org**
   A preprint argues that widespread adoption of artificial intelligence could reduce humanity’s long-term expansion into space, offering one resolution to the absence of detected extraterrestrial civilizations. The model examines how AI-driven priorities might shift resources away from interstellar missions. It contrasts this pathway with earlier assumptions about exponential colonization. Further modeling will test sensitivity to different AI governance scenarios.

10. **PFAS molecular chain length alters environmental persistence and treatment response — Phys.org**
    Longer-chain PFAS compounds behave differently in water systems than shorter ones, affecting both how long they remain and which removal technologies work. The distinction matters for designing filters and predicting contamination spread. Researchers mapped these structure-dependent patterns across multiple treatment methods. Utilities may need tailored strategies rather than uniform PFAS regulations.

11. **Declining school performance raises later criminal justice contact risk — Phys.org**
    A King’s College London study linked relative drops in academic performance during school years to higher odds of later justice system involvement. The association held after accounting for baseline socioeconomic factors. Researchers tracked cohorts to identify the timing of performance changes that mattered most. Early academic support programs are now being examined as potential preventive measures.

12. **Daily temperature and sunshine shifts drive mental health service demand — r/science**
    An analysis of 4.6 million NHS contacts in England found warmer days and fewer sunshine hours increased emergency visits for anxiety and depression. The effect appeared even without extreme heatwaves. Researchers controlled for seasonal patterns and holidays. Service planners are considering weather forecasts when scheduling staffing.

13. **Lipids and DNA nanostructures separately tune artificial cell stretching and bending — Phys.org**
    University of Tokyo scientists showed that lipid composition controls membrane stretching while DNA nanostructures govern bending stiffness in engineered cells. The two properties can be adjusted independently. This separation allows precise mechanical programming of synthetic vesicles for drug delivery or soft robotics. Next experiments will test stability in physiological conditions.

14. **Supplier-specific procurement cuts emissions more effectively than uniform rules — Phys.org**
    Research published in the International Journal of Procurement Management found tailored contracts with different suppliers reduced environmental impact better than one-size-fits-all policies. The study compared outcomes across product categories with varying supply-chain complexities. Companies gained larger reductions by matching relationship depth to material type. Implementation requires better supplier data sharing.

15. **AI model predicts fire resistance of epoxy resins before synthesis — Phys.org**
    IMDEA Materials Institute developed a machine-learning tool that forecasts flame-retardant performance of new epoxy formulations. The model was trained on existing resin datasets and validated against experimental burn tests. It reduces the need for iterative physical trials during material design. Industry partners are evaluating its use for aerospace and construction applications.

### Planetterrian Spotlight
The identification of a protein that packages and moves toxic tau between neurons adds a concrete cellular mechanism to ongoing Alzheimer’s research. If the packages can be blocked, progression might slow even after initial aggregates form. The work builds on earlier observations of tau spread and now points to a possible intervention point. What remains unclear is whether the same protein operates in human brains at the same scale seen in models. Which patient groups would be prioritized for the first trials of a package-blocking therapy?

### Science Deep Dive: Weather and Mood Variability
Most people assume only heatwaves or storms affect mental health, yet everyday temperature and light changes also shift symptom burden. Right now, as you notice a warmer afternoon or a cloudy stretch, your autonomic and circadian systems are registering those shifts and adjusting stress hormone output and sleep pressure accordingly. An English study of 4.6 million service contacts showed measurable rises in anxiety and depression visits on warmer, darker days even without extremes. The practical number to remember is that these non-extreme fluctuations already drive detectable changes in emergency demand. Paying attention to your own patterns during ordinary weather swings can help you anticipate when extra sleep, light exposure, or movement might blunt the effect. Future public-health planning may incorporate routine weather data into staffing and outreach.
