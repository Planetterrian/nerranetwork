MIT researchers, according to MIT Health, scanned adults aged eighteen to thirty-nine and sixty to eighty-four during sleep with electroencephalography, or EEG, and specialized magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI.

During non-REM sleep, slow waves ordinarily push larger pulses of cerebrospinal fluid that clear debris.

Sleep length was comparable across groups, yet the older adults still generated smaller fluid pulses.

Reduced neurovascular coupling accompanied that change, and blood-flow responses to breath-holding tasks were weaker in the same older adults.

The team plans to test whether boosting cerebrospinal fluid flow could alter neurodegenerative risk.

Most people assume deep sleep is for topping up energy and filing memories, yet those same slow oscillations also drive rhythmic pulses of cerebrospinal fluid that wash metabolic debris out of brain tissue.

What the body is doing is tightly timed: on each large, low-frequency EEG wave, blood volume falls a measurable amount, then cerebrospinal fluid rushes in.

That incoming fluid carries waste toward venous drainage, so the electrical wave, the blood-volume drop, and the fluid surge are one coupled sequence rather than separate night-time events.

The MIT comparison was run under identical scanning conditions in the sixty-to-eighty-four group and the eighteen-to-thirty-nine group, and average cerebrospinal fluid wave amplitude dropped substantially in the older adults.

Sustained waste accumulation is one hypothesized contributor to later neurodegeneration.

Consistent schedules, light-exposure timing, and treatment of sleep apnea are the supports tied to the deep-sleep architecture that runs this fluid clearance.
