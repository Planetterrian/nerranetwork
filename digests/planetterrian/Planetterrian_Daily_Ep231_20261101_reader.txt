A team at the Johns Hopkins Kimmel Cancer Center ran a genome-wide CRISPR screen in colon epithelial cells, with the findings published in Nature.

Turning off the claudin-4 gene stopped Bacteroides fragilis toxin from attaching to those cells.

Structural biologists confirmed that the toxin and claudin-4 form a direct complex. Claudin-4 is the protein that maintains tight junctions between neighboring cells.

Maxwell White led the CRISPR screen in collaboration with Matthew Waldor's laboratory at Harvard Medical School.

A molecular decoy built from the binding interaction protected mice from toxin damage in tests.

The bacterium lives in roughly twenty percent of healthy people, and only certain strains make the toxin tied to inflammation and tumors.

The same group showed in two thousand nine that toxin-producing strains promote colon tumors through chronic inflammation.

Senior author Cynthia Sears noted that pinning down the receptor opens routes to new detection and therapy strategies for diarrhea, colorectal cancer, and bloodstream infections.

Most people assume bacterial toxins latch onto signaling receptors that cells use to take in outside messages and fire internal responses.

Certain Bacteroides fragilis strains instead bind claudin-4, a protein whose primary role is forming the physical seals between neighboring intestinal cells.

That binding lets the toxin reach and cleave E-cadherin, the molecule that holds the gut barrier together.

Once that adhesive protein is cut, the lining becomes leaky and inflamed.

Every person carries this same structural protein in their colon lining.

The toxin is treating a junction brick as a docking port, not a message receiver.

The team then built a molecular decoy that mimics the binding site.

In mice, that decoy intercepted the toxin before it reached real cell junctions.

Future experiments will need to determine whether the same decoy strategy works in people without disrupting normal tight-junction function.

A working decoy would block toxin-driven colon damage without removing the bacterium from the gut entirely.

YAP1 drives hepatocellular carcinoma through DNA demethylation rather than canonical gene mutations.

Researchers submitted the work, highlighted on r/longevity, showing that epigenetic mechanisms alone can start liver cancer.

The result challenges the assumption that driver gene mutations are always required for hepatocellular carcinoma.

The finding broadens how tumors can form when mutation-driven pathways are absent.

High dietary cholesterol activates the Ral protein in liver cells, which then triggers cathepsin A to reduce LDL receptors, in work published in Nature from UC San Diego.

Blocking cathepsin A with a small-molecule inhibitor stabilized those receptors and lowered circulating LDL in mice.

Senior author Alan Saltiel explained that the liver normally clears LDL cholesterol through LDL receptors on cell surfaces.

Experiments combined mouse models with human cell studies to map how Ral activity leads to receptor loss.

An existing cathepsin A inhibitor, a CTSA inhibitor, had already been evaluated in a Phase one trial for heart failure before that program ended.

The newly traced Ral-CTSA route operates separately from statin and PCSK9 targets already in use.

An inhibitor previously tested for safety in people could shorten the usual timeline to a phase two efficacy trial.

If the approach holds in humans, it would give clinicians an option for patients who do not reach target levels with current drugs.

What remains to be shown is whether the same CTSA inhibitor lowers LDL without new side effects when given at the doses needed for lipid control.

Prior Phase one safety data on that inhibitor provides a concrete starting point rather than requiring an entirely new compound.

Further mouse work will need to confirm that receptor stabilization translates into sustained LDL reduction over longer periods.
