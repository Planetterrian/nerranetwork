Ice cores from Canada, Greenland and Russia, according to Science News, hold particles that fell as early as two hundred ten BCE, and the work appears in Nature Sustainability. Layers dated from eighteen seventy to nineteen fourteen show the largest arsenic increases in the Greenland cores, pointing to North American sources.

Researchers identified the Anaconda Copper Mining Company in southwestern Montana as the likely emitter, a smelter that processed copper ore containing arsenic and lead. Models built from the ice-core concentrations tied the spike to that operation. Devices installed in nineteen nineteen cut arsenic emissions by about sixty percent.

Most people assume industrial pollution stays local or regional, yet ice-core chemistry shows that fine particles travel thousands of kilometers within days or weeks. Air above any northern latitude carries microscopic traces of past industrial activity that settled into snow and became locked in ice.

Peak output reached an estimated thirty tons of arsenic a day, comparable to one quarter of Chile's current national total from more than twenty operations. Those particles followed prevailing west-to-east circulation, which is why Russian Arctic cores showed smaller increases.

What remains uncertain is exactly how much of the deposited material later entered food webs or drinking water in distant communities.

Resuloğlu Mound in Türkiye, in work covered by Phys.org, was reconstructed before and after excavations that ran from two thousand three to two thousand nineteen, with the results published in Geoderma Regional. The team combined nineteen ninety aerial photos, two thousand nineteen drone surveys, soil data, and twenty-four years of weather records.

They ran the QGeoWEPP model on eight hillslopes. On one cemetery slope, modeled annual runoff rose from twelve point six to twenty point one millimeters per year after excavation. Average annual soil loss increased from one point one to two point one kilograms per square meter. Sediment yield rose from about eleven to twenty-one tonnes per hectare per year.

Fossil carbon dioxide from fuel use declined in the Global Carbon Project tally, which appears in Earth System Science Data Discussions. The project estimates forty-two point two billion tons of carbon dioxide from fossil fuels in twenty twenty-six, a drop of zero point four percent.

Oil emissions dropped nearly two percent and natural gas emissions fell zero point three percent, while coal emissions rose zero point six percent. The decline coincides with the closure of the Strait of Hormuz after the Iran conflict. China, the United States, Europe, and Japan each showed roughly one percent drops, and India's emissions rose four point four percent.

Land-use changes such as deforestation increased overall carbon dioxide emissions by one percent despite the fossil-fuel decline.

Inherited Alzheimer's mutations disrupted nerve-muscle connections in miniature human-cell systems, in findings covered by ScienceDaily. Scientists built those systems from human cells that carried the inherited mutations. The mutations altered connections between nerves and muscle cells without any brain or spinal-cord involvement. The work used induced pluripotent stem-cell models.

The changes may explain why some patients show balance, walking, and coordination shifts years before memory problems appear.

Mount Sinai researchers compared rodent-derived and human-derived Andes virus isolates, in a Phys.org report. They found one amino-acid difference that changes how a key viral protein behaves inside human cells. The change helps explain biological differences between closely related virus isolates.

Anaerobic methanotrophic archaea convert methane into electricity and, in work published in Nature Communications, produce nanowires that transmit it. New results show the organisms build those electrically conductive nanowires as the route for the current. Researchers cultivated the microbes in nearly pure form in the laboratory to study the process.

Schooling behavior in fish evolved and vanished more than once, in a machine-learning analysis published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Scientists applied those methods across thousands of fish species. The analysis shows schooling appeared multiple times and disappeared repeatedly.

The study ranks among the largest examinations ever conducted on the evolution of any behavior.

A dopamine pathway from the gut teaches the brain to prefer flavors that start out unpleasant, in findings covered by ScienceDaily. Researchers identified that gut-to-brain dopamine signaling route as the teacher for the craving. The mechanism helps explain why bitter coffee and similar tastes become preferred over time. The discovery points to new ways of reshaping eating habits.

Agroforests in one of the world's most biodiverse regions, in an international study covered by Phys.org, used birds and bats as pest control for cacao, with results in Communications Earth & Environment. An international team studied those tree-rich systems in Peru. Birds and bats provided natural pest control that boosted cacao production. Tree-rich landscapes nearly doubled cacao yields there.

Tel Aviv University researchers examined objects at early human sites for a pattern of keeping ordinary items. The sites date to roughly five hundred thousand years ago. The pattern suggests early humans picked up and kept ordinary items as reminders of specific moments or places. The behavior has implications for the evolution of memory.

CERN recorded a particle decay long predicted by theory, in findings covered by ScienceDaily. The observation came after a forty-year search. The decay occurs roughly once in ten billion events. The result supplies evidence long predicted by theory.

University of Rochester and Technion researchers found a way to rearrange molybdenum atoms at room temperature, in a Phys.org report. The discovery was accidental. Molybdenum normally requires temperatures above two thousand six hundred degrees Celsius to restructure. The change could expand uses in semiconductors and high-temperature components.

In brief, a KAIST-led team measured how light propagates through and is absorbed by barium titanate, resolving inconsistencies caused by the material's complex internal structure.

Sixty-five years of radiocarbon measurements in sugar maple leaves from Gatineau Park show that effects from atmospheric nuclear weapons tests have only recently disappeared from the local biosphere.

Hebrew University researchers found that a parasite common in cats can produce potentially sight-threatening ocular disease in dogs.

University of St Andrews work shows that the rocks supplying tin, not the rate of cooling, primarily control where tin deposits form.
