### Top 15 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Violence linked to health and economic hardship in US adults — Phys.org**
 A new national study reveals that more than half of U.S. adults have experienced physical violence and nearly half have experienced sexual violence in their lifetimes. The U.S. National Study on Violence Experiences Across the Lifespan (USVEX) also shows stark disparities in who experiences violence. Violence correlates closely with economic hardship, mental health issues and substance use. The findings document lifetime prevalence across a representative adult sample and tie exposure directly to measurable differences in economic and health outcomes.

2. **Chimpanzees hand tools to youngsters for learning — Phys.org**
 Adult chimpanzees in Senegal pass tools to young chimpanzees to help them learn termite-fishing techniques. The practice builds on observations first made by Jane Goodall in the 1960s. Two-thirds of a century after the initial reports of tool use, the new data show adults actively handing implements to juveniles during foraging.

3. **Neurons use special mechanism to transcribe extra-long genes — Phys.org**
 Neurons express many genes longer than 100 kbp and up to more than 2 Mbp that are essential for synapse formation. Standard transcription takes longer for these genes and requires additional regulatory steps. Researchers identified dedicated mechanisms that allow accurate and stable reading of these sequences. The work clarifies how neurons manage the extended time and regulatory demands of transcribing these exceptionally long genes.

4. **Diet change lowers biological age markers in four weeks — ScienceDaily**
 Older adults who altered the balance of fat, carbohydrates and animal protein showed reduced biological age after four weeks. Participants on an omnivorous, lower-fat, higher-carbohydrate diet recorded the strongest effect. The study tracked changes in established biological-age indicators following controlled shifts in macronutrient ratios and protein sources.

5. **High-resolution data maps urban heat stress neighborhoods — Phys.org**
 A new dataset from the U.S. National Science Foundation National Center for Atmospheric Research supplies city governments with neighborhood-level heat information. The data combine urban heat island effects with local weather patterns. Officials can use the maps to target interventions that reduce heat-related health risks. The resource is designed to help identify which city blocks face the greatest heat stress during extreme events.

6. **New Triassic species refines dinosaur timeline — Phys.org**
 An international team described Dinodontosaurus isiyavamanda from Tanzania, dating to roughly 240 million years ago. The fossil helps date one of the world's key Triassic sites. The find may adjust estimates for the earliest dinosaur origins. Led by the University of Bristol, the discovery adds a new species to the record of early archosauriform reptiles.

7. **Protein PTCHD4 emerges as senescence regulator — Lifespan.io**
 Researchers linked the sparsely studied protein PTCHD4 to cellular senescence pathways. The work establishes a previously unreported connection in the literature on aging cells. Very little prior research had examined this protein in the context of senescence, leaving an open mechanistic node for further investigation.

8. **Plasma and electrocatalysis turn CO₂ into chemicals — Phys.org**
 Yale researchers combined plasma with electrocatalysis to convert carbon dioxide into methanol, butane and other products. The approach improves efficiency for practical gas-to-chemical conversion. Results appear in Nature Catalysis. The dual-method system addresses longstanding challenges in turning captured CO₂ into usable fuels and feedstocks at scale.

9. **Eco-anxiety shapes support for protest tactics in Canada — Phys.org**
 A McGill University and Université Laval study found that younger, eco-anxious Canadians show higher tolerance for disruptive environmental activism. Overall, Canadians reject vandalism and violence as protest methods. The findings are published in the Canadian Journal of Political Science. The survey examined how emotional responses to climate change correlate with attitudes toward different forms of activism.

10. **Morocco site reveals ancient built structure — Phys.org**
 Excavations at Oued Beht uncovered a substantial built structure from the second millennium BC. The discovery indicates that northwestern African communities actively modified their environment and participated in Mediterranean networks. Research at the site is rewriting understanding of regional development during the Bronze Age.

11. **Models track New World screwworm spread risk — Phys.org**
 USDA researchers developed predictive models to forecast where New World screwworm infestations may occur next. The tools support earlier intervention and resource allocation for livestock, pets and wildlife. The Agricultural Research Service effort responds to recent detections in the United States and aims to limit future economic and ecological damage.

12. **Report weighs costs of Great Salt Lake dust control — Phys.org**
 University of Utah researchers produced a comprehensive analysis of dust risks from the exposed Great Salt Lake lakebed. The report outlines control options, associated costs and long-term benefits of stabilizing lake levels for state agencies. Coordinated by the Wilkes Center for Climate Science & Policy, the document supplies data for policymakers weighing dust mitigation strategies.

13. **River catchments vary in storm response over time — Phys.org**
 A global study in Nature Water shows that identical rainfall on the same landscape can produce different river flows at different times. The work maps how consistently catchments convert rain into streamflow. The findings demonstrate that catchment behavior is not fixed and that the same storm can yield markedly different runoff depending on prior conditions.

14. **Bacterial protein converts plant flowers to leaves — Phys.org**
 Scientists identified a bacterial protein that disrupts the developmental controls governing flower formation. The mechanism turns reproductive organs into leaf-like structures. The pathogen is expected to expand northward with climate-driven insect vectors. Findings used synchrotron imaging at ESRF and appear in the Journal of Biological Chemistry.

15. **Disordered atoms direct light pulses asymmetrically — Phys.org**
 A TU Darmstadt team showed that completely disordered, constantly moving atoms can still emit light preferentially in one direction. The degree of asymmetry can be tuned. The result is published in Physical Review Letters. The demonstration reveals that collective emission can be directed even without ordered structure or external alignment.

### Planetterrian Spotlight
The PTCHD4 finding identifies a protein with almost no prior aging literature as a direct participant in senescence. Its near-absence from earlier studies means researchers can now test whether modulating this molecule alters the rate at which cells enter or exit the senescent state. Because the protein sits at a previously unmapped node, it could open routes to interventions that complement existing senolytic approaches without overlapping their known targets. One question worth tracking is whether PTCHD4 levels in accessible tissues track with other validated aging biomarkers in longitudinal human cohorts.

### Science Deep Dive: How the Body Handles Everyday Heat
You've probably heard that people lose most body heat through their heads. The claim is a myth that traces to a single military study from decades ago. Right now, as you sit or move, your body sheds heat primarily through the skin across the entire surface, with blood flow and sweating doing the main work once core temperature rises. A single bout of moderate exercise can increase skin blood flow by roughly tenfold, moving heat outward far more effectively than any localized exposure. The next time you feel overheated during daily activity, notice how quickly flushing and perspiration spread across the torso and limbs rather than staying confined to the scalp. The practical takeaway is to focus cooling efforts on the whole body during heat waves—light clothing, shade, and hydration—rather than assuming a hat or head covering solves the problem. Future public-health alerts that emphasize neighborhood-level heat data will make these whole-body strategies easier to time and target.

Today's findings show steady progress across environmental health, cellular aging, and basic biology.
