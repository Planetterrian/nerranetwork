Next up is work on genome stability mechanisms.

Repair enzymes favor certain sequence contexts, leaving other regions more mutation-prone.

This bias helps explain non-random mutation spectra observed in human cells.

Chromatin state and replication timing further modulate repair efficiency.

Most people picture DNA repair as a uniform cleanup crew that patches damage wherever it occurs.

In reality, repair enzymes show clear sequence preferences that leave some genomic regions more vulnerable to permanent mutation.

Right now, as your cells replicate, these enzymes are scanning for breaks and mismatches, yet they allocate effort unevenly across the genome.

One memorable pattern is that mutation rates are measurably higher in stretches the repair machinery tends to bypass.

This bias helps explain why certain cancer-driving mutations appear repeatedly while others remain rare.

Studies in human cell lines already show that mismatch-repair complexes linger longer at certain triplet repeats, altering the local mutation supply.

Because these preferences are sequence-dependent rather than damage-dependent, the same lesion can produce different outcomes depending on its genomic neighborhood.

Repair efficiency also varies with chromatin state, so regions that remain open during replication may receive faster attention than tightly packed segments.

Tracking how repair kinetics change with age or environmental stress could reveal new intervention points for preserving genome stability over decades.

The practical takeaway is that future genomic-risk models will need to incorporate repair-sequence rules rather than assuming random damage distribution.
