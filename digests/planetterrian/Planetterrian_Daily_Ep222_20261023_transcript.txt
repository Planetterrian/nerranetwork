Sohei, Sukunaga, and colleagues at Tahoku University examined 13 silicate glass compositions
to see what actually sets how well the material conducts heat.
Their paper in applied physics letters reports that the speed of sound through the glass plays
the dominant role in thermal conductivity.
At room temperature, the team measured thermal conductivity, sound velocity, volumetric
specific heat, and phonon mean free path.
Volumetric specific heat is how much energy a given volume of glass can store as temperature
rises, and phonon mean free path is the average distance of phonon travels before it scatters.
Those two quantities traded off, as one increased, the other tended to decrease, and their
products stayed approximately constant across the compositions, while thermal conductivity
stayed proportional to sound velocity.
Sukinaga, an associate professor at Tahoku, noted that thermal conductivity has been studied
for a long time, yet remains one of the least-understood properties of non-crystalline materials,
and the result offers a guideline for designing glass heat flow by controlling sound velocity,
rather than treating several thermal parameters as independent variables.
The next step is to determine whether this relationship also applies to other types
of materials, and whether it holds at temperatures beyond room temperature.
