### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

1. **Arctic ice records Montana mining pollution** — Science News
   Ice cores from Canada, Greenland and Russia contain particles that fell as early as 210 BCE. Layers from 1870 to 1914 show arsenic concentrations up to 15 times higher than pre-industrial levels in some cores. The largest increases appeared in Greenland cores, pointing to North American sources because atmospheric circulation moves material west to east. Researchers identified the Anaconda Copper Mining Company in southwestern Montana as the likely source. The company processed copper ore containing arsenic and lead. Models based on ice-core concentrations estimate the operation emitted roughly 30 tons of arsenic per day during peak years. Devices installed in 1919 cut arsenic emissions by about 60 percent. The study appears in Nature Sustainability. Source: [sciencenews.org](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/toxic-secrets-hiding-arctic-ice-harm)

2. **Excavation changes erosion patterns at ancient sites** — Phys.org
   Researchers reconstructed the landscape at Resuloğlu Mound in Türkiye before and after digs from 2003 to 2019. They combined 1990 aerial photos, 2019 drone surveys, soil data and 24 years of weather records. The team ran the QGeoWEPP model on eight hillslopes. On one cemetery slope, modeled annual runoff rose from 12.6 to 20.1 millimeters per year after excavation. Average annual soil loss increased from 1.1 to 2.1 kilograms per square meter. Sediment yield rose from about 11 to 21 tonnes per hectare per year. The study was published in Geoderma Regional. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-ground-beneath.html)

3. **Global fossil CO₂ emissions fell 0.4 percent in 2026** — Phys.org
   The Global Carbon Project estimates 42.2 billion tons of CO₂ from fossil fuels this year. Oil emissions dropped nearly 2 percent and natural gas emissions fell 0.3 percent. Coal emissions rose 0.6 percent. The decline coincides with the closure of the Strait of Hormuz after the Iran conflict. China, the United States, Europe and Japan each showed roughly 1 percent drops. India’s emissions rose 4.4 percent. Land-use changes such as deforestation increased overall CO₂ emissions by 1 percent despite the fossil-fuel decline. The report appears in Earth System Science Data Discussions. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-fossil-emissions-decrease-energy-growth.html)

4. **Alzheimer’s mutations disrupt nerve-muscle links outside brain** — ScienceDaily
   Scientists built miniature laboratory systems from human cells carrying inherited Alzheimer’s mutations. The mutations altered connections between nerves and muscle cells without any brain or spinal-cord involvement. The finding may explain why some patients show balance, walking and coordination changes years before memory problems appear. The work used induced pluripotent stem-cell models. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261108233103.htm)

5. **Single amino-acid change alters Andes virus protein in human cells** — Phys.org
   Mount Sinai researchers compared rodent-derived and human-derived Andes virus isolates. They found one amino-acid difference that changes how a key viral protein behaves inside human cells. The change helps explain biological differences between closely related virus isolates. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-amino-acid-andes-virus-protein.html)

6. **Methane-eating microbes build electrically conductive nanowires** — Phys.org
   Anaerobic methanotrophic archaea convert methane into electricity. New work shows the organisms produce nanowires to transmit that electricity. Researchers cultivated the microbes in nearly pure form in the laboratory. The study was published in Nature Communications. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-methane-microbes-grown-electrodes-electrically.html)

7. **Machine learning maps repeated evolution of fish schooling** — Phys.org
   Scientists applied machine-learning methods across thousands of fish species. The analysis shows schooling behavior evolved multiple times and disappeared repeatedly. The study is among the largest ever conducted on the evolution of any behavior. It was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-machine-reveals-fish-schooling-evolved.html)

8. **Gut-to-brain pathway explains acquired taste for bitter coffee** — ScienceDaily
   Researchers identified a dopamine pathway from the gut that teaches the brain to crave initially unpleasant flavors. The mechanism helps explain why bitter coffee and similar tastes become preferred over time. The discovery points to new ways of reshaping eating habits. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261108233100.htm)

9. **Tree-rich landscapes nearly double cacao yields in Peru** — Phys.org
   An international team studied agroforests in one of the world’s most biodiverse regions. Birds and bats provided natural pest control that boosted cacao production. The study was published in Communications Earth & Environment. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-tree-rich-landscapes-birds-cacao.html)

10. **Early humans collected keepsakes 500,000 years ago** — Phys.org
    Tel Aviv University researchers examined objects at sites dating to roughly 500,000 years ago. The pattern suggests early humans picked up and kept ordinary items as reminders of specific moments or places. The behavior has implications for the evolution of memory. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-early-humans-keepsakes-years.html)

11. **CERN detects rare particle decay after 40-year search** — ScienceDaily
    The decay occurs roughly once in 10 billion events. The observation supplies evidence long predicted by theory. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261108233047.htm)

12. **Accidental find enables room-temperature molybdenum restructuring** — Phys.org
    University of Rochester and Technion researchers discovered a method to rearrange molybdenum atoms at room temperature. Molybdenum normally requires temperatures above 2,600 °C to restructure. The change could expand uses in semiconductors and high-temperature components. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-accidental-discovery-enables-room-temperature.html)

### Science Deep Dive: How distant industrial emissions reach remote ecosystems
Most people assume pollution remains largely local or regional, yet ice-core data show that fine particles travel thousands of kilometers within days or weeks. In the listener’s own environment, the air above any northern latitude carries microscopic traces of past industrial activity that settled into snow and became locked in ice. One clear measurement is the 15-fold elevation in arsenic concentrations recorded in Greenland cores from the 1870–1914 period compared with pre-industrial layers. The same cores reveal that a single Montana copper smelter emitted an estimated 30 tons of arsenic daily at peak output, an amount comparable to one-quarter of Chile’s current national total from more than twenty operations. These particles followed prevailing west-to-east circulation, explaining why Russian Arctic cores showed smaller increases. What remains uncertain is exactly how much of the deposited material later entered food webs or drinking water in distant communities. Continued analysis that pairs ice-core chemistry with historical health records would clarify whether measurable population-level effects followed the documented exposure window.
