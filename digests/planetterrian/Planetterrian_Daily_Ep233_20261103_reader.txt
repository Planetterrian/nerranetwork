University of Colorado Boulder researchers at the Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences rebuilt a record of fire risk to structures from nineteen forty-five through twenty twenty-three, using historical burned-area and housing data, as reported by Phys.org.

They combined that archive with weather, climate, and housing-density projections and ran the model out to twenty sixty.

Under a moderate emissions path and no new adaptations, the simulation reaches as many as four hundred forty-nine thousand one hundred buildings damaged or destroyed.

The largest relative jump in risk sits in the central United States, where grassland fires are increasing.

In the west, thirty percent of census tracts facing the steepest risk increases already show high poverty and housing burden.

The paper appears in Nature Communications, and lead author Virginia Iglesias said inaction on climate, construction patterns, and community involvement will push risk much higher.

She added that eastern fires can be as destructive as western ones even though communities there have seen fewer such events, and that Colorado’s wildfire resiliency code adopted in July twenty twenty-five cannot stand in for work on social vulnerability.

Pierre Etienne Banville, a master’s student in geography at Simon Fraser University, used an Earth-system model to trace climate effects of large-scale forestation that have nothing to do with carbon dioxide.

Forests absorb more sunlight than grasslands, especially where snow is present, and the warmer air can hold more water vapor.

When that air crosses oceans it lifts sea-surface temperatures and evaporation, which then strengthens the greenhouse effect.

Oceans take up the extra heat slowly, so the full remote warming unfolds over centuries, according to the paper in Earth System Dynamics.

Banville explained that carbon dioxide removal arrives relatively fast as trees grow, while the non-carbon-dioxide warming can lag and cancel part of that cooling.

He said policies for nature-based solutions have to count both time scales if climate targets are tied to a calendar date.

La Selva in Costa Rica and El Verde in Puerto Rico supplied the long-term station records for a PLOS Climate paper on tropical extremes.

Average temperatures barely moved, yet days above the ninety-fifth-percentile heat threshold at La Selva climbed from eight per year to thirty by twenty twenty-four.

Alonso Ramirez of North Carolina State University, the lead author, reported that rainfall events above the ninety-ninth percentile at La Selva rose from about one per year between nineteen eighty-three and nineteen eighty-five to six per year in twenty twenty-four.

El Verde instead saw dry days increase from one hundred fifty-two per year to one hundred seventy.

Co-author Marcelo Ardon, also at NC State, said it was surprising that one site grew wetter and the other drier despite their relative proximity.

Ramirez noted that protected tropical research sites were expected to show little change from human-driven climate shifts, yet both stations recorded changes in climate and ecosystem function.

Low pressure over Iceland relative to the Azores shifts the jet stream north and steers storm surges onto northern and central United Kingdom coasts.

Cambridge University scientists pulled seventy years of sea-level records around northwestern Europe, isolated extreme surge events, and used machine learning to group coastlines that flood together, in work published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

The reverse pressure pattern sends the storms south.

Lead author Lisanne Blok of Cambridge Earth Sciences found that Northern Hemisphere, and especially North Atlantic, pressure patterns were the strongest control on where surges land, and that the links held more clearly across large distances than expected.

She said spotting those Atlantic setups could let forecasters identify which stretches of coast should prepare for flooding.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel and shallow basin amplify surge height, and storms that hit southwest England often start off eastern Canada, while those that reach the North Sea and Scotland often come from off Greenland.

Antarctic strains of the soil bacterium Arthrobacter form genetic lineages that did not appear in comparison samples from the Atacama Desert, Svalbard, or the Tibetan Plateau.

The comparison is published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Authors Caray A Walker and Melody Clark wrote that these organisms evolved solutions to extreme cold, desiccation, and high ultraviolet-B radiation.

Their earlier work cultured bacteria from eight Antarctic marine invertebrate species and found that two strains slowed growth of epidemic methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Micrococcus luteus, and Candida albicans in laboratory tests.

Soil temperatures at the collection sites range from eight degrees Celsius in summer to below minus twenty-five degrees Celsius in winter.

Most people assume that microbes hitchhike so readily on wind and water that the same lineages appear wherever conditions match.

On the Antarctic continent the picture is different, because long geographic isolation has let certain bacterial lineages evolve apart from populations on other landmasses.

The circumpolar current has kept the continent cut off for ten to fifteen million years, a span far longer than the roughly one and a half million years the Arctic has been cold while remaining connected to surrounding continents.

That extended separation, stacked on rapid temperature swings, intense ultraviolet radiation, and scarce nutrients, favors survival traits that are not required in less isolated cold regions.

Distinct genetic clusters present only in the Antarctic isolates support the idea that these microbes evolved in place rather than arriving recently from elsewhere.

The same traits that permit life in that setting also appear to produce compounds with medical interest, including the laboratory inhibition of clinically important resistant pathogens.

A separate investigation around the Rothera Research Station found that treated wastewater exerts only limited influence on surrounding marine microbial communities.

Those bacteria readily adapted to warmer temperatures when grown in the lab, a hint that rising global temperatures could change the conditions that have kept this uniqueness intact.

These microbes also cycle nutrients and sit at the base of Antarctic food webs.

Documenting which strains exist only on the continent raises practical questions about conservation priorities and about the risk of introducing non-native microorganisms through tourism and scientific operations.

University of Reading researchers loaded nitric oxide into organosilica nanoparticles that give off the gas slowly over hours.

One hour after infecting wax-moth larvae with skin-infection bacteria, they injected the particles.

Nine in ten treated larvae survived three days, compared with roughly six in ten left untreated.

In separate flask tests the particles cut bacterial counts by four to five orders of magnitude, and the work appears in the Journal of Drug Delivery Science and Technology.

Lead researcher Vitaliy Khutoryanskiy explained that nitric oxide hits germs on several fronts at once, which makes resistance harder to evolve, and that the particles solve the problem of storing the gas and metering it out slowly.

Wax-moth larvae, Galleria mellonella, weigh about a quarter of a gram yet mount immune responses comparable to those in vertebrates, so the team can test safety and effect while cutting back on mammalian experiments.

Khutoryanskiy said treatments that work in a different way are urgently needed as bacteria become harder to kill with antibiotics, and the next required step is mammalian toxicity and dosing data before any human trial.

King’s College London tested KCL-two hundred eighty-six, an oral small molecule first developed for spinal-cord injury, in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease, according to ScienceDaily.

The compound activates a protein in the retinoic-acid pathway and lowered both DNA double-strand breaks and brain inflammation.

KCL-two hundred eighty-six has already completed phase one human safety trials for its original indication.

Professor Jonathan Corcoran noted that the drug is first-in-class and orally bioavailable, which shortens the usual multi-year path for a new compound.

Dr. Maria Goncalves, who project-managed the development, stated that the work shows the molecule targets DNA damage and inflammation, two processes that appear very early in Alzheimer’s progression.

The approach differs from treatments aimed mainly at amyloid-beta or tau, and earlier research had already linked disruptions in the retinoic-acid pathway to amyloid-beta deposits in rat brains.

Mitochondrial replacement in the United Kingdom produced eight healthy births last year and lowered transmission of disease-causing mitochondrial mutations, STAT News reports.

The technique combines genes from a mother and a father with mutation-free DNA in the mitochondria of a donor egg.

Subsequent United Kingdom data and small pilot studies elsewhere have raised questions about whether preventing mitochondrial disease is the best first clinical use of the method.

Emerging results have prompted scientists and ethicists to rethink potential risks and benefits before wider adoption.

Regulators in Australia are preparing their own national trial of the approach.

Chinese professional master’s and PhD students in clinical medicine may soon graduate without a thesis, Nature reports.

Draft policy documents released on October twenty-second allow those students to submit a practical achievement such as a clinical-case analysis instead.

The change follows a similar pathway introduced for engineering students in twenty twenty-four, and universities will set their own assessment standards for the new route.

Futao Huang, who studies higher-education policy at Hiroshima University, said students headed for professional careers should be trained and judged in ways that match those careers.

Zhou Feifei, a spinal surgeon at Peking University Third Hospital, said the pathway lets students be assessed on what their research will bring to patients and society.

Tie Dazhao became the first to finish with a practical PhD in clinical medicine in June after building artificial-intelligence software to help physicians classify a rare spine condition, and Liping Duan, chief physician of gastroenterology at the same hospital, said the hardest task will be a uniform standard for practical work across medical fields.
