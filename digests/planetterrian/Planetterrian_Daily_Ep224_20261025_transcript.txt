Something most people get wrong about the Mediterranean diet is that its health effects come only
from antioxidants or from changes in blood lipids and inflammation.
An additional route runs through two tiny proteins produced inside mitochondria.
Humanin and SHMOS originate from short open-reading frames within mitochondrial DNA.
Those segments were once considered non-functional.
They are now known to encode biologically active molecules.
Humanin was first identified in 2003 by Pinchus, Cohen, and colleagues.
That identification sits inside more than 20 years of work on small proteins encoded by mitochondrial DNA.
Later work linked humanin to improved insulin sensitivity.
It has also been tied to better cardiovascular outcomes, increased longevity and protection against cognitive decline.
Mitochondria generate most of the cell's energy.
They also regulate stress responses and aging processes.
The microproteins appear to function as internal messengers that convert dietary information
into adjustments in mitochondrial output.
Clinicians might one day monitor humanin or SHMS levels to assess whether dietary interventions
are producing the expected cellular effects rather than relying solely on self-reported
food logs.
Open questions include whether other eating patterns or lifestyle factors influence the
same mitochondrial signals.
Another is whether genetic variation in mitochondrial DNA affects an individual's response to the Mediterranean diet.
