### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

1. **CASS4 gene disruption weakens microglial plaque control — Science Daily**
   Researchers at UVA Health found that the CASS4 gene regulates microglia, the brain's immune cells that surround and compact amyloid-beta plaques. When CASS4 activity was disrupted in laboratory mice, microglia became less effective at containing plaques, allowing them to expand and spread more aggressively. Neurons near the plaques suffered increased damage and the animals showed worsening cognitive function. The study indicates that CASS4's primary role occurs in immune cells rather than neurons. Defective CASS4 activity may serve as an early biomarker, and boosting its function could offer a treatment approach. More than 6 million Americans live with Alzheimer's, the sixth-leading cause of death in the United States. The research also showed that losing CASS4 triggered changes in the activity of other genes with potential implications for additional neurodegenerative diseases. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261120075600.htm)

2. **Sugar-based hydrogel strengthens under repeated strain — Phys.org**
   A team at Washington University in St. Louis created a biodegradable hydrogel from sugar crystals and polyethylene glycol that becomes stronger when bent or stretched. Cyclodextrin rings in the material slide into position to reinforce areas under strain, making the polymers more uniform and organized. The material was tested on a flexible knee-like joint and withstood 10,000 bending cycles while performing as predicted. It is compatible with 3D printing and biological tissues. Potential uses include supporting artificial joints or repairing damaged tissues where strength is needed only in stressed areas. The polymer mimics collagen in the human body. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-sugar-based-hydrogel.html)

3. **AI visual models fail reverse prediction test against brain data — ScienceDaily**
   York University researchers tested whether recorded neuronal responses could predict internal features of artificial neural networks that model vision. AI models successfully predicted brain cell responses to 1,320 natural and synthetic images, yet brain activity poorly predicted many model features in the reverse direction. The mismatch did not appear when comparing neuronal activity across different brains. The results indicate that AI systems can match brain outputs while using different internal strategies. The study used 300 additional images in outlines, drawings, and artistic styles. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261120075701.htm)

4. **Essential-gene mutation protection theory lacks support in bacteria and yeast — ScienceDaily**
   An analysis of mutations across bacteria and yeast genomes found no evidence that essential genes mutate less frequently than other genes. Earlier studies may have missed harmful mutations because natural selection removed them before detection. The findings challenge a 50-year-old assumption that evolution protects critical genes from change at the mutation level. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261119194538.htm)

5. **Human family tree classification may require major revision — ScienceDaily**
   Monash University researchers argue that species such as Australopithecus and Paranthropus should be grouped within the genus Homo. Additional fossils and evolutionary analyses show that traditional boundaries between these ancient relatives have become difficult to maintain. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261118074039.htm)

6. **Ancient DNA reclassifies supposed woodcock fossil as snipe — Phys.org**
   Florida Museum researchers sequenced ancient DNA from 1984 Haitian cave fossils previously identified as a woodcock and determined the specimen is a snipe. Woodcocks and snipes diverged from a common ancestor about 25 million years ago. The study also incorporated DNA from museum specimens of 29 extant species and one additional extinct snipe to build a time-calibrated phylogeny. There are eight living species of woodcock, six of them endemic to islands. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-avian-champions-distance-migration-scientists.html)

7. **Scientists disagree more than the public on the nature of science — ScienceDaily**
   A new study finds that researchers hold more divided views on what constitutes science than members of the general public. The variation may help explain public frustration with scientific uncertainty. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261119194541.htm)

8. **New pancreatic cancer mechanisms mapped before visible tumors form — BGNES**
   Researchers are mapping early cellular changes, KRAS mutations, and interactions with immune cells and surrounding tissue that precede visible pancreatic tumors. Most early tissue changes do not progress to malignancy. The work focuses on how inflammation and failed immune clearance may favor abnormal cell survival. Source: [bgnes.com](http://www.bgnes.com/health/new-scientific-discoveries-shed-light-on-the-development-of-pancreatic-cancer)

### Science Deep Dive: Why observed mutation rates do not reflect built-in gene protection
Most people assume that evolution has arranged for the most important genes to mutate less often than less critical ones, preserving essential functions across generations. The actual pattern arises from natural selection acting after mutations occur. Harmful changes in essential genes are more likely to prevent reproduction or survival, so those variants disappear from populations before scientists can count them in living organisms. Studies that examine only surviving genomes therefore record lower mutation rates in critical genes even though the initial mutation process itself shows no such bias. In the recent analysis of bacteria and yeast, researchers found equivalent mutation frequencies across gene categories once they accounted for the filtering effect of selection. This distinction matters because it changes how geneticists interpret variation data when estimating disease risk or evolutionary timelines. One concrete implication is that efforts to predict mutation hotspots should focus on biochemical features of DNA rather than assumptions about gene importance. Future whole-genome sequencing of early embryonic cells, before selection has acted, could settle remaining questions about raw mutation rates.
