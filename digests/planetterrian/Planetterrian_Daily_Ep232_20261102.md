### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Northwestern launches AI cloud lab for rapid protein design — The Daily Northwestern**
   McCormick Prof. Julius Lucks leads the DREAM Cloud Lab after the National Science Foundation awarded the Center for Synthetic Biology $20 million over four years. The facility pairs computational infrastructure with robots that semi-automate synthesis in cell-free lysate systems on plates. AI models draw conclusions from large data sets to streamline the trial-and-error process of testing protein sequences. Co-Principal Investigator Michael Jewett at Stanford notes that protein shape, set by amino acid order, determines function as antibodies, detergents or contaminant detectors. The DREAM Brain chatbot will let remote users specify desired protein parameters and receive a data package after synthesis and characterization. Four initial science drivers focus on metal recovery, plastic degradation, biosensing and protein-based materials. The lab aims to serve as a Midwest engine for biotechnology and ease the protein engineering bottleneck faced by many developers. In October 2025 the center received 135 letters of interest from AI companies, research groups and academic institutions across 48 states within 48 hours. Lucks assembled a partnership between Northwestern scientists, Stanford University and the Chicago Biomedical Consortium. The existing biofoundry already owned by the Center for Synthetic Biology will be augmented with new computational infrastructure, robotic liquid handlers and staffing. Senior personnel Ashty Karim explained that proteins will be assembled reproducibly at small volumes so robots can pipette and move trays through measurement and analysis steps. Executive director Michelle Hoffmann of the Chicago Biomedical Consortium described the DREAM Brain as a chatbot that accepts natural-language requests for proteins with specified parameters and then returns job status updates plus final data packages. Source: [dailynorthwestern.com](https://dailynorthwestern.com/2026/11/01/lateststories/nus-new-dream-cloud-lab-looks-to-reshape-biotechnology/)

2. **Measles infections and deaths rose sharply after 2020 — Nature**
   A global study documented nearly 16 million children under age five contracting measles in 2023, with 118,000 deaths. Vaccination rates fell after pandemic disruptions to public-health programmes and funding reversed a decades-long decline. The GBD 2023 Measles Collaborators reported the findings in Nature Medicine. The study tracked both infections and mortality across multiple regions where routine immunization schedules had been interrupted. Authors noted that the reversal affected the previous trajectory of steady reductions achieved over prior decades. The data compilation drew on surveillance systems that captured both confirmed cases and modeled estimates for areas with incomplete reporting. Source: [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03387-3)

3. **Human metabolism now releases over two billion tons of CO₂ yearly — Phys.org**
   A University of Bath-led analysis of 216,502 people estimated that bodily CO₂ output rose 74 percent between 1985 and 2015 to reach 2.05 billion metric tons annually. The work helps clarify the contribution of human respiration and energy metabolism to the global carbon budget. Researchers calculated individual metabolic rates from demographic and dietary data before scaling to national and global populations. The 74 percent increase reflects both population growth and shifts in average body mass and activity patterns over the three-decade span. The resulting figure represents a measurable flux within the overall terrestrial carbon cycle that had previously received limited quantification. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-human-metabolism-billion-metric-tons.html)

4. **Daily peanuts improve insulin response in memory-related brain regions — ScienceDaily**
   Researchers gave older adults two servings of unsalted skin-roasted peanuts each day for 16 weeks. The regimen enhanced insulin responsiveness in brain areas tied to memory, a function that typically declines with age and links to Alzheimer’s risk. Participants consumed the peanuts as a single addition to their usual diets without other prescribed changes. Imaging and metabolic assessments before and after the period showed measurable shifts in regional insulin signaling. The study focused on brain regions where age-related insulin resistance has been associated with later cognitive decline. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261030003001.htm)

5. **Common pesticide tied to more than double the Parkinson’s risk — ScienceDaily**
   A UCLA study linked long-term exposure to one widely used pesticide with over 2.5 times higher Parkinson’s disease risk. Investigators traced how the chemical harms brain cells and identified a potential protective route. The work combined epidemiological records with cellular assays to map both the elevated disease odds and the intracellular damage mechanism. Researchers also tested interventions that preserved cell viability despite the exposure. The findings add a specific environmental factor to the set of known contributors to Parkinson’s incidence. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261031013035.htm)

6. **Crown-of-thorns starfish can increase coral diversity when numbers stay low — ScienceDaily**
   On Australia’s Great Barrier Reef, nearly 75 percent of observed starfish feeding events left living coral tissue intact rather than destroying colonies. By consuming fast-growing dominant corals, the predators open space for slower-growing species. The observations covered multiple reef sites where starfish densities remained below outbreak thresholds. Partial feeding events allowed surviving coral fragments to recover and permitted recruitment of additional species. The pattern suggests that controlled predation pressure can function as a natural diversity maintainer rather than solely a destructive force. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261030002957.htm)

7. **Regular tea drinking linked to 11 percent lower cardiovascular risk — ScienceDaily**
   A study of nearly 486,000 adults associated habitual tea consumption with an 11 percent reduction in heart and blood vessel disease incidence. Risk reached its lowest point at roughly six to eight cups daily, with black tea showing steadier benefits than green tea. Data collection captured self-reported intake frequency alongside verified medical outcomes over an extended follow-up period. The dose-response curve indicated a plateau effect beyond the six-to-eight-cup range. Black tea maintained the association across subgroups while green tea benefits appeared more variable. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261031221203.htm)

8. **17-million-year-old ape fossil points to North African origins for modern apes — ScienceDaily**
   The specimen, recovered in Egypt, challenges the long-standing view that East Africa was the sole cradle of ape evolution. Researchers now suggest North Africa and the Middle East may have played earlier roles. The fossil dates to the early Miocene and preserves dental and skeletal features that align with later ape lineages. Its location extends the geographic range previously considered for the earliest diversification events. The discovery prompts re-examination of migration and speciation models that had centered exclusively on East African sites. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261031011638.htm)

9. **Conifers planted outside native ranges grow up to four times faster — Phys.org**
   Across 192 sites on four continents, Southern Hemisphere plantings of conifers outperformed their Northern Hemisphere source populations in both growth rate and drought resistance. The pattern holds implications for carbon sequestration strategies. Trees grown far from their evolutionary origins exhibited accelerated biomass accumulation without increased mortality under variable rainfall. The study aggregated long-term forestry trial data to compare performance metrics across matched genetic provenances. Faster growth combined with maintained drought tolerance suggests potential advantages for plantation-based carbon capture in certain climates. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-trees-home-carbon.html)

10. **Oxford group reports advance in non-invasive liver assessment — Radcliffe Department of Medicine**
    Researchers at the Radcliffe Department of Medicine developed a new approach to liver testing that avoids invasive procedures. Details of the method and validation data appear on the department site. The technique aims to provide equivalent diagnostic information through imaging or biomarker combinations that do not require tissue sampling. Early validation focused on accuracy relative to established biopsy standards. The advance addresses patient burden and procedural risks associated with conventional liver evaluation. Source: [rdm.ox.ac.uk](https://www.rdm.ox.ac.uk/news/breakthrough-in-non-invasive-liver-testing?ref=image)

### Planetterrian Spotlight
The DREAM Cloud Lab pairs large language models with automated synthesis hardware so that a researcher can describe a desired protein function in natural language and receive both the physical molecule and performance data days later. The system first interprets the request through the DREAM Brain interface, then directs robotic liquid handlers to assemble DNA constructs in cell-free lysate reactions before running functional assays. Early test cases target plastic-degrading enzymes and environmental biosensors, fields where conventional protein engineering has been limited by the sheer number of sequence variants that must be screened. By removing the need for every lab to maintain its own biofoundry, the platform could let smaller groups test ideas that previously required industrial-scale resources. One open question is how broadly the AI models will generalize once they move beyond the four initial science drivers chosen for validation. The lab already possessed a functional biofoundry before the NSF award, allowing rapid addition of computational and robotic layers rather than starting from empty facilities. Lucks emphasized that the goal is to solve the protein engineering bottleneck once so that multiple downstream biotechnology applications can proceed without repeating the same manual optimization work. Karim highlighted the importance of reproducible small-volume reactions that robots can handle consistently across thousands of variants. Hoffmann noted that the user-facing chatbot will present menus of parameters after the initial prompt, guiding non-specialists through feasible design choices before synthesis begins.

### Science Deep Dive: How AI changes the speed of protein engineering
Most people assume that designing a new protein still requires years of painstaking manual experiments in which researchers test one amino-acid sequence after another. In reality, the Northwestern DREAM Cloud Lab shows that AI models can predict folding patterns and functional properties from sequence data alone, then hand the best candidates to robotic systems that build and assay them in days rather than years. The mechanism begins with large data sets of known protein structures; machine-learning algorithms identify statistical patterns that link sequence to shape and activity, allowing the model to propose variants likely to perform a requested task. When those predictions reach the physical lab, automated liquid handlers assemble the corresponding DNA in small-volume cell-free reactions, and plate readers record whether the resulting proteins bind their targets or catalyze the desired reaction. The article states that this workflow can shrink the timeline of protein sequence testing from years to days. Proteins perform essential roles inside every human body, from antibodies that recognize pathogens to enzymes that process nutrients, yet most people never consider how new versions of these molecules are created for medicines, industrial cleaners or environmental sensors. The DREAM system makes that creation step faster by letting the AI handle the initial exploration of sequence space while robots execute the physical construction and testing. One concrete number from the project is the reduction of protein engineering cycles from a multi-year process to a matter of days once the models and automation are combined. What changes for a listener is that enzymes capable of breaking down environmental plastics or detecting water contaminants could move from computational idea to field-ready molecule on a much shorter schedule, provided the same shared platform becomes accessible beyond the initial partner institutions. The next results that would settle remaining uncertainty are performance data from the four planned science drivers on metal recovery, plastic degradation, biosensing of environmental contaminants and protein-based material production. Those outcomes will show whether the AI predictions hold across diverse functional requirements and whether the robotic assembly steps maintain consistency at the scale needed for broader adoption. The project also records that 135 external groups expressed interest within two days of the announcement, indicating demand for exactly this kind of accelerated workflow. By centralizing the biofoundry and computational resources, the lab removes the requirement that every research team maintain its own expensive automation suite. The four science drivers were deliberately chosen to represent common protein-engineering goals that many groups encounter, providing a practical test of whether the end-to-end pipeline delivers usable molecules rather than just faster calculations.
