### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries
1. **Scorpion venom contains diverse bacterial communities** — Hartford Courant
   Researchers at Eastern Connecticut State University identified previously unknown microbial communities in scorpion venom using novel sequencing techniques. The study, titled “Microbiota Discovered in Scorpion Venom,” examined venom from multiple species and found bacterial diversity that varied between individuals and species. A distinctive group called “Scorpion Group 1” appeared in the eastern swollen-stinger scorpion, expanding its known evolutionary range. The work refutes the prior assumption that scorpion venom is sterile. Microbes in venom could explain infection complications after stings and point to new antibiotic sources. Peptides already isolated from scorpion venom have shown activity against Staphylococcus and drug-resistant tuberculosis strains in lab and animal tests. Source: [courant.com](https://www.courant.com/2026/11/17/ct-researchers-make-discovery-that-could-fight-antibiotic-resistance-it-lies-in-venomous-arachnids/)

2. **Drone blow samples enable whole-genome whale sequencing** — Phys.org
   University of St Andrews researchers, working with the University of Copenhagen, North Coast Cetacean Society and Fisheries and Oceans Canada, obtained full nuclear and mitochondrial genomes from 58 blow samples of 26 North Pacific humpback whales. Approximately 84 percent of the DNA in each sample came from the whale. Genomes matched those from traditional biopsies on the same animals and showed consistent results across repeat samples collected over two years. The method also captured the whales’ respiratory microbiome, which made up roughly 16 percent of the DNA. Genetic diversity estimates aligned with biopsy data, and the population showed little recent inbreeding. Three previously undescribed mitochondrial variants were identified. All sampling occurred under Gitga’at First Nation agreements and a Fisheries and Oceans Canada permit. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-humpback-whale-snot-genome-sequencing.html)

3. **Plasma protein model predicts insulin resistance and disease** — Nature Metabolism
   A 13-protein panel plus clinical variables predicted insulin sensitivity measured by hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp with an R² of 0.67 in 161 individuals. In roughly 20,000 UK Biobank participants the model outperformed the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio for type 2 diabetes prediction. It also forecasted hepatic, renal, cardiovascular and respiratory disease where HbA1c did not. The proteins reflect contributions from liver, adipose tissue and immune cells. A subset shifted toward an insulin-sensitive profile after exercise. The signature was validated in an independent cohort of 1,700 people. Source: [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-026-01662-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=35944c69-07bb-40d9-8f31-3b6fe8c390e5)

4. **Enhanced rock weathering tested for faster carbon capture** — Phys.org
   University of California researchers flooded pasture plots at the Sierra Foothill Research and Extension Center to measure how irrigation affects rock-weathering rates in soils. Water moved through different soil depths and downslope paths over three-hour periods. The approach aims to accelerate natural mineral reactions that bind atmospheric carbon dioxide. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-weathering-carbon-capture.html)

Researchers outline how these engineered materials can replace bulk lenses and mirrors in imaging and sensing devices. ### Planetterrian Spotlight
**Humpback whale genomes sequenced from drone-collected blow without biopsies** — Phys.org
Researchers flew sterile petri dishes on commercial drones through the exhaled breath of individual whales in British Columbia’s Kitimat Fjord System. Fifty-eight samples from 26 animals produced low-coverage nuclear genomes and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes that matched biopsy-derived sequences from the same whales. The approach captured both host DNA and airway microbes in a single collection. It was conducted under Gitga’at First Nation research agreements and a federal permit, with more than one-third of flights launched directly from land. The data set supplies the first population-level whole-genome resource for North Pacific humpbacks. Lead researcher Éadin O’Mahony noted plans to examine kinship and social-bond formation among foraging whales. ### Science Deep Dive: Microbes inside venom
Most people assume venom glands evolved to stay sterile so the host avoids self-infection. Scorpion venom instead supports distinct bacterial communities that differ by species and individual. These microbes live in a protein-rich, ionically harsh fluid previously considered inhospitable. The finding comes from the first systematic sequencing of scorpion venom microbiota, which recovered a previously rare bacterial lineage now shown in additional species. Listeners who picture venom only as a toxin delivery system now have evidence that it also functions as a selective microbial habitat. One practical implication is that sting-related infections may trace to venom-resident bacteria rather than external contamination alone. Follow-up work can test whether any of these microbes produce compounds with antibiotic activity, directly linking the discovery to new drug leads.
