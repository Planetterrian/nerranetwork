### Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

1. **Global Canopy Atlas maps forests in 3D across 56 countries — Phys.org**
   An international team led by the University of Bristol assembled nearly 3,500 aerial LiDAR surveys covering more than 5.5 million hectares. The atlas supplies harmonized maps of canopy height and terrain elevation from airplanes, helicopters and drones. Almost 20 percent of the surveyed area includes repeated measurements that capture ecological change over time. Researchers from more than 100 institutions contributed data spanning 224 ecological regions. A public website and archive let users locate surveys by location, date and collector. The maps support carbon-stock estimation, biodiversity monitoring and validation of satellite systems such as the European Space Agency’s BIOMASS mission. The study appears in Nature Ecology and Evolution after a five-year project. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-atlas-laser-surveys-forests-countries.html)

2. **Virginia Tech converts PVC waste into engine-lubricant base oil — ScienceDaily**
   Chemists placed common PVC from pipes, frames and cards in solvent with aluminum trichloride and alpha-olefins. The mixture heated to 158 degrees Fahrenheit for three hours yielded polyalphaolefin, a stable synthetic lubricant base stock. The process upcycles material that resists conventional recycling because of its chlorine content and additives. Earlier work from the same lab turned other plastics into surfactants used in soaps. The resulting oil belongs to a family valued for stability across wide temperature ranges. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261126033501.htm)

3. **Lower oxygen triples lifespan in mice lacking HTRA2 protein — ScienceDaily**
   Gladstone Institutes researchers found that defective HTRA2 impairs mitochondrial Complex 1, allowing oxygen to accumulate to toxic levels in brain tissue. Mice deficient in HTRA2 that breathed reduced-oxygen air lived three times longer than littermates in normal air and showed less striatal inflammation. The same screen identified 75 other genes linked to disorders that might respond to hypoxia. The team traced HTRA2’s role to a clean-up partnership with CLPB that prevents misfolded proteins from clogging Complex 1. Postdoctoral fellow Ankur Garg led the mouse experiments. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261125045304.htm)

4. **Recreational exercise lowers dementia risk while occupational labor raises it — ScienceDaily**
   University of Southern California analysis of 74 studies covering more than 4.2 million adults found recreational activity linked to 24 percent lower all-cause dementia risk. Physically demanding work correlated with 20 percent higher risk after median 10-year follow-up. Household chores showed benefit in the single study that measured them. The pattern held for Alzheimer’s and vascular dementia and was non-linear: risk declined with more leisure activity but rose with more occupational activity before plateauing. First author Natan Feter noted that social and environmental factors around work likely drive the difference. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261125045246.htm)

5. **MIT engineers attach calicheamicin to albumin and add an antidote protein to reduce toxicity while preserving antibacterial action — MIT Health**
   The conjugate is cleaved only by neutrophil elastase at infection sites. An accompanying antidote circulates systemically and further shields the liver. In mice the modified compound cleared bacterial pneumonia with lower liver damage than the free drug. The approach re-uses a cancer-approved molecule whose safety data already exist, shortening the path to antibiotic development. Lead author Tahoura Samad and senior author Sangeeta Bhatia conducted the work. Source: [news.mit.edu](https://news.mit.edu/2026/engineering-antibiotics-could-expand-new-drug-candidates-pool-1127)

6. **Naturally released exercise molecule strengthens muscle and resists fatigue — ScienceDaily**
   The compound improves force generation, delays fatigue and protects against damage in laboratory tests. It is already sold as a dietary supplement and is being examined for muscle loss tied to type 2 diabetes. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261126063315.htm)

7. **Screen exposure in infancy and early school years predicts weaker memory and academics — ScienceDaily**
   Greater screen time during those two windows tracked with poorer working memory and school performance measured years later. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261126033220.htm)

8. **Harvard device multiplies ultraviolet output 120-fold from red light — ScienceDaily**
   The microscopic chip converts red light into UV at efficiencies that could shrink quantum computers, atomic clocks and environmental sensors. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261126063333.htm)

9. **Shark-cartilage discovery points to sustainable biomaterial routes — University of Auckland**
   Researchers identified structural features in shark cartilage that may be replicated without harvesting animals. Source: [auckland.ac.nz](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2026/11/27/shark-cartilage-breakthrough-opens-new-possibilities-sustainable-biotechnology.html)

10. **Wernicke’s area contributes to speech planning beyond word recognition — Nature**

### Science Deep Dive: Why Context Changes How Movement Affects the Brain
Most people assume every form of physical movement benefits the brain equally. In reality the setting of activity determines whether it protects or elevates dementia risk. Recreational exercise occurs at chosen intensities with built-in recovery and social contact, whereas occupational labor often involves repetitive strain, limited rest and workplace stressors that compound over years. The USC review of more than 4.2 million adults quantified this split: leisure activity tracked with a 24 percent risk reduction while work activity tracked with a 20 percent increase. Listeners can therefore prioritize scheduled movement they control rather than relying solely on job demands for brain-health benefits. The next decisive measurement would be whether adding protected recovery time to physically demanding jobs narrows the observed gap.
