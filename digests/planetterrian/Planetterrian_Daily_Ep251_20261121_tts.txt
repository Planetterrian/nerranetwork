Columbia University and University of California Berkeley researchers published findings in Nature, with ScienceDaily carrying the account, that as plastic particles break into smaller pieces, more surface is exposed.

Contact with water at those extra surfaces then yields high-energy radicals.

Wei Min noted that such radicals are able to harm animal and human tissue.

In two thousand twenty-six, a federal program put one hundred forty-four million dollars toward research on toxic microplastics inside the body.

Something most people get wrong is the belief that plastic sits chemically idle once it has broken into microscopic pieces.

Hydrophobic surfaces change how water molecules and charged particles behave at the boundary, and that shift lets electrons move between species, so reactive oxygen species form right there rather than drifting in from somewhere else.

After particles reach blood or tissues that hold abundant water, the same boundary chemistry can run inside a human body.

Wei Min's group traced the radicals directly to the plastic-water interface rather than to any additive or contaminant.

That interface chemistry supplies one concrete mechanism that could connect documented microplastic exposure to reported associations with cardiovascular and neurological conditions.

What remains uncertain is the quantitative contribution of this pathway to actual tissue damage in people.

One practical step is to reduce unnecessary plastic contact with food and water.

Ribonucleic acid, or R N A, acts as a scaffold that organizes the protein synapsin-1 into condensates at neurotransmitter release sites, according to work from Duke University and Charité Berlin University Institute of Biochemistry published in Advanced Science and described by Phys dot org.

The work combined imaging in live neurons with a reconstituted system that showed translation happening in place.

Amy Gladfelter and Dragomir Milovanovic led the team, which included Branislava Rankovic and Zachary Geisterfer.

The results establish a structural job for R N A beyond carrying information.

Senghor Seamount off Cape Verde produced two new armored tardigrade species after researchers from Senckenberg am Meer examined seamount sediment.

They described Megastygarctides hekatae and Parastygarctus potercha from two hundred nineteen specimens, and two hundred twelve of those animals came from the summit.

The plateau supported twenty tardigrade taxa, while a deep-sea reference site one hundred kilometers away supported only two.

Megastygarctides hekatae carries rare genital spines and turned up at about one hundred meters depth.

Parastygarctus potercha shows pedomorphism, with a smaller body and fewer claws.
