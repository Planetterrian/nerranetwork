Yale School of Medicine, according to Science Daily, pooled clinical studies that tracked DNA methylation biomarkers used to estimate biological age.

More than one hundred ten of those biomarkers went into the analysis, and sixteen major epigenetic clocks sat among them.

The trials measured methylation patterns before each intervention and again after it, giving every clock a paired reading.

All of that data came from blood samples, which were run through established epigenetic-clock algorithms.

Lead author Raghav Sehgal noted that the work marks the first demonstration that certain therapies produce measurable shifts in these markers.

Mouse models in which a T-cell enzyme was selectively deleted, reported in Immunity, showed slower aging across multiple organs.

Immune function remained intact in the modified animals.

Organs in the deficient animals showed reduced age-related decline compared with controls, so organ aging slowed while the immune system still worked.

Penn Dental Medicine scientists, according to Phys.org, mapped a previously unknown molecular switch that lets bacteria rapidly compromise epithelial defenses.

The mechanism allows pathogens to alter host-cell junctions without triggering immediate immune detection.

That switch acts through a previously unrecognized pathway, which is why the breach can begin before an immune alarm sounds.

Experiments showed that blocking the mechanism preserved barrier function in cell and tissue models.

The work identifies a potential target for interventions that preserve barrier integrity during early infection, aimed at host-side protection rather than direct bacterial killing.

One open question is whether the same pathway operates across different epithelial surfaces in living animals.

Intestinal bacteria produced a compound that, according to Medical Xpress, increased nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, or NAD, in models of gut injury.

The same molecule restored tissue integrity after the injury.

The effect ran through direct metabolic support rather than immune modulation.

Researchers traced that activity to a specific bacterial biosynthetic pathway.

Elevated serotonin output from a defined brain region, reported by Lifespan.io, suppressed dopamine release in older mice.

That drop in dopamine went with lower voluntary movement.

Selective ablation of the serotonin-producing cells restored activity levels.

The study identifies a neural circuit that contributes to age-related inactivity.

Most people assume that deliberately smiling will lift their mood, yet University of Florida testing covered by ScienceDaily found the opposite when the action was repeated.

More than one thousand one hundred participants completed tasks that required them to smile either once or repeatedly under controlled conditions.

A single artificial smile produced a small positive shift in self-reported mood.

Repeating the action erased that benefit or reversed it, leaving people below baseline.

The pattern held after the team controlled for each person’s initial emotional state.

The mechanism appears tied to the brain registering a mismatch between the expression and the underlying feeling, and that mismatch grows more salient each time the smile is forced again.

Performed only once, simple motor feedback can still nudge affect upward.

Kept going, the repeated mismatch registers as inauthentic and subtracts from mood.

The briefing places that pattern in ordinary settings such as meetings, commutes, and family photos, where a smile is often held longer than a single beat.

Further trials that vary the duration and social context of forced smiling would clarify how widely the reversal applies.

Climate-oriented agricultural papers, in a Frontiers review carried by Phys.org, describe partnerships and institutional links often in project write-ups.

Those social relationships are seldom measured against actual scaling outcomes.

The authors examined how such ties influence whether innovations spread beyond pilot sites.

They conclude that explicit documentation of the relationships would improve monitoring and evaluation of scaling efforts.

Florida International University tested common classroom accommodations in students with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD.

The supports included extended time, frequent breaks, and quiet workspaces.

None of those supports produced measurable gains in task completion or accuracy relative to control conditions.

Performance was tracked on standardized academic measures across multiple sessions.

A Tsukuba team isolated short peptides from the Drosophila Nemuri protein, a fruit-fly sleep protein.

Those peptides killed drug-resistant bacteria in culture.

They retained activity against strains that had already developed resistance to conventional antibiotics.

The work was published in Nature Communications.

Circulating NAD in more than one thousand adults, according to Medical Xpress, tracked cardiovascular disease status more closely than chronological age.

Participants with established heart disease displayed lower NAD independent of their birth year.

The association persisted after statistical adjustment for common risk factors.

Comparative genomic analysis, according to Phys.org, revealed sequence changes that increase progenitor-cell proliferation in the developing primate cortex.

The alterations differ from previously known human-specific variants.

They still produce comparable expansion of cortical layers.

The work examined multiple primate species to trace the evolutionary timing of the changes.
