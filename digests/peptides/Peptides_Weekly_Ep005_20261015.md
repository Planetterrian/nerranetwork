# Peptides Weekly
> **Three to four extra rare vision-loss cases per ten thousand showed up in an observational GLP-1 study.**

**What You Need to Know:** Three to four extra rare vision-loss cases per ten thousand showed up in an observational GLP-1 study. The link was seen in adults with type 2 diabetes and does not prove the drugs injured the optic nerve. Elsewhere this week, an investigational oral GLP-1 program stopped a mid-stage study, and the healing peptide BPC-157 remains a story of laboratory and rodent work rather than a proven human medicine.

### The Week in Peptides
**US study links GLP-1 drugs to rare vision loss: UA.NEWS:** Rutgers researchers, writing in Annals of Internal Medicine and reported by ScienceDaily and UA.NEWS, looked at US adults ages 18 to 65 with type 2 diabetes who had recently started medicines for the disease. In that observational comparison, starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist was associated with a small rise in ischemic optic neuropathy, a rare condition from reduced blood flow to the optic nerve. Chintan Dave of Rutgers said the increase was about three to four additional cases per 10,000 patients over 18 months, and that the condition can involve sudden vision loss. Semaglutide, sold as Ozempic and Wegovy, and tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, are approved medicines used to control blood sugar and body weight; the same class can also reduce the risk of certain cardiovascular and kidney problems. More than 80 percent of ischemic optic neuropathy cases, on estimates cited by UA.NEWS, are the nonarteritic anterior form, and recorded cases were more often in men and in people ages 50 to 65. The authors said the association does not prove a direct causal effect, because other health conditions may have shaped the results, and they advised not delaying an eye exam after a sudden change in vision. **Scientists may have overlooked a hidden Alzheimer's culprit for decades: ScienceDaily:** Researchers at UC Santa Cruz are drawing attention to a peptide called P3 that had been treated as harmless. This is not a human trial: the report describes P3 forming harmful-looking protein clumps even faster than amyloid beta and possibly damaging brain cells. Amyloid beta has been the long-standing target of Alzheimer’s drug work that often disappointed, and this write-up puts P3 beside that peptide as another possible contributor. The piece does not present a treatment result in people. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261013015838.htm)

**Verdiva ends mid-stage study of oral GLP-1 amid ‘market evolution’: BioSpace:** Verdiva has ended a mid-stage study of an oral GLP-1, citing “market evolution.” That is a company decision about an investigational program, not a published randomized result and not an approval. Source: [biospace.com](https://www.biospace.com/drug-development/verdiva-ends-mid-stage-study-of-oral-glp-1-amid-market-evolution)

**As FDA Weighs Wider Peptide Access, Adverse Event Reports and Two Las Vegas Intubations Sharpen Safety Questions: Medical Daily:** Medical Daily reports that the FDA is weighing wider peptide access while adverse-event reports and two Las Vegas intubations sharpen safety questions, in a piece tied to BPC-157. This is news reporting of harm reports and a policy question, not a clinical trial. Unregulated peptide products carry purity and contamination risks that sit beside any access decision. Source: [medicaldaily.com](https://www.medicaldaily.com/fda-peptide-decision-bpc-157-harm-reports-las-vegas-intubations-479941)

**FDA Peptide Compounding Policy: What a Temporary Enforcement Reprieve Could Mean for Pharmacies: Buchanan Ingersoll & Rooney PC:** A legal briefing describes a temporary FDA enforcement reprieve in peptide compounding policy and what it could mean for pharmacies. That is a regulatory-policy report, not evidence that a compounded peptide works or is safe. Compounded products are not the same thing as an approved, fully reviewed medicine. Source: [bipc.com](https://www.bipc.com/fda-peptide-compounding-policy-what-a-temporary-enforcement-reprive-could-mean-for-pharmacies)

**MU Health Care physician warns against use of unregulated peptides: KOMU 8:** A physician at MU Health Care has warned against use of unregulated peptides. The warning is clinical commentary reported by a local news outlet, not a new trial. Unregulated products can differ in purity and contamination from anything studied under a regulator’s rules. ### Peptide Spotlight: BPC-157: Popular, Unapproved, Mostly Animal Data
BPC-157 is a pentadecapeptide of 15 amino acids, a partial sequence of a body-protection compound discovered in and isolated from human gastric juice.

In plain terms, the research groups studying it treat it as a healing signal that also touches blood-vessel chemistry. A 1997 rat study in the European Journal of Pharmacology found that, in stomach-tissue homogenates, BPC-157 induced nitric-oxide generation comparable to L-arginine, and that L-NAME, which blocks nitric-oxide synthase, did not inhibit that peptide effect the way it inhibited L-arginine. Combining the peptide with L-arginine blunted nitric-oxide synthesis in that in vitro setup. In the same paper’s rat experiments, the peptide reduced ethanol-induced gastric lesions and altered blood-pressure responses to L-NAME and L-arginine in a pattern that was not identical to L-arginine itself. A 2014 review in Current Pharmaceutical Design frames this as participation in the nitric-oxide system’s response to lost vascular integrity: depending on the injury model, the authors describe less thrombosis or less bleeding and thrombocytopenia. That review also says the peptide stimulates egr-1 and naB2 genes. A 2018 review in Current Pharmaceutical Design adds angiogenic growth-factor signaling—EGF, FGF, and VEGF—as the comparison set, and says BPC-157’s own angiogenic effect shows up in the healing models those authors reviewed.

The strongest evidence in these abstracts is still preclinical, and it is uneven. The 2011 tendon study is ex vivo and in vitro work on rat Achilles-tendon fibroblasts, not a trial in people. BPC-157 accelerated outgrowth from tendon explants. It did not directly change fibroblast proliferation on an MTT assay. Cells treated with it survived hydrogen-peroxide stress better. Migration rose in a dose-dependent way on a transwell assay, spreading on culture dishes accelerated, and F-actin formed. Phosphorylation of FAK and paxillin rose as the amount of peptide rose, while total protein did not change. The authors’ conclusion is that outgrowth, stress survival, and migration are likely mediated by the FAK-paxillin pathway. Separately, a 2026 critical review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences, with searches through 24 June 2026, evaluates rodent ischemia-reperfusion and related vascular-injury models. Across that literature, administration was associated with less oxidative injury, shifts in nitric-oxide-related vessel responses, lower inflammatory and apoptotic markers, and changes in VEGF/VEGFR2-Akt-eNOS-related signaling. Organ-specific reports in that review include biochemical and histological protection in lower-extremity skeletal-muscle ischemia-reperfusion, less distant-organ injury after limb ischemia-reperfusion, neuronal and functional effects in hippocampal ischemia-reperfusion, vascular and tissue responses in intestinal and colonic ischemia-reperfusion, and hemodynamic and histological effects in a hepatic Pringle-maneuver model. The reviewers did not pool the studies: organs, doses, routes, timing, and outcomes were too heterogeneous. They also flag short observation, single-dose designs, and incompletely described risk of bias, and they say major-vessel occlusion models are not the same as conventional organ-specific ischemia-reperfusion.

A 2018 review claims BPC-157 was consistently effective, given alone, across acute and chronic injury models of esophagus, stomach, duodenum, and lower gut, by intraperitoneal, oral, or local routes, and that the same regimens improved tendon, ligament, and bone healing in the work reviewed. Those are animal and experimental models summarized by the reviewers, not outcomes measured in patients. A 2013 review in Current Pharmaceutical Design calls BPC-157 an anti-ulcer peptide “proven in clinical trials to be both safe in inflammatory bowel disease” under the codes PL-10, PLD-116, and PL 14736, and in wound healing, and says no toxicity has been reported and an LD1 was not achieved. That is a review’s assertion. The abstract does not report how many people were enrolled, what was measured, or what the trials found beyond that safety claim. The same review argues it could counter several NSAID injuries in stomach, duodenum, intestine, liver, and brain, plus effects on bleeding and thrombosis, again from the experimental work the authors assemble. A 2014 review is explicit that translating nitric-oxide modulation into better clinical performance “remains to be determined.”

A 2026 materials paper in 3 Biotech is not a healing trial. It built a chitosan hydrogel carrying BPC-157, saw a porous structure, and by infrared spectroscopy found hydrogen bonding between the peptide and the chitosan backbone. Water-vapor transmission was 2,270 ± 35 g/m²/day. Encapsulation was 98.9 ± 0.8 percent, with 81.2 ± 2.9 percent released within 24 hours. Antibacterial inhibition reached 45.9 percent against Escherichia coli and 65.0 percent against Staphylococcus aureus. Hemolysis was under 5 percent, which the authors read as acceptable hemocompatibility in that lab test. Those are physicochemical and in vitro antibacterial findings.

These abstracts do not establish BPC-157 as an approved medicine. Medical Daily’s report this week, kept separate above, ties adverse-event reports and two Las Vegas intubations to questions about wider peptide access and BPC-157; that reporting is not efficacy evidence. Authors of the older reviews describe a high preclinical safety margin. That is not the same as a completed human safety record, and it says nothing about purity or contamination when a peptide is sold outside a controlled study.

What it is: BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid fragment related to a peptide found in human gastric juice.
Humans given it: Named inflammatory-bowel-disease trial codes appear in a 2013 review, which does not report how many people were studied.
Risks: Preclinical “no toxicity” claims are not a human safety proof, and harm reports are already part of the access debate.
What it does not show: Rodent and cell findings do not show that BPC-157 heals tendon, gut, or ischemia-reperfusion injury in people.

### Worth Knowing
- An observational association between starting a GLP-1 medicine and a rare optic-nerve injury, in adults with type 2 diabetes, is a signal to understand, not proof the drug caused the injury.
- A rare association can still matter when a medicine is used widely, which is why the study authors called the finding clinically important even while describing the absolute risk as low.
- Stopping a mid-stage oral GLP-1 study is a development decision about an investigational peptide drug, not evidence that the class failed or succeeded in people.

### Evidence Ledger
- BPC-157, a 15-amino-acid gastric-juice peptide, sped tendon-fibroblast outgrowth, stress survival, and migration in rat cell and explant work via FAK-paxillin phosphorylation, without changing proliferation on an MTT assay; ex vivo/in vitro animal-cell evidence. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21030672
- In rats and in gastric-mucosa homogenates, BPC-157 interfered with nitric-oxide effects on gastric lesions and blood pressure in a pattern distinct from L-arginine, and L-NAME did not block its nitric-oxide generation the way it blocked L-arginine; animal and in vitro evidence. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9298922
- A review links BPC-157 to nitric-oxide homeostasis after vascular injury and states that clinical translation remains to be determined; narrative review of preclinical work. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23755725
- A review says only BPC-157, among the peptides compared, was consistently effective in the gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal injury models surveyed; narrative review of experimental models. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29998800
- A review asserts clinical-trial safety in inflammatory bowel disease under codes PL-10, PLD-116, and PL 14736, and assembles experimental counter-effects to NSAID injury, while reporting no achieved LD1; review assertion, trial headcount not in the abstract. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22950504
- A 2026 critical review finds rodent ischemia-reperfusion studies associated BPC-157 with less oxidative, inflammatory, and apoptotic injury and with VEGF-related signaling changes, but judges the evidence heterogeneous and bias-prone; preclinical review, no meta-analysis. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42794771
- A chitosan/BPC-157 hydrogel showed high encapsulation, substantial 24-hour release, antibacterial inhibition, and hemolysis under 5 percent in laboratory tests; materials and in vitro evidence, not a human trial. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42555375

Knowing which rung a peptide claim sits on is the useful habit this week.
