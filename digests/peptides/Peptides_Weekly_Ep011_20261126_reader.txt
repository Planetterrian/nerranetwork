Gombos and colleagues lined up screening mammograms from before and after at least a year on a GLP-1 receptor agonist used for weight loss, an observational before-and-after look rather than a randomized trial, scheduled for the Radiological Society of North America meeting in Chicago from November twenty-ninth to December third, twenty twenty-six, and not yet peer-reviewed in a journal.

The named drugs include Ozempic and Wegovy. One hundred seven women, ages thirty-four to seventy-nine, lost anywhere from six pounds to one hundred seven, forty on average, across two hundred fourteen scans.

Eight women jumped two density categories, nobody in the set moved down, and Everyday Health, quoting Gombos, put the share classed as dense at a near tripling, about fifteen percent before and about forty-two percent after. Reuters’s account of the same meeting report said density rose in every woman, and that women who lost more weight showed a larger density rise.

Reviewer to reviewer, smaller breasts showed up in roughly fifty-eight to seventy-nine percent, and thicker skin in about nine to eighteen percent. Callbacks for more imaging hit twenty-two point four percent, against an average U.S. recall rate near ten percent. Two of those callbacks were technical, for positioning or the image itself.

Four women went on to biopsy, and all four results were benign. Gombos reads the pattern as fat leaving the breast faster than the glandular tissue, so the picture looks denser even if the absolute amount of fibroglandular tissue falls.

She and Melanie Chellman, a Cleveland Clinic breast radiologist who was not on the study, both say nobody yet knows whether this particular density rise changes long-term breast cancer risk. Chellman adds that smaller, less fatty, denser breasts after weight loss are an old observation, with or without a GLP-1, and that the new part is how often large weight loss now happens.

Gombos said the report is not a reason for patients to be alarmed about taking these medicines.

GHK, this week's spotlight, is a human tripeptide of glycine, histidine, and lysine, glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, and a twenty eighteen review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences carries the copper-bound name GHK-Cu in its title while the abstract’s claims are written for the peptide itself. Those papers never state a drug approval, a trial phase, or a cosmetic authorization.

The review says GHK stimulates blood-vessel and nerve outgrowth, raises synthesis of collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycans, and supports dermal fibroblasts. Collagen and elastin are the structural proteins of connective tissue. Glycosaminoglycans are the sugar chains that hold water in that matrix. Fibroblasts are cells that build much of it.

On that telling, the peptide is a signal to repair machinery the tissue already has, not a filler parked in the skin. The abstract says improved repair has been demonstrated in skin, lung connective tissue, bone, liver, and stomach lining, and it does not split those demonstrations into cell, animal, or human work. 

The authors, writing from what they call current knowledge, also list actions they treat as health-positive: several anti-cancer activities, anti-inflammatory effects, lung protection, restoration of fibroblasts taken from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, suppression of N F kappa B, which they describe as a molecule thought to speed diseases of aging, plus effects they label anti-anxiety, anti-pain, and anti-aggression, DNA repair, and switching on cell cleansing through the proteasome, the cell’s protein-disposal system.

They suggest gene data may explain the spread, because GHK looks as if it regulates multiple biochemical pathways. That is a review reading gene findings, not a new count of people, wrinkles, wounds, or cancers.

The other paper is from nineteen eighty-three, and it is not a GHK experiment. In the test tube, L-ascorbic acid plus copper two ion, or plus a copper two tripeptide, cut several viral DNAs and proteins extensively. The tripeptide was diglycyl-L-histidine, a copper-binding three-amino-acid peptide of the same general kind, not identified there as GHK. Ascorbate alone did nothing apparent.

The copper tripeptide alone did nothing apparent. Of the transition metals tried with ascorbate, copper and iron were the most effective. Catalase, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide, partly blocked the DNA cutting. Superoxide dismutase did not. The authors read that as hydrogen peroxide in the pathway, and they say one product is probably the hydroxyl radical made from that peroxide.

Among reducing agents they tried, ascorbate was the strongest at cutting DNA and protein. Cell-free chemistry on viral material, not a human safety trial, and not a test of a cosmetic cream.

What is missing is the human rung. These abstracts never say how many people received GHK or GHK-Cu. They never show a randomized comparison of a copper-peptide cream against a plain cream. Anti-cancer language in a review summary is not fewer cancers in patients. The nineteen eighty-three cleavage is not shown to occur in living skin, and it is not shown to be irrelevant there either.

A list of repair pathways is not a drug claim, and nothing in these papers shows a product label matching a measured human result. The risk that is actually measured is narrow and chemical: a related copper tripeptide, quiet by itself in that assay, cut viral DNA and protein once ascorbate was added, on a peroxide route the authors tie to the hydroxyl radical.

That is a reason not to hear “copper peptide” as automatically gentle. It is not a dose, a protocol, or a reason to start or stop anything. Contamination of unregulated products is a separate problem these two papers do not document.

One thing worth knowing from the human evidence is that a density-category change on paired mammograms can alter a screening picture after GLP-1 weight loss without answering whether cancer risk moved.

Another is that a phase three trial in children with obesity separated semaglutide from placebo on weight, with more than a third crossing under the obesity line on body mass index, which is a trial result and not an instruction.
