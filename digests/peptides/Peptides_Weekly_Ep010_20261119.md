### The Week in Peptides
**Hims & Hers starts selling five compounded peptide and longevity treatments the FDA has not approved: MLQ.ai**
Hims & Hers introduced Peptides+ in the United States on November 18, 2026, a commercial launch of compounded products rather than a clinical trial. JournalArta reported that none of the specific products has Food and Drug Administration approval, and that the five formulations are sermorelin, glutathione, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), vitamin B-12 and L-carnitine, made in the United States by licensed pharmacies inspected by state pharmacy boards. More than 10,000 people joined an early-access list within 15 days of a late-October opening. Chief medical officer Anant Vinjamoori said, “We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this,” and said people with serious cardiovascular, liver or kidney disease will not be candidates for now. Tufts chemist Joshua Kritzer said that without large trials, rare serious complications are hard to rule out, and that anecdotal information is not enough to put a substance in the body; UC Davis professor Paul Knoepfler called dosing another uncertainty. Peradaban reported that the company also plans to add BPC-157 and TB-500, peptides whose existing research is mostly in animals rather than humans. ### Peptide Spotlight: Teriparatide and Bone-Building Peptides
Teriparatide is recombinant human parathyroid hormone, amino acids 1 to 34. A 2005 review in Endocrine Reviews placed parathyroid hormone and this analog in an anabolic class for severe osteoporosis, with a chance to change skeletal microarchitecture, against drugs that mainly inhibit bone resorption and reduce remodeling.

Daily use is the pattern the clinical reviews describe. A 2016 review in Osteoporosis International reported that teriparatide appears to increase bone formation more than bone resorption, on biochemical markers and on histology. It described two contributions. One is direct stimulation of formation inside sites that are already remodeling, and on surfaces that had been inactive. The other is an increase in new remodeling sites. Both, the authors wrote, contribute to the bone-density rise measured by DXA. Remodeling is the repair process that keeps skeletal tissue young, and when teriparatide stimulates it the review says each remodeling cavity ends in a positive bone balance, a piece the authors linked to fracture benefit. The same review said teriparatide reduces fracture risk and that the effect appears to increase with longer therapy. It judged a full continuous 24-month course better for skeletal outcomes than shorter periods, and said shorter novel regimens should wait for controlled trials against that 24-month daily course. Clinical use, it stated, is limited to 24 months. In 2016 the authors called teriparatide the only agent then available that increases formation of new bone and can partly remediate architectural defects. That “only” is dated to the paper.

The strongest human fracture evidence here is the phase III trial summarized in the 2005 Endocrine Reviews paper: elderly women with at least one prevalent vertebral fracture, and significant reductions in both vertebral and appendicular fracture rates. The review said there was not yet evidence that this antifracture effect was superior to bisphosphonates, and that cost-utility estimates found teriparatide significantly more expensive. A 2005 Health Technology Assessment systematic review included 90 randomized controlled trials on alendronate, etidronate, risedronate, raloxifene and teriparatide, among other comparators. All five of those interventions had reduced vertebral fracture risk in women with severe osteoporosis and adequate calcium intakes. None had been shown by direct comparison to be significantly more effective than the others. In the economic model, cost per quality-adjusted life-year fell sharply with age. Assuming severe osteoporosis at the diagnostic threshold, no treatment had a cost per quality-adjusted life-year below £35,000 at age 50. At age 60, raloxifene was estimated at £26,000 if hip fractures were unaffected and £31,000 if the hip effect was adverse, figures the authors said were driven by breast-cancer assumptions. No other intervention in that model was under £35,000 per quality-adjusted life-year at age 60.

A 2026 Regenerative Therapy paper is not that kind of evidence. Investigators built mouse bone-marrow exosomes carrying teriparatide messenger RNA and surfaced them with an Asp6 peptide meant to bind hydroxyapatite. In mouse stromal cells, the preparation raised teriparatide message and secreted protein, increased a CRE-dependent reporter signal, and increased osteogenic gene expression, alkaline phosphatase staining and matrix mineralization. It bound bone samples more than unmodified exosomes. After intravenous injection, bone-associated fluorescence remained detectable for at least 72 hours, with greater femoral and tibial signal and lower relative hepatic signal than unmodified exosomes. Those are cell findings and mouse biodistribution findings.

The 2005 review said teriparatide is not recommended for more than two years, based in part on osteosarcoma in a rat carcinogenicity model. It discussed use for postmenopausal women and men with severe osteoporosis, for established glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis when long-term steroids are required, and for some people younger than 65 with a T score of −3.5 or lower. Those are the review’s considerations, not a prompt to start or stop treatment. The review also treated mild hypercalcemia as something that can appear and that clinicians then managed. A 2026 case report in Bone Reports, a single patient, described a contralateral impending atypical femoral fracture after one dose of denosumab despite a radiologically normal opposite femur at the index fracture. The patient had long bisphosphonate exposure and incomplete teriparatide therapy. The authors called atypical femoral fracture a rare but serious complication of prolonged antiresorptive therapy, and sequential teriparatide-then-antiresorptive risk poorly characterized.

These papers present teriparatide as an established osteoanabolic agent with a 24-month clinical limit and phase III fracture data in older women. They do not show that it prevents more fractures than bisphosphonates, and the exosome work does not show a human result.

What it is: Teriparatide is a 34-amino-acid recombinant copy of human parathyroid hormone, described in these reviews as a bone-forming drug for severe osteoporosis.
Humans given it: A phase III trial studied elderly women who already had a vertebral fracture, and a health-technology review drew on 90 randomized trials in postmenopausal osteoporosis.
Regulatory status: The reviews treat teriparatide as an established clinical osteoporosis therapy whose use is limited to 24 months.
Risks: A rat carcinogenicity model produced osteosarcoma, which the 2005 review cites in the two-year limit, and a 2026 case report describes an impending atypical femoral fracture after a later denosumab dose.
What it does not show: The human reviews do not show superiority over bisphosphonates, and the mouse exosome experiments do not show that this delivery method works in people.

### Worth Knowing
- A real-world association between GLP-1 drugs and less alcohol use, or different eating, is a pattern seen in people, not evidence that the drugs caused the change.
- A fall in Medicaid weight-loss GLP-1 prescribing after four states withdrew coverage shows how payment rules change who receives these medicines, not whether the drugs work.
- Teriparatide’s fracture findings come from a randomized trial in women who already had a spine fracture, which is a different evidence class from compounded products sold without approval.

### Evidence Ledger
- A 2005 review described teriparatide, recombinant human PTH(1-34), as an anabolic therapy with phase III reductions in vertebral and appendicular fractures in elderly women with a prior vertebral fracture, no demonstrated superiority to bisphosphonates, a two-year limit tied in part to rat osteosarcoma, and mild hypercalcemia as a managed finding. Human review of a randomized trial, plus a stated animal carcinogenicity finding. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15769903
- A 2016 review reported that daily teriparatide increases bone formation more than resorption by remodeling-based and modeling-based formation and by starting more remodeling sites, that fracture benefit appears to grow with duration, and that clinical use is limited to 24 months. Human clinical review. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26902094
- A 2005 health-technology assessment found 90 randomized trials and reported vertebral-fracture reductions for five drugs including teriparatide, without direct proof that one was significantly more effective, and published age-dependent cost-per-QALY estimates. Meta-analysis of human randomized trials and an economic model. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15929857
- A 2026 mouse study of bone-targeting exosomes carrying teriparatide mRNA reported cell-level protein production, osteogenic readouts, bone binding and 72-hour skeletal fluorescence. Cell and animal study. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42926909
- A 2026 case report described a contralateral impending atypical femoral fracture after one denosumab dose in a bisphosphonate-pretreated patient with incomplete teriparatide therapy, despite a normal contralateral image at the first fracture. Single-patient human case report. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42965087

Unapproved availability and a signup list are not the same thing as a human result.
