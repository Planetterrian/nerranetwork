### Peptide Spotlight: Collagen Peptides as Supplements
Collagen peptides in these papers are short collagen fragments taken by mouth and studied as supplements, including as a nutricosmetic ingredient, not as a named approved drug.

The 2015 Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology paper says preclinical work had already suggested these peptides can improve skin barrier function, induce synthesis of collagen and hyaluronic acid, and promote fibroblast growth and migration. In human skin explants, an ex vivo model, collagen peptides induced collagen and glycosaminoglycan production. That is a tissue-level mechanism, not a proof of what happens after digestion in a living person. The clinical papers then look at downstream markers: skin hydration and dermal collagen structure, hair gloss and elasticity, and blood markers of bone turnover and inflammation.

Strongest human evidence in this set is randomized and placebo-controlled. In two placebo-controlled clinical trials reported in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology in 2015, oral collagen peptide supplementation increased skin hydration after 8 weeks, measured by corneometry. Collagen density in the dermis rose, and fragmentation of the dermal collagen network fell, after 4 weeks, measured by high-resolution ultrasound and reflectance confocal microscopy. Both of those structural changes persisted after 12 weeks.

In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial in the International Journal of Stem Cells, Korean adults aged 19 to 60 with mild to moderate hair damage and without alopecia received low-molecular-weight collagen peptide GT for 24 weeks. Compared with placebo, the treatment group had significant improvements in hair gloss, measured with a gloss meter and scanning electron microscopy, and in hair elasticity, assessed by tensile strength and hair diameter. Subjective scores also moved toward better gloss, elasticity, thickness, texture, fullness, and appearance, and scalp elasticity rose in the treatment group. No treatment-related adverse events were observed. The authors call that course safe and effective for overall hair condition in people with damaged hair and without alopecia. The same abstract says earlier preclinical work found that low-molecular-weight fish collagen peptides promoted hair regrowth in animals or laboratory models by activating proliferative signaling and suppressing inhibitory pathways, and that clinical efficacy for hair damage had not been clearly established before this trial. This human trial measured damage, gloss, and elasticity, not regrowth in alopecia.

In a pilot randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in Frontiers in Nutrition, 22 endurance-trained premenopausal women aged 18 to 35 who ran at least 35 miles a week were assigned to collagen peptides or an isocaloric maltodextrin placebo for four weeks. Blood was drawn in the early follicular phase. A group-by-time interaction for P1NP, a marker of bone formation, was significant at p = 0.04, with an increase in the collagen group and no change on placebo. CTX-1, a marker of bone breakdown, showed no significant interaction (p = 0.13). The results text reports a significant interaction for IL-6 (p = 0.03). It also reports a significant interaction for sRANKL (p = 0.046) and, later, no significant interaction for sRANKL, osteoprotegerin, or the sRANKL/osteoprotegerin ratio, so the osteoclast-signal finding is not cleanly established in this write-up. The discussion says short-term supplementation increased a bone-formation marker, altered osteoclast-related signaling, and reduced IL-6, and that structural bone outcomes still need larger trials.

In a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial in the British Journal of Nutrition, 53 men with sarcopenia, mean age 72.2 years (standard deviation 4.68), completed 12 weeks of guided resistance training three sessions a week plus either collagen peptides or a silica placebo. Everyone who finished improved: fat-free mass, bone mass, isokinetic quadriceps strength, and sensory motor control rose, and fat mass fell (all P < 0.01). The collagen arm changed more than placebo: fat-free mass +4.2 kg (standard deviation 2.31) versus +2.9 kg (standard deviation 1.84); quadriceps strength +16.5 newton-metres (standard deviation 12.9) versus +7.3 (standard deviation 13.2); fat mass −5.4 kg (standard deviation 3.17) versus −3.5 kg (standard deviation 2.16); each comparison P < 0.05. That extra change is a training-plus-supplement result in older men, not an effect of collagen peptides alone.

What is not established: these abstracts do not show fracture prevention, treatment of alopecia, or a drug-style disease indication. Skin and hair findings are appearance and tissue measures. The bone pilot is 22 women over four weeks and does not include structural bone outcomes. The sarcopenia result cannot be separated from the resistance program. One abstract contradicts itself on sRANKL.

These papers present collagen peptides as oral supplements and a nutricosmetic ingredient. They do not describe an approved drug use. The hair trial recorded no treatment-related adverse events. The other abstracts emphasize benefits and do not catalogue harms. Unregulated supplement products can differ from the material used in a trial; these papers do not test marketplace purity.

What it is: Collagen peptides here are oral collagen fragments studied as supplements, including a nutricosmetic ingredient, not as a named approved drug.
Humans given it: Human data in these sources include 22 women in a four-week bone-marker pilot and 53 older men in a 12-week training trial, plus placebo-controlled trials in adults with skin aging and in adults with damaged hair without alopecia.
What it does not show: They do not show fracture prevention, regrowth in alopecia, a peptide-only muscle effect, or that a biomarker change is a clinical outcome.

### Worth Knowing
A reported link between GLP-1 receptor agonist use and lower colorectal cancer risk in inflammatory bowel disease is an association, so it does not establish that the drugs prevent cancer.
Human collagen-peptide trials in this set measured skin hydration and dermal structure, hair gloss and elasticity, bone-turnover markers, and training-related body composition, which is a different evidence rung from a product claim about disease treatment.
Policy reporting on temporary sale and advisory-panel backing describes access and process, not a human efficacy result for those peptides.

### Evidence Ledger
- In two placebo-controlled trials, oral collagen peptides increased skin hydration after 8 weeks and improved dermal collagen density and fragmentation from 4 weeks through 12 weeks; ex vivo human skin explants showed induced collagen and glycosaminoglycan production. Evidence level: randomized human trials plus ex vivo tissue. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26362110
- In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of Korean adults with damaged hair and without alopecia, 24 weeks of low-molecular-weight collagen peptide GT improved gloss, elasticity, and scalp elasticity versus placebo, with no treatment-related adverse events. Evidence level: randomized human trial. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42624827
- In a four-week pilot randomized trial of 22 premenopausal endurance runners, collagen peptides raised P1NP versus placebo (p = 0.04) and showed an IL-6 interaction (p = 0.03), without a CTX-1 interaction, and without structural bone outcomes. Evidence level: pilot randomized human trial. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42221768
- In 53 older men with sarcopenia, 12 weeks of resistance training plus collagen peptides increased fat-free mass and quadriceps strength and reduced fat mass more than training plus placebo. Evidence level: randomized human trial, combination with training. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26353786

A peptide claim is only as strong as the rung it was measured on.
