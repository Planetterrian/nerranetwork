Hims and Hers, JournalArta and Peradaban reported, put a specialty called Peptides Plus on sale in the United States on November eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. The offering is a commercial launch, not a trial result. The five compounded products are sermorelin, glutathione, NAD plus, vitamin B twelve and L-carnitine, prepared by licensed United States pharmacies that state pharmacy boards inspect.

A waitlist had opened in late October. Chief medical officer Anant Vinjamoori said the company does not have the data clinicians are used to having with compounds like this, and that people with serious cardiovascular, liver or kidney disease will not be candidates for now.

Joshua Kritzer, a chemist at Tufts, said that without large trials it is hard to rule out rare serious complications, and that anecdotal information is not enough reason to put a substance in your body. Paul Knoepfler at UC Davis called dosing a separate uncertainty.

The company also plans to add BPC one fifty-seven and TB five hundred, two peptides marketed for healing, though most of the research on them is in animals rather than people.

The American Medical Association has opposed a Health and Human Services proposal to expand access to unapproved peptides, Florida Hospital News reported. That objection comes after federal officials, earlier this month, moved to widen compounding access to those same kinds of products. The Washington Times added that health professionals are sounding the alarm on the policy.

This is a professional and political response, not a new study of whether any peptide works.

GLP-1 drugs turn up in a PsyPost report on a real-world study that links them with less alcohol use and with eating the outlet calls healthier. That is an observation in people. It is not a randomized trial, and a link is not proof the drugs caused either change.

Weight-loss GLP-1 prescriptions paid by Medicaid fell after four states withdrew coverage, 2 Minute Medicine reported, which is a before-and-after pattern in who gets a prescription. HealthDay cast the same drop as Medicaid cuts causing prescriptions to plummet, a harder causal claim than a coverage withdrawal by itself can carry.

Australia has approved orforglipron, Streamline Feed reported, and the approval is for the country's first non-peptide oral GLP-1 pill. Same hormone pathway, different kind of molecule. This one is not a peptide.

Teriparatide, this week's spotlight, is recombinant human parathyroid hormone, amino acids one through thirty-four. A two thousand five review in Endocrine Reviews put it in an anabolic class for severe osteoporosis, a drug with a chance to change bone microarchitecture, set against medicines that mainly slow breakdown and cut remodeling. 

The clinical reviews talk about daily treatment. A two thousand sixteen paper in Osteoporosis International reported that teriparatide appears to raise bone formation more than bone resorption, on blood markers and on tissue slides. Two things happen together. Formation is stimulated inside sites that are already remodeling, and on surfaces that had been quiet. New remodeling sites also start up.

Both feed the density gain seen on DXA scans. Remodeling is the repair cycle that keeps bone young, and under this drug each of those cavities is described as finishing in positive bone balance, which the authors treated as one reason fractures might fall. The same review said fracture risk does fall, and that the effect looks larger as therapy goes on longer.

It judged a full continuous twenty-four-month course better for skeletal outcomes than shorter stretches, and said novel shorter regimens should wait for controlled trials against that twenty-four-month daily course. Clinical use, that paper stated, is limited to twenty-four months.

In two thousand sixteen the authors called it the only agent then available that builds new bone and can partly repair architectural defects. That exclusivity belongs to the date of the paper.

The strongest fracture evidence in this set is a phase III trial, summarized in that two thousand five Endocrine Reviews paper. Elderly women who already had at least one vertebral fracture had significant reductions in spine fractures and in fractures of the limbs.

The review said there was not yet evidence those reductions beat bisphosphonates, and cost-utility estimates found teriparatide significantly more expensive. A separate two thousand five health-technology assessment pulled in ninety randomized trials covering alendronate, etidronate, risedronate, raloxifene and teriparatide, among other comparators.

All five of those drugs had cut vertebral fracture risk in women with severe osteoporosis and adequate calcium intake. Head-to-head comparisons had not shown any one significantly more effective than the others. In the cost model, the price of a quality-adjusted life-year fell sharply with age.

At age fifty, assuming severe osteoporosis right at the diagnostic threshold, nothing in the model came in under thirty-five thousand pounds per quality-adjusted life-year.

At age sixty, raloxifene was estimated at twenty-six thousand pounds if hip fractures were unaffected and thirty-one thousand if the hip effect was harmful, and the authors said those figures were driven by breast-cancer assumptions. No other option in that model was under thirty-five thousand pounds at age sixty.

A two thousand twenty-six paper in Regenerative Therapy is a different kind of result. Researchers built mouse bone-marrow exosomes carrying teriparatide messenger RNA and coated them with an Asp six peptide meant to stick to hydroxyapatite.

In mouse stromal cells, that package raised teriparatide message and secreted protein, switched on a CRE-linked reporter, and increased osteogenic genes, alkaline phosphatase staining and mineral in the matrix. It bound bone samples more tightly than plain exosomes.

After an intravenous injection, fluorescence on bone was still detectable at seventy-two hours, stronger in the femur and tibia and relatively weaker in the liver than with unmodified exosomes. Cell work and mouse biodistribution. Not a human fracture trial.

The two thousand five review said teriparatide is not recommended beyond two years, in part because a rat carcinogenicity model produced osteosarcoma. It discussed the drug for postmenopausal women and men with severe osteoporosis, for established steroid-related osteoporosis when steroids have to continue, and for some people under sixty-five with a T score of minus three point five or lower.

That is what the review considered. It is not a reason to start or stop anything. The same review treated mild high blood calcium as something that can show up and that clinicians then managed. A two thousand twenty-six case report in Bone Reports, one patient, described an impending atypical femoral fracture on the opposite side after a single denosumab dose.

The other femur had looked normal on imaging when the first fracture happened. The patient had long bisphosphonate exposure and had not finished teriparatide. The authors called atypical femoral fracture a rare but serious complication of prolonged antiresorptive therapy, and they called the risk of teriparatide followed by an antiresorptive drug poorly characterized.

Across these papers, teriparatide is an established bone-building medicine with a twenty-four-month clinical limit and phase III fracture data in older women. They do not show it prevents more fractures than bisphosphonates, and the exosome experiments do not show a result in people.

What is worth knowing is that a real-world link between GLP-1 drugs and less drinking, or different eating, is a pattern in people, not proof the medicine caused it. A drop in Medicaid prescribing after coverage disappears shows how payment rules change who receives these drugs, not whether they work.

And a fracture result from a randomized trial in women who already had a spine fracture sits in a different evidence class from a compounded product sold without approval.
