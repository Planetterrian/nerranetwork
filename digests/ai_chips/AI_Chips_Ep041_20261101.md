# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Seven point three billion dollars of quarterly data-center revenue still leaves Intel short of Xeon demand.**

**What You Need to Know:** Intel's data-center and AI business booked $7.3 billion in the third quarter of 2026, up 17% from the prior quarter and nearly 80% from a year earlier, and the company is still short of Xeon supply. It is converting some Oregon Intel 18A wafer capacity to Intel 3 for Xeon 6 Granite Rapids, with that added Intel 3 output expected in the second half of 2027. Samsung's chairman was in the Yokohama packaging lab the same weekend, and the locked Vera Rubin sheet is what production hardware is shipping against.

### Top Story
Intel is reallocating leading-edge wafer tools from client chips to server CPUs because data-center demand is outrunning what the lines can ship. On the third-quarter 2026 earnings call, CEO Lip-Bu Tan said the industry is navigating one of the most significant supply shortages in history, driven by demand for what he called Super Intelligence, and that server supply grew in the quarter while later quarters will still struggle to keep pace. The Data Center and AI business generated $7.3 billion, up 17% sequentially and nearly 80% year over year. CFO Dave Zinsner said wafers being taken offline in Oregon are Intel 18A wafers that generally go to Panther Lake; weaker PC demand, which Intel expects to last at least through the first half of next year, frees some of that capacity for data-center wafers. Those tools will run Intel 3, the node behind Xeon 6 Granite Rapids. Tan said the added Intel 3 capacity should be in production in the second half of 2027. Intel 3 manufacturing costs have fallen about 15% since the first quarter of 2026. Granite Rapids is already the company's fastest-ramping server family, and management says demand still exceeds supply. Intel expects data-center CPU demand to stay strong in 2027 and 2028 as operators deploy more CPU-heavy agentic and inference work. Fab 24 near Leixlip, Ireland, is being expanded for more Xeon output, with that capacity online by the end of 2027 unless the move is accelerated. In Arizona, Intel 18A is ramping at Fab 52, and equipment installation into adjacent Fab 62 has been pulled forward; Fab 62 is set to be the second high-volume site for 18A, 18A-P, and derivatives. Intel also bought tools aggressively in the third quarter and said it expects to take some client share with Core Ultra 400-series Nova Lake, which will need high-volume 18A and 18A-P wafers. ### Silicon
**Samsung chairman highlights chip packaging, visits Japanese unit: The Korea Times**
Samsung Electronics executive chairman Lee Jae-yong visited the Advanced Package Lab in Yokohama on Sunday and said the company will keep expanding the research workforce there and turn the lab into a global R&D hub for next-generation packaging. He discussed materials and process work with researchers and ways to tighten cooperation with Japan's materials, parts, and equipment industry. Samsung says advanced packaging is now critical for AI processor chips because it integrates several dies, including GPUs and high-bandwidth memory, in one package. APL was established in 2023 and held its official opening in September this year. Starting in 2024, Samsung has been investing 40 billion yen, about $254 million, over five years in the Japan research infrastructure. The lab develops and validates packaging materials and processes and is meant to sit between Samsung's design and manufacturing and Japanese suppliers. Its research areas differ from the packaging unit at the Semiconductor R&D Center in Korea; the two groups plan to work together. APL's next scope includes wafer bonding, glass substrates, and new high-performance packaging architectures aimed at energy-efficient parts in volume. **NVIDIA Updates Rubin GPU Specs as First Production Hardware Arrives: ServeTheHome**
With the first Vera Rubin NVL72 systems being made generally available to hyperscaler customers, NVIDIA has locked shipping specifications below the preliminary bandwidth targets and above the preliminary scale-out figures. A single Rubin GPU is now listed at 19.2 TB/s of HBM4, not the 22 TB/s target, which ServeTheHome calculates as about 9.375 Gbps per pin across eight stacks versus about 10.75 Gbps per pin before, a drop of about 1.375 Gbps per pin, or roughly 11%. The official HBM4 rate cited in that account is 8 Gbps per pin on a bus widened from 1,024 bits to 2,048 bits per stack. NVLink scale-up per GPU is 3.0 TB/s, down from 3.6 TB/s. Scale-out networking per GPU is 450 GB/s, up from 400 GB/s. Memory capacity is unchanged at 288 GB per GPU. Paper compute is unchanged at 50 PFLOPS of FP4 inference and 35 PFLOPS of FP4 training. On the NVL72 rack, aggregate GPU memory bandwidth is 1,400 TB/s, down from 1,580 TB/s, and scale-up bandwidth is 216 TB/s, down from 260 TB/s, while scale-out networking is 32.4 TB/s bidirectional, up from 28.8 TB/s. Rack HBM4 capacity stays at 20.7 TB. AMD's MI455X, in the same account, runs HBM4 at 7.6 Gbps per pin, so NVIDIA's revised rate is still above that rival's. TechRadar, comparing pages and a Wayback window, places the quiet edit between September 1 and September 11, after August results that already had Vera Rubin racks running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure. ### Data Centres & Power
**Measuring the environmental impact of delivering AI at Google Scale: arXiv**
Google's published full-stack measurement of Gemini Apps puts the median text prompt at 0.24 watt-hours, 0.03 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent, and 0.26 milliliters of water. The method counts active AI accelerator power, host system energy, idle machine capacity, and data-center energy overhead, not accelerator draw alone. The same paper says software efficiency work and clean-energy procurement cut energy for that median prompt 33 times and the carbon footprint 44 times over one year. Google's infrastructure blog equates the 0.24 watt-hours to watching television for less than nine seconds. Source: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734)

### The Teardown: A Word That Is Two Bytes
A PDP-11 memory transaction is a word, not a byte, and the lowest address bit is a lane select rather than part of the location the memory board is allowed to treat as a full address. Dave Plummer, building a replacement board because a megabyte of period memory for his machine was priced in too many zeros, ran into that rule the hard way. Tom's Hardware reports the project: a Q-bus card, a Lattice MachXO2 FPGA as the memory controller, four ISSI 1 MB chips, a U2S I/O chip, DIP switches, comm ports, and capacitors, tested on a backplane beside an M8190 CPU board.

The bus is 22 bits wide. The M8190 design reserves the top 256 KB for I/O and ROM, so not every address the 22 bits can name is RAM the CPU may use as main memory. Later PDP-11 models could take as much as four megabytes. Four 1 MB chips are four megabytes of physical storage on the card, which matches that ceiling on paper and then has to obey the word rule in the controller.

On a read, the CPU may want one byte, but the controller still has to return a whole word, two bytes. The low bit tells the CPU which lane, low byte or high byte, it will keep. On a write, the same bit has to be decoded before the store. The bus still carries a full word. If the controller writes both bytes, the lane the CPU did not mean to touch gets whatever else was on the bus. Plummer's first controller returned every second word. A commenter on the account joked that he had built 4 MB of memory that turned out to be 2 MB. That is the failure mode in one sentence: the array was sized for four megabytes, and the address decode treated the lane bit as a word address, so half the locations never participated in a legal transaction.

The fix sits in the FPGA, not in a respin of the ISSI chips. Reads always present both bytes. Writes mask to the selected byte and leave the other byte in the word alone. Plummer had not flash-programmed an FPGA before this board; that was a separate bring-up loop, and it is not the same bug. The lane bug is a protocol mismatch between a modern byte-oriented mental model and a 1970 machine whose memory bus never offered a byte strobe the way a later DRAM controller would.

He had already sidestepped the whole class of problem a week earlier with a QBone DEC interface that carries a Beaglebone Black and implements the memory in software on the far side of the bus. That works, and it is a different product: a single-board computer pretending to be Q-bus RAM, not four discrete megabyte chips behind a small FPGA. The discrete board is the right shape when the goal is a period-correct card that the M8190 can cycle as if the chips were of the era. It stops being the right shape when the controller cannot guarantee a byte lane mask on every write. At that point the software path on the QBone is the one that cannot corrupt the untouched half of the word, because the lane select is handled in code before any store, and the four ISSI chips are no longer in the path at all.
