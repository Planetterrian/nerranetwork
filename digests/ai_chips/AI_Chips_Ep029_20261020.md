> **One Vera CPU replaces the dual-CPU BlueField-4, giving context-memory servers a cheaper board.**

**What You Need to Know:** One Vera CPU now anchors the BlueField-4 STX context-memory server NVIDIA showed at OCP 2026, a lower-cost board than the dual-Vera design. Intel has asked the FCC for a waiver so foreign automated guided vehicles in its US fabs can keep receiving updates after a July import ban. ### Top Story
NVIDIA showed the BlueField-4 STX server for context memory at OCP 2026, and ServeTheHome describes it as the lower-cost board partners had been asking for. The earlier BlueField-4 paired two Vera CPUs, each with SOCAMM memory, and four ConnectX-9 SuperNICs, closer to a dual-socket server than to a BlueField-1 or BlueField-3 DPU. The STX card is much smaller, uses high-density connectors for PCIe and system links, and carries a Vera-class package rather than an embedded-class DPU CPU. Memory is SOCAMM, so a heatsink or a liquid-cooling block can sit on the package, and an M.2 slot is onboard for what is usually an operating-system drive. A soldered CPU and LPDDR5X, instead of DDR5 DIMM slots, keep the board about the size of an Intel DMR 16-channel socket or a 16-channel AMD EPYC Venice platform, and the layout is meant to be assembled automatically. ServeTheHome did not see a ConnectX-9 SuperNIC on the OCP boards, and notes that NAND prices have been rising meaningfully over the past year. **Is front-of-the-meter power the next data centre play?: Data Center Knowledge**
Front-of-the-meter power, as Data Center Knowledge defines it, is generation a data-centre operator develops or backs so electricity feeds the grid rather than a single campus. Amazon has been linked to a possible arrangement of that kind at a large AI data centre it is building in Texas, and public statements still have the company exploring rather than committed. The piece distinguishes that from behind-the-meter generation, which serves one facility, and from grid-interactive designs whose on-site plant still exists first to support the data centre. Building or backing a grid-serving plant is capital-intensive, and most operators are still on utility partnerships, long-term procurement, storage, and behind-the-meter generation. ### Supply Chain & Policy
**Intel seeks an FCC waiver to keep foreign fab robots running: BigGo Finance**
Intel filed a waiver request with the FCC on October 16, made public Monday, after Bloomberg's account of the petition. In July the FCC added advanced robotic equipment made outside the United States to its covered list, alongside Wi-Fi routers and drones, barring those products from the US market; gear already in use can stay, but companies can no longer update its hardware, software, or firmware. Intel said its fabs rely on automated guided vehicles long supplied by Japan's Murata Machinery and Daifuku, that no validated US-made alternative meets its standards, and that fully replacing the fleet would take years. The company called continued operation critical to US national security and said a disruption would immediately and negatively affect its US manufacturing capacity, seeking a conditional waiver because it is unclear whether upgrades would violate the rules. TSMC, Micron, GlobalFoundries, Samsung Electronics, and Texas Instruments may need similar waivers; there is no information that they have filed. The covered list has shifted to category-wide controls; Netgear obtained a license in April after the Defense Department found no national-security threat, and AT&T, Verizon, and a broadband association were allowed hardware upgrades on consumer Wi-Fi routers. Source: [finance.biggo.com](https://finance.biggo.com/news/d41f6671-7aad-4986-9cd0-efc912166d3c)

### The Teardown: When the Checkpoint Outgrows the Sky
Thirteen point four trillion particles stay in the calculation only if every tracer's state can sit in memory while the bytes that merely keep the run alive go somewhere else. HPCwire reports that an Argonne team used the Aurora exascale machine at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility to finish two of the largest cosmological simulations yet run. Each one followed 13.4 trillion dark-matter particles across a region 28.6 billion light-years wide. The code is HACC, the Hardware/Hybrid Accelerated Cosmology Code. Gravity is the only force in these runs. Particles stand in as tracers of dark-matter density and velocity, so the hot state is position and velocity for each tracer, not gas, stars, or radiation.

Aurora is built for that residency. The system has 10,624 nodes and 63,744 GPUs. Each of these runs drew on 8,100 nodes and roughly 50,000 GPUs. The simulations and much of the analysis finished in about four days. High-capacity memory is the constraint the team names: trillions of particles had to stay readily available as the calculation moved. A gravity-only state is what made that volume fit.

The other limit is the restart. A multi-day run on most of the machine cannot assume it finishes in one sitting. Aurora's Distributed Asynchronous Object Storage, DAOS, took the checkpoint stream. The two simulations wrote more than 85 petabytes through DAOS, mostly as restart checkpoints, so an interruption resumes instead of starting over. About 8 petabytes were kept for scientific analysis.

Walk those two write figures. More than 85 petabytes went through DAOS. About 8 petabytes stayed for science. The checkpoint stream is more than ten times the analysis archive. Most of the bytes the runs produced were not the sky. They were the right to continue the sky. Eighty-five against eight is the worked ratio, and it sits on top of the machine split already named: 8,100 nodes out of 10,624, and roughly 50,000 GPUs out of 63,744, for about four days, twice, once for each 13.4-trillion-particle volume.

That cut stops being the right design when the question needs the gas and the stars in the same volume. The same HACC team had already used Frontier for a different cosmology run, a hydrodynamic simulation that added gas, stars, and other galaxy-formation processes, and that effort produced the largest hydrodynamic simulation of the universe to date. Adding those fields multiplies the state per particle. HPCwire is explicit that focusing on gravity alone is what let Aurora hold a larger volume and more particles. When galaxy formation has to live inside the same box as the largest-scale density field, the resident set and the checkpoint both grow, and the gravity-only cut is no longer the run the survey analysis requires.
