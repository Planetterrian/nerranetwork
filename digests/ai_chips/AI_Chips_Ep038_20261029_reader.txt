A physics engine has to take it apart before the next tool is allowed to run, and Semiconductor Engineering carries Siemens' account of that rule for agentic design software.

An agent can check its own work. A physics-validated flow is stricter: every decision is confirmed on a deterministic engine before the next step starts, and the engine, not the model, decides what is correct.

Tapeout stays binary. Silicon works or it does not.

If the run only reasons about physics and never checks the engines, risk stacks at every step, and the longer that goes on, the further the design can drift.

Hours across many tools and physical domains, with nobody in the loop, is where an early miss becomes the next tool's assumption.

The case they use is a 3D stack. Active dies piled up make heat and temperature interactions harder to predict, and the package can add mechanical stress.

Electrical, power, thermal, and mechanical effects move together, so a change in one hits the others. Handing that from tool to tool slows the loop and limits how many options a team can try.

The example is a nine-stage electro-thermal flow. Tool servers feed Fuse, and in this write-up Claude Code is the coding agent.

It spans seven Siemens EDA tools and runs one hundred to two hundred agentic turns over more than twelve hours.

Stages go from floor planning, library characterization, and power analysis through thermal signoff, system thermal simulation, parasitic extraction, and power- and temperature-aware SPICE.

Each stage is checked on signoff-grade physics solvers. A missed target sends the agent back. A stage is not parked for a final signoff to discover.

The sweep varies heat-transfer-coefficient boundaries, power budgets, and material stack-ups across two hundred to five hundred or more design points in a cycle.

Five hundred divided by two hundred is two and a half, so the top of that range is two and a half times the bottom, in one cycle.

Siemens says teams can configure tools up to twenty times faster, cut engineering effort by up to ninety-five percent, and look at up to fifty times more design options than a manual flow.

That twenty-times setup speed is what lets a twelve-hour, one-hundred-to-two-hundred-turn run touch hundreds of points instead of a few hand-built decks.

This control fails when a stage advances because the agent is content and the solver speaks only after those twelve hours.
