HPCwire's account of the November fifth announcement puts IBM, Oak Ridge's Quantum Science Center, and AMD on one bench for that split.

IBM's Nighthawk brings one hundred twenty qubits, square-lattice links, a high circuit rate, and pulse control that can act while a circuit is still running. Oak Ridge's Umbra, with AMD Instinct MI355X G P U's and fifth-generation EPYC CPUs, is the classical side. Together they let a team score each code by qubits, time, samples, and classical cost.

On the quantum box a researcher can measure mid-calculation and branch at once, time the guard pulses, see what actually ran, and work from the raw signal. Many noisy physical qubits stand in for fewer logical ones. The decoder reads the syndrome and names the correction. Late means the name no longer matches the state.

Umbra is where that decoder gets tried on G P U nodes big enough to resemble a later machine, and where codes get ranked by the classical load they impose.
