> **Six fabs near Dallas are under discussion as TSMC's second U.S. hub.**

**What You Need to Know:** Reported plans put six advanced wafer fabs near Dallas on the table as TSMC's second U.S. chip hub after Arizona. Chosunbiz, citing Taiwan's United Daily News and Economic Daily News, says TSMC has not officially announced the complex, while some analyses put the spend above the $265 billion planned for Arizona. The capacity, if it is built, is framed as sitting close to Nvidia, AMD and Apple.

### Top Story
Chosunbiz reports that equipment makers, via Taiwan's United Daily News and Economic Daily News, expect TSMC to be reviewing the Dallas area as a second U.S. advanced-chip complex after Arizona, aimed at AI-chip demand and at customers including Nvidia, AMD and Apple. Six advanced wafer plants are being discussed, and TSMC has not officially announced the plans. Some analyses say the investment could surpass the $265 billion, about 360 trillion won, planned for the Arizona base, and some expect a finer process than the one applied in Arizona. Phoenix is already being built out with wafer fabs plus advanced packaging and R&D so AI and high-performance computing chips can be produced locally. If Texas went ahead, TSMC would have two large southern U.S. hubs; there is also word of shaping that pair into a "southern semiconductor dual-core corridor," in a region already called Silicon Prairie, where Texas Instruments and Samsung are investing in large semiconductor plants. The packaging-plant additions reported yesterday were in Taiwan; this item is a reported U.S. wafer complex, not an announced fab. Source: [biz.chosun.com](https://biz.chosun.com/en/en-it/2026/09/29/VLJD5FS2PFAS7GW2CIYKXYJLBA/)

### Data Centres & Power
**The 200 GW Moment: Reinventing the Grid for the AI Economy:** Data Center Knowledge
Data Center Knowledge reports global data-centre power demand projected to exceed 200 gigawatts by 2030, with individual campuses scaling into the hundreds of megawatts and beyond. At Data Center World Power in Dallas, held September 21–23, Scott Hart, executive vice president of NRG Business, said data centres were traditionally grid-connected 20- to 40-megawatt facilities, cloud campuses then reached about 100 megawatts, and conversations have now pivoted to a gigawatt and beyond. Hart said a project of that scale makes it hard for a utility to commit to building a power plant, because a technology clock runs in months or a few years while power infrastructure runs in decades, and utilities plan and finance assets over 15–30 years. ### The Teardown: When the Rack's Coolant Has to Change
Heat leaves the rack, or the accelerators throttle. Data Center Knowledge, citing Bill Kleyman, reports AI workloads driving typical rack densities from roughly 10–30 kilowatts into 80–150 kilowatts, with advanced deployments approaching 1 megawatt per rack.

Air cooling is a volume problem. Fans push room air across a heatsink; the air carries heat to a coil elsewhere in the building. The ceiling is flow. More flow means more pressure drop through the cabinet, more risk that hot-aisle air comes back to the inlet, and a point where the electrical feed and the airflow fail together. Another card in that rack then adds temperature, not useful work.

Liquid cooling changes the carrier, not the need to reject the heat. A pump moves water or a dielectric across a cold plate, or the board sits in a bath, and a coolant distribution unit hands the load to the building loop. A small liquid flow moves heat that would have required a large air flow. Kleyman tied that shift to AFCOM's State of the Data Center 2026 survey: 69 percent of respondents expect rack density to increase like nothing seen in the past, and liquid-cooling adoption is showing 36 percent growth.

The parts in the rack show why the old air budget breaks. Kleyman said a standard rack can hold six Nvidia DGX-class systems, each built around eight H100 accelerators, with individual accelerator cards costing up to $40,000. That population does not fit a 10–30 kilowatt cabinet as a full set. It starts to fit between 80 and 150 kilowatts. Near 1 megawatt per rack, the design is busbar plus coolant loop, not a deeper air plenum.

The tradeoff flips inside the old 10–30 kilowatt band. Pumps and plates then add leak paths without buying accelerators the air system could already feed. Once a deployment approaches 1 megawatt per rack, staying on air means leaving some of those $40,000 cards out, or asking the hall for airflow it cannot move.
