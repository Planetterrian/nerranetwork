In vacuum, waste heat has nowhere to convect. It has to be conducted or pumped to a surface that radiates it away. EE Times, on Google's Project Suncatcher, counts that radiator, the solar arrays and the years of survival as part of what the processor is worth, because a dead board up there is not swapped at the next window.

On October first, twenty twenty-six, a Suncatcher prototype reached orbit on a SpaceX mission, built with Planet, and made contact with the ground.

Before launch, Google ran Trillium TPUs through vibration and proton-beam tests and reported encouraging tolerance to accumulated ionizing radiation, while still chasing errors that show up in AI workloads.

It also tested heat pipes and radiators in a simulated vacuum. The lab result is only a first look. The flight has to show how often faults hit, and what they do to output, over years.

An April twenty twenty-six study by Slava Turyshev, as EE Times recounts it, put a representative one-megawatt orbital plant at about five thousand six hundred forty square meters of photovoltaic array and two thousand five hundred square meters of radiator.

Those are model outputs, tied to assumptions on sun and heat rejection. One megawatt is still small next to a terrestrial hall, and every square meter is mass you have to launch, along with power distribution, cover for dark periods, and links between satellites and back to Earth.

A faster chip that draws more power can force larger arrays, heavier distribution and more radiating area, and that mass can wipe out the throughput gain.

Two architectures on the same workload make the point. One finishes more operations per second and needs a bigger thermal system. The other is slower, draws less, and fits a smaller craft that may last longer.

The count that matters is total computation over the mission, after interruptions, degraded running and an early failure. A benchmark winner can still deliver fewer productive hours, and the operator who cannot swap the board pays for those lost hours directly.
