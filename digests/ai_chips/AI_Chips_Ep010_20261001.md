> **Sixty-nine kilovolts is now the cutoff for a foreign-equipment ban on the US grid.**

**What You Need to Know:** Sixty-nine kilovolts is now the cutoff for a foreign-equipment ban on the US grid, under an August 26 emergency order that can reach transformers, inverters and control systems. CoreWeave has put NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72 into limited cloud availability, with Cognition reporting up to 4.8 times the token throughput per GPU of a GB200 NVL72 on its own test. A Taiwanese press report adds a six-fab Dallas outline to talk of a Texas TSMC campus, still without a board approval.

### Top Story
On August 26, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order declaring a national emergency to ban or restrict acquisition and installation of high-risk foreign-produced equipment in the US electric grid, citing hidden cybersecurity vulnerabilities, malware and remote-access risk. It covers equipment, hardware, software and firmware used in generation, transmission or control facilities operating at 69 kilovolts or above, including large transformers, grid-tied inverters, circuit breakers, battery energy storage systems, SCADA software and industrial control systems. Equipment used only in local low-voltage distribution falls outside it, including standard residential solar inverters and commercial distribution below 69 kilovolts. A covered foreign entity is any company owned by, controlled by, or subject to the jurisdiction of a foreign adversary, including arms-embargo countries such as Russia, Iran and North Korea, with China described as the real power-sector concern, and a US or European manufacturer can still be caught if critical chips, communications modules or internal software come from a covered jurisdiction. Transactions after August 26, 2026 can be restricted, the Secretary of Energy can require earlier installs to be monitored, disconnected, replaced or removed, and US utilities are already pausing procurement tied to foreign-linked supply chains. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/us-executive-order-on-energy-grid-supply-chain-security/)

### Silicon
**CoreWeave Puts Vera Rubin NVL72 Into Limited Availability: Techgenyz**
After racks were already reported shipping, CoreWeave said on September 30 that NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72 is in limited availability on CoreWeave Cloud, with Cognition the first customer running production workloads. On SWE-2 inference drawn from a sampled subset of FrontierCode tasks, Cognition measured up to 4.8 times higher total token throughput per GPU than a GB200 NVL72 baseline at matched interactivity. Cognition’s engineers took those figures on CoreWeave infrastructure, and the report says independent third-party verification has not yet appeared. In CoreWeave testing, the Vera CPU produced more than 3 times faster agent-sandbox startup than an unnamed alternative x86 CPU. The same account states that higher token throughput is not a higher task-success rate. Source: [techgenyz.com](https://techgenyz.com/cognition-running-devin-workloads-nvidia-vera-rubin/)

### Data Centres & Power
**Can Data Center Design Choices Change Public Acceptance?: Data Center Knowledge**
Operators are using biomimicry, community co-design and brownfield redevelopment to cut the visual impact of data centers and ease local objections that already include noise, energy and water. Microsoft first used biomimicry at a Noord-Holland site in 2022, planting 150 native trees and more than 2,000 square meters, about 21,500 square feet, of shrubs and grasses. The company says it has since expanded the practice to more than 20 sites across the US and Europe. Director of global land strategy Kaitlin Chuzi told Data Center Knowledge that the work starts from how the local ecosystem functions, combining ecological research with community input to shape land, water and restoration plans. The Virginia-based Piedmont Environmental Council, cited in the same report, describes proposed county hyperscale sites as massive, windowless concrete buildings that can bring noise, substations and transmission lines, and that sit out of scale with existing communities. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/sustainability/can-data-center-design-choices-change-public-acceptance-)

### Supply Chain & Policy
**UDN Adds a Six-Fab Dallas Outline to the Texas Talk: TechPowerUp**
Taiwan’s Economic Daily News, citing semiconductor equipment industry sources, reports that TSMC is planning a second US manufacturing campus in Texas, separate from Arizona, with six advanced fabs and Dallas the most likely location. UDN estimates the investment could exceed the $265 billion committed to Arizona, on the view that the new fabs would probably use more advanced nodes. Reuters, as cited in that report, says TSMC is evaluating a potential Texas investment, plans are not finalized, and one source said it would come on top of the Arizona commitment. TSMC had not commented when the report went out, has not asked suppliers to prepare, and the board has not signed off. UDN says the Arizona site still faces power and labor shortages while it sorts out its water supply, after TSMC added another $100 billion to those Arizona plans in July, and points to a renewed tariff threat of up to 200 percent for chipmakers that do not produce in the US. Source: [techpowerup.com](https://www.techpowerup.com/353281/tsmc-reportedly-evaluating-texas-expansion-besides-usd-265-billion-arizona-plan)

### The Teardown: Why a Limb Log Wears Out Flash
A joint that has to keep the last safe pose does not lose that pose in the neural network. It loses it in the log. Take the write path apart at the limb microcontroller, not at the vision computer. Semiconductor Engineering describes a zonal machine: a central compute core runs vision, model execution and motion planning, and a time-sensitive network pushes commands to microcontrollers in the limbs, the joint actuators and the battery system. Those edge controllers are picked for deterministic low latency and for power-loss persistence, so a brownout does not bring a joint back in an unknown state. The same piece says logging motor torque, velocity and limb position at sampling rates above 10 kilohertz can exceed the endurance of conventional non-volatile memory. The wear mechanism is block erase. Embedded flash does not rewrite a single sample in place. It erases a whole block, and a shift-long sample stream never pauses long enough for that block to be written once and left alone. Small log lines therefore become repeated erases. That is write amplification, and it spends the cell’s life on bookkeeping rather than on the sensor count. Memory sized for the vision workload does not stand in. The article separates high-bandwidth memory, used for large networks on the central processor, from latency-optimized memory on the distributed controllers. A wide inference path does not hold the last torque command through a power cut beside the motor driver. Goldman Sachs figures cited there put annual humanoid shipments at fewer than 15,000 units in 2025 and possibly more than 6 million by 2035. Endurance that looks acceptable on a demo stand is a fleet problem at the second figure. The tradeoff flips when logging stops being a continuous stream and becomes a checkpoint on a fault or a mode change. Block erases then fall back inside what ordinary flash is built to survive, and the high-endurance part on the limb is no longer what limits the design.
