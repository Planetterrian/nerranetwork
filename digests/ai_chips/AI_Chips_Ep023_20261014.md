# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Twenty times more AI compute by 2030 still stalls if memory cannot feed it.**

**What You Need to Know:** SK hynix told SEDEX 2026 that AI-infrastructure compute demand could reach about 20 times this year’s level by 2030, and that HBM alone will not hold the inference working set. The company is pushing High Bandwidth Flash and 3D-stacked DRAM as new tiers between today’s HBM and SSD. Specification work on HBF is aimed at January 2027.

### Top Story
SK hynix president Ahn Hyun, speaking at SEDEX 2026 in Seoul, said computing performance required for AI infrastructure could reach about 20 times this year’s level by 2030, and that raising the speed of a single memory device will not close the gap. Asiae reported his account of context length moving from about 128K tokens in 2024 to around 1 million, with a further path toward 100 million, so data spills from HBM and DRAM onto SSDs. High Bandwidth Flash stacks NAND with the through-silicon-via approach used for HBM; SK hynix is aiming for about half the bandwidth of same-generation HBM and more than 10 times the capacity, and it plans to finalize the specification next January after a July draft, with SanDisk and cloud providers including Google in the standards work. On 3D-stacked DRAM, which places DRAM on a GPU or NPU and ties it with TSVs, Money Today reported about 10 times the bandwidth of HBM and about half the power, while Asiae reported dozens of times more input/output pathways and a large cut in power. Ahn also described custom HBM with customer functions on the base die, CXL pooled memory, and storage whose input/output performance would rise four to eight times. At the booth, SK hynix showed an NVIDIA Vera Rubin superchip and an HBM wafer signed by Jensen Huang, plus a 64GB RDIMM on a 10-nanometer-class sixth-generation (1c) process, a 256GB 3DS RDIMM, and a 128GB MRDIMM. ### Silicon
**Quadric’s GPNPU puts scalar, vector, and matrix on one ISA:** Semiconductor Engineering
Semiconductor Engineering carried Quadric’s account of a General-Purpose Neural Processor delivered as configurable IP: one instruction stream, one ISA, and a C++ and Python programming model covering scalar, vector, and matrix execution. The Chimera core pairs a scalar unit with a 2D SIMD processing-element array; each element has a 32-bit ALU, a configurable MAC bank, local register-file SRAM, and a neighbor-communication path. On-chip memory, both that local SRAM and L2, is software-managed rather than hardware-cached. Semiconductor Engineering posted a Cadence methodology for early, iterative optimization of chiplet packages as CoWoS-R, CoWoS-L, high-bandwidth memory, and high-speed interfaces multiply signals. ### The Teardown: Why the Frame Stays in the Building
A city operations agent starts with a camera frame, a meter, and a file that already live in the building. NVIDIA’s blog on European city deployments describes the split. Cosmos open models and the Metropolis blueprint for video search and summarization look at the stream and name a condition: congestion, equipment state, an unused space, a safety hazard. Nemotron open models then reason over that result and answer in ordinary language. Omniverse libraries hold the twin, so a planner can try a change before the street does. The accelerators sit in what NVIDIA calls a locally operated AI factory, packaged by system builders including Cisco, Dell Technologies, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro. The design choice is not a faster chatbot. It is that the frame, the model, and the operations log do not have to leave the city’s control to be useful.

Take the Department of Côte-d’Or as the worked case, as NVIDIA’s blog tells it. Akila and ThinkDeep built operations agents that combine sensor data, infrastructure data, and historical data in a sovereign digital twin using Nemotron, Omniverse, and accelerated computing, with Cosmos adoption planned for the video. The estate is more than 500 public buildings. The agents cover energy, compliance, safety, and climate resilience. Document search time went from weeks to minutes. Total operating costs fell by 10 percent, with a target of 30 percent at scale. That is the local-factory arithmetic in one department: the same buildings, a shorter search, a stated cost cut, and a larger cut that is still a target rather than a result.

A second worked step is the clock on a citizen request, not a camera. In Italy’s Milan region, Galene.AI, Dell Technologies, and TD Synnex are deploying a locally operated AI factory, starting with Cinisello Balsamo and planning expansion to four additional municipalities. NVIDIA’s blog says the applications are meant to put municipal data in front of people in seconds versus hours, with local governance of the data kept in place. The four-municipality plan is still a plan. The seconds-versus-hours claim is the latency the local factory is supposed to buy: the record stays in the region, and the answer does not wait on a distant queue.

The condition that breaks the choice shows up when the camera has never seen the event the agent is asked to warn on. At the Port of Antwerp-Bruges, Milestone and Dell Technologies are testing agentic vision with Cosmos and the same video-search blueprint for oil-spill warnings, training on synthetic data because real incident footage is scarce. A local factory can score a frame it holds. It cannot invent a spill physics the harbor has not recorded and then treat that score as an operations order. The same break appears when the job is no longer “what is wrong in this building” but a training run or a cross-city simulation larger than the local accelerators. Ten percent off operating cost does not, by itself, pay for that larger machine, and the 30 percent figure is a scale target, not a measured result across the 500 buildings. When the question needs either a hazard the synthetic set has never stood in for, or more compute than the city’s own factory holds, the frame has to leave the building or the warning has to wait.
