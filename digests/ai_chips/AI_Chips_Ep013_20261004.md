### Silicon
**OpenAI says its own models optimised Astra Ultrafast's inference on Nvidia GPUs: MIXED Reality News**
OpenAI says its own models tuned the inference software serving GPT-6 Astra Ultrafast on Nvidia GPUs, a step past the earlier claim that the fast tier runs on Blackwell. Uday Ruddarraju, chief technology officer of compute at OpenAI, is quoted in an Nvidia post published October 1: “We used our internal models to optimize inference on NVIDIA GPUs, and NVIDIA's programmability helped us deliver the acceleration behind Astra Ultrafast.” Nvidia says Ultrafast is available now in the OpenAI API and to eligible ChatGPT Work and Codex users, and that it offers up to 8x faster token generation than Astra Standard. That 8x figure is Nvidia’s, covers token generation rather than end-to-end request time, and comes with no benchmark, prompt, model size, batch configuration or named Blackwell part; Nvidia’s artwork shows 17.0 seconds for Standard against 6.4 seconds for Ultrafast, which is not the 8x claim. Ultrafast costs $300 per million output tokens, six times the standard rate. Philippe Tillet, inference lead at OpenAI, says the same tooling has made OpenAI’s models good at programming Blackwell and Rubin GPUs and at turning that into high-performance kernels. Source: [mixed-news.com](https://mixed-news.com/en/openai-internal-models-optimised-astra-ultrafast-nvidia-blackwell/)

### Supply Chain & Policy
**This Under-the-Radar Supplier Could Be Nvidia's Secret Weapon: The Globe and Mail**
The Globe and Mail reports that high-bandwidth memory is packaged with AI chips such as Nvidia GPUs, stacked beside the GPU die, because without it the processors wait on data and power use climbs. It names three HBM makers — Samsung, Micron and SK Hynix — and says new clean-room space takes years to build. HBM takes upwards of three times the wafer capacity of conventional DRAM. Those memory makers are also competing with logic chipmakers for extreme-ultraviolet lithography tools, and ASML is the only company that makes those machines. The same piece reports SK Hynix as Nvidia’s primary HBM supplier, with a large supply agreement locking in volume for the GPU maker. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/investing/markets/stocks/ASML-Q/pressreleases/4948788/this-under-the-radar-supplier-could-be-nvidia-s-secret-weapon/)

### The Teardown: When a Wafer Chain Goes Offline
A wafer-scale part is sold as one computer the size of a wafer. Chaining is the next claim: stitch many of those wafers into one logical machine so a single job can see all of them. Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis, writing on X on October 4, pushed back on a reading that treats such a chain as delivered and fully utilized. His point was narrow and physical: chaining dozens and dozens of wafers together brings quite a few offline and utilization issues.

The mechanism is a failure domain, not a brochure diagram. Each wafer is its own yield lottery. A conventional packaged chip throws out the bad die and ships the good ones. A wafer that is the product cannot do that as cleanly. Defects, a dead link, a power fault or a cooling fault take that wafer out of the chain. Once wafers are wired as one machine, the job scheduler either shrinks the job to the wafers still answering or waits on the missing slice. Waiting is idle silicon that still occupies power, network ports and floor space. Shrinking the job means the nameplate — dozens and dozens of wafers — is not the capacity a customer can book every hour.

Utilization then splits in two. Delivery utilization asks whether every wafer in the purchase order is on the floor and powered. Online utilization asks whether those wafers are in the same chain at the same time, with links healthy enough to pass traffic. A chain multiplies the second problem. One offline wafer does not merely subtract one wafer’s compute. It can force a rebalance across the remaining chain, and a second fault in another wafer can split the machine into islands that cannot see one another. Spares help only if they are already cabled, already cooled and already in the address map. A spare in a crate is not a spare in a chain.

That is why “all delivered and fully utilized” is a stronger claim than “wafers shipped.” Shipment counts wafers that left the dock. A chained system counts wafers that are up, linked and scheduled together. Patel’s objection is that the second number is the one that pays for the power and the building, and chaining dozens and dozens of wafers makes that second number harder to hold. The tradeoff flips when the job can be cut into pieces smaller than the chain, so one offline wafer costs one wafer, not a stall of the whole machine. Until the software can do that, the chain’s headline size and its usable size are different machines.
