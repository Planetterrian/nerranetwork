# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Maia 300's memory bandwidth rises two and a half times, so inference stops waiting on compute.**

**What You Need to Know:** Maia 300's memory bandwidth rises two and a half times versus Maia 200, to 18 terabytes per second, while FP4 compute rises about 37 percent. Microsoft says production deployments begin in 2027 and that it will scale Maia to multi-gigawatt deployments as its primary inference system. The chip is announced, not shipping: a 950-watt N2P part with 288 gigabytes of HBM4, 96 of them in a 167-kilowatt rack.

### Top Story
Microsoft announced Azure Maia 300, with production deployments beginning in 2027, and said it will scale the Maia family to multi-gigawatt deployments starting in 2027 so Maia becomes the primary inference system for Microsoft products and services. StorageReview reports each accelerator pairs a TSMC N2P compute die with eight HBM4 stacks for 288 gigabytes at 18 terabytes per second inside a 950-watt TDP, and a rack holds 96 accelerators in a 167-kilowatt envelope, with 32 ports of 400-gigabit links. Maia 200 was a monolithic N3P die with six HBM3E stacks, 216 gigabytes and 7.1 terabytes per second; Maia 300 splits into a main compute die, two I/O dies and four memory dies. Peak compute is 13.8 POPS at FP4 and 6.9 POPS at FP8, up from 10.1 and 5.1 — about a 37 percent FP4 gain against a 2.5 times bandwidth gain, 33 percent more capacity, and about twice the HBM bandwidth per watt. Microsoft claims 40 percent more tokens per megawatt than leading GPU-based systems and up to 7 times the rack-scale token throughput of Maia 200. Maia 200 is already in production, including on Copilot, with GitHub Copilot and Microsoft Foundry to follow by year-end; Maia 300 remains a 2027 production plan, not a shipped rack. ### Silicon
**CXMT plans a 4F² DDR5 server module by year-end: Global Times:** ChangXin Memory Technologies plans to launch a next-generation DDR5 RDIMM for servers on a 4F² DRAM architecture by the end of 2026, president Cao Kanyu said at an industry conference on October 10 in Shanghai. Each cell occupies four times the square of the minimum feature size, against the mainstream 6F² cell. TrendForce, citing MyDrivers, says that smaller footprint can raise density 30 to 50 percent in the same area. The approach uses a vertical surrounded-gate transistor to improve electrostatic control and cut leakage, and TechNode reports more than 300 patent families on the architecture plus work on hybrid bonding so the memory array and peripheral logic can be processed separately. This is a stated launch plan for a server module, not a part in volume shipment. ### The Teardown: Why a Hundred Wafers a Year Is Nobody’s Job
A packaging tool is paid for by the wafers that keep it busy, not by how clever the process is. Europe’s advanced-packaging trouble, as EE Times reports it from Fraunhofer IZM, starts after the prototype works. A company can need fan-out, through-silicon vias or some other nonstandard flow at tens, hundreds or thousands of units. That run is too long for a research institute to carry indefinitely and too short for a high-volume outsourced assembly and test house to staff.

Rolf Aschenbrenner, deputy director of the Fraunhofer Institute for Reliability and Microintegration, sees that gap as an access problem: volume too low for advanced packaging at a commercial OSAT, while the institute can do prototypes and, in some cases, small-volume builds. The intended path is to adapt the process, prove it, and transfer it. The transfer dies when no industrial line will take the book of business.

Fan-out wafer-level packaging makes the arithmetic plain. An RF customer might need around 100 wafers a year. For a large OSAT, Aschenbrenner’s account is that 100 wafers a year does not register, so the job is declined. A medical miniature-camera customer is smaller still: about 20 wafers a year. One wafer can hold tens of thousands of cameras, so those 20 wafers are the entire market, not a pilot that grows. Nobody else’s depreciation schedule is built for that book.

The same reporting covers detector arrays for a novel CT scanner. Sensors have to sit very close together. Conventional wire bonds open gaps that hurt the image, so the design routes connections through the silicon to the backside. The customer wanted that work done in Europe. A research line can hold the process. A production OSAT will not, unless the annual wafer count looks like utilization rather than a favour. The research-fab hold stops being the right home for the flow when an industrial packager will take that yearly wafer count as a real run — 20 wafers, or 100 — instead of a volume that never pays the tool.
