> **120 megawatts went up in a year in Inner Mongolia, at a fifth of US cost.**

**What You Need to Know:** 120 megawatts went up in a year in Inner Mongolia, at a fifth of US cost. SemiAnalysis puts Galaxy, in Ulanqab, at up to 800 megawatts of IT capacity, with four phases built to suit two internet giants. ### Top Story
SemiAnalysis reports a September visit to Galaxy, a campus in Ulanqab, Inner Mongolia, planned for up to 800 megawatts of IT capacity, about the size of Stargate Abilene's core campus. The first 120-megawatt phase was delivered within a year at a fifth of US costs, and all four phases are built-to-suit for two internet giants. When the team visited, the rest of the campus was still a construction site. A local report put nearly 30,000 construction workers across 22 data-centre projects in the surrounding Chahar High-tech Zone. Envision Group owns the campus, has more than 100 gigawatts of wind turbines installed worldwide, builds its own wind farms and a dedicated power line to the site, and has a blade factory across the road. **CPU-Z Data Shows 16 GB RAM Gaining Ground While 32 GB and 64 GB RAM Shares Shrink: TechPowerUp**
CPU-Z developer Doc TB says validator statistics show PC RAM capacity declining year over year for the first time in that dataset, covering systems submitted over the last three months. Configurations of 32 GB are still the most common, at 37.2 percent, down 2.5 percent from a year ago, while 64 GB fell 18 percent to 7.3 percent. Shares moved the other way at 16 GB, up 12.9 percent to 33.0 percent, and at 8 GB, up 5.5 percent to 11.0 percent, while 96 GB and 128 GB setups each dropped by more than 26 percent. DDR4 is 54.5 percent of validations, up 6.0 percent, and DDR5 is 32.9 percent, down 2.9 percent. Steam's September survey had 32 GB ahead of 16 GB among gamers, and the October survey flipped that order. Team Group expects the worst of the shortage between the fourth quarter of 2026 and the second quarter of 2027, and DDR4 motherboards are expected for unannounced LGA1700 processors in early 2027. ### The Teardown: Ten Kilovolts, Then the Rack
A solid-state transformer takes medium-voltage alternating current and turns it into direct current for the rack in one box. In a 5-megawatt hall on the Galaxy campus, SemiAnalysis saw that box fed with 10-kilovolt alternating current and delivering 800-volt direct current. The stages it replaces are the ones a conventional hall stacks in a row: a low-voltage transformer, an uninterruptible power supply, and a rectifier. Each stage exists to change voltage or to clean it. Each stage also turns a slice of the incoming power into heat, and each one takes floor area, switchgear, and a maintenance window. Cutting them out shortens the path from the feeder to the server busbar and takes a whole electrical room out of the building programme.

Chinese data centres have been on some form of direct-current distribution for almost two decades, which is why an 800-volt solid-state step reads as a continuation rather than a first experiment. China Telecom ran 240-volt direct-current pilots in 2007. Alibaba's Panama power system came later on that same path. SemiAnalysis puts roughly a quarter of Chinese data centres built in the past decade on direct current, against about 97 percent of US data centres still on alternating-current uninterruptible supplies. The visitors note that this 800-volt architecture is still on the US roadmap, not in widespread American halls. An operator that already runs direct current at rack scale can treat the solid-state transformer as the next voltage step. An operator whose plant is still an AC UPS chain has to change the distribution practice, the protection scheme, and the spare-parts bench at the same time.

Walk the 5-megawatt hall as a power budget. Five megawatts of IT load has to cross from a 10-kilovolt feeder to an 800-volt bus. In the conventional chain that power would be stepped down, passed through or around a UPS, then rectified before it reached a rack power shelf. Three conversions, three sets of losses, three places a fault can open the path. The solid-state transformer does the voltage change and the AC-to-DC conversion together, so the hall's electrical plant is that transformer, the battery storage used to smooth the load, and the rack. The battery still matters because a wind-fed line does not hold a flat megawatt hour after hour. What disappears is the low-voltage transformer room and the UPS row that would otherwise sit between 10 kilovolts and the servers. Yield on that path is not a packaging yield. It is whether the medium-voltage gear holds its setpoint while the IT load swings, and whether a fault on one bus can be cleared without dropping the hall.

Cooling on the same campus uses one water loop for liquid-cooled and air-cooled servers, and it does so with no central chiller plant. That only works if the water the air-cooled gear can accept is water the climate can make. Nvidia has said Vera Rubin can run on 45-degree water without chillers. A US hall often keeps a chiller anyway, for air-cooled equipment and for the option to move load between the two cooling types. Galaxy drops the central plant and, SemiAnalysis writes, keeps much of that flexibility, in a design northern China has already run at scale. One loop means one water temperature for both kinds of server. The liquid-cooled machines can take warm water. The air-cooled machines set the ceiling, because their inlet has to stay inside what their fans and heat sinks can reject.

The shared loop fails its job the day air-cooled machines need inlet water colder than towers can make in that weather, because then either those machines run hot or a chiller has to pull the entire loop down. The 10-kilovolt solid-state step loses its reason when the utility handoff is already at low voltage, or when the site cannot maintain a medium-voltage direct-current bus with the practice that has been normal in Chinese halls since the 240-volt pilots of 2007.
