High-level synthesis starts from a short, untimed description and emits register-transfer logic a later flow can place, route, and time. Semiconductor Engineering has Stuart Clubb of Siemens EDA putting that tool on blocks too slow or too power-hungry to stay on a CPU, and not already done as reusable IP. A bus interface or a peripheral, he says, is more likely plain IP reuse.

The new blocks are where HLS landed: problems not tackled before, where someone still has to pick a hardware architecture.

Badru Agarwala of Rise Design Automation gives the productivity figure in that piece. HLS handles complex control and datapath with ten to twenty times less code than RTL. Less code is easier to review, which is how he says you trust what you ship, and easier to edit when the spec moves. That is the scaling claim, one block or a large subsystem, whether a person or an AI writes the text.

Amdahl’s law is the brake. Writing RTL was never the biggest bottleneck. Speeding one small slice of development does little if the rest stays put. Most of the schedule, he says, is verification and the loops that chase performance, power, and area.

The offset is that checking C is orders of magnitude faster than checking RTL, and HLS can synthesize quickly, so an agent could try many alternatives in a short time. He also says that coupling is not here yet.

Diptesh Nandi at Microchip draws the line in power, not in lines of code. The goal is not to push every block through HLS, and not to have a language model emit all of the RTL. Some portion exists for time to market. Use HLS, he says, and it will probably burn more power, because it will not be optimized the way hand-crafted RTL is.

His FPGA picture of the flow is a path into a device, not a claim that every block belongs on it. Tom De Muer at Keysight EDA orders the stack the same way: software leads, digital design comes next, then mixed analog, then full physics, and high-level synthesis sits in the digital part, behind software. His limit on prompting is specific.

You cannot hand a vague prompt to this industry and expect a production-ready chip, because a tape-out needs context a vague sentence does not carry.

Trust is the older constraint, and it is why the short description does not retire the engineer. Dave Kelf at Breker Verification Systems says HLS now has real value for a specific kind of design, and that a historic barrier, the same one logic synthesis once had, was the gap between the abstract model, especially timing, and the finished circuit.

Engineers struggled to make that connection, and that made the result hard to trust. De Muer sets a field failure next to a clean lab run. A design can pass a full one-hundred-percent regression and still fail in the field, and then someone has to understand how it works in order to fix it.

He says it took a long time before HLS was trusted, because people in that seat have been burned by circuits they did not understand.

The rules that keep the output recognizable have been stable. Clubb says the synthesizable subset of C++, and by extension SystemC, has stayed relatively constant for the last ten to fifteen years. Streaming interfaces in an untimed sequential language needed IP, because the language does not show explicit concurrency.

Siemens built AC channel and bit-accurate data types so streaming could live inside that subset. Mehir Arora at ChipAgents reads the whole climb as a bargain. For fifty years, EDA progress meant climbing abstraction, RTL and then HLS, because a person cannot hold the full design space.

Abstraction narrows the space so an engineer can reason about it, and that narrowing costs potential quality of result. HLS, in his line, was never about better hardware. It was the price of designing for human capacity.

Take a block new enough to miss the IP shelf and walk the article’s ratio through it. The description is ten to twenty times shorter than the RTL it replaces, so review and a spec change land on the short text. C-level checks, orders of magnitude faster than RTL checks, are what would let an agent try architectures before a netlist exists.

The output still has to be implementable RTL that meets power, performance, and area inside a defined context. Nandi’s comparison is the bill on that last step. The HLS result is the one that was not hand-optimized, so the power sits higher than crafted RTL. If the block was a bus or a peripheral, Clubb’s path never entered this flow.

IP reuse was the likely answer, and the ten-to-twenty-times saving was never on offer, because that code was not going to be written either way.

The repair is where this choice stops paying. When a one-hundred-percent regression still fails in the field, De Muer’s case, the short description has already shipped and the fix needs the timing connection Kelf says engineers have always had trouble making.

It also stops paying when the power envelope cannot take Nandi’s unoptimized result, and when a generator leaves the synthesizable subset Clubb says has held for ten to fifteen years. Outside that subset, the circuit is no longer one those rules taught an engineer to recognize, and Arora’s fifty-year bargain, a smaller space held by a person, no longer covers the repair.
