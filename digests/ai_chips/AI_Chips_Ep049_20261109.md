**Building AI Kill Switches in Data Centers: Data Center Knowledge**
A data-center-level kill switch would shut down or isolate AI workloads facility by facility, cutting power, connectivity, or both, without fine-grained control of individual agents or models, and without necessarily stopping every process at once. Cutting the utility feed is unlikely to halt GPU servers, because UPS units and backup generators are built to ride through a grid outage and do not distinguish that outage from an intentional kill; abrupt power loss also risks the hardware, which is why operators resist it. Severing the network can stop external communication while workloads keep running inside the hall, but most modern facilities have multiple upstream carriers, redundant paths and diverse interconnection points, so every external path has to go dark. A single-site cut does not stop a model spread across facilities, and it does nothing once copies have already replicated outside; early detection is what makes the switch useful. Operators are unlikely to volunteer the capability, because the business is uptime, and a regulatory requirement aimed at network disconnection rather than power interruption only works if jurisdictions align — otherwise high-risk workloads move to facilities elsewhere. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/ai-data-centers/building-ai-kill-switches-in-data-centers)

### The Teardown: The Capacitor in the Wiring
A one-transistor, one-capacitor cell can sit in the metal stack above a processor instead of taking a die of its own. BigGo Finance describes that cell as back-end-of-line memory: the capacitor and its access transistor are built in the interconnect layers, not in the silicon where the logic transistors are drawn. The channel is an amorphous oxide semiconductor, deposited cold enough that the copper wiring underneath is not put through a process above 400 degrees Celsius. Conventional silicon transistors leak when they are off. The oxide channel's off-current is low enough, in the account BigGo Finance carries from the foundry's own presentation, to wipe out gate-induced drain leakage, which is what lets a capacitor hold charge without a six-transistor latch watching it.

That six-transistor latch is the SRAM bit cell. Six devices per bit is why cache eats area that could have been arithmetic. A capacitor cell uses one transistor and one capacitor. Spread across the wiring above a large processor, the same footprint holds more bits because the memory is no longer competing for the transistor layer. It is also not monolithic 3D DRAM in the usual sense, where memory cells are stacked vertically on a memory die. The array uses the fact that a processor die is typically much larger than a memory die, so the bits can spread out instead of climbing.

The test chip BigGo Finance cites, presented at the 2025 Symposium on VLSI Technology and Circuits, combines memory and logic on a single die and ran at 0.75 volts. It retained data beyond 128 milliseconds, finished random read and write cycles in under 8 nanoseconds, and withstood more than 10^14 program-and-erase cycles. Walk those in order. At 0.75 volts the array sits on a low logic-like rail, so the reported test did not need a separate high-voltage domain just to store a bit. Retention beyond 128 milliseconds means the cell held data for longer than a tenth of a second in that test; a designer still refreshes, but the leakage that forced SRAM's six transistors is the thing the oxide channel is there to cut. Under 8 nanoseconds is the random-cycle time the test chip showed, an access time, not a bandwidth for a stack of DRAM dies. More than 10^14 program-and-erase cycles is the endurance the presentation reported for that test chip.

Frederick Chen, quoted by BigGo Finance, puts the area argument in one comparison. Because the array can use the processor's larger footprint instead of a DRAM die's usually much smaller one, "It could be >10x," so the embedded array would not need as many layers as standalone monolithic 3D DRAM.

The same article records the foundry's public line. A spokesperson said TSMC focuses on its core business, dedicated IC foundry, and that its mission is "to be the trusted technology and capacity provider of the global logic IC industry for years to come." The statement never used the word DRAM. Chao Yu-chun, chair of Winbond Electronics, said a direct entry into conventional DRAM is unlikely, pointing to proprietary process technology and a tangled patent landscape, and noted that TSMC already works with memory makers including Samsung and Winbond on advanced packaging. An embedded layer, in the article's distinction, extends the foundry business. A full DRAM push would put the foundry in the merchant memory market beside Samsung Electronics and SK hynix.

This wiring-layer cell stops being the right substitute when the customer needs a merchant memory die with its own stack, sold beside the logic chip, rather than bits spread through the interconnect of a processor that is already large. If the logic die is not the bigger footprint Chen describes, the greater-than-ten-times area does not show up, and the layer count of a standalone 3D DRAM is back in the problem. An embedded capacitor also does not, on the figures reported, replace a high-bandwidth stack: the test chip's numbers are 0.75 volts, retention beyond 128 milliseconds, access under 8 nanoseconds, and endurance past 10^14 cycles, not a per-stack bandwidth. When the workload is that stack, the foundry's logic-capacity business and the memory makers' dies stay on opposite sides of the package.
