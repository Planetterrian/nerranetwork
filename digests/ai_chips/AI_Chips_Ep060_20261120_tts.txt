Take the colocation choice apart at the breaker, where Data Center Knowledge puts a historical rack at no more than about ten kilowatts. A G P U server does not merely raise that number on the same bus. Feeders, breakers, and a cooling path have to match the rack. Then someone contracts the energy. Then a utility agrees to interconnect on a clock the project can live with.

Utilization sits on that physics, because an accelerator that sits dark is capital that is not earning. Walk the piece’s own figures as a planning case, not a measured hall. Start at about ten kilowatts. The article says G P U servers often multiply that by ten. Ten times about ten kilowatts is about one hundred kilowatts at the rack — their multiplier on their ceiling, not a nameplate.

That is the load you design the power train, the cooling plant, and the interconnection request around if you take the “often” case seriously. Their other split is the fleet, not the watt. If only ten percent of planned servers carry G P U's, that density need not decide the whole campus. Cost, city, and room to grow can still run the rest.

Once G P U's are a substantial share of the hall, power and cooling become the decision. They never print the percent where “substantial” starts, so the line stops at the ten percent case they do print. Duty cycle picks the owner. Bursty training can rent a building that is already dense, instead of funding a private hall that sits idle for long stretches between runs.

Continuous inference, large-scale inference in their example, can justify owning the building if you buy power cheaper than a colocation provider that marks the kilowatt-hour up. Speed cuts against ownership. A new private site can take years, longer if permits slip, and a finished building can still wait on the grid. In many markets, they say, interconnection time is now a major constraint.

A provider that already holds power, cooling, and staff can stand the same dense racks up sooner. Sustainability follows the same pipe, because these machines draw far more power than ordinary infrastructure.

A landlord that already holds renewable contracts, a low-carbon mix, or offsets can offer that mix before a private site in a thin renewable region, or a site where on-site generation is impractical. The private build stops being the right choice when the accelerator share is large, the work comes in bursts, and the utility’s queue is measured in years.

The capital then buys a dense hall that waits on the grid and on the next training run, while a colocation contract rents density that is already energized. Owning the hall fits again only when the G P U's run continuously and your own power price undercuts the provider’s markup by enough to repay those years of construction.
