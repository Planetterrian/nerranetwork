# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **TSMC halted Chinese foundry orders at sixteen nanometres and below without a US licence.**

**What You Need to Know:** TSMC has halted Chinese chip-design orders at 16 nanometres and below unless the customer gets a US export licence. Commerce rules that took effect Thursday also led Samsung to suspend some shipments, and they closed the path that let packaging firms clear designs without a case-by-case licence. Affected customers are generally held to 28-nanometre and 40-nanometre processes when the chips use neither high-bandwidth memory nor advanced packaging.

### Top Story
Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing has told affected Chinese chip designers that orders at 16 nanometres and below are suspended unless a US export licence is in hand, and it said it will comply with the new US rule. The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security set the restrictions on November 16; they took effect Thursday, November 19, and they reach beyond AI processors. Samsung Electronics has also told Chinese customers that some processor shipments are paused while licences are sought, the Financial Times reported. The closed route was the one in which packaging and test firms checked a design and could ship without direct US approval. TSMC’s guidance generally limits those customers to 28-nanometre and 40-nanometre processes, and only if the chips do not use high-bandwidth memory or advanced packaging. Advanced designs are now presumed to need a licence unless an exemption applies. TSMC will also review operating locations, parent companies, major shareholders, packaging providers, and shipping routes, including orders placed through subsidiaries or intermediaries in Taiwan, the United States, Europe, Japan, and Southeast Asia. A US list of approved designers names Nvidia, AMD, Apple, MediaTek, and Realtek, and no mainland Chinese companies. A separate source told Reuters that some Chinese AI chip designers could still use TSMC’s 7-nanometre process until November 19; Reuters could not independently verify that access. Samsung’s named Chinese customers include Baidu’s Kunlun unit and ByteDance. TSMC supplies smartphone processors to Xiaomi, Oppo, and Vivo; an exemption that had covered chips designed for non-AI uses such as smartphones is gone, and an industry official predicted Commerce would likely approve smartphone chips while warning that the process could delay production. ### The Teardown: The Rack That Outgrew Ten Kilowatts
A GPU server changes the colocation decision at the electrical service, before lease rate or city enters the comparison. Data Center Knowledge sets the older test as location, how much infrastructure was going in, and how the operator expected to grow. Those questions remain. What moved is how much of the answer is now owned by power delivery, heat removal, the wait for a utility interconnection, and whether the accelerators stay busy enough to earn their cost.

The mechanism is density stacked on duty cycle. Power density is not a larger number on the same busbar. A rack that draws several times a conventional load needs feeders, breakers, and a cooling path sized for that rack, then a contract for the energy, then a utility willing to interconnect on a timeline the project can survive. Utilization sits on top of that physics. Accelerator servers cost enough that an idle machine is a capital loss, so a hall built for a peak and left dark between runs fails the return test even when the megawatts exist on paper.

Walk the figures Data Center Knowledge actually gives, as a planning case rather than a measured hall. The historical ceiling it states is no more than about 10 kilowatts for a typical rack. GPU-enabled servers, it says, push that figure higher, often by a factor of ten. Ten times about 10 kilowatts is about 100 kilowatts at the rack — the article’s multiplier applied to the article’s ceiling, not a vendor nameplate and not a utility filing. That product is the load the power train, the cooling plant, and the interconnection request have to be ready for if the “often” case is the one you design to. The second figure in the piece splits the fleet, not the wattage. If only 10 percent of planned servers will include GPUs, GPU-specific power and cooling need not decide the whole campus. The other share of the plan can still turn on cost structure, location, and room to expand. The article’s contrast is binary after that: once GPUs are a substantial portion of the environment, power and cooling become central. It does not publish the percentage at which “substantial” begins, so the worked line stops at the 10 percent case it does publish.

Duty cycle then chooses the owner of the hall. For training, where demand may be bursty rather than constant, Data Center Knowledge argues colocation can be the cheaper path: deploy in a building already equipped for high density rather than fund a GPU-ready private hall that sits idle for long stretches. For GPU-intensive work that runs continuously, such as large-scale inference, the private hall can win over time if the operator buys power more cheaply than a colocation provider that builds a markup into the power price. The higher upfront capital is then a bid to cut operating cost, not a bid to arrive sooner. Speed runs the other direction. A new private data centre can take years, longer if permitting slips, and a finished building can still wait on utility power. In many markets, the piece says, grid interconnection timelines are now a major constraint. A colocation provider that already holds power capacity, cooling, and staff can bring the same dense racks up sooner.

Sustainability follows the same pipe, because the accelerators consume far more power than conventional infrastructure and so raise the stakes on the mix behind the meter. Colocation can be the nearer path to renewables when the provider already holds renewable contracts, a low-carbon mix, or offsets. Matching that in a private hall is harder where renewable supply is limited or on-site generation is impractical. An aggressive sustainability target can therefore pick the landlord even when the long-run power price would have favoured owning the building.

The private build stops being the right choice when the accelerator share is large, the workload is bursty, and the interconnection is measured in years rather than in capacity a provider already holds. In that case the capital buys a dense hall that waits — on the utility, and on the next training run — while a colocation contract rents density that is already energized. The private hall fits again only when the GPUs run continuously and the operator’s own power price undercuts the provider’s markup by enough to repay those years of construction.
