# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **TSMC's 17.61 billion dollar October shows AI chip demand still filling the foundry.**

**What You Need to Know:** TSMC's October consolidated revenue reached 17.61 billion dollars, the foundry's clearest monthly print yet that AI chip orders are still filling leading-edge capacity. The month beat August's record, and the company's own fourth-quarter range still implies two more heavy months. Separately, filings described to Bloomberg put Nvidia B300 servers inside China, and that report is now sitting beside a live congressional push on export bills.

### Top Story
TSMC reported October consolidated revenue of NT$586.89 billion, which ANI put at 17.61 billion dollars, up 59.7 percent from October 2025 and 14.7 percent from September's NT$511.86 billion (15.36 billion dollars). Asia Business Daily notes that print cleared the prior monthly record, August's NT$514.81 billion, and puts October near 23.8 trillion won; October 2025 had been NT$367.47 billion, or 11.02 billion dollars. January through October reached NT$4,485.62 billion, about 134.57 billion dollars, up 43.3 percent from 93.91 billion dollars a year earlier. The company's own fourth-quarter forecast remains 52.3 billion to 54.3 billion dollars; the midpoint, 53.3 billion, is 58 percent above the year-ago quarter and 14 percent above the prior quarter, and readers of that range expect November and December each at NT$538.1 billion to NT$569.9 billion. A second Asia Business Daily report, citing United Daily News and CNBC, says the 2-nanometer process has entered mass-production ramp, that 3-nanometer and 5-nanometer demand from NVIDIA, AMD and cloud providers remains strong, and that TSMC is still adding CoWoS capacity. That report also says capital spending for 2026 through 2028 is planned substantially above the prior three years; third-quarter operating margin was 58.7 percent, above the top of the company's 58 percent forecast band, on revenue of NT$1.49 trillion and net income of NT$763.1 billion. **AMD's RDNA 5 Inches Closer as GFX13 Support Lands in Linux Driver Patches: TechPowerUp**
AMDGPU maintainer Alex Deucher submitted eight patches on November 9 that add GFX13 support to the open-source AMDGPU driver, more than 12,600 lines excluding the usual large headers. The series brings version 13 of the graphics block, the MicroEngine Scheduler, and the IMU, and it names two targets, GFX 13.0.1 and GFX 13.0.4, which are IP configurations rather than product names. On the compute side, AMDKFD gains initial GC 13 support, a CWSR trap handler that saves and restores compute wave state, and a temporary disable of queue reset in the topology. The last patch turns on MGCG, CGCG, and CGLS clock gating so idle blocks draw less power. AMD has not confirmed that GFX13 is RDNA 5; TechPowerUp notes the assumption rests on GFX12 having been tied to RDNA 4. **Fraunhofer IPMS Highlights Semiconductor Technology Developments at SEMICON Europa: Semiconductor Digest**
Inside the European APECS pilot line, Fraunhofer IPMS has for the first time integrated chiplets into pockets in a silicon wafer, planarized the surface, and prepared it for wafer-level interconnect — the first major milestone on its quasi-monolithic integration roadmap, on work it runs at 200-millimetre and 300-millimetre. Director Wenke Weinreich framed the problem as integrating chiplets that carry memory or photonic functions while validating the processes under industry-relevant conditions, including back-end steps moving into front-end flows. Its hafnium-oxide ferroelectric FRAM and FeFET pilot-line devices switch below one volt and in the nanosecond range. The same portfolio is what IPMS will show at SEMICON Europa, alongside FAMES pilot-line work, under the Chips for Europe Initiative. ### Data Centres & Power
**AI’s Efficiency Challenge For The Semiconductor Industry: Semiconductor Engineering**
John Kibarian's October 15 keynote at PDF Solutions CONNECT, written up today, treats a McKinsey base case as a capacity problem rather than a capital problem: device revenue from 975 billion dollars in 2025 to about 2.3 trillion in 2030, an 83.5 percent rise, with 58 percent of that growth from data centers and servers compounding at 29 percent a year. Installed AI data-center capacity of 45 to 60 gigawatts in 2026 is projected at 100 to 160 gigawatts by 2030. At 150,000 to 200,000 wafers per gigawatt, half of them HBM, that implies 9.6 million to 17.5 million additional wafers and 16 to 29 new fabs sized at 50,000 wafer starts a month; Kibarian notes a modern leading-edge logic fab runs closer to 20,000 starts, which could double the fab count. The same talk puts 2030 AI revenue at 3.9 trillion dollars and margins at roughly 30 million dollars per megawatt, and says a GPU on inference can spend over 90 percent of its power on memory updates and idle time. A manufacturing executive, he said, had already been told he could not get more tools than had been promised, so the only path left was more output from the tools on the floor. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/ais-efficiency-challenge-for-the-semiconductor-industry/)

### The Teardown: The Insertion That Cannot See the Next Model
A test insertion used to be a cut the process and the physics already knew how to make. Semiconductor Engineering's account of where AI silicon is now starts from the opposite problem: the workload can change in the time it takes to get the chip out of the building, so the question is no longer only whether a contact is good. It is which slice of the build is still worth a tester hour once the algorithm that justified the die has already moved on.

The path is a chain of partial views. Wafer sort and singulated-die probe can sit at the fab, with that site's test program and its own record of failure mechanisms. The next assembly — a compute die, a stack of high-bandwidth memory, other chiplets, an interposer — is likely an OSAT job, on a different program, and the two data sets do not have to meet. Advantest's Scott Segura describes the physical reason the chain is getting longer: more active parts in the substrate, parts moved closer together, functional test creeping into what used to be a contact check, and performance probe at die level that now has to hold thermals. Known-good die, in that account, has to handle 3 kilowatts while it is still a singulated die. Silicon photonics, already, is five insertions. The substrate those dies land on is leaving the round wafer for a 310 by 310 millimetre panel, chosen so the tools can hold more dies, and that panel test is the step Teradyne's Jeorge Hutarte puts at the OSAT.

Walk the clock against that chain. ChatGPT arrived in November 2022. Widespread commercial use of agentic systems took slightly more than two years — about the time to design, verify, validate, ride a shuttle, package the part, and drop it into a working system. The design cycle those customers are trying to shrink is still 18 to 24 months, and some large chipmakers have told their EDA suppliers they want six. Even if the drawing board hit six months, the fab, the package, and the board would still be staring at a model generation that turned over inside the same window. Segura's customers already tape out, run the model or look at the end application, and then do not ship that chip. They ship a derivative, or a different version. Every insertion written for the first tapeout — the 3-kilowatt singulated probe, the five photonics stops, the panel-level functional test — was aimed at a part the lab may already have walked away from.

The cut stops being the right cut when the failure you still need to catch only exists after the compute die, the memory stack, the interconnect, the firmware, and the software are in one box, and the company holding that box cannot see the wafer-sort mechanisms from the company that probed the die. More insertions then buy contact confidence on a workload that has already left, while the combined behavior the field will actually run sits in a data set the previous insertion never receives. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/test-struggles-to-keep-pace-with-ai/)
