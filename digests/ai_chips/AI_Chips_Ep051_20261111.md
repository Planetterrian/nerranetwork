> **TSMC cleared up to $1 billion for two AI labs, beside $40.93 billion for fabs.**

**What You Need to Know:** TSMC’s board cleared up to $1 billion for OpenAI and Anthropic, set beside about $40.93 billion in manufacturing appropriations. The lab stakes are caps, not completed purchases, and the Anthropic shares are priced off a future IPO. The same meeting also cleared up to $500 million for an unnamed semiconductor-ecosystem vehicle.

### Top Story
On a November 10 statement, TSMC’s board approved buying up to $500 million of OpenAI preferred stock and up to $500 million of Anthropic common shares at the price of a future initial public offering. Those figures are maximums; the full purchases have not been completed, and the Anthropic stake still depends on an IPO and other conditions. At the same meeting the board approved approximately $40.93 billion in capital appropriations for advanced and specialty process capacity, advanced packaging, facilities systems, and research and development, tied to long-term capacity plans based on market demand forecasts. OpenAI and Anthropic run on data-centre processors TSMC already manufactures for Nvidia and other customers, and the proposed stakes stay small next to that manufacturing program. The board also approved third-quarter financial statements and routine capital and personnel matters. ### The Teardown: When the Next Hall Leaves the Hub
Two-thirds of new US data center projects are targeting rural locations, Data Center Knowledge reports, while nearly nine-tenths of the halls already standing remain in developed areas. The installed base and the pipeline are no longer the same map.

Site selection used to stack three tests on top of each other: access to power, proximity to end users, and a high-performance network already in reach. Those tests explain the old concentration in or near cities, and in hubs such as Northern Virginia, Texas, and California. A rural parcel fails the old tests on paper. It sits farther from users, so latency rises, and both power and network gear may be less developed or simply farther away.

Power is the test that moved. Public grids, including grids in wealthy, highly developed regions, cannot always meet what a new data center asks for. Operators are exploring power they generate themselves — dedicated generation assets or an on-site plant. From that angle, a thin local grid stops being a veto. Distance from existing electrical infrastructure becomes something you build around, not a reason to discard the parcel. Land works the same direction: undeveloped urban parcels large enough for a hall are limited and expensive, while many rural markets are less tight. Fewer neighbors can also mean simpler permitting and less organized opposition.

Fiber does not get that waiver. A data center still needs high-performance network infrastructure, which in most cases means fiber-optic cable, and many rural areas have little of it. The cost comparison is what makes the split workable. Extending fiber is typically less expensive and less complex than building long-distance power infrastructure or developing major on-site generation. If fiber already exists within a few hundred miles of the proposed site, connecting to it can be economically viable. On-site generation covers the electrons; a few hundred miles of fiber covers the packets. Neither substitute covers the other.

The two-thirds figure is a pipeline count, not a census of what is already energized. Nearly nine-tenths of existing data centers are still in developed areas, so the industry is not moving the old halls. It is siting the next wave somewhere else. The idea itself is not new. Amazon explored data centers in Chile and Argentina more than eight years ago, and rural siting as a discussion goes back at least a decade. Hyperscalers are again looking at remote parts of South America. What changed is execution: over the past few years, operators have begun breaking ground on rural projects at a greater scale, pulled by power sourcing, land, and community response.

That design choice stops being the right one when the job cannot spend the distance. Halls that need ultra-low latency stay near users. Builds whose scale does not justify the infrastructure spend a remote site requires stay near a grid and a fiber route that already exist. If power, land, and local opposition stay tight, a larger share of the next large projects lands outside the old metro hubs. A workload whose round trip cannot spare those miles never makes that shortlist. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/data-center-site-selection/the-rural-shift-in-data-center-development)
