### Top Story
University of Waterloo researchers are developing a hybrid cooling method that puts tiny drops of coolant on chip hotspots, HPCwire reported on November 12. The method cut the temperature of simulated chip hotspots by up to 17°C in 15 seconds. The group says data centres at the centre of the AI buildout could be cooled more effectively and economically with the system, which is still under development. ### Supply Chain & Policy
**Malaysia moves to tighten data centre controls amid scrutiny over Nvidia chip exports: The Vibes**
Investment, Trade and Industry Minister Datuk Seri Johari Abdul Ghani said the government is considering a law that would make companies planning data centres declare their operators, facility locations and the number of GPUs they intend to import, so chips under US export controls stay in domestic use and are not diverted. He said the country must not be used for what is defined as an illegal transaction. Malaysia introduced export controls last year on the movement of high-performance AI chips of US origin. Johari said there are no plans to investigate Speedmatrix, the Malaysian subsidiary of Singapore-based Megaspeed, because he does not see a law that applies if GPUs are bought for the buyer's own data centres; Megaspeed chairman and chief executive James Tan Yong Pong said, in a statement Reuters cited, that the firm takes US export-control duties seriously and will cooperate with Washington. Johari said trade and Taiwanese investment continue, Malaysia has followed the One-China policy since 1974, and both sides remain willing to negotiate under a 1993 agreement. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim told the Dewan Rakyat the postponed sessions were the Economic Cooperation Committee, the Trade Working Committee and the Investment Working Committee, not all trade and investment talks, and that Kuala Lumpur has already asked for those meetings to resume. ### The Teardown: The Voltage Where Delay Bends
Near threshold, a transistor sits so close to the voltage where it just begins to conduct that a small sag in the supply stretches delay much more than the same sag would on a normal rail. Semiconductor Engineering's November 12 account of edge-AI system-on-chip design places that regime at about 0.4 to 0.5 volts in advanced FinFET processes. Energy per operation falls as the rail falls. Dynamic energy tracks the square of voltage, so the same switching activity on a lower rail spends sharply less energy. What arrives with that saving is delay that grows rapidly, device-to-device variation that widens, and noise margins that shrink. Timing paths become sensitive to small voltage changes. IR drop and local voltage droop become first-order effects. A guard band drawn from nominal-voltage behaviour stops predicting the silicon, and models taken at the nominal voltage no longer describe the part.

Memory is often the floor under that rail. A single-rail memory with silicon-validated assist features can support operation down to about 600 millivolts, which lets the designer take the V-squared energy cut without splitting the power grid. A dual-rail memory separates the logic supply from the memory supply so compute can drop further while the array stays where it still holds state. Semiconductor Engineering points to an ISSCC 2025 result: a dual-rail SRAM at 38 megabits per square millimetre, logic at 380 millivolts and the memory rail at 540 millivolts, built in a 3-nanometre FinFET. Ultra-low-power register files hold local data next to compute. The split of jobs follows the block. Multiply-accumulate tiles want small, distributed SRAM. Activation units want those register files with wide data paths. Shared system buffers want a high-bandwidth AXI interface.

Logic libraries have to be built for the bend in the curve, not borrowed from a nominal-voltage deck. The account calls for moment-based LVF timing and noise models across a wide process-voltage-temperature range, delay and slew models that capture the nonlinear sensitivity near threshold, and extra checks for MIS effects, pulse-width integrity, skew interactions and mixed-device behaviour, because noise margins are thinner. Silicon validation, including extended timing and functional sweeps, is what keeps those models honest. I/O is the quiet veto on the whole plan. Control interfaces such as GPIO, I3C, I2C and SWI3S, high-speed links such as LVDS and RGMII, clock and reference inputs, SD and eMMC, test I/O, and 2.5D or 3D integration I/O for chiplets each have to run consistently across the supplies the core wants. If the periphery cannot, it sets the system minimum voltage, and the core's near-threshold work never appears in the product.

A closed loop among foundation IP, EDA tools and silicon feedback is what the account says makes operation around 0.4 to 0.5 volts predictable. Memories, cell libraries and I/Os have to be chosen and validated early. The near-threshold rail stops being the right choice when the memory or the I/O cannot follow the compute supply down, or when the extra delay and variation force guard bands that hand back the energy the lower voltage was meant to buy. In that case a dual-rail split, or a rail held above the sharp part of the delay curve, is what keeps a real-time edge workload inside its timing budget.
