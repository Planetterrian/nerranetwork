Co-packaged optics stalls in the factory when the fiber array has to find the die without ever touching it.

Semiconductor Engineering's panel on scaling that test past one die treats fiber alignment as the bottleneck on the optical engine.

The fiber array unit is meant to couple light and never contact the device.

Crash it in bring-up and the array is damaged. Leave contamination on it and the reading is useless, so cleaning sits inside the test cell.

Dave Armstrong of ISE Labs, part of ASE Group, draws the power limit. An eight-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides asks the tester to push that power in and pull the heat out, and that is already hard past one die.

Matt Griffin at Teradyne says most production cells today are single-site, and that multi-site probe cards would ease fiber alignment if they actually work.

Ira Leventhal at Advantest uses the ball-grid array as the warning. Socket a part too many times and the balls are at risk.

The optical connector on the device is built to be mated once and, the hope is, never unplugged.

A test connector that survives twenty to fifty insertions is not a production tool. It needs tens of thousands of insertions, or a hundred thousand.

The panel's answer is hardened metal on the tester side, mating to a device receptacle that is not hardened, so a rough mate can kill the device connector before its spec life.

Kazuo Yamaguchi at Keysight splits the easy contact from the one that qualifies the engine. DC pads plus an optical probe are mechanical. Modulator and photodetector proof is S-parameters, so light and high-frequency radio-frequency probes have to land together.

Probe cards carry only so many radio-frequency lines, and their bandwidth is limited, while these devices are characterized past two hundred gigahertz.

Leventhal adds warpage. Alignment has to hold on several axes, from the top or the side, while a dense ball-grid array still has to connect.

Griffin's package map puts an optical face and an electrical face on every insertion, sometimes top, sometimes bottom, sometimes both, and at module level on two to four sides across several optical engines.

Optical alignment alone, he says, is not the hard part. Doing it on every face at once with power and radio frequency is.

Walk Armstrong's split. The eight-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides already strains one tester insertion, so a second site buys nothing if the tester cannot feed it.

Photonic-integrated-circuit wafer test, in his experience, is single-site now, with a chance to rise if probe-card makers, and he names Technoprobe, can put more optical sites on one card.

Near-packaged optics is usually one connector. High volume of that class forces multi-site, because one alignment per tester hour will not clear the line.

He calls insertions two and three the fertile ground, the long insertions where multi-site can take time out.

Single-site stays rational while that eight-kilowatt ASIC still exceeds what the tester can give a second die, while each site still needs its own clean fiber array that must not touch the die, and while the measurement that matters is an electro-optic S-parameter past two hundred gigahertz that a probe card cannot fan out.

It stops being the right cell when the product is a single-connector near-packaged engine in high volume, insertions two and three own the test time, and a hardened test connector can survive on the order of a hundred thousand matings without destroying the softer receptacle on the part.
