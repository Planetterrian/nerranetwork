Take the backup clock apart at the gap between a dead utility feed and a live replacement, not at the generator nameplate. Data Center Knowledge treats a centralized uninterruptible power supply as stored battery energy plus voltage regulation, sized to the critical load. A typical bridge is 10 to 15 minutes. Those batteries get replaced every 3 to 5 years.

When the building's draw rises, UPS capacity has to rise with the critical load, so the same outage minute costs more battery, more floor, and another replacement cycle. A battery energy storage system is the same idea at another scale.

The publication says it can hold loads for hours, depending on size and configuration, against that 10-to-15-minute window, at a higher upfront cost and a harder integration. The sites it has in mind see longer outages, store renewable energy, or want a more flexible on-site strategy. Rack batteries move the short store into the cabinet.

One centralized UPS can cover dozens or even hundreds of servers and eats floor that could have raised density. Rack batteries localize protection and can cut the heat of a battery room. They still get replaced on a cycle. They do not, on these facts, turn minutes into hours. Buffer modules used to cover milliseconds.

Newer ones, the publication says, can cover up to 20 seconds, sometimes enough for a diesel or another on-site source to start. If that transfer is the whole goal, the buffer can stand in for a full UPS as the cheaper, lower-maintenance ride-through. It is not a 15-minute voltage regulator.

A microgrid that already serves a substantial share of the load can take the transfer instead of a long battery bridge, if capacity and redundancy are there. It does not, by itself, remove power conditioning or a short ride-through. Walk it. Grid fails, clock starts. Generator start means the 20-second buffer is the budget.

A traditional UPS means 10 to 15 minutes, on a 3-to-5-year battery replacement. A BESS means hours, paid up front. The UPS hall still occupies floor that could have held those dozens or hundreds of servers. The hybrid the publication calls practical keeps a short bridge, buffer or UPS, in front of the BESS or the microgrid that is supposed to take the load after the transfer.

A centralized UPS stops being the right primary design when the outage is much shorter than 10 to 15 minutes, or much longer than a battery room you will replace every 3 to 5 years. Generation online inside 20 seconds points at the buffer. An hours-long outage on a site already storing energy points at the BESS.

A microgrid transfer still leaves voltage regulation and a short ride-through unsolved, which is what keeps a conditioning stage in the path.
