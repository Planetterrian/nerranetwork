# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Nearly twenty fabs of tool demand still chase AI orders clients have not cut.**

**What You Need to Know:** TSMC senior vice president Cliff Hou said on November 23 that clients have not adjusted planned orders, and he has put AI-tied equipment demand at nearly the equivalent of building 20 new fabs at once, even as the foundry tightens credit. The same day TSMC publicly said advanced-process capacity is still short of demand. Intel, separately, has a first outside customer for 18A-P.

### Top Story
TSMC says advanced-process orders are still being placed as planned, and that the tool build behind them has not shrunk with the market's AI doubts.

On November 23, senior vice president Cliff Hou said clients have not adjusted their orders. He has also described AI-related equipment demand as nearly equivalent to building 20 new fabs simultaneously. The same day the company issued a public statement that global AI demand is still growing, that its long-term outlook stays positive, and that advanced-process capacity falling short of demand continues. It separated that from consumer electronics, where it acknowledged rising component prices and macroeconomic uncertainty.

The credit side moved the other way. Between November 18 and 20, TSMC began reviewing customer balance sheets and setting stricter credit limits, with the tighter look falling most heavily on custom AI chip buyers, the ASIC customers. Orders above those limits may have to be paid in cash upfront. As of June 2026 the company held NT$234 billion in customer prepayments. Hou's account is that orders are unchanged and the credit terms are tighter. ### The Teardown: The Twenty-Second Bridge
A data-centre outage is a timing problem before it is a generation problem. The IT load has to stay powered across the gap between utility failure and the moment another source is actually carrying that load. Data Center Knowledge describes the traditional instrument for that gap as a centralized uninterruptible power supply: stored battery energy, plus voltage regulation, sized to the critical load. The publication puts a typical UPS bridge at 10 to 15 minutes. Operators replace those batteries every 3 to 5 years. As the facility's normal draw rises, UPS capacity has to rise with the critical load, so the same outage minute costs more battery, more floor area, and another replacement cycle.

Battery energy storage uses the same stored-energy idea at a different scale. Data Center Knowledge says a BESS can support loads for hours, depending on system size and configuration, against that 10-to-15-minute UPS window. The tradeoff it names is a higher upfront investment and more complex integration. The sites it points to are the ones that see longer outages, want to store renewable energy, or are building a more flexible on-site energy strategy. In that design the UPS need not be the only ride-through asset. It can sit as a complement.

Rack-level batteries move the short store into the rack. A centralized UPS, the publication says, can support dozens or even hundreds of servers from one installation, and it consumes floor space that could otherwise be used to raise density. Rack batteries localize protection and can cut the heat concentration of a battery room or a large UPS installation. They still need periodic replacement and lifecycle management. On the facts given, they do not turn a minute-scale bridge into an hour-scale one. They change where the minutes live.

Buffer modules are the short end of the same clock. Historically they bridged milliseconds of downtime or a voltage dip. Newer systems, Data Center Knowledge says, can cover up to 20 seconds — long enough in some cases to cover startup of a diesel generator or another on-site source. If the design goal is only to hold servers until that transfer, the publication treats the buffer as a possible replacement for a full UPS, and calls it a lower-cost, lower-maintenance option. It does not claim the buffer regulates voltage across a 15-minute outage. Twenty seconds is the design window, not a smaller UPS.

On-site microgrids change what the transfer lands on. If generation is already able to serve a substantial share of the load on an ongoing basis, the preferred response to a grid break can be a transfer to local power rather than a long battery bridge. Data Center Knowledge says that, with enough capacity and redundancy, a microgrid can reduce reliance on large centralized UPS systems for extended backup. It also says microgrids do not automatically eliminate power conditioning or ride-through protection.

Walk the clock with only those figures. A grid break starts it. If the plan is generator start, the buffer's 20 seconds is the whole budget the publication is willing to treat as enough. If the plan is the traditional UPS, the budget is 10 to 15 minutes, and the batteries behind it are on a 3-to-5-year replacement cycle. If the plan is a BESS, the contrast the publication draws is hours rather than those minutes, paid for with a higher upfront cost and a harder integration. The centralized UPS still occupies floor that could have held the servers — dozens or hundreds of them — whose density the operator is trying to raise. The hybrid the publication calls the practical path keeps a short ride-through, buffer or UPS, in front of whichever longer asset, BESS or microgrid, is supposed to take the load after the transfer.

The centralized UPS stops being the right primary design when the outage being bridged is either much shorter than its 10-to-15-minute rating or much longer than a battery room the operator is willing to replace every 3 to 5 years. If on-site generation is the real source and it is online inside 20 seconds, Data Center Knowledge's own comparison points at the buffer, not the UPS hall. If the outage runs for hours and the site is already storing energy for renewables or flexibility, the same comparison points at the BESS. What still forces a UPS, or something that does its conditioning job, to stay in the path is the limit the publication states at the end: a transfer onto a microgrid leaves voltage regulation and a short ride-through unsolved.
