A six-hundred-millimetre panel can hold a rectangular interposer the round wafer keeps trimming, and that geometry is worth taking apart.

Semiconductor Engineering’s account, from ASE and Intel Foundry, starts there: the package grew into a rectangle, and the tools still expect a circle.

Teck Chong Lee at ASE says assembly on the full six-hundred-millimetre panel is high risk, and the equipment for the whole flow is not all there.

One path finishes the interposer at six hundred millimetres, then cuts it into four before assembly and the C4 bumps.

Count a five-point-five-reticle interposer.

Nine fit on a three-hundred-millimetre wafer.

Sixteen fit on a three-hundred-millimetre panel.

Split a six-hundred-millimetre panel into four of those pieces and you get sixty-four.

Leave it intact and you get seventy-two.

Seventy-two against nine is eight times the wafer, past the roughly fivefold gap in raw area, because the rim waste almost disappears.

Intel’s Tarik Ibrahim, in the same coverage, used a five-hundred-ten by five-hundred-fifteen millimetre panel.

A package of about four reticle fields is roughly ten units on the wafer and about forty-eight on that panel.

The biggest square the wafer can hold is only about two hundred ten by two hundred ten millimetres.

Past that outline the wafer is not merely dearer. It cannot hold the part.

Wafer process is single-sided. Panel process is dual-sided, and the capability index has to hold across a sheet the tools were not built for.

Spin coat wants a circle. On a rectangle the film thins in the corners and most of the resist flies off.

In the six-hundred-millimetre flow, resist goes down by slit or slot-die across an active area of about five hundred eighty-six by five hundred eighty-six millimetres, and lithography often leaves the stepper for maskless imaging.

Line and space is being pushed from about five micrometres toward two micrometres and below.

Front-end tools that already hold that precision still assume a round three-hundred-millimetre wafer, and older substrate tools were built for coarser features.

Ibrahim’s rule is the fork: for some form factors the wafer is still the cheaper workpiece, and past a size he does not pin to one number the panel wins on unit count.

The intact panel loses that argument at the step with no qualified tool. Assembly and C4 are that step, and seventy-two falls to sixty-four when the sheet is sawn into pieces the line can already hold.
