# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **JEDEC previews DDR6 at 12.8 GT/s, quadrupling DDR5 module bandwidth.**

**What You Need to Know:** JEDEC's DDR6 preview sets a 12.8 GT/s baseline and 153.6 GB/s per module, four times DDR5's 38.4 GB/s, on a module cut from 133 mm to 74 mm. TrendForce now has NAND contract prices turning down from the first quarter of 2027 after Chinese cloud SSD cuts outweigh North American order growth. A Fluke survey of 500 U.S. operators finds power quality inside the building, not only the grid interconnect, already limiting GPU rollouts.

### Top Story
JEDEC has previewed DDR6, and Tom's Hardware reports the baseline transfer rate at 12,800 MT/s against DDR5's 4,800 MT/s and DDR4's 1,600 MT/s. Baseline bandwidth per module is 153.6 GB/s, against 38.4 GB/s for DDR5 and 12.8 GB/s for DDR4 — a fourfold lift versus DDR5's baseline, from a 96-bit bus split into four 24-bit sub-channels rather than DDR5's two 32-bit sub-channels. Peak JEDEC rates in the preview table are 14,400 MT/s and 172.8 GB/s per module, against DDR5's peak of 9,200 MT/s and 73.6 GB/s. Modules are specified at 74 mm, 44 percent shorter than the 133 mm DDR5 and DDR4 modules, and only about 6 percent longer than a DDR5 SO-DIMM. Bank count doubles to 64. DDR6 is described as supporting 128 Gb (16 GB) dies, four times the density of early DDR5, with through-silicon-via stacking; Tom's Hardware writes that server RDIMMs could scale to 1 TB or more per slot, against 512 GB reached on server DDR5 RDIMMs and 64 GB on current consumer DDR5 modules. On the server side, RDIMMs are described as starting at DDR6-12800, where DDR5 RDIMMs topped out at DDR5-9200. Potential form factors named in the preview include RDIMM, UDIMM, and CAMM. Tom's Hardware says many believe 2029 is a realistic commercialization target, and that JEDEC has no official launch date. ### Data Centres & Power
**AI workloads hit by power-quality issues: Data Center Knowledge**
A Fluke survey of 500 U.S. data-center operators and professionals, reported by Data Center Knowledge, finds electrical problems constraining AI deployments inside facilities that may already have grid power. Nearly a third said power-quality issues have reduced their ability to deploy or operate high-density AI and GPU workloads. Just 36 percent said their electrical infrastructure is fully prepared for higher-density AI workloads, while 74 percent said insufficient power and grid capacity are moderately or severely limiting expansion. About 76 percent reported electrical or power-system issues. Separately, 79 percent said power-quality problems had contributed over the past year to delays, service disruptions, unplanned downtime, or serious near-misses — a grouping that includes incidents that did not become outages. Some 78 percent said they lacked comprehensive, continuous, real-time visibility across critical electrical systems, and 15 percent said electrical problems were identified only after performance degradation, equipment failure, or downtime. Recruiting qualified electrical technicians has become harder for 57 percent; 65 percent said skills shortages had not delayed infrastructure upgrades or new capacity. Fluke's Michael Stuart told Data Center Knowledge the risk is whether internal infrastructure can distribute power at scale, and that harmonics, voltage sags and swells, thermal hotspots, cooling, and undocumented connections show up where power, cooling, and cabling meet. He recommends measuring power quality as close as possible to the GPU loads before deployment. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/energy-power-supply/ai-workloads-hit-by-power-quality-issues-at-data-centers)

### The Teardown: The Recipe Space an Etcher Actually Has
An etch step is a point picked from RF power, chamber pressure, gas chemistry, temperature, and time, and moving one of those knobs changes more than one thing the metrology will later score. Critical dimension can tighten while a film somewhere else in the stack thins, or a profile that looked clean at one pressure bows once the incoming wafer is a little different. Semiconductor Engineering's account of SEMICON West 2026, drawing on Lam Research and Intel Foundry, treats that as a search problem before it is a machine-learning problem: the tool already exposes a space no engineer can walk by habit.

David Fried of Lam Research's Semiverse group described, on a panel, how a process engineer looking at a data set reaches for the region that worked a decade ago — lower pressure, higher power — because that is the bias the last fix left behind. The current Lam Research etcher he described has a potential recipe space on the order of 10²³ combinations. That is not a menu of twenty-three settings. It is the product of every knob the recipe can turn, each with its own legal range, multiplied out. A human team samples the neighborhoods it already trusts. An optimizer that is allowed to ignore that history can land in an operating region the team would not have opened, because the interactions were not visible in the plots they were used to reading.

Intel's side of the same problem, from Vikas Sharma of Intel Foundry R&D, is larger still and already has a clock on it. One process he discussed has 350 parameters and about 10⁷⁰⁰ theoretical combinations. Engineers working with the equipment suppliers spent about a year getting a minimally acceptable recipe for that tool and that layer, then kept working with limited success. After AI-assisted optimization they were finding better results within a few months, and more than one of those results beat the original baseline. The search did not stop at "better." Sharma's point, as reported, is that AI could identify thousands of candidates, and each one still had to be judged with silicon experiments that are expensive. Parameters in that space are nonlinear: a relationship that holds in one corner of the recipe does not hold in another, so a model tuned on the first corner mis-ranks the second.

Two recipes can hit the same performance point in development and then diverge once the line is real. Sharma's framing is stability, not the hero number: minor perturbations in incoming material or at the tool can drop performance sharply. Fried's separate caution is that there is no single AI method for every layer. Full physical simulation, surrogate models, machine learning, deep learning, large language models, and agentic setups each cover a different job — exploring conditions versus coordinating the engineering workflow — and the time is going into choosing which form addresses which workflow, not into declaring the search finished. Handing one model's recommendation to the next model imports the first model's assumptions whether or not anyone wrote them down.

Inspection is the place this already touches production, and advanced packaging is where the count gets physical. Some packages contain hundreds of thousands of interconnects, each a possible open or short. Automated optical inspection and X-ray collect the images; machine learning can separate shades a person reads as one gray. Ingu Yin Chang of ASE put that at a thousand shades of gray against the few a human eye sorts. Finding the shade is not the same as authorizing a process change. The candidate still has to be a defect, not a tolerable variation, before anyone moves a knob.

The design choice — let the optimizer nominate the recipe, then confirm it on wafers — stops being the right one when the nominated point is only stable inside the slice of incoming material and tool condition the model saw. Nonlinear regions mean a ranking earned in one corner does not travel. Thousands of candidates against a silicon budget means most of the 10²³, and essentially all of the 10⁷⁰⁰, will never be run. Authority to change the line belongs to the experiment that still fails the perturbation, not to the search that found the point.
