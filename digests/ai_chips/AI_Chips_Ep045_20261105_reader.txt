A die-to-die wire in an advanced package is a stack of discontinuities, not a short board trace. Semiconductor Engineering, in a piece by Muhammad Hassan, Mahmoud Mahany, and Mohamed Saleh, takes that channel apart at the bumps, the through-silicon vias, the redistribution layers, the interposer or silicon bridge, and the package step into the next die.

Skin effect dominates those microscopic wires, so insertion loss falls steeply and almost in a straight line as frequency rises. Low frequency, the channel is a resistive-capacitive lump. High frequency, it is a transmission line. The edge disperses, symbols bleed into their neighbors, and the eye at the receiver pad gets shorter while jitter grows. Crosstalk is louder than the dielectric.

Gaps of half a micrometre to two micrometres let capacitive and inductive coupling inject more noise than the insulator absorbs. The copper is on that same scale, half a micrometre to two micrometres, in silicon dioxide or a low-k film polished flat because any wave in the surface misaligns the mask and snaps the wire.

A board can live with thickness variation and traces coarser than fifty micrometres. Model the die, the interposer, and the package apart, and you miss the coupling that only exists in the stack.

Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express writes that physics as two contracts. Advanced packaging, the interposer or bridge, is for a reach of two millimetres or less, up to sixty-four gigatransfers a second per pin under UCIe three point zero, with sixty-four single-ended mainband lanes in a module. Those lines are unterminated at the receiver, budgeted at a quarter of a picojoule per bit.

Standard packaging, the laminate, may run out to twenty-five millimetres, at sixteen to thirty-two gigatransfers a second, on sixteen mainband lanes, with fifty-ohm terminations to ground so the reflection dies, at half a picojoule per bit. Physical-layer latency on the open spec is described as under two nanoseconds.

The extracted insertion loss has to stay above a mandatory voltage-transfer mask.

One bit on the short path is budgeted at a quarter of a picojoule. The same bit on the laminate path is half a picojoule, twice the energy, because the termination has to swallow a reflection the short link never builds. The advanced module also carries four times the lanes, sixty-four against sixteen, at a peak of sixty-four gigatransfers a second against a standard band of sixteen to thirty-two.

The price of that bargain is the span: two millimetres versus twenty-five. Push the unterminated quarter-picojoule link toward the longer run and the reflection returns, the eye that already shrank under the linear roll-off closes further, and the insertion-loss curve fails the mask even if the link math still looks fine on a short channel.

That unterminated choice stops being the right electrical design once the span leaves two millimetres, or once the bump map cannot hold aggressors inside those half-to-two-micrometre gaps without coupling beating the dielectric. Then the contract is the laminate one: fewer lanes, a lower rate, a fifty-ohm termination, and half a picojoule a bit.
