A facility-level kill switch, in a Data Center Knowledge analysis, would isolate AI workloads hall by hall, by cutting power, connectivity, or both. It would not pick off a single agent, and it would not necessarily stop every process at once.

Pulling the utility feed is a weak kill, because UPS units and backup generators are built to ride through a grid outage and cannot tell that outage from a deliberate shutdown. A hard power cut also risks the GPU servers, which is why operators resist it.

Cutting the network can stop the hall from talking to the outside while the jobs keep running inside, but a modern site has several upstream carriers, redundant paths and more than one interconnection point, so every external path has to go dark. A one-transistor, one-capacitor cell can live in the metal above a processor instead of on a die of its own. BigGo Finance calls that back-end-of-line memory. The capacitor and its access transistor are built in the interconnect, not in the silicon where the logic transistors are drawn.

The channel is an amorphous oxide semiconductor, laid down cold enough that the copper underneath never sees a process above four hundred degrees Celsius. Ordinary silicon transistors leak when they are off. The oxide channel's off-current is low enough, in the foundry presentation that article carries, to wipe out gate-induced drain leakage.

That is what lets a capacitor hold charge without a six-transistor latch standing over it. Those six transistors are the SRAM bit. Six devices per bit is why cache eats area that could have been arithmetic. One transistor and one capacitor, spread through the wiring of a large processor, hold more bits in the same footprint because they are no longer fighting for the transistor layer.

This is not the usual monolithic three-D DRAM, where cells stack vertically on a memory die. It uses the fact that a processor die is typically much larger than a memory die, so the bits can spread instead of climb. Yesterday the foundry's public line was that it stays a logic foundry. The cell in the wiring is what that line did not close.

The test chip in the BigGo Finance account, shown at the twenty twenty-five Symposium on VLSI Technology and Circuits, puts memory and logic on one die and ran at zero point seven five volts. It held data beyond one hundred twenty-eight milliseconds, finished random read and write cycles in under eight nanoseconds, and withstood more than ten to the fourteenth program-and-erase cycles.

At that voltage the array sits on a low, logic-like rail, so the reported test did not need a separate high-voltage domain just to store a bit. Holding past one hundred twenty-eight milliseconds is longer than a tenth of a second in that test. A designer still refreshes. The leakage that forced SRAM's six transistors is what the oxide channel is there to cut.

Under eight nanoseconds is an access time, not the bandwidth of a DRAM stack. The endurance figure is how many times that test chip was written and erased.

Frederick Chen, quoted there, says the array can use the processor's larger footprint instead of a DRAM die's usually much smaller one, and that the area could be greater than ten times, so the embedded array would not need as many layers as standalone monolithic three-D DRAM.

A spokesperson said the company focuses on dedicated IC foundry and that its mission is to be the trusted technology and capacity provider of the global logic IC industry for years to come. The statement never used the word DRAM.

Chao Yu-chun, chair of Winbond, said a direct move into conventional DRAM is unlikely, because a newcomer would have to clear proprietary process technology and a tangled patent map, and because the foundry already packages with memory makers including Samsung and Winbond. An embedded layer, in that distinction, extends the foundry business.

A full DRAM push would put it in the merchant memory market beside Samsung Electronics and SK hynix. The wiring-layer cell stops being the right substitute when the customer needs a merchant memory die with its own stack, sold beside the logic chip, rather than bits spread through the interconnect of a processor that is already large.

If that logic die is not the bigger footprint Chen describes, the greater-than-ten-times area never shows up, and the layer count of a standalone three-D DRAM is back in the problem. On the figures reported, the embedded capacitor also does not stand in for a high-bandwidth stack. Those figures are the voltage, the retention, the access time and the endurance, not a per-stack bandwidth.
