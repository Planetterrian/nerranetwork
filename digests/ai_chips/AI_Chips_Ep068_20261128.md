### Top Story
Datasection Loses an 80 Billion Yen AI Order from Tencent Amid U.S. Export Controls: KuCoin
HuoXing Finance reports that Japan-listed Datasection had leased about 15,000 Blackwell GPUs primarily to Tencent, and that a ¥80 billion AI order was canceled after U.S. export-control rules issued November 16. The company cut full-year revenue guidance from ¥162 billion to ¥17 billion, a decline of about 89 percent. The new rules require an export license for any data center globally that provides substantial computing power to Chinese headquarters enterprises using controlled advanced chips, and they add Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil to enhanced licensing. Asian operators are struggling to secure bridge financing and are hunting new customers; loan-to-value ratios offered by lenders have fallen from 70–80 percent in the summer to 50–60 percent. Compute redirected from the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia is hitting the market at discounted prices and shorter terms, with supply pressure expected to intensify over the next 6 to 12 months, while buyers favor U.S.-based, immediately deliverable, short-term, low-down-payment capacity. The B300 rental price index has softened but has not collapsed, and global demand is still described as strong. ### Supply Chain & Policy
**Restricted Blackwell racks turn up at a Shanghai project: Engadget**
Engadget, citing Bloomberg's review of Chinese regulatory filings, reports Shanghai Shuoyao listed a GB300 NVL72 rack at the Yangtze River Delta Project as collateral for a loan of about $14 million (96 million yuan) from Shanghai Innovation Bank. One GB300 NVL72 holds 72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs and 36 Grace processors and can weigh about 3,300 pounds; a public warranty serial traces that rack to Gigabyte, which declined to name the buyer and said transactions follow applicable law. KuCoin adds that Shanghai Kezhan reported a GB200 server at the same site against a 90 million yuan loan late in 2025 from a leasing firm tied to a Shanghai government agency, and that Caixin describes Kezhan's majority owner as a Tokyo firm said to be controlled by Alice Huang. Kang Wei is listed as president of the facility; Alice Huang took a controlling stake in Megaspeed in 2023 and left in August 2025, while Shuoyao calls the relationship ordinary commercial cooperation. Bloomberg says it found no proof Megaspeed diverted the hardware, the company denies wrongdoing, the Justice Department is investigating export-rule compliance, and Commerce blacklisted Megaspeed this week; Hopper-generation chips were also found at the site, and the facility may eventually house as many as 800,000 servers. How the racks arrived is unclear. The New York Post reports Commerce opened the Megaspeed inquiry in 2024, sought to interview Alice Huang in June 2025 — the month Nvidia put the firm on a no-sell list — and that BIS cited a significant risk of diversion of advanced computing commodities. ### The Teardown: The Wafer Bid Away From the Console
A memory wafer is started for one product. Capacity and investment that move to HBM and server DRAM, where AI customers pay more and sign long-term agreements, are starts that never become the conventional DRAM a console maker was counting on to get cheaper. The products differ. The queue does not.

EE Times lays out the price path that follows that queue, using TrendForce's revised contract-price forecast. Conventional DRAM contract prices were revised up to an increase of 90 to 95 percent quarter over quarter in the first quarter, then a further 58 to 63 percent in the second quarter. The fourth-quarter increase has moderated to 10 to 15 percent. That last figure is still an increase, and it sits on a record base, so a buyer who waited for the curve to roll over is paying the compounded level, not last year's contract. Meaningful ramp-ups of new capacity are not expected until the second half of 2027, and that capacity is expected to contribute meaningfully only in 2028. Until those wafers exit the line, the shortage is a scheduling fact, not a headline.

Walk a console bill of materials through those quarters. For decades the machine launched near cost and the price fell as components cheapened. Memory was the part makers could count on for that decline. Sony told investors that rising memory prices increase the PlayStation 5's bill of materials. The disc edition launched at $499. After a second increase in less than a year, effective April 2026, that disc edition costs $649.99. A 90 to 95 percent contract jump in one quarter, followed by 58 to 63 percent in the next, does not have to be the entire retail gap to explain the direction: the component that used to fund a price cut is now a reason for a price rise, on top of advanced wafers that no longer make a same-complexity die meaningfully cheaper. The fourth quarter's 10 to 15 percent does not unwind the first two jumps. It adds to them.

The same bid shows up in who captures the silicon that gaming once paid to develop. In Nvidia's fiscal first quarter of 2023, gaming and data center were nearly equal, at 43.68 percent and 45.25 percent of revenue. By fiscal 2026 the entire graphics business, gaming included, was 11.45 percent. The second use is now large enough to outbid the first for memory from those three suppliers.

That allocation stops being the right start for the memory maker when new capacity contributes in volume in 2028 and the shortage that lets an AI buyer outbid a console contract no longer clears the line. Until those wafers contribute, a long-term HBM agreement still takes the start, and conventional DRAM remains the residual even after a near-doubling of its own contract price. Source: [eetimes.com](https://www.eetimes.com/gta-grand-three-assumptions/)
