# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **3.6 gigawatts of contracted power now back SWI Compute's planned NVIDIA factories in Europe.**

**What You Need to Know:** SWI Compute emerged from stealth with 3.6 gigawatts of secured power and about $1 billion of NVIDIA systems on order, starting with an announced 30-megawatt factory in the UK. NVIDIA's updated Vera Rubin NVL72 sheet cuts rack memory bandwidth to 1,400 TB/s. E2E Networks has proposed 5.4 MW more for HGX B300 and other Blackwell GPUs on top of about 9.2 MW already in service.

### Top Story
SWI Compute's first large-scale AI factory is announced at 30 MW in the UK, with close to 7,000 Rubin GPUs and 3,500 Vera CPUs in NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72 racks expected online by the end of 2027. That 30 MW is the initial phase of a UK campus planned to grow to 530 MW, and the company's first step toward 2.3 GW of NVIDIA AI factory capacity by 2029. SWI Group says it holds 3.6 GW of power capacity across 24 sites in Europe and the US after six years securing land, power and shell; five campuses sit in Ireland, the UK, Denmark, Spain and Italy, and one is already leased to a major hyperscaler. The new company became a Preferred NVIDIA Cloud Partner in August, has ordered approximately $1 billion of NVIDIA systems, and says it has finalized contracts with its first customers, including a US-based AI inference platform. The first deployment is specified as NVIDIA Blackwell Ultra, with Vera CPU racks to follow, linked by NVIDIA Quantum-X800 InfiniBand and NVIDIA Spectrum-X Ethernet; Delta Computers is the systems integrator for a build the company describes as more than 8,000 GPUs. From 2027, new sites, starting with the UK campus, are to follow the NVIDIA Vera Rubin DSX reference design, which NVIDIA says can put up to 40% more GPU capacity in the same power budget. ### Silicon
**NVIDIA Cuts HBM4 Speed and NVLink Bandwidth Targets for Vera Rubin: TechPowerUp**
Yesterday the open question on the roadmap was whether announced rack targets would still be the ones that ship; TechPowerUp now reports that NVIDIA has cut them on the updated Vera Rubin specification sheet. HBM4 speed on that sheet moves from 11.7 Gbps per pin to 9.6 Gbps per pin, and Vera Rubin NVL72 total memory bandwidth moves from the previously advertised 1,580 TB/s to 1,400 TB/s. NVLink bandwidth per GPU falls from 1.8 TB/s to 1.5 TB/s, and bidirectional NVLink GPU bandwidth from 3.6 TB/s to 3 TB/s. TechPowerUp reports the NVLink Nyquist frequency target was lowered from 56 GHz to 47 GHz so the SerDes, cables and materials face an easier electrical demand, accepting less peak bandwidth for yield and stability. The outlet says the memory-speed cut is not laid directly on NVIDIA's suppliers, and reads it as NVIDIA's bus width and signaling not supporting the higher rate. Source: [techpowerup.com](https://www.techpowerup.com/354133/nvidia-cuts-hbm4-speed-and-nvlink-bandwidth-targets-for-vera-rubin)

**Silicon Photonic Modulator Reaches 200 Gb/s PAM4 With Reduced DSP: Semiconductor Engineering**
Researchers at the Institute of Science Tokyo, Yokohama National University, AIST and the Photonics Electronics Technology Research Association reported a low-voltage slow-light silicon modulator aimed at multi-terabit optical interconnects for AI data centers. The device is described as 200G per wavelength PAM4 with reduced DSP, using a built-in electro-optic equalizer. The paper sets that approach against conventional silicon Mach-Zehnder modulators, which the authors say struggle to meet the speed, energy and size those links need. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/silicon-photonic-modulator-reaches-200-gb-s-pam4-with-reduced-dsp-science-tokyo-yokohama-aist-petra/)

**Vertical DRAM Stacking Expands Thermal Design Space Beyond HBM-on-GPU: Semiconductor Engineering**
imec researchers evaluated a 3D memory stack that rotates DRAM dies vertically above a GPU and puts cooling cavities between the dies, as an alternative to conventional 2.5D HBM and to HBM stacked directly on the GPU. Package-level thermal simulations in the September 2026 paper vary stack height, memory capacity and organization, cooling-cavity materials, stack orientation, mold insertion, and a distributed memory-controller and network-on-chip tier. ### Supply Chain & Policy
**Bringing Down Test Costs For Co-Packaged Optics: Semiconductor Engineering**
A Semiconductor Engineering panel — Matt Griffin of Teradyne, Ira Leventhal of Advantest, Dave Armstrong of ISE Labs, and Kazuo Yamaguchi of Keysight — says optical-component test cost is high and approaching manufacturing cost. Leventhal's frame for the budget is that an inexpensive optical engine cannot be allowed to take down a $40,000 or $50,000 GPU or ASIC. Customers are adding monitor photodiodes and loopback taps so a failed bit-error result can be traced, while a "golden" laser is treated by test engineers as a known-good source rather than the device under test. A retimed near-packaged engine can be compliance-tested on its own DSP; a linear-drive engine, and co-packaged optics, share one electrical-optical path, so a failed link is harder to pin on either side, and rework after attach remains a reason to demand known-good optical engines even when they can be detached. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/bringing-down-test-costs-for-co-packaged-optics/)

### The Teardown: Useful Life in Vacuum
In vacuum, convective cooling does not exist. Heat leaving a processor has to be conducted or pumped to an outer surface, and that surface has to radiate the energy away. EE Times, writing on Google's Project Suncatcher, treats the radiator, the solar arrays, the power distribution and the years the hardware must survive as part of the processor's real performance, because a failed board in orbit is not swapped at the next maintenance window.

On October 1, 2026, a Project Suncatcher prototype reached orbit aboard a SpaceX mission. Developed with Planet, the satellite established contact with the ground. Its task is to test how commercial AI hardware behaves where replacement is not available. Before launch, Google subjected Trillium TPUs to vibration and proton-beam tests and reported encouraging tolerance to accumulated ionizing radiation, while still investigating errors that arise during AI workloads. It also tested a thermal system of heat pipes and radiators in simulated vacuum. Laboratory exposure is an initial indication of resilience. The flight still has to establish the rate of faults, and what those faults do to sustained output, over years.

The plant required to turn that experiment into something like a data center shows up in a model, not in the satellite now on orbit. An April 2026 study by Slava Turyshev, as EE Times recounts it, examined a representative one-megawatt installation and required approximately 5,640 square meters of photovoltaic arrays and 2,500 square meters of radiators. Those are model outputs, dependent on assumptions about solar generation and heat rejection. They are the physical scale of one megawatt, still small beside a terrestrial hyperscale hall, and every square meter is mass that has to be launched. A spacecraft also needs electrical generation and distribution, provision for periods of insufficient sunlight, and data links both between satellites and back to Earth. Each kilogram lands in the launch budget.

The processor sets part of that mass. A faster accelerator that draws more power can require larger solar arrays, more electrical distribution equipment and more radiating surface. Those supporting pieces add mass and can offset the benefit of higher throughput. Software efficiency, error rates, utilization and expected service life all move the comparison between two chips with similar benchmark scores. Take two architectures on the same workload. One completes more operations per second and needs a larger thermal system. The other is slower, draws less power, and allows a smaller spacecraft that may fly a longer mission. The useful comparison is total computation completed over the mission, adjusted for interruptions, degraded operation and the chance of premature failure. A processor that wins the initial benchmark can still deliver fewer productive hours before the spacecraft is retired. For an operator that cannot replace a failed board, that lost lifetime output is a direct cost.

Turyshev's model also examines launch cost, spacecraft construction, utilization and operating life. Under one set of assumptions, the combined cost of launch and spacecraft construction would have to fall below a benchmark based on the published price of a dedicated Falcon 9 launch for the modeled system to compete. That comparison is sensitive to the launch configuration chosen, and it says little about future launch prices. The U.S. Government Accountability Office, in an April assessment, identified cooling, communications and deployment economics as unresolved. Processing observations near the satellites that collect them is the earlier use case, because less raw data might need to be sent to Earth. A general-purpose orbital data center faces the larger infrastructure problem. Communications sit beside power and heat: a distributed workload that moves data often between processors makes the network part of the system cost, and a functioning accelerator does not by itself make the power, thermal and link systems work together at commercial scale.

NASA's High-Performance Spaceflight Computing processor, developed with Microchip Technology, is specified for spacecraft workloads that need autonomy, radiation resilience, power management and fault tolerance. In May 2026, NASA reported preliminary testing that suggested performance roughly 500 times that of radiation-hardened processors then in use. The same lifetime arithmetic applies: throughput only counts for the hours the machine keeps producing.

This design choice stops being the right one when the extra array and radiator a hotter accelerator demands consume the mission mass budget before those extra operations are delivered, or when the job is only local reduction of sensor data and never required a megawatt of general-purpose compute in orbit.
