Texas A&M published a paper, 5G CATS, on using fifth-generation round-trip time to a trusted landmark to bound where an AI accelerator sits after it ships, with the caveat that jitter in an honest reply forces the checker to loosen that bound.

Take the supply rail down near the point where a transistor only just turns on, and a small sag stretches delay far more than the same sag would on a normal voltage. Semiconductor Engineering's account of edge AI chips puts that region at about four-tenths to half a volt in advanced FinFET processes.

Energy per switch falls with the rail, and because dynamic energy follows the square of voltage, the same activity costs sharply less. Delay grows fast. Part-to-part variation widens. Noise margin shrinks. Timing paths feel small voltage moves. IR drop and local droop become first-order.

A guard band drawn at the nominal voltage stops telling you what the silicon will do, and a model taken there misses the part.

Memory usually sets how far you can go. A single-rail memory with assist that has been checked on silicon can hold a minimum around six hundred millivolts, so you take the voltage-squared saving without splitting the grid. A dual-rail memory lets logic drop while the array stays high enough to hold state.

The same account points to an ISSCC 2025 result: a dual-rail SRAM at thirty-eight megabits per square millimetre, logic at three hundred eighty millivolts, the memory rail at five hundred forty millivolts, in a three-nanometre FinFET. Small register files sit next to the compute for local data. Multiply-accumulate tiles want small distributed SRAM.

Activation units want those register files on wide paths. Shared buffers want a high-bandwidth AXI port.

The cell library has to be characterized for that bend. Moment-based LVF timing and noise models across a wide process, voltage and temperature range, delay and slew models that catch the nonlinear sensitivity, and extra checks for MIS effects, pulse width, skew and mixed devices, because the noise margin is thin. Silicon sweeps on timing and function are what keep the models honest.

The I/O can veto the whole plan. GPIO, I3C, I2C, SWI3S, LVDS, RGMII, clocks, SD and eMMC, test pins, and the 2.5D or 3D links for chiplets each have to behave across the supplies the core wants. If they cannot, the periphery sets the chip's minimum voltage and the low-voltage core never shows up in the product.

The account says a closed loop of foundation IP, design tools and silicon feedback is what makes four-tenths to half a volt predictable, and those blocks have to be chosen early. That rail stops earning its place when the memory or the I/O cannot follow the compute supply down, or when the extra delay and variation force guard bands that hand back the energy the lower voltage was supposed to buy.

Then a split rail, or a supply held above the sharp part of the delay curve, is what keeps a real-time edge job inside its timing budget.
