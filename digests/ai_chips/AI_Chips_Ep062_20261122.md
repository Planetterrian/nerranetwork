# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Sixteen months after working transistors, Rapidus still has no SRAM for 2027.**

**What You Need to Know:** Sixteen months after Rapidus showed working two-nanometre transistors, it still has no functioning SRAM, and a 2027 mass-production start is at risk. Nvidia has now named a science accelerator, Rubin HPC, with more than 50,000 CUDA cores and a 2028 arrival. Treasury says older-chip rules need no further tightening, while a House tally counts 879,868 Nvidia and AMD chips already licensed toward Chinese-tied buyers.

### Top Story
Rapidus has missed a summer target to show a working SRAM cell on its two-nanometre-class gate-all-around process, and Tom's Hardware, drawing on Bloomberg, reports that the government-backed startup has not produced functioning static random-access memory cells. The company did make test structures with gate-all-around transistors at its IIM-1 fab in Hokkaido in July 2025; chief executive Atsuyoshi Koikem told Hot Chips 2025 that on July 10 those transistors showed the expected electrical characteristics. A single working transistor is a much smaller circuit than a six-transistor SRAM bitcell with contacts, wordlines, bitlines and power rails, and SRAM arrays are how process teams usually expose defects and variability before a real processor will yield. The same report says Rapidus is also fighting contamination in its cleanroom. In a Japan Times interview, Rapidus said it was on track to mass-produce chips in the second half of fiscal 2027, which ends on March 31, 2028; earlier targets had been stated without separating fiscal years from calendar years. For scale, Tom's Hardware notes that TSMC's N2 showed more than 50 percent yield on a 256-megabit SRAM test chip in April 2023, more than 80 percent SRAM yield by April 2024, and over 90 percent average yield on that vehicle by May 2025, about half a year before mass production. Only Intel, TSMC and Samsung have begun mass production on nodes that use gate-all-around devices. ### Silicon
**Nvidia unveils Rubin HPC GPU with more than 50,000 CUDA cores, expected in 2028: Mezha**
Nvidia announced Rubin HPC at the SC26 supercomputing conference as an accelerator for scientific simulation and data analysis, not for consumer graphics, and expects it in deployed systems in 2028. The chip has more than 50,000 CUDA cores in one GPU, against 21,760 CUDA cores in the GeForce RTX 5090, which Mezha describes as almost three times as many. It joins the Vera Rubin platform. Nvidia says the design pairs that core count with faster data movement so units spend less time waiting, and it adds sparse-data acceleration for sparse matrices, stencil calculations, sorting and reduction. The accelerator uses seventh-generation NVLink for GPU-to-GPU communication. Ian Buck, vice president of hyperscale and high-performance computing, said: "For agents tackling science and engineering, faster simulation means faster discovery." Independent testing has not established how much faster specific workloads will run. The part is announced, not shipping. Source: [mezha.net](https://mezha.net/eng/news/mezha-ef50243c0beadc952a973cde_nvidia_unveils_rubin/)

**Phison Previews an SSD That Runs AI Frameworks on Its Own NPU: TechPowerUp**
At SC26 in Chicago, Phison showed a computational-storage design, still in development and presented as a proof of concept, that puts an NPU and 64 GB of onboard memory running at 130 GB/s on the drive and supports PyTorch, vLLM and OpenVINO. Phison says suitable workloads could cut data transfers by 10 to 100 times, because the drive scans and processes the dataset in parallel and returns results instead of the whole set. It rates the NPU in DMIPS rather than TOPS. At the same event, a mixed-media design puts high-endurance pSLC and QLC behind one 16-channel controller so a dedicated cache drive is not required; Phison says losing one drive in a 24-drive server would cost 4 percent of the cache instead of the usual 25 percent. The showcase also included a PCIe Gen 6 X3 controller first shown at Computex. Source: [techpowerup.com](https://www.techpowerup.com/355089/phison-previews-an-ssd-that-runs-ai-frameworks-on-its-own-npu)

**AMD grabs over 40% of new TOP500 list for supercomputers, up 14% since last year: Tom's Hardware**
Tom's Hardware's SC26 roundup says AMD is now in over 40 percent of the new TOP500 list, up 14 percent since last year. ### Data Centres & Power
**Trump wants to ban US companies from building overseas data centers as bans slow development: Tom's Hardware**
President Donald Trump said he wants to stop U.S. companies from building data centres in other countries, or to charge a "tremendous tax" if they proceed, and Tom's Hardware, citing Axios, reports he has not discussed the idea with his cabinet. He said, "They're making so much money they don't know what to do with it," and, "We're not allowing data centers to go into other countries because China will take all of them, and we're not allowing that to happen." Moratoriums on data centres have surged past 500 across the United States. A Pennsylvania town has listed 43 specific demands for one project; New York State has recommended that municipalities seek 1 million dollars of community investment per megawatt; one developer has offered 10,000 dollars per household; SpaceX is buying 418 homes near a flagship AI data centre. The House passed the Remote Access Security Act in January 2026 to close overseas-compute workarounds; the bill remains in the Senate. Nvidia has warned clients that customers should be at least 51 percent not from China. Congress, not the White House, imposes federal taxes, and the opposing party controls both chambers. A ban would also collide with foreign data-residency rules and would lengthen latency for users outside the United States. ### Supply Chain & Policy
**Bessent says no further restrictions are needed for Nvidia chip exports to China: PBS**
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Friday that no further restrictions are needed on sales of older Nvidia chips to China, and that remote access has been curtailed, after Jensen Huang stepped down amid allegations involving Megaspeed. A day earlier, the House Foreign Affairs Committee released Commerce Department licensing detail: as of May 11, 2026, the Bureau of Industry and Security had approved export, re-export or transfer of 879,868 Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X chips to Chinese-tied companies across 21 applications, out of more than 3.4 million chips requested. Approvals are not completed sales. The count includes 75,000 for Alibaba, 150,000 for Tencent and its subsidiaries, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD, companies the Defense Department has identified as tied to the Chinese military. BIS said the approach follows a Federal Register rule published in January and that it has a long record of telling Congress about H200 licensing, including rejections. Nvidia said the limited H200 shipments it made were legal and authorized, that review took more than half a year, and that it is no longer shipping H200 chips to China despite prior approvals. AMD said it complies with U.S. export rules. Bessent has publicly faulted Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over delay on Megaspeed controls; President Trump said Friday he has not lost confidence in Lutnick. Source: [pbs.org](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/bessent-says-no-further-restrictions-are-needed-for-nvidia-chip-exports-to-china)

### The Teardown: Charge on the Gate, Not on a Capacitor
A Z2RAM cell holds its bit as charge on the gate of a read transistor, and that read does not empty the charge. IEEE Spectrum describes the prototype from Nagare Labs, spun out of Georgia Tech by Suman Datta and Shimeng Yu. A conventional DRAM cell stores the bit as charge on a capacitor. The act of reading drains the capacitor, so the array must write the bit back before it can be trusted again. Datta puts that write-back loop at 40 to 50 nanoseconds. Z2RAM throws out the capacitor and uses two transistors. One writes. The other reads without disturbing what was stored, so read and write no longer share a single port the way they do when every read forces a restore. Charge sitting on the read transistor's gate also amplifies the signal, which means a smaller stored charge can still be sensed quickly. Datta's best-case estimate for that read is one to two nanoseconds. SRAM remains the faster cell, because it is built in the same front-end process as the processor it serves. It is also the expensive cell: every extra bit competes for area on a logic die, and the SRAM cell has proved hard to shrink.

The density argument is vertical. Datta says the memory transistors can be built with plasma-enhanced atomic layer deposition at less than 450 degrees Celsius, cool enough to add layers on a finished CMOS wafer without degrading it. Nagare's plan is to tape out the control circuits first on a mature logic process, build the array above them with multiple layers per lithography step, and bond the finished chip onto a processor as a chiplet. The first generation targets six layers and 185 megabits per square millimeter, about five times what SRAM achieves. Datta projects that the same approach could eventually stack up to 106 layers, which would put density at about 90 times SRAM. Take the near-term claim first. Six layers and five times SRAM is the number a customer could be asked to design around. The 90-times figure is not six layers repeated; it is the same cell carried to 106 layers, a stack more than seventeen times as tall as generation one, and the article does not publish a per-layer bit count a buyer could multiply alone. A patented metal shield inside each cell is there because the write transistor, when it switches off, couples into the stored charge and narrows the margin the read needs. The shield separates the two devices. It also makes each cell wider. Stacking is how Nagare expects to earn that width back.

This added layer stops being the right one on the package when the voltage that turns those oxide transistors on drifts under bias and temperature. Datta calls threshold-voltage stability the biggest challenge for this class of device. If that turn-on point moves, a stored bit is misread. The shield does not restore a threshold that walked, and another deposited layer copies the same film. Nagare is still tuning indium and gallium oxides, sometimes with zinc, and tungsten-doped indium oxide. There is also no agreed way to attach this memory to an AI chip; Datta's opening is only that high-bandwidth memory for accelerators is already moving from standard parts toward custom ones. Gelsinger's gate, before any of that attach work counts, is a device out of the university lab, a fabrication partner, and a demonstration on standard production wafers. Until a returned wafer holds its threshold, the one-to-two-nanosecond read remains a cell estimate, not a latency a scheduler can book. Source: [spectrum.ieee.org](https://spectrum.ieee.org/sram-memory-z2ram)
