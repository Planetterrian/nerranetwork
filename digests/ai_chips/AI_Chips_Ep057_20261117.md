# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Twelve million TPP now needs a US licence if a data centre serves China.**

**What You Need to Know:** Twelve million TPP now needs a US licence if a data centre serves China. The Bureau of Industry and Security interim final rule sets that cap at roughly 750 H100s or about 330 B200s, and it also licences advanced-chip shipments into Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Centres already serving the covered customers have 90 days to get a licence.

### Top Story
The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security released an interim final rule requiring a licence to ship advanced accelerators to any data centre worldwide that provides more than 12 million aggregate TPP to companies headquartered in Macau or Country Group D:5, which includes China, TechPowerUp reported. TPP is a chip’s peak dense throughput multiplied by the bit length of the operation; an H100 rates about 15,800 TPP, so the cap is roughly 750 H100s or around 330 B200s, and it also covers linked data centres and indirect access such as a cloud reseller. Fortune and Silicon UK report that a licence is now required to export, re-export or transfer advanced semiconductors to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, which the rule treats as a higher-than-normal diversion risk. Data centres already serving the covered customers have 90 days to obtain a licence. Only commercial recommender systems are exempt; anything that generates text, images, video or code does not qualify. BIS said the rule was written by and at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council. **Nvidia Rubin HPC GPU Unveiled at SC26 With 50,000+ CUDA Cores: Tech Times**
Nvidia announced Rubin HPC on Monday at the SC26 supercomputing conference in Chicago: a Vera Rubin-family GPU with more than 50,000 CUDA cores, hardware for sparse-data acceleration and seventh-generation NVLink, due in 2028. **MLPerf Training v6.1 Adds Post-Training Test: Tech Times**
MLCommons on Monday published MLPerf Training v6.1, adding the first post-training benchmark and the first results from Nvidia’s Rubin-generation GPUs and Google’s Ironwood TPU. Twenty-four organizations submitted 153 systems that produced 250 results; 57 of those systems, 37 percent, ran in the cloud, up from 29 percent in v6.0 six months ago, twelve used 1,000 or more accelerators, and the largest used 10,240. On the new Qwen3.5-397B reinforcement-learning test, Nvidia reports 10.44 minutes on Vera Rubin NVL72 at 1,024 GPUs, Google Cloud reports 18.02 minutes on 640 Ironwood TPUs that reached 0.765 accuracy against a 0.69 target, and CoreWeave reports 22.78 minutes on 256 GB200 GPUs across four racks, with 128 GPUs on training and 128 on generation. Those runs differ in chip generation, chip count and how the work was split, so Tech Times says they show what each system did rather than which chip is faster. On DeepSeek-V3 at 1,024 GPUs, Nvidia reports 4.14 minutes for Rubin and 8.03 minutes for GB300; a 10,240-GPU GB300 NVL72 cluster across 160 racks set the round’s fastest absolute time at 1.38 minutes. Nvidia claims up to 2x per-GPU versus an optimized GB300 NVL72 baseline, and CoreWeave reports 17.65 minutes for DeepSeek-V3 on 256 Rubin GPUs against 48.40 minutes on its own GB200 at the same count, a comparison it says MLCommons has not verified. **Singapore's Megaspeed under criminal investigation over Nvidia chip exports, US confirms: TRT World**
The US Departments of Commerce and Justice confirmed on Monday that they are conducting a criminal investigation into Singapore-based cloud company Megaspeed over suspected semiconductor export-control violations. Commerce launched the inquiry in 2024 after detecting suspicious chip-diversion activity, requested Nvidia’s sales records in May 2025, and Nvidia placed Megaspeed on an internal no-sell list the following month. Bureau of Industry and Security agents then inspected Megaspeed sites in Singapore with local law enforcement and interviewed then-executive Alice Huang; the Justice Department subsequently took the lead. The agencies said authorities under the Trump administration have charged 17 defendants in six cases involving billions of dollars worth of semiconductors, and that monetary penalties for export-control violations had increased 18-fold compared with the previous administration. Fortune, citing a November 16 Straits Times report, says Indonesian officials who inspected PT Megaspeed International Indonesia found unused server racks with missing GPUs. ### The Teardown: A Million Dollars a Megawatt in the Tank
Immersion cooling removes heat by putting the server in a dielectric liquid instead of blowing air across a heatsink. The fluid touches the board, so the thermal path is component to liquid to a heat exchanger, not component to air to a coil. In single-phase immersion the liquid stays liquid; pumps move it, and a heat exchanger dumps the heat elsewhere. Because the bath does the cooling, the hall no longer has to be designed around airflow, and the method uses no water for evaporative cooling. Data Center Knowledge notes that this also cuts some operational risk by removing fans and airflow dependencies, and that the technique has existed for decades. As of 2026 it is still not how major operators cool production halls. Headlines have been pilots, demonstrations and proofs of concept, including Microsoft’s experiment earlier this decade. Direct-to-chip liquid, which runs coolant in pipes beside the processor and leaves the server in a conventional rack, is the liquid method projected to grow.

The money is why the tank loses the bid. Data Center Knowledge cites a study putting immersion at about $1 million per megawatt of IT load, against $650,000 per megawatt for direct-to-chip. That is $350,000 more capital for every megawatt before a watt of IT is turned on. Operators trying to energize capacity quickly feel that gap on day one. The operating side looks better, and the article gives a worked cluster rather than a slogan. Adam Silva Consulting estimates that cooling a 64-rack AI cluster over 10 years costs $42 million with advanced air, $31 million with direct-to-chip liquid, and $28 million with single-phase immersion. Air to immersion is a $14 million difference across that decade. Direct-to-chip to immersion is a $3 million difference. The tank wins the 10-year cooling bill, and it wins by less against the liquid method operators can adopt without throwing out the rack. A $350,000-per-megawatt premium has to be earned back from that $3 million gap, plus whatever footprint and energy the bath saves. The article does not convert the 64 racks into megawatts, so the payback cannot be stated as a number of years; the comparison that is in the source is already enough to see the problem. Three million dollars of operating advantage against a capital premium measured per megawatt is a hard sell when the alternative keeps the cabinet, the lift, and the technician’s existing procedure.

The rest of the hesitation is how the building is run. Airflow stops being the constraint that sets row pitch and white-space layout, so the architecture can change, and the article argues it should. Servers come out of a tank, not a cabinet. Monitoring, service workflows and technician training all move with that. Direct-to-chip also changes design, but it preserves more of the server-and-rack habit, which is why incremental adoption is easier. The point at which the $1 million-per-megawatt tank stops losing to the $650,000 direct-to-chip install is the point at which heat density and water limits make a $3 million, 10-year edge on a 64-rack cluster, and a $14 million edge against advanced air, worth redrawing the hall and retraining the floor. Data Center Knowledge’s condition is concrete: if power-hungry training and inference keep expanding, and if pressure to cut water and energy rises with them, the bath gets more attractive. If that boom flattens before operators make the capital and the cultural commit, immersion stays where it is in 2026, a pilot beside a production hall still built for racks.
