SemiAnalysis is reading construction, not a press release.

One set of photographs covers more than twenty-four gigawatts already in an advanced stage of construction, on forty campuses.

Most of those buildings are past halfway, with exterior cooling and electrical gear already in place, and the firm puts delivery in 2027.

Those forty sites are only a small share of the several hundred advanced-stage projects it tracks.

Year to date, slightly under one trillion dollars of binding, non-cancellable data-centre leases have been signed.

A typical deal runs fifteen to twenty years, at thirty to fifty billion dollars of total contract value per gigawatt, and a developer usually spends only five to ten percent of project capital before a customer signs.

Altimeter’s Brad Gerstner, on the All-In Podcast, called a plus-forty-three-gigawatt version of that 2027 forecast too aggressive, and put his own number near twenty-five gigawatts.

Synopsys, in a paper written up by Semiconductor Engineering, says embedded memory now eats more than half the die on AI and high-performance system-on-chips.

Compute is not the limiter in that account. Bandwidth, latency and energy are stuck behind the memory arrays.

On Intel’s eighteen-A process, SRAM and register-file test chips using RibbonFET and PowerVia showed density gains in the five to ten percent range.

Iso-power performance on those same test chips rose by up to about four percent.

A hundred-megawatt data centre can drink about two point five billion litres of water a year, Data Center Knowledge reports, roughly what five thousand or more homes use.

Almost all of that water is there to throw heat away.

The common method is direct evaporative cooling: air is pulled through wetted pads, and the evaporation itself takes the heat out of the airstream.

Most operators do not recycle it, because the alternatives cost more to build and are harder to run than the current economics will pay for.

Uptime Institute says there is no single water template. Each site has its own signature. Older halls often sit on open cooling towers, and a new hall can be designed to use little water, or none, for cooling.

Yesterday’s report had Samsung taking a TSMC three-nanometre base die for Nvidia. SammyGuru now puts numbers on the shortage behind that ask.

Industry estimates put high-bandwidth memory demand next year at about sixty-one point five billion gigabits, up ninety-one percent from this year.

Makers may ship only fifty-four point six billion gigabits, a gap of about ten percent.

Nvidia’s custom stack, NVHBM, uses a physical layer sixty percent smaller than standard HBM, which is why the base die needs a leading-edge logic process.

Those same estimates put TSMC’s three-nanometre yield above ninety percent, against about sixty percent for Samsung’s comparable two-nanometre SF2 process.

Samsung’s own four-nanometre base-die lines are limited to Hwaseong Line seventeen and part of Pyeongtaek P3.

Yole Group sees the advanced-packaging market moving from fifty-five billion dollars in 2025 to more than one hundred twenty billion dollars by 2031, and to more than half of all semiconductor packaging revenue by then.

Hybrid bonding joins a dielectric face and a copper pad in one step, so the interconnect can sit on a pitch a solder bump struggles to hold. The copper has to land on copper.

BigGo Finance quotes Applied Materials’ Prabu Raja calling this a materials-engineering problem that needs wafer-level precision and cleanliness, not back-end assembly alone.

A particle, a dished copper pad, or a dirty surface from an earlier tool can waste whatever alignment accuracy the bonder has.

Applied Materials and Besi started a joint project in Singapore in 2020, and the Kinex die-to-wafer tool pulls cleaning, activation and alignment into one tighter flow.

Imec and EV Group, in a May result that account cites, hit a two-hundred-nanometre copper pitch in wafer-to-wafer hybrid bonding.

After the bond, copper-pad overlay stayed under forty nanometres on every test chip across three-hundred-millimetre wafers.

Forty nanometres is a fifth of that pitch. A miss that large eats a big share of the contact before corrosion or dishing is even counted.

Imec still flags polishing topography, copper corrosion, dielectric reliability, and metal diffusion along the bond line as open problems for any tighter pitch.

ASML’s TWINSCAN XT:260, delivered in 2025 for advanced packaging, scans two hundred seventy wafers an hour. That tool prints layers. It does not bond them.

A patent published on the fifteenth of May, filed by ASML Netherlands, talks about holding alignment by controlling the surface of the substrate support.

ASML has not confirmed a commercial delivery date, a customer qualification, or mass-production performance for an actual hybrid-bonding machine.

On the first of October, Besi joined Applied Materials’ EPIC centre in Silicon Valley, a site planned at up to about five billion dollars as customer work arrives.

That scope runs past die-to-wafer bonding into thermocompression scaling, die-to-panel, and optical links. Five days later the same firm deepened Foveros work with Intel.

Lam Research is on a different path: Syndion etch for deep through-silicon vias, and SABRE 3D plating to fill them. That covers the hole. It does not replace the bonder.

Hybrid bonding stops being the right HBM step when those extra surface controls raise stack cost faster than the tighter pitch pays back, or when polish, corrosion and interface diffusion cannot stay inside the overlay the pitch needs.

Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron have not said which HBM generation will split thermocompression bonding from hybrid bonding. Until they do, two hundred nanometres on a three-hundred-millimetre wafer is a research result, not a process commitment.
