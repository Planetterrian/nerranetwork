# AI Chips & Data Centres Daily
> **Twenty-four percent a year of data-centre power growth still leaves supply behind compute demand.**

**What You Need to Know:** McKinsey’s Global Energy Perspective 2026 puts data-centre electricity demand on a 24% compound annual path through 2030, and its most likely cases still have power supply below IT compute demand. The binding limit is how fast interconnection, turbines, transformers and construction can be delivered, not how many CPUs and GPUs are on order. Operators chasing speed are already turning to on-site engines, turbines, fuel cells and batteries.

### Top Story
McKinsey’s Global Energy Perspective 2026 says data-centre electricity demand could grow at a 24% compound annual rate through 2030, with the volume of computing that actually comes online set by power infrastructure rather than by chip orders alone. Sam DeFabrizio, a partner at McKinsey, told Data Center Knowledge: “From now to 2030, our most likely scenarios all have power supply lower than IT compute demand.” The projection mixes monthly tracking of CPU and GPU purchase commitments and hyperscaler capital-spending announcements with a delivery test: interconnection approvals, permitting, moratoriums, transformer lead times, turbines, reciprocating engines, fuel cells, and engineering, procurement and construction capacity. DeFabrizio said more construction and turbine manufacturing capacity, plus faster interconnection approvals and permitting, could let supply grow faster than the baseline; limited construction capacity or stronger efficiency gains — from models, from the buildings, or from shifting when workloads draw power — could pull electricity demand below it. The report’s wider point is that inexpensive generation cannot carry electrification unless transmission, storage and reliable capacity are ready to deliver it. Developers looking for faster power are turning to gas engines and turbines, fuel cells and battery storage. ### Data Centres & Power
**EMEA build shifts past FLAP-D, and delivery is the test: Data Center Knowledge**
Power limits are pushing development beyond Frankfurt, London, Amsterdam, Paris and Dublin, and grid access, construction capacity and community support decide which announcements become operating halls. Oscar Gomez Comellas, research manager for EMEA at DC Byte, told Data Center Knowledge that power availability is the principal obstacle, and that several steps have to land before an early-stage project counts as realistic. The Nordics are bringing capacity online fastest because development started earlier than in Milan and Madrid. Michael Winterson, secretary general of the European Data Centre Association, said he expects Europe’s roster of markets with more than 1 GW installed to expand from five to between 12 and 14, citing national demand, lower deployment risk, and energy and planning limits that steer projects toward markets that still have capacity. Established hubs still hold the connectivity and customer-demand advantage, and a new market has to show it can support successive projects, not only a first campus. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/data-center-site-selection/emea-data-center-expansion-faces-a-delivery-test)

### Supply Chain & Policy
**Musk limits TSMC to a possible Terafab sublease; Intel stays in: Benzinga**
After confirming early-stage talks with TSMC, Elon Musk wrote on Wednesday that his companies will build and run Terafab: “We will build and run the fab. Let there be ZERO doubt about that. Maybe TSMC subleases part of the Terafab if they want, but nothing more than that.” The post answered speculation that the likeliest deal had TSMC owning and running the fab, with SpaceX investing or committing to buy chips. Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan told Bloomberg, ahead of a Tokyo event marking 50 years of Intel’s operations in Japan, that Intel will keep working on Terafab. TechPowerUp reports that the project wants to license Intel’s 14A node and other technologies, that Intel IP is the base for now, and that TSMC would not operate capacity on its own — a knowledge role and a possible sublease, not a transfer of TSMC node IP.
