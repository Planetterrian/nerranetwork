> **Nineteen point two terabytes per second is the Vera Rubin bandwidth buyers get, not twenty-two.**

**What You Need to Know:** Nvidia’s production Vera Rubin sheet sustains 19.2 terabytes per second of HBM4, under the 22 terabytes per second development target. Nvidia and AMD are putting a combined $4 billion into Meta as that company raises equity for AI infrastructure. AUO’s board has approved a NT$38.8 billion sale of a Taichung plant to TSMC and killed a smaller Quanta disposal.

### Top Story
Nvidia told Wccftech that specifications published during Vera Rubin’s development were preliminary architectural targets, and that the figures now on nvidia.com and the Vera Rubin datasheet are what the system sustains at qualified, volume production. HBM4 bandwidth on that sheet is 19.2 terabytes per second, against 22 terabytes per second at unveiling, which Wccftech calculates as 12.7 percent lower; sixth-generation NVLink is 3 terabytes per second, against 3.6, a 16.7 percent cut, and both cuts apply to NVL72 and NVL8. Each Rubin GPU had been shown with 288 gigabytes of HBM4. Yesterday’s Oracle availability was these production systems; what moved is the bandwidth Nvidia now prints for them. Nvidia said Vera Rubin NVL72 is in full production and shipping, that third-party agentic benchmarks already used those production specifications, and that those measurements show 30 times the performance per watt and 30 times the tokens per dollar versus the prior GB300 NVL72. The company said individual component specifications do not determine application performance for agent workloads, which it described as decided at data-centre scale. Source: [wccftech.com](https://wccftech.com/nvidia-official-statement-on-vera-rubin-spec-change/)

**TSMC To Commence A14 Process Node Mass Production in the First Half of 2028: Wccftech**
DigiTimes, as reported by Wccftech, says TSMC’s A14 node is set for risk production in the second half of 2027 and mass production in the first half of 2028, with Hsinchu Science Park Baoshan Fab 20 and Central Taiwan Science Park Fab 25 as the primary sites. The same report says A14 capacity in 2028 will be 7.5 times Intel’s 14A capacity. Wccftech reports A14 at 15 percent higher speed at the same power, up to 30 percent better power efficiency, and up to 20 percent density versus N2, using second-generation gate-all-around nanosheet transistors and NanoFlex Pro. ETNews, cited there, says A14 is the node that scales the SRAM bit cell from 0.021 square micrometres to 0.017, and that TSMC will announce research results by year-end on a cell under 0.017 square micrometres. Intel’s 18A, already in volume production, is described with a 0.021-square-micrometre high-density cell and up to 38.1 megabits per square millimetre, matching the 2-nanometre SRAM figures Wccftech quotes for TSMC. Cadence locked TSMC A14 tool certification last month, which Wccftech says cuts design-iteration time by 2.5 times. Pilot production of A14 is reported as started. Source: [wccftech.com](https://wccftech.com/tsmc-a14-process-node-mass-production-first-half-2028-7-5x-capacity-intel-14a/)

### The Teardown: The Solver Signs the Stage
Every autonomous step in a chip flow is either inside the physics or it is a risk the next step inherits. Semiconductor Engineering carries a Siemens account of what that means once agents drive electronic design automation. A self-verifying agent can inspect its own work. A physics-validated workflow is the stricter object: every agent decision is confirmed against a deterministic, physics-based engine before the next step begins. The engine, not the model, is the authority on correctness. Tapeout does not grade on a curve. Silicon works or it does not, and a model that can discuss heat or timing does not relax that binary.

The check cannot wait for the end of the run, because misses compound. A workflow that reasons about physics and does not validate outputs against those engines adds risk at every autonomous step. The longer it runs without validation, the further a design can drift before an error surfaces. Across multiple tools, multiple physical domains, and hours without a person in the loop, unvalidated decisions stack. Continuous validation is what keeps an early miss from becoming the assumption the next tool is built on.

Three-dimensional integrated circuits are the case the account uses. Stacked active dies make heat removal and temperature interactions harder to predict, and the package can add mechanical stress. Electrical, power, thermal, and mechanical effects are tied together, so a change in one moves the others. Doing that by hand, tool by tool, creates engineering handoffs, slows iteration, and caps how many alternatives a team can try. AI factories, the piece notes, are asking for more compute, memory bandwidth, and thermal performance inside a tighter footprint, which is why chiplet 2.5D and 3D packages are carrying the scaling once a monolithic die runs out of room.

The worked example is a nine-stage electro-thermal workflow. MCP servers and skill files for the tools connect to Fuse, which aggregates them, and in this example Claude Code is the coding agent. The flow spans seven Siemens EDA tools and runs 100 to 200 agentic turns over more than 12 hours. Stages run from floor planning, library characterization, and power analysis through thermal signoff, system-level thermal simulation, parasitic extraction, and power- and temperature-aware SPICE simulation. Each stage is validated with signoff-grade physics solvers. The agent executes, reads the result, and keeps refining when a target is missed. It does not bank a stage and hope a final signoff catches it.

The sweep is what those hours are for. Teams can vary heat-transfer-coefficient boundaries, power budgets, and material stack-ups across 200 to 500 or more design points in a cycle. Five hundred divided by two hundred is 2.5, so the top of that stated range is two and a half times the bottom, inside one cycle. The account says the same machinery lets teams configure tools up to 20 times faster, cut engineering effort by up to 95 percent, and evaluate up to 50 times more design options than a conventional manual flow. The 20-times configuration speed is what makes a run of more than 12 hours and 100 to 200 turns able to touch hundreds of points instead of a handful of hand-built decks. The 50-times claim and the 200-to-500 point range are the article’s own measures of that wider search, not two labs reporting the same experiment.

This control stops being the right one when a stage is allowed to advance because the agent is satisfied and the solver speaks only after the twelve hours. At that point a search up to 50 times wider than a manual flow is also up to 50 times more ways for an early, unvalidated miss to propagate through seven tools, and the binary tapeout check arrives after the drift has already been designed in. Source: [semiengineering.com](https://semiengineering.com/physics-based-validation-the-foundation-of-trustworthy-agentic-eda/)
