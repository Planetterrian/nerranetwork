Take the bit apart at the gate, not at a capacitor, and the read no longer destroys what it just measured. IEEE Spectrum is describing Z2RAM, a prototype from Nagare Labs, the Georgia Tech spinout of Suman Datta and Shimeng Yu. Ordinary DRAM keeps the bit as charge on a capacitor. Reading it empties that capacitor, so the array writes the bit back.

Datta times that loop at forty to fifty nanoseconds. Z2RAM uses two transistors and no capacitor. One transistor writes. The second reads without disturbing the charge, so the two operations stop sharing one port. Charge on the read transistor's gate amplifies the signal, and a smaller packet can still be sensed quickly. His best-case read is one to two nanoseconds.

SRAM is still quicker, because it is built in the same front-end flow as the processor, and it is costly because every extra bit fights for logic-die area.

Density here is a stack. The memory transistors can be deposited below four hundred fifty degrees Celsius, cool enough, Datta says, to land on a finished CMOS wafer without wrecking it. Control circuits tape out first on a mature logic process. The array goes on top, several layers per lithography step, and the die bonds onto a processor as a chiplet.

Generation one aims at six layers and one hundred eighty-five megabits per square millimeter, about five times SRAM. Carry the same cell to one hundred six layers, his projection, and density lands near ninety times SRAM. Six layers and five times is the near-term number.

A metal shield inside the cell blocks the write transistor from kicking the stored charge when it switches off. The shield widens the cell. The stack is supposed to pay that width back.

The cell loses its place on the package when the voltage that turns those oxide transistors on drifts under bias and heat. Datta calls that threshold stability the hardest problem in this device class. A walked threshold misreads the bit. The shield cannot pull a threshold back, and another layer only copies the film.

Until a wafer comes back with that voltage held, the one-to-two-nanosecond read is a lab estimate, not a latency anyone can schedule.
