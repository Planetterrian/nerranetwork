### Lead
**Falklands weighs curbs on Argentine visitors after flag-waving incidents: MercoPress**
Residents of the Falkland Islands have been angered by Argentine visitors waving Argentine flags and banners denying British sovereignty, and members of the community are calling for such visits to be restricted. Argentine nationals can currently enter with a valid passport, proof of return travel, confirmed accommodation, health insurance and sufficient funds. Michael Goss, a Member of the Legislative Assembly, said he is personally not opposed to restrictions, but that “we can’t turn around and directly stop people coming in at this moment in time,” because it comes down to what can be done legally. He said the Assembly has already begun, at an early stage, to look at ways it might stop incidents of this kind, though that also depends on administration, and that people “visiting the Falkland Islands” to further a political agenda is “not acceptable.” Goss said the islands are hearing “a lot of noise” from Argentina, including sparks at the United Nations when he and Dom were away, but that “it’s just noise, and we’re not going to let it get to us.” MercoPress reports that such incidents have recurred as President Javier Milei’s government has stepped up Argentina’s sovereignty claim, including legal action against companies exploring for oil near the Islands. ### Both Sides: Paving Praça do Pomar
Rio de Janeiro’s Barra subprefecture has begun rebuilding Praça do Pomar, in Jardim Oceânico, at a stated cost of 2.8 million reais, with completion expected in December. The question is how much of the square to pave, and the decision sits with the subprefecture after a letter from neighbours.

The strongest case for changing the plan comes from the residents’ association Amojo. Its president, Paulo Oighenstein, says a residents’ commission asked for less paving: keep part of the paths in saibro; on the main allée, lay concrete blocks on only one strip, with gaps for grass, and leave saibro on the rest; and pave toward the fountain only at gates that stay open, so wheelchairs and prams can pass. He says those changes, if made before the work is executed, would not raise the cost. Resident and lawyer Rogério Zouein says covering every path in concrete blocks will make the square hotter and harm its landscape, and that this runs against depaving programmes, including ones linked to Rio’s former environment secretary. An unnamed resident on the commission wants sand kept in the play space for children up to four, rather than the planned rubber floor, because she says the city does not maintain the sand.

The strongest case for going ahead comes from the subprefecture. It says that for years it received complaints that the soil is impassable on rainy days, and that residents asked for a pedestrian path and a draining surface. It says the tender followed a project approved after an earlier consultation, and that requests made later were sent to the technical area. It says no tree will be removed. The municipal conservation secretariat says damaged fence bars will be replaced and the existing fence repainted after sanding, cleaning, anti-corrosion paint and a finish coat. It says municipal decree 36,180 of 2012, which Zouein cites on work signs, covers private works and does not apply to public ones.

Architect and urbanist Vinicius Mattos, a UFRJ researcher, says the heat worry is justified if the mineralized area grows much, especially without shade. He also says hollow blocks with grass are a poor path surface, because grass fails under footsteps, compaction, shade and weak upkeep, and the ground can become uneven and less accessible. A paved path under trees, he says, can stay cooler than a so-called green surface in full sun.

Both sides accept that the rebuild is already under way and is scheduled to finish in December. The city says the later requests remain under technical review while demolition, new surfaces and the resetting of Portuguese stone continue.
