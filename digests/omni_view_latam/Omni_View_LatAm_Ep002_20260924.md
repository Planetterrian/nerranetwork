### Lead
**Brazil set to keep a R$2.12-a-litre diesel subsidy for 30 more days: O Globo**
Brazil’s federal government is expected to announce, still this week and on the eve of the presidential election, that it will keep a diesel subsidy of 2.12 reais a litre for another thirty days. Planning Minister Bruno Moretti said the subsidy will be extended. The extra real a litre published this month has been running on top of an existing 1.12-real subsidy, and that combined measure loses effect on Saturday. The provisional measure that authorized the newer subsidy lets the amount be changed at any time; the government says the war in the Middle East may require a benefit larger than one real after September 26, because higher oil prices and refinery problems threaten local prices and domestic supply, above all for trucks and buses. Early this month the government also announced Pis/Cofins relief of 0.63 reais a litre on gasoline and 0.19 reais a litre on fuel ethanol. Together with the newer diesel subsidy, those extra fuel-price benefits cost the Union seven billion reais a month. ### Across the Region
**Military health-fund administrator resigns as the debt crisis deepens: La Nación**
Retired colonel Ariel Guzmán has resigned as administrator of the armed forces’ health fund, a day after meeting Defense Minister Carlos Presti. La Nación reports the debt now reaches 200 billion pesos, while the old Instituto Obra Social de las Fuerzas Armadas still owes providers 213 billion pesos. A new Obra Social de las Fuerzas Armadas receives members’ contributions; a source close to the agency said Presti’s design left a residual Iosfa structure to pay the old debt, with a commitment that the Economy Ministry would supply the money. Presti called Guzmán in because he was not satisfied with the pace of the liquidation, which the minister considers a fundamental step toward the past debt and a new management model. The State Workers’ Association, led by Rodolfo Aguiar, says an emptying-out puts about 600 jobs at risk and that benefit cuts now reach the interior; at the Aeronautical Military Lyceum in Funes, Santa Fe, civilian staff are protesting pay deductions while care is cut. The union protested at Paso 551, whose doors had been shut over fear of an occupation, and then took the building after forcing the shutter. **Honduras Congress left 786 bills pending after a busier first half: Infobae**
The Association for a More Just Society counted 926 bills filed in Honduras’s National Congress between January 25 and June 30, of which 140 were approved, about 15 percent, leaving 786 pending. The Honduran civil-society group logged 62 sessions averaging 3.7 hours, with average attendance of 98.9 percent, helped by fines for absences without a justification it accepted. About 713,000 lempiras, roughly 26,589 dollars, were deducted from the pay of 48 deputies. Of the bills that passed, infrastructure and transport led with 28, health with 18, and economy and finance with 12. Democracy specialist Emily Flores asked, at the report’s presentation, whether the country wants quantity or quality. ### The Region and the World
**Guatemalan business group backs deeper U.S. cooperation against cross-border crime: Infobae**
Guatemala’s main business chamber, CACIF, on September 23 backed tighter cooperation with the United States against transnational crime after Guatemalan authorities met a U.S. Justice Department delegation in Guatemala. President Bernardo Arévalo, Interior Minister Marco Antonio Villeda, and Attorney General Gabriel García Luna met five federal prosecutors named by President Donald Trump and representatives of nine federal districts, a first for that grouping in Guatemala, in a visit coordinated by Erik Peterson’s overseas prosecutorial training office. The government said acting Deputy Attorney General Brian Skaret took part, along with the DEA, the FBI, Homeland Security Investigations, and Joint Task Forces Alpha and Vulcano. The agenda covered prosecutions, border security, extraditions, and building cases for U.S. courts, including drug trafficking, money laundering, and human trafficking and smuggling. CACIF said joint investigations can hit networks that operate across countries and asked for a professional, objective, and independent prosecution based on evidence; the executive said Guatemala takes the cooperation as a strategic partner. ### Both Sides: Anonymous accusations and platform orders
Argentina’s Supreme Court has ordered Facebook Argentina, the local unit of Meta, to delete anonymous Facebook and Instagram posts that called F.C., a regular student at the National University of La Plata’s Faculty of Humanities and Education, an “abuser,” and to turn over available data identifying the account holders. The order sets aside a ruling of the Federal Chamber of La Plata that had rejected his habeas data claim.

F.C.’s case, which the Court accepted, is that the campaign left him unable to defend himself. Posters with his face and the word “Abuser” went up across the faculty. Anonymous profiles, presented as linked to the faculty gender commission, accused him of imposing sex on his partner even when she objected, while he publicly backed feminism and abortion rights. He argued that a private matter that did not rise to public interest had been turned into a public shaming. The Court called it a kind of virtual escrache, said it put his honor and privacy at risk, and held that freedom of expression, including on the internet, is especially broadly protected but is not absolute and must be reconciled with honor, privacy, and the right to a defense. It told Facebook to remove the challenged URLs if they are still up.

The strongest case for leaving the posts in place is the one the La Plata chamber acted on when it rejected the claim, together with the weight the Supreme Court itself gave speech. The posts’ authors are not quoted in the reporting; what they published is the accusation, framed as a warning about someone they called a hypocrite. The chamber declined to order removal or identification. The Court did not treat the abuse allegation as a finding.

Both the student and the publishers of the posts are dealing with the same material: anonymous posts on Facebook and Instagram that used the word “abuser.” The Court and the chamber split on whether that required takedown and naming.
