# Omni View Central & South America
> **At least one point one million reais in Brazil's campaigns paid candidates' own firms and kin.**

**What You Need to Know:** At least 1.1 million reais in Brazil's 2026 campaigns went to candidates' own companies and to relatives. The law does not ban that by itself; the fight is whether the work was real and priced like the market, especially when public campaign money paid. Elsewhere, a two-month HIV injection is nearing Brazil's public system, and Uruguay has cooled an abortion-law rewrite ahead of a papal visit.

### Lead
**Candidatos contratam próprias empresas e negócios de familiares para campanhas: O Globo**
O Globo reports that candidates in the 2026 elections hired firms they own, or businesses and relatives, for aircraft, transport, marketing, printing and administration, totaling at least 1.1 million reais. About 31 percent of that sum went to companies in which the candidates are partners, and 69 percent went to relatives or family-linked businesses, including a wife, a brother-in-law, nephews and sons. Electoral law does not itself prohibit the hiring. Fernanda Lobo de Pinho, an electoral lawyer and member of the Brazilian Academy of Electoral and Political Law, says the test is whether the contract is real, necessary and at market rates, or whether campaign money could return to the candidate, with closer scrutiny when the Special Campaign Financing Fund or the party fund pays. In Bahia, federal deputy Dal Barreto of União Brasil paid 160,000 reais to a company he owns with his wife to rent an aircraft and says the money was his own; deputy Elmar Nascimento rented a helicopter from his own firm, a figure the campaign revised from 100,000 to 59,000 reais and says came from his personal account. In Rio, Clébio Lopes, known as Jacaré, also of União Brasil, paid 100,000 reais from the public campaign fund to a marketing firm he administers; on September 14 the Rio electoral court rejected his candidacy registration, citing a criminal case in which he is accused of helping lead a group involved in diverting public funds, a ruling he can appeal to the Superior Electoral Court, and he says his own firm was cheaper. In São Paulo, Governor Tarcísio de Freitas paid 120,000 reais to a company of his brother-in-law for financial services to the reelection campaign; the campaign says the hire rests on professional experience and that the law allows it. ### Across the Region
**HIV: SUS poderá ter Prep de longa duração ainda neste ano; entenda o que vai mudar: O Globo**
Injectable cabotegravir, made by GSK, could enter Brazil's public health system this year if Conitec, the health-ministry committee that decides which drugs the SUS covers, issues a positive opinion in October, GSK virology business director Roberta Correa told O Globo. Anvisa approved the drug three years ago. Protection lasts two months; Correa says that in GSK's study it exceeded 99 percent, with monthly injections for the first two months and then one every two months. Health-ministry HIV director Draurio Barreira says the shot will not replace daily oral PrEP, already used by more than 150,000 people, and that the first supply is about 3,000 treatments, part for an implementation study and part for specific groups. He says the ministry's access panel shows 80 percent of daily oral users are men who have sex with men, and that the injection is aimed at cis and trans women, travestis, and young people aged 18 to 24, more than half of whom stop the pills. Six-month lenacapavir, from Gilead, still has no price agreement for Brazil; Gilead did not accept O Globo's interview request, while the Pan American Health Organization says 14 countries, including Brazil, may order it from October 2026, with prices kept confidential. ### Both Sides: Whether Uruguay should amend its abortion law before the pope arrives
Yamandú Orsi's government in Uruguay had set four changes to the 2012 abortion law as a goal for the administration, including raising the legal limit from twelve to fourteen weeks. Infobae reports that the changes cooled once Pope Leo XIV's visit was confirmed, while sexual and reproductive rights groups are pressing the authorities to proceed.

The case for moving now is the groups'. About ten organizations asked for meetings with Vice President Carolina Cosse, Public Health Minister Cristina Lustemberg, the National Human Rights Institution and the Bicameral Women's Caucus, the weekly Búsqueda reported. They want to set out what they see as limits in the voluntary-interruption law, which has not been amended since 2012, and activists say it is time to make procedures more flexible. A meeting a month ago among academics, NGOs and health authorities produced a consensus that the changes are necessary, El Observador reported.

The case for waiting comes from the governing Frente Amplio. Senators and deputies said it was not convenient to open the debate so near the visit. As Infobae's account puts their reason, they did not want to make waves or "buy a problem" for Orsi's government, given a visit that will matter to Catholics. Some officials in the executive say an abortion debate polarizes and that the moment before the papal visit is the wrong one. The Frente Amplio does not hold a single position. Leo XIV arrives on November 6 and stays through November 8, the first stop of a regional trip that also includes Argentina and Peru. In Uruguay he is to go to Montevideo, Florida and Paysandú.

Both sides start from the same statute: a 2012 law never modified since, and four changes the government itself had wanted, including the two-week extension. The organizations are seeking those meetings. The pope's dates are November 6 to 8.
