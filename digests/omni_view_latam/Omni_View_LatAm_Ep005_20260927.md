### Lead
**Brazil’s Debt Buyout Joins Up to US$8.2 Billion in Costs Not in 2027 Budget: The Rio Times**
The Rio Times reports that Brazil’s Desenrola 3.0 will buy old household debts from banks at discounts of ninety percent or more. With a betting ban and a rise in Bolsa Família, the package comes to as much as forty-two point three billion reais, about eight point two billion US dollars. Most of that cost falls in two thousand twenty-seven. The sum is not in the two thousand twenty-seven budget bill. The buyout is aimed at households whose old bank debts would be purchased; the budget gap is a hole in the accounts that do not yet show the money. ### Both Sides: When a school should expel a student
Ten days ago at unit two of Colégio Ícone, in Taquara, Rio de Janeiro, a physical-education teacher refereeing a football match marked a foul. Some students disagreed and physically attacked him. Police were called. The school suspended those involved, then a general council of the network decided they should not remain.

Fernando Venancio, the school’s maintainer and director-general, says three of the students had their enrollment locked, meaning they were expelled, under a scale — verbal and written warnings, suspensions, then closure of enrollment — explained when a student matriculates. He says the school suspended them at once, supported the teacher, reviewed the images, called a council, and summoned the families because they have a right to speak. The teacher is away and receiving the school’s support. The adolescents were summoned to testify at the thirty-second police station in Taquara, and the Guardianship Council is following the case. Venancio says the school will still, this year, review protocols for interclass games, hold a talk on competitiveness, and meet a representative of the Jacarepaguá-area Guardianship Council on school safety, relationships, and the consequences of acts.

Mônica Donetto Guedes, a psychoanalyst and parent guide, argues that violence is a limit that cannot be crossed or treated as normal, because settling conflict by force breaks the use of words. She distinguishes a fight between students, which she says the school should mediate, from an attack on a teacher, which she calls an attack on authority and on the rule. She argues that expulsion also shows the other students that aggression is not an accepted way to end a conflict, and that families have to help build limits.

Priscilla Montes, a specialist in neuroeducation and child development, argues that the productive question is which intervention protects the school community and still teaches, in proportion, not which punishment the student deserves. She says a bad act is not an identity, and that a purely punitive answer can deepen a student’s distance from school. She argues that a transfer does not end the school’s and the family’s responsibility, that schooling should continue, and that moving a student without a plan for the behavior can carry the problem somewhere else.

Natália Xavier, a pedagogue at Afya Unigranrio, argues that expulsion can be used when other strategies are not enough or when there is a concrete risk to the school community, and that a right to education is not tolerance of violence. She says the gravity has to be proved, and she lists serious physical assault, persistent threats, repeated bullying or cyberbullying, harassment, discrimination, carrying weapons, drug trafficking, or intentional destruction that puts people at risk. She says everyday indiscipline, low achievement, one-off conflicts, undone assignments, or family disagreements with the school do not justify expulsion, and that a student should not be punished for acts done only by the adults responsible for them.

Gabriel de Britto Silva, a lawyer, argues that expulsion must stay reasonable relative to the act, and that repeated disciplinary offenses, insubordination, disrespect of staff, and a serious insult can allow enrollment to be closed or not renewed only if the constitutional rights to a full defense and to answer the case are respected, under an administrative procedure based on an ethics code or the school’s internal rules.

The speakers accept that students physically attacked the teacher and that the school has to protect its community. Venancio’s next steps inside the school are the protocol review, the competitiveness talk, and the guardianship-council meeting, all still this year, while the police station and the Guardianship Council follow the case. ### Progress Watch
**Llegan 82 personas por día y las marcas quieren estar: cómo cambia Neuquén al ritmo de Vaca Muerta: La Nación**
La Nación, citing the Integrated Argentine Pension System, reports that Neuquén’s formal employment has risen by more than seven percent against November two thousand twenty-three, the fastest gain among the provinces, and that Neuquén, Río Negro, and San Juan are the only provinces generating formal jobs. The latest official Indec figures in the same report put Neuquén’s unemployment at three point two percent, against seven point nine percent nationally, and poverty at nineteen point six percent of the population, against thirty-two point three percent for the country.
