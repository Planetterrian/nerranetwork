**Woman dies outside Port Hardy Hospital: Surrey Now-Leader**
Courtney McCowan, 48, died outside Port Hardy Hospital on November 19, and her daughter, Hailey Schubert, says a friend brought her about 10 to 11 minutes after the emergency department’s daily 5 p.m. closing. Schubert told Surrey Now-Leader her mother arrived at 5:11 p.m., that staff would not let her friend in, and that a paramedic already in the parking lot started CPR while two more ambulances came; she said the hospital later refused a paramedic trying to bring her mother in, then allowed entry after an estimated 39 minutes, a timeline the paper noted is unconfirmed. The union for Ambulance Paramedics of B.C. told Global News a paramedic noticed McCowan about 10 minutes after the doors shut and that paramedics gave CPR for upwards of 45 minutes on the sidewalk; BC Emergency Health Services said she was transferred to hospital staff. Health Minister Ryan Peterson said Island Health and BC Emergency Health Services are investigating, that care providers and paramedics responded and gave emergency interventions, and that he will work so the review’s findings lead to more reliable emergency care. Island Health said people who arrive just after closing are assessed for immediate treatment or a transfer, and that emergency-trained physicians and nurses, along with paramedics, provided emergency treatment in this case. Schubert is calling for rural emergency departments to stay open 24 hours, and she said people in town fear that after 5 p.m. the next hospital is a 40-minute drive to Fort McNeill. **Man charged with second-degree murder in killing of B.C. actor Stewart McLean: North Shore News**
Nathan Neima, 35, of New Westminster, was arrested Saturday and charged with second-degree murder in the death of actor Stewart McLean, 45. He appeared Monday in provincial court in North Vancouver, with another procedural appearance set for January 6. McLean, whose credits include “Virgin River,” “Murder in a Small Town,” “Arrow” and “Travelers,” was reported missing on May 18 after he was last seen at his home in Lions Bay; Squamish RCMP said the Integrated Homicide Investigation Team took over after officers found evidence he was killed, and his remains were found days later in the Lions Bay area. Cpl. Esther Tupper said investigators believe the death was targeted, that the men were not strangers, that how they knew each other will be evidence in court, and that Neima does not have a criminal record. Actor and coach Jeff Seymour, speaking from Los Angeles, called the charge a “big relief” and said he was pleased police had pursued the case; he said McLean “didn’t cross lines” and was not involved in shady dealings, and that friends in Vancouver had not heard of the accused. Agent Jennifer Lee of Lucas Talent Inc. said she had worked with McLean since 2014 and that he was “the most positive, kind, funny, chill” person you could know. Tupper thanked Lions Bay residents and people in the film community, and said she hoped the charge brings some measure of peace. **East Vancouver park identified for potential new community centre with 50-metre pool and two ice rinks: Daily Hive Vancouver**
Park Board staff, in a report for the November 30 meeting, identified the north playing fields at Kensington Park as the better East Vancouver site for a new community centre with a 50-metre pool and an ice rink, ahead of a simpler replacement at Hastings Community Park. The Kensington idea is still preliminary, with major construction funding likely left to capital plans beyond 2030; building on the field north of the 1980 centre would let people keep using that centre and its 15-metre pool, which has no lap lanes, during construction. A first rink could come in a later phase, the old buildings would then be demolished for replacement green space, and a second rink could follow on that footprint. The park sits between Knight Street, East 33rd Avenue, Dumfries Street and East 37th Avenue. Staff recommend seeking 2027 capital-budget money to design a replacement for Hastings Community Centre, parts of which date to 1936 and which ranked third for renewal in the 2022 strategy, behind Kensington; new buildings there would have to sit back from Renfrew Creek. The city’s 2027–2030 capital plan sets aside $496 million for recreation facilities. A 2025 Auditor General report found 72 per cent of Vancouver’s community and recreation facilities were in poor or very poor condition in 2022. The Downtown Vancouver Aquatic Centre, one of two city 50-metre pools along with Hillcrest, closed this past summer for redevelopment as a 25-metre, eight-lane pool, and demolition resumed this month after a Court of Appeal injunction expired. Last week the board opened the $91-million Marpole Community Centre, its first new community centre in 14 years, and this November 30 meeting is the first held at Vancouver City Hall under a pilot, instead of the Stanley Park headquarters. ### The Closer Look
**A private partner could be suitable to help achieve SFU Burnaby Mountain gondola, says TransLink CEO: Daily Hive Vancouver**
Design work on TransLink’s gondola from SkyTrain’s Production Way-University Station to Simon Fraser University’s Burnaby Mountain campus is about 50 per cent complete. Chief executive Kevin Quinn told the Greater Vancouver Board of Trade on November 20 that a private partner could help get it built, and that TransLink still needs the provincial and federal governments to help pay for construction and to keep the service running. He said he wants to work with SFU and both governments to secure that funding.

The heavy-duty line would run straight for 2.7 kilometres and climb about 370 metres, using cabins of about 30 passengers, similar to Whistler Blackcomb’s Peak to Peak. Peak capacity would be 3,000 passengers an hour each way, about 66 per cent higher than the 99 B-Line, with possible frequencies below a minute and a seven-minute end-to-end trip. Earlier renderings showed a large indoor concourse at the base as a transfer hall with the SkyTrain station. Opening the gondola would change the mountain bus network, especially by discontinuing the No. 145 from Production Way-University. Quinn said the other gain is buses put back into service: “our bus depots are packed to the gills.”

In 2022, TransLink estimated construction at about $210 million and said the project could be built in about 18 months once funding, design, planning and procurement were done. It had aimed to begin construction in 2025 or 2026. Daily Hive reported that after years of inflation in labour, equipment and materials, that figure is now likely highly outdated, and that it does not include possible extra geotechnical and design costs identified since.

Asked whether a public-private partnership could help build more transit, Quinn said, “Sure, certainly, the gondola [and] some other projects could be good candidates for that type of a [public-private partnership] model.” He also said TransLink can already borrow at low rates by issuing bonds, which gives it less reason than some other authorities to seek private financing. Daily Hive noted that Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government has been encouraging private investment in capital projects.

Quinn pointed to Surrey as proof that elections can change transit plans. On November 9, Mayor Doug McCallum and the new Safe Surrey Coalition council unanimously approved pursuing an Expo Line extension along King George Boulevard from Surrey City Centre to Newton Town Centre, now a key city priority. The previous council approved the King George Boulevard bus rapid transit line in May 2026; that line, between Surrey City Centre and Semiahmoo Town Centre via Newton, is expected to cost about $700 million, and Daily Hive reported it is unclear what would happen to it. Premier Lorne Doerkson has promised to build the Newton SkyTrain extension and, after he and his cabinet were sworn in, said it was a commitment, not merely a proposal. He cited that pursuit as one reason he chose Surrey-Newton MLA Harj Dhaliwal as parliamentary secretary for TransLink. Quinn said the Mayors’ Council decides which projects take priority. The council held its first meeting of the new term last week and reappointed Port Coquitlam Mayor Brad West as chair; 14 of the 21 mayors are newly elected. Quinn expects the new councils in Maple Ridge and the Township of Langley to reaffirm support soon for a proposed 22-kilometre Langley-Haney Place bus rapid transit line across the Golden Ears Bridge, linking Haney Place with the future Willowbrook Station on the Surrey-Langley extension.

Asked whether a Millennium Line extension from the new Arbutus Station to UBC could advance in the next several years, Quinn directed the question to the new provincial government. Extending the line west from Arbutus remains a priority in the Mayors’ Council plan through 2035, but the province is leading the detailed technical design, planning and business case. The previous BC NDP administration completed a significant portion of that technical work and business case. Quinn called the UBC extension “very complex” and “very expensive.” Source: [dailyhive.com](https://dailyhive.com/vancouver/translink-sfu-burnaby-mountain-gondola-capital-projects-update-november-2026)

### Both Sides: Who must be consulted on the KSM mine
The B.C. Supreme Court has ordered the province to reconsider a 2024 ruling that the Seabridge Gold KSM project had substantially started, and to consult the Tsetsaut Skii km Lax Ha Nation. Global News describes KSM, in the Golden Triangle, as one of the world’s largest undeveloped gold reserves and says it would be Canada’s largest gold mine if it proceeds.

The case for that consultation, as put by Ryan Beaton of Power Law for the TSKLH Nation and Chief Darlene Simpson, is that the province already had evidence the proposed waste-facility area is TSKLH territory. He said a 2023 assessment found that all evidence suggested that, and that the court agreed the province disregarded its own conclusions, which amounted to a failure to consult adequately. The nation says it is not opposed to the project. Beaton said members work in mining, that a strong economy and good projects matter to them as to other British Columbians, and that they want this one done efficiently, responsibly and sustainably, with their rights honoured.

The case against treating TSKLH as a rights holder there comes from the Nisga’a Nation and the Tahltan Nation, who support the project and the substantial-start decision. They said they are the rights holders and decision makers for their territories, that they do not recognize TSKLH as holding any Aboriginal rights or title there, and that they reject TSKLH claims on KSM, which they say is in Tahltan territory and in the Nass Area and Nass Wildlife Area under the Nisga’a Final Agreement. They called such claims “opportunistic and unsupported,” and said B.C.’s wider consultation approach has increased claims by groups with unsupported, marginal or speculative claims, creating uncertainty and disrespecting treaty rights and proper rights and title holders. They said they are concerned that approach empowers groups who do not hold rights-holders’ obligations to hijack and derail project reviews.

The designation now goes back for reconsideration and consultation with TSKLH. The B.C. Mining Association estimates Golden Triangle projects will generate nearly $800 billion in economic activity over the next two decades.
