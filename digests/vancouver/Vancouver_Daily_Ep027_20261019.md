> **Two B.C. soldiers are among four killed in a CFB Wainwright fire.**

The Canadian Armed Forces has named the four, and Premier Danielle Smith said 39 soldiers were injured, 19 of them critically. Separately, four people went to hospital after a Saturday crash that closed 30 kilometres of the Coquihalla near Merritt.

### Top Stories
**Two soldiers from B.C. among four dead in CFB Wainwright fire: Vancouver Sun**
The Vancouver Sun reports that two of the four Canadian Armed Forces members killed in a fire at CFB Wainwright were from B.C. Major-General L.W. Rutland, Commander of 1st Canadian Division, said Sunday afternoon that three of the men were with the Third Battalion, Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry, based at CFB Edmonton, and that the fourth was from the Loyal Edmonton Regiment. The PPCLI soldiers killed are Pte. Aidan Goulding, Pte. Shaun Farrell and Pte. Hayden Lykkemark. The Loyal Edmonton member is Sgt. Morgan Wong, from Edmonton. Rutland said the four were killed during an outdoor exercise on a section attack range when a fire swept into the area. He said he spoke to his commander on the ground Saturday night and Sunday morning, and that commander said that within 30 seconds to a minute they saw no fire, and then there was a fire right on top of them. Premier Danielle Smith said Sunday afternoon that 39 soldiers were injured, including 19 critically. Acute Care Alberta said in a Sunday afternoon statement that 30 units, including ground ambulances, planes and helicopters, took patients to hospitals in Edmonton, Red Deer and Calgary. Farrell’s mother, Patricia, posted on Facebook that her son Shaun had died, thanking people for love, support and prayers. In a separate post Saturday night she had said Shaun was at CFB Wainwright taking part in the training and asked people to pray for him. A post earlier this year, the Sun reports, said Shaun had only recently joined the military. Kim Goulding posted that Aidan was bright, kind and proud to serve, that he had just graduated in August, and that he was way too young. The Loyal Edmonton Regiment is a reserve regiment. Wong, a University of Alberta graduate, had been with the Forces since 2017, according to his LinkedIn page, and in his day job over the past three years had worked in the Treasury Board Secretariat and in Hospital and Surgical Health Services. ### The Closer Look
**The Bookless Club: What are your 'deer in the headlights' encounters?: Vancouver Sun**
Jane Macdougall, writing in the Vancouver Sun, describes four Columbian black-tailed deer grazing on the wrong side of the road at Qualicum Beach. She identifies them as the native, compact subspecies of mule deer that inhabit Vancouver Island. They had been drawn, she writes, to a verdant strip of grass in a section of waterfront park. At some point they would have to cross a two-lane roadway back to a wooded slope. A Peterbilt truck rumbled past in the opposite direction. Swerving, let alone stopping, was not an option for that truck, she writes.
The column then sets that scene against provincial collision counts. Each year in B.C. there are about 12,500 wildlife collisions. More than 9,400 deer-and-vehicle collisions account for 80 per cent of those accidents. Such crashes result in close to 900 human injuries each year and an average of four fatalities. The cost to the provincial government for animal-related claims is more than $41 million, and Macdougall stresses that figure is annual.
She also recounts a family crash with a different animal. An uncle, driving home one night, broadsided a moose and spent weeks in hospital. His car was a write-off. A moose can weigh 1,500 pounds, or 680 kilograms, she writes, and when a driver hits one, the heavy body tends to launch onto the hood and through the windshield. She says two lives are oftentimes lost in that kind of collision. Not every wildlife crash is a direct hit on the animal. Sometimes the driver smashes into something other than the creature, and the outcome can be just as bad for the car and its occupants.
Macdougall writes that she used to wish rental cars in rural areas came with a deer whistle, an inexpensive, wind-activated ultrasonic whistle that attaches under a car and is meant to warn wildlife. There is no statistical evidence that these devices work, she writes. Wildlife may not react as hoped. How far the sound carries, whether that is enough time to brake, and whether wind or a nearby river drowns it out are open questions in the column. The only thing authorities advise, she writes, is to drive slower and with extreme caution at dusk and dawn, to always expect deer to be in clusters, and to brake rather than swerve if wildlife is on the road ahead. She lives in Vancouver and writes The Bookless Club on Saturdays in the Sun.
