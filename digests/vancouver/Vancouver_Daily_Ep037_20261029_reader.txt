Global BC reports this is the first fall in more than a century that British Columbia will not turn the clocks back.

Vancouver Coastal Health says the morning trip will be darker than usual, and that could put people off walking or biking to school or work.

They want bright colours, reflective gear, and eye contact with drivers. Drivers should slow down for the conditions.

Ralph Mistlberger, a psychology professor at Simon Fraser University, told Global News that people depend on morning light to set the body clock.

Push that light later, he said, and it gets easier to stay up and harder to wake, while a fixed workday does not move with it. He co-wrote a Canadian Medical Association Journal report on the risks of year-round daylight time.

Patrick Baylis, at the University of British Columbia’s School of Economics, said the hit is worse when hours are rigid and the start sits near the old sunrise, including a required eight a.m. start.

Penny Hill, a learning support teacher at Maple Green Elementary in Surrey, received a Dyslexia Canada Educational Excellence Award at a school assembly on October sixth.

Alicia Smith, executive director of Dyslexia Canada, came from Ottawa to present it. Angie Calleberg, who leads curriculum and assessment at the Ministry of Education and Child Care, sent congratulations.

The award is for teachers who push structured literacy, universal screening, effective intervention, and who help colleagues do the same.

Hill says the work started in her first year, when some students responded to her teaching and others did not. In two thousand three a workshop in California introduced her to dyslexia.

She remembers crying, and thinking she could help those kids. She started tutoring, then taught every student as if they were dyslexic, building what she calls metaphonological awareness.

That idea became her master’s thesis in special education, under Dr. Linda Siegel. Her line on explicit, systematic, evidence-based instruction is that it helps everyone, harms no one, and is crucial for some.

Screening for early reading trouble started at Maple Green when she joined. Staff use Acadience and DIBELS.

Former principal Jennifer Pilchak pushed lunch-and-learn sessions. Hill joked the real incentive was a free lunch.

She has spoken at conventions in Canada and abroad, guest lectured in university special-education classes, and now mentors a group of learning support teachers.

Principal Michael Speak said the award fits a career spent making sure students with dyslexia are seen, understood, and taught in ways that work for them.

Hill said she has knocked on policy doors for decades, and that more people now want to know how to lessen dyslexia’s effects. The award lands on screening, classroom method, and mentoring that already shape early reading at Maple Green and among the teachers she guides.
