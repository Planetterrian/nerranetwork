On grocery prices, the case on both sides is a campaign argument, not a law yet, and voting ends Saturday.

The Vancouver Sun reports the NDP and the Greens want the bill at the till to fall. The Conservatives say cut taxes instead, so people have more money for food.

NDP leader David Eby wants a cap on the mark-up for milk, eggs, cheese, butter, and poultry, aimed at corporate retailers.

He named Loblaws, Save-On-Foods, Walmart, Costco, and Sobeys, which runs Safeway.

The Greens would build government-owned grocery stores, twenty to start, with a one hundred million dollar investment.

Those stores would buy local and sell a basket of core goods thirty per cent under market prices.

Chris Bodnar, an assistant professor of agriculture at the University of the Fraser Valley, says milk is often a loss leader, priced thin or even at a loss to get people in the door.

A cap gets attention, he said, and it does not fix what made food expensive: climate, conflicts that raise fertilizer and fuel, and whether B.C. has the land and water to grow more.

The Sun cites a Harvard Business School study that found a small, temporary break on capped items, then higher prices on everything else.

It also says the International Monetary Fund warns that broad grocery controls send the wrong signals and can disrupt supply, and that cash transfers are the better tool.

Hungary has capped staple prices since twenty twenty-two, and in twenty twenty-five capped retail profit at ten per cent on more than thirty essential foods.

France, in twenty twenty-three, negotiated a freeze with the big chains instead of passing a law.

Tammara Soma, an associate professor at Simon Fraser University, says public stores would give shoppers and farmers another buyer.

She points to about two hundred forty U.S. military commissaries, where shoppers save about twenty-five per cent on average, and to Mexico's subsidized stores selling staples under the market price.

The five main grocers in B.C., she said, sometimes will not take produce that looks a bit off, and a public store could.

The Sun notes those public stores come with an ongoing cost.
