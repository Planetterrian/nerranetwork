**B.C. election sets record for lowest voter turnout: Global BC**
Elections BC’s preliminary figures show 48 percent of eligible voters cast a ballot, fewer than half, and about 10 percentage points below the 2024 provincial election. Stewart Prest, a political science lecturer at UBC, told Global News, “This is Mr. Eby’s low-turnout election to own, and this is going to be part of his legacy.” The NDP received more than 270,000 fewer votes than in 2024. The Conservatives received about 49,000 fewer. Prest said the early call looked politically opportunistic to some previous NDP supporters, and that the party did not present a positive vision on housing affordability and jobs. He said some Conservative voters, asked back to the polls two years after the last election, were motivated to send the governing party a message. Prest said people are more likely to vote when they believe the vote matters and when governments deliver on housing and jobs. **Victoria backs amalgamation, but Saanich voters reject becoming a single larger city with B.C.'s capital: Daily Hive Vancouver**
Saanich voters rejected becoming one municipality with Victoria, so the non-binding idea does not have the yes from both communities that Saanich said was required before any further work. In Saanich, 24,663 votes, 68 percent, were against, and 11,626, 32 percent, were in favour. In Victoria, 17,523 votes, 62 percent, were in favour, and 10,890, 38 percent, were against, among 28,413 responses. Both municipalities asked the same question on October 17, 2026: “Do you support Saanich and Victoria becoming one municipality?” Saanich had said a no in either community meant no further amalgamation work. The outgoing BC NDP government had said that even a yes in both places would not merge them: more information on costs, transition and services, plus consultation with area First Nations, would be needed before a binding referendum could be considered. In 2018, voters in both communities supported funding a citizens’ assembly. That assembly of 48 randomly selected residents recommended a merger in 2025, and its final report was presented that July. ### The Closer Look
**Vancouver International Airport records its busiest month ever with over 2.8 million passengers: Daily Hive Vancouver**
August 2026 was the first month in Vancouver International Airport’s history to reach and pass 2.8 million passengers, arrivals and departures combined. Daily Hive, using YVR’s latest official statistics, put the August total at 2.815 million. That beat July 2026, itself a record at 2.73 million, by about 83,000 passengers. Before this summer, the busiest month had been August 2018, at 2.71 million.

Compared with August 2025, when YVR recorded 2.65 million passengers, August 2026 was higher by about 168,000, or 6.3 percent. July and August together brought nearly 5.55 million passengers through the airport. WestJet labour disruptions in late July and early August affected both months. August alone averaged about 90,800 passengers a day. September’s final figures are due next month.

Domestic flights were the largest share of August traffic and a large part of the increase in raw numbers, from 1.33 million in August 2025 to 1.45 million, almost 119,000 more passengers, or 8.9 percent. International volumes outside the United States reached over 727,000, up 12.7 percent from about 645,000 in August 2025. Within that, Asia-Pacific routes accounted for about 441,000 passengers, up 18.6 percent. YVR has told Daily Hive it has seen a resurgence in volumes from Mainland China since last year, which it links to the strength of that market. European routes recorded another 240,000 passengers, up 5.2 percent from the same month in 2025.

U.S. transborder volumes moved the other way. They fell to about 635,000 in August 2026, from 668,000 in August 2025, a decline of more than 33,000 passengers, or about five percent. In August 2024, before the re-election of U.S. president Donald Trump, YVR saw 700,000 passengers on U.S. routes. For well over a year, some airlines have reduced frequencies or suspended Canada-U.S. routes and shifted capacity to other domestic and international flying. Air Canada confirmed last week that two seasonal routes linking YVR with Nashville and Raleigh will not return for summer 2027 as previously scheduled.

July’s volumes were driven in part by visitation for the 2026 FIFA World Cup. Growth in July and August was also supported by tourism demand, including a cruise season that brought 1.4 million passengers through the Canada Place terminal. August is typically one of YVR’s busiest months, with summer trips and students heading back for school. Over the first eight months of 2026, YVR recorded 18.92 million passengers, up 4.1 percent from 18.18 million in the same stretch of 2025, and the highest January-to-August total the airport has recorded. The full-year record is 26.9 million passengers in 2025. Daily Hive noted that if the rest of 2026 holds this pace, the annual total could exceed that mark. ### Both Sides: Repealing DRIPA
The B.C. Conservative government-elect says it will repeal the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act. The Union of B.C. Indian Chiefs says the law should stay and be used, not torn up. The act passed unanimously in the legislature in 2019. Premier-designate Lorne Doerkson said the day after the election that his party is committed to repealing it.

Doerkson’s case is that the law has not solved the conflicts it was meant to settle. He said, “The reality is it hasn’t solved anything.” He pointed to court fights over docks at Pender Harbour and over mining opportunities, and said the province needs to “work together for the betterment of all people in British Columbia.”

Grand Chief Stewart Phillip, president of the Union of British Columbia Indian Chiefs, calls DRIPA the roadmap to reconciliation and the way to recognize inherent title and rights. He said the chiefs will not stand idly by if the government-elect rips it up, and he called that “a declaration of war.” He framed the choice as whether British Columbia becomes “a war zone” or keeps doing “the challenging work of reconciliation.” The Union of B.C. Indian Chiefs urges the new government to use the act as the framework for that work rather than repeal it.

Doerkson said his party is contemplating a shortened fall session, so the repeal is not likely to reach the legislature until spring. First Nations groups are already stating the consequences they see if it does.
