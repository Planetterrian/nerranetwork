### Top Stories
**What advance voting numbers tell us about the B.C. election: Vancouver Sun**
Elections B.C. recorded 891,348 advance votes from October 16 to 21, about 24 per cent of registered voters, compared with 1,001,331 and about 28 per cent in 2024. CBC reports that advance voting is down 11 per cent from 2024. David Black, a political-science professor at Royal Roads University, told the Sun that advance turnout is not a reliable indicator of election-day turnout, and that a large undecided block plus a snap election called during a municipal campaign may have held numbers down. CityNews reports that Elections B.C. says ballots cast on election day Saturday will be counted by hand. Black said advance ballots, already tabulated electronically, are likely to be released sooner than Saturday's hand count. Advance turnout rose in Surrey-Serpentine River and in Vancouver-Quilchena, where OneBC leader Dallas Brodie is running, and fell across most of Surrey and Vancouver, including 22 per cent versus 24 per cent in Surrey and 22 per cent versus 27 per cent in Vancouver. ### City & Province
**Daniel Fontaine hopes to move on Metro Vancouver accountability charter: Vancouver Sun**
New Westminster mayor-elect Daniel Fontaine says he will push a five-point Metro Vancouver accountability charter after October 17, when 14 of 21 municipalities elected new mayors. West Vancouver mayor-elect Kirk LaPointe and White Rock mayor-elect Chris Shields have also signed. The charter calls for no extra pay for mayors on Metro's board and committees, a transparent review of cost overruns at the North Shore wastewater treatment plant, a smaller mayors' council, an auditor general for Metro and its municipalities, and an independent critical services review. Fontaine said Metro has 41 directors and has spread into housing, parks and an investment arm; he wants a review, not abolition, and will bring the charter to Metro if New Westminster council votes him onto the board. LaPointe said Metro should be smaller in mandate or directly elected, with oversight and more transparency. Coquitlam mayor-elect Craig Hodge has not signed but said he supports the reforms, that some are already being looked at, and that the public has lost confidence. ### The Closer Look
**Health care a major election issue as B.C. doctor says he's hit breaking point: Global BC**
Dr. Kevin McLeod, an internal medicine specialist at Lions Gate Hospital, says he has hit a breaking point and is seriously considering resigning from the hospital. He posted Thursday that doing a specialist consult on a complex patient in 20 minutes is not safe: if he takes longer, urgent care is delayed and the pile gets bigger. He told Global News this week has been especially difficult and is taking a toll on him and his family. On a typical call day, he said, he might have 20 to 22 new complex patients and another 35 to 50 people in an office. What tipped it, he said, was Sechelt phoning to send patients to Lions Gate because Sechelt hospital has no physician capacity.

Patients who need neurological care at Penticton Regional Hospital could see reduced access to specialist services after three neurologists resigned their hospital privileges. The resignations followed a change Interior Health introduced last month requiring neurologists to provide on-call services across the entire health region. Dr. Adam Thompson, president of Doctors of B.C., said there are always concerns when a facility loses doctors. He said the new on-call rule would substantially increase workload without additional support in their communities, that neurologists are already in short supply, and that there is a tipping point where those cases cannot all be juggled at once.

McLeod wants the next government to make people in highly paid administrative jobs accountable. He said the number of people in those admin roles has risen sharply, and he would not argue the system is being run more efficiently. With the provincial election days away, B.C. NDP leader David Eby promised to put minimum nurse-to-patient ratios into law so health authorities and hospital managers know where a government he leads would go. B.C. Nurses' Union president Adriane Gear said ratio legislation is a chance to build on progress already made, give patients and nurses more certainty, and support a planned expansion of ratios in emergency departments, operating rooms, long-term care, assisted living, and community and non-hospital care. She said safe staffing has to outlast party politics and stay a commitment no matter which party forms government. ### Both Sides: Grocery prices
The B.C. NDP and the B.C. Greens are offering plans to lower grocery costs in the provincial campaign. The B.C. Conservatives say tax cuts, not shelf-price rules, are the way to help people pay for food. Voters finish casting ballots on Saturday.

NDP leader David Eby wants a cap on the mark-up on essentials such as milk, eggs, cheese, butter and poultry, applied to corporate retailers. He singled out Loblaws, Save-On-Foods, Walmart, Costco and Sobeys, which operates Safeway. The Greens propose government-owned and government-managed grocery stores, starting with 20 stores and a $100 million investment, sourcing local products and selling a basket of core goods 30 per cent below market prices. The Conservatives say lowering taxes puts more money in people's pockets for groceries.

Chris Bodnar, an assistant professor of agriculture at the University of the Fraser Valley, says milk is often a loss leader, with thin or negative margins, used to pull shoppers toward other goods. He says a cap gets attention but does not solve the problem, and that it misses climate change, conflicts that raise fertilizer and fuel costs, and the land and water B.C. needs to grow more food. The Vancouver Sun cites a Harvard Business School study that found small, temporary relief on capped items while major grocers raised prices on other products, and says the International Monetary Fund advises against broad grocery price controls because they send the wrong signals and can disrupt supply, preferring cash transfers. Hungary has capped staples since 2022 and, in 2025, capped retail profit at 10 per cent on more than 30 essential foods. France in 2023 negotiated a price freeze with major chains instead of legislating one.

Tammara Soma, an associate professor at Simon Fraser University, says public stores would give shoppers and farmers another option. She points to about 240 U.S. military commissaries, where shoppers save an average of 25 per cent, and to Mexico's subsidized stores selling staples below market prices. She says the five main B.C. grocers sometimes will not buy produce that looks slightly off, and a public outlet could take that food. The Sun notes that government-run stores carry an ongoing cost.
