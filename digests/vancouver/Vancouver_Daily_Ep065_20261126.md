### Top Stories
**Falkland fentanyl lab property forfeited to the B.C. government: Vancouver Sun**
A B.C. Supreme Court judge last week signed a consent order forfeiting the 66-hectare property at 5011 Hoath Rd. in Falkland to the province's director of civil forfeiture. A lawyer for owner Michael Driehuyzen, an Abbotsford electrician who was never charged, signed the order; five per cent of the net proceeds of any sale goes to him as partial relief. He had claimed he was only the landlord and knew of no illegal activity. The government's claim said that between January and Oct. 25, 2024, the site was used to produce, store and process illicit drugs; officers seized 52 kilograms of fentanyl and several tonnes of precursor chemicals, and a senior Mountie said at the time that those materials could have produced 95 million doses. Gaganpreet Randhawa, the second defendant, pleaded guilty in September to attempting to smuggle 310 kilograms of methamphetamine to the Philippines and admitted possessing fentanyl, carfentanil, cocaine and MDMA, plus a prohibited Glock while under a lifetime firearms ban; he is to be sentenced in January. None of those convictions were specifically about the Falkland lab, though a Nov. 5, 2026 filing says police saw him frequenting the property, and he never responded to the lawsuit. Postmedia News earlier reported that people connected to the Wolfpack gang alliance had set up the lab. A Public Safety Ministry spokesperson confirmed Wednesday that the property claim is settled while claims over personal property and equipment continue. The assessment is now $883,000, down $55,000 from $938,000, and the RCMP estimated it spent at least $1 million cleaning up the site. **As B.C. child killer leaves prison, mother of victim calls for changes to system: Vancouver Sun**
The Parole Board of Canada has granted Shane Ertmoed a two-month unescorted absence, starting Dec. 1, to take cooking classes while living at a community residential facility in the Victoria area. He is serving a life sentence, imposed in 2002 with no parole for 25 years, for the abduction, sexual assault and murder of 10-year-old Heather Thomas in Cloverdale in October 2000. This is his first unescorted absence, and his case management team did not support it. The board said the absence will let him work on risk-related issues outside prison, noted he has taken arousal-reducing medication for about a year, and set conditions: four and a half hours a day, five days a week, with a Red Seal chef, escorted by staff or volunteers whenever he leaves the facility, and no leave privileges. Heather's mother, Jody Aspin, said she keeps asking who he will hurt next and that yearly requests have kept her from healing. Lawyer Rebecca Darnell said the family should never have had to hear his name again. Conservative MP Frank Caputo, who has met Aspin and Darnell, wants federal law changed so full parole cannot be sought until half a sentence, rather than the typical one-third, and so applications come every five years instead of every two. ### The Closer Look
**B.C. premier moves to end safe supply, expand treatment and toughen enforcement: Daily Hive Vancouver**
Premier Lorne Doerkson has directed Health Minister Ryan Peterson and Public Safety and Solicitor General Jessy Sahota to end the provincial safe-supply approach and put the emphasis on treatment, recovery and, where he says it is appropriate, involuntary care. Mandate letters dated Nov. 24, 2026 tell them to end that approach to drug use, expand drug courts, move funding toward prevention, treatment and recovery, stop forcing drug-friendly facilities on communities, and review and reform overdose prevention sites and injection sites. The letters set no date for ending safe supply and do not say how people who now receive those prescriptions would move to other care.

The prescription program expanded in July 2021, when the province widened access to drugs of known strength for people at high risk of overdose, hoping to cut reliance on the street supply. In 2025 the Ministry of Health said emerging evidence showed hydromorphone, the most commonly prescribed alternative, did not adequately ease withdrawal or replace street drugs for many patients. A review by the provincial health officer found some patients shared hydromorphone with people who could not get a prescription, and others used it to obtain drugs they preferred. The former BC NDP government later tightened the rules. It had already asked Ottawa, in May 2024, to restore police authority over small-amount possession of certain illicit drugs in most public places, and it let the decriminalization pilot expire in January 2026. Critics had accused that government of tolerating open drug use and doing too little for residents and businesses.

Peterson is to expand detox and residential treatment, improve access to psychiatric care, and work with Local Government, Housing, Sport and Culture Minister Cathie Ounsted on more housing during recovery. Doerkson also wants better follow-up after treatment and closer coordination among police, mental-health workers and clinical teams when someone is in crisis. He has ordered an independent review of the Mental Health Act, including how it applies to people with brain injuries, schizophrenia or dementia, and he wants involuntary care for people who cannot protect themselves or face a serious risk of harm.

In Vancouver's October 2026 civic election, 68 per cent of voters backed asking the province to turn part of the old St. Paul's Hospital campus on Burrard Street, or a similar site, into a secure facility with at least 100 involuntary beds plus voluntary beds. Nearly 69 per cent supported stronger municipal laws against open-air use of hard drugs. Both questions were non-binding. The new left-leaning Vancouver council has signalled it does not intend to act on either result.

Sahota is to work with Attorney General Caroline Elliott on a dedicated court stream for repeat offenders, tighter enforcement of bail conditions and outstanding warrants, and wider electronic monitoring. Drug courts would grow. In the existing Drug Treatment Court of Vancouver, eligible people who plead guilty to offences tied to addiction can enter treatment under a judge. Five-year staffing targets in the letters include 250 additional police recruits, 500 more RCMP officers, 30 sheriffs and 150 civilian staff, plus broader powers against street crime, gangs and money laundering, specialized extortion investigators, and published figures on repeat offending, bail compliance, court delays, organized crime and community safety. A Greater Vancouver Board of Trade survey earlier this year, before the civic and provincial elections, listed street disorder, vandalism, and the safety of staff and customers among leading business concerns. ### Both Sides: Public access to Crown land
Premier Lorne Doerkson has directed Environment Minister A'aliya Warbus to bring legislation restoring public access to Crown land, beaches and the backcountry for all British Columbians, subject only to legitimate conservation and safety requirements. The Nov. 24, 2026 mandate letter would turn a Conservative campaign promise into a bill expected in the spring 2027 session. The sharpest example is Joffre Lakes Provincial Park near Pemberton.

The case for a statutory right of access, as Doerkson and his party put it, is that a provincial park is meant to stay open. Scott McInnis, then a Conservative MLA and now Minister of Indigenous Relations and Reconciliation, told the legislature in May 2025 that Joffre Lakes is a tourism draw for Pemberton and Whistler, that the parks website says it is open year-round, and that the previous government was allowing a public park to be closed for nearly a third of the year. In 2026 the former BC NDP government closed the park to the general public for 31 days, June 20 to 27 and Sept. 8 to 30, except for members of the Lilwat and N'Quatqua First Nations. Closures since a late-August 2023 notice of about five weeks for a harvest celebration also cancelled day-use passes and camp reservations made months ahead. Nearly 200,000 people visited in 2019; since 2021 a day-use pass has held peak-season visits to 500 a day.

The case for exclusive periods, as the Lilwat and N'Quatqua First Nations assert it, is that the closures are necessary for their cultural practices. The previous provincial government also cited the need to protect land at one of B.C.'s busiest hiking and camping sites. In 2025 the nations sought exclusive access from Aug. 22 to Oct. 23; the province set a shorter fall closure, Sept. 2 to Oct. 3, and the nations objected. The access bill is expected in the spring 2027 legislative session. Source: [dailyhive.com](https://dailyhive.com/vancouver/bc-government-crown-public-lands-access-legislation-mandate-november-2026)
