That acre is city land at 722 Gore Avenue, the northeast corner of Gore and Union, on the southeast edge of Chinatown. Daily Hive reports that on Tuesday, November 17, council locked in permanent park status for roughly 31,000 square feet. Until then the triangle was still booked as road, leftover right of way for a highway ramp in the old freeway plan between downtown and Highway 1.

The Dunsmuir and Georgia viaducts were the only piece of that plan that got built. The Park Board now controls the grass. Crews have looked after it for decades, and the trees include a giant sequoia. In 2024 the city wanted a temporary fire hall there so Firehall Number 2 could handle Downtown Eastside calls. Neighbours fought it. Staff pulled the plan and built at 1756 East Hastings instead.

Fire Hall Number 11 opened there in spring 2025, and there is a separate direction to rebuild 496 Alexander Street as a larger permanent Fire Hall 2. Pete Fry, then a councillor and now mayor, asked in October 2024 how the city could make 722 Gore a park, and the strip of green north to Keefer Street as well. Park Board commissioner Tom Digby had a similar motion pass in September 2024.

Tuesday's vote covers only the triangle, not that strip. Fry said people in Strathcona, Chinatown and the Downtown Eastside have used the space for years, and protecting it keeps it there. Digby, the board chair, called it a joint effort of the board and council. No name and no improvements are planned.

Taking permanent park status away later would need at least 66 per cent of all elected members on both council and the Park Board. The grass sits just north of the new St. Paul's Hospital, due to open in February 2027.

Amazon's shift deeper into The Post emptied about 250,000 square feet in two other downtown towers in the third quarter, Daily Hive reports from CBRE. About 160,000 square feet came open in July at Telus Garden, 510 West Georgia, a 24-storey tower where that space is about 29 per cent of the offices and can now be leased from the landlord. Amazon's first big Vancouver office opened there in 2015.

Another 90,000 square feet, about a quarter of The Exchange at 475 Howe, is up for sublease through 2035. CBRE says that one move took downtown leased-office vacancy from 12.2 per cent to 13.1 per cent, the highest in the city centre in more than 22 years. Metro Vancouver as a whole went from 11.7 to 12.2.

Outside downtown the change was 11.2 to 11.3, and downtown has now sat above the suburban market for six quarters. Occupied space across the region fell by about 259,000 square feet. CBRE figures the region would have held steady without Amazon, and local vacancy is still under the national average of 16.7 per cent.

Filling those big floors will take time, CBRE says, and later it sees a possible shortage of premium downtown space, because today's rents do not cover the cost of a new downtown tower. It expects no new city-centre supply before 2032 at the earliest. All 492,000 square feet under construction in the region at the end of September sat outside downtown.

Those two empty Amazon floors may go into Vancouver's private-sector bid for the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank headquarters, with formal city bids due in early December. Surrey Police Service has a new member who does not write reports. The Vancouver Sun says Nyx, a two-year-old black Labrador, is trained to find hidden electronics that might hold evidence. Her title is electronic storage detection dog, and police say she is the first at a municipal force in British Columbia. She will work with the cybercrime team.

Sergeant Ali Gailus said in a Tuesday release that dogs like her are trained to detect chemical compounds in cellphones, USB sticks, GPS trackers and memory cards, so investigators can find devices that were deliberately hidden. Deputy Chief Constable Mike Procyk said those devices can hold important evidence and are hard to locate once concealed, and that Nyx is another way to recover it.

Indiana-based Jodan Detection K9 trained Nyx and Sergeant Emily Ovington. The money came from a gang violence action fund, coordinated with the province. Police say she has already finished six successful searches, and they say digital crime is still rising.

In New Westminster, a Victorian Gothic house at 239 Fourth Street, in Queen's Park, was built in 1888, and a neighbour is asking the new council to keep it standing. The Vancouver Sun says Marnie Ford fell for the house on night walks, and when the for-sale sign appeared she went looking for a way to save it.

In October she asked council either to buy and restore it or to help a group of people do that. She said the councillors she met were supportive, and she is waiting to see if this council feels the same. Mayor Daniel Fontaine and five New Westminster Progressives councillors were elected last month on financial restraint. Only one councillor outside that slate won a seat.

The corner lot was listed in August at 1.425 million dollars and can be bought now for 1.25 million. Samuel Boisvert, a director at heritage firm Luxton and Associates, said the firm was hired to write a heritage values assessment. The owner is the estate of William Shordee, who lived there from 1970 until his death in 2025.

Boisvert said the city usually asks for this kind of assessment when someone wants to develop a property older than 100 years. The house is on the 1986 heritage inventory and is protected under the Queen's Park heritage conservation area, adopted in 2017. It got a water hookup in 1893.

Boisvert links the building boom to the Canadian Pacific Railway's arrival in 1886 and a branch line into New Westminster from Westminster Junction, now Port Coquitlam. Between 1888 and 1892 the city's population more than doubled, to 7,500. The original lot owner was Methodist minister Edward White, who arrived in 1859. The lot was once much larger and was subdivided over time.

Boisvert calls the house a rare Victorian Gothic, drawing on English and medieval styles. Rupinder Basi, deputy director of planning and development, said the owner has asked about demolition options, and the city has no further information about a development. The community heritage commission can advise on protection. That advice does not bind council. Ford's request is still a request.

The new council has not said whether it will buy the house, help a private group buy it, or leave it alone.
