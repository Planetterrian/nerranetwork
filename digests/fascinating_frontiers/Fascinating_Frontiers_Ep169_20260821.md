### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Teams Complete Flight Readiness Review for NASA’s Roman Telescope** — NASA Science
   Mission managers from NASA, the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope team, and SpaceX finished the Flight Readiness Review at Kennedy Space Center. The review clears the path for the telescope’s upcoming launch on a SpaceX vehicle. The observatory will map wide areas of the sky to study dark energy, exoplanets, and infrared astrophysics. Engineers confirmed all spacecraft systems and integration steps met requirements before the final pre-launch checks. The next milestone is the targeted launch window later this year.

2. **Stellar Eruptions in the Laboratory: First Experimental Evidence** — Phys.org Space
   An international team of astrophysicists and plasma physicists produced the first lab evidence that strong magnetic fields can fully suppress coronal mass ejections on active stars. The work explains why such eruptions appear rare on stars other than the Sun despite similar surface activity. Researchers recreated stellar magnetic conditions in a plasma device and observed complete suppression once field strength crossed a measured threshold. The result gives a physical mechanism for the observed difference in stellar behavior. Follow-up experiments will test how varying field geometries change the suppression threshold.

3. **Stellar Spin May Explain Why Repeated Black Hole Flares Grow Dimmer** — Phys.org Space
   Repeated flares from supermassive black holes at galaxy centers show a consistent pattern of decreasing brightness over successive events. New modeling ties this dimming to the spin of the surrounding stellar population and how it interacts with the black hole’s accretion flow. The study links the observed flare sequence to changes in the angular momentum of material feeding the black hole. Data from multiple galaxies show the same dimming trend once spin parameters are included. Further observations will test whether spin rates measured by other methods match the flare-decay predictions.

4. **Interplanetary Spacecraft Capture a Coronal Mass Ejection Component Hidden from Earth** — Phys.org Space
   During a recent solar eruption, spacecraft positioned away from the Sun-Earth line recorded plasma and magnetic structures that Earth-based instruments could not see. The data reveal how coronal mass ejections evolve differently depending on the observer’s location in the inner solar system. Measurements include changes in magnetic field orientation and plasma density that only appear off the Sun-Earth axis. The findings improve models used to forecast whether an eruption will reach Earth and with what intensity. Additional multi-spacecraft campaigns are planned for the current solar maximum.

5. **Luna 24: The Last Soviet Lunar Sample Return Mission – 50 Years Ago** — r/space
   Fifty years have passed since the Soviet Luna 24 probe returned the final lunar samples collected by the USSR. The spacecraft landed in Mare Crisium, drilled 2.25 meters into the regolith, and sealed the core sample for return to Earth. The 170-gram sample provided new data on lunar mantle composition and volcanic history. Archival studies continue to yield insights as modern instruments re-analyze the material. No subsequent Soviet or Russian mission has repeated a lunar sample return.

6. **Aug. 21, 1914: Total Solar Eclipse Tensions** — Astronomy
   On the eve of World War I, astronomers across Europe prepared for a total solar eclipse that offered a rare test of Einstein’s newly proposed general relativity. Erwin Findlay-Freundlich of Berlin Observatory led an expedition to Crimea to measure star positions near the Sun during totality. The outbreak of war halted the effort and prevented the planned photographic plates from being exposed. The missed opportunity delayed the first empirical confirmation of light bending by gravity until 1919. The episode remains a notable intersection of science and geopolitics.

7. **UF Astraeus Space Institute Launches New Hub** — University of Florida
   The University of Florida has opened the Astraeus Space Institute to coordinate research across engineering, planetary science, and space policy. The institute will serve as a central point for faculty and student projects involving spacecraft instrumentation, orbital debris studies, and mission concept development. Initial focus areas include small-satellite technology and lunar surface operations. The ribbon-cutting marks the formal start of coordinated university-wide space efforts.

8. **Ancient Stars Dazzle in a Celestial Chandelier** — Space.com
   The Chandelier Cluster presents a dense collection of ancient stars whose combined light creates a striking visual effect in deep-sky images. Observations show the cluster’s stars formed early in the Milky Way’s history and have since migrated to their current location. Color-magnitude diagrams place the population among the galaxy’s oldest known groupings. The image highlights both the cluster’s compact core and its extended halo of fainter members.

9. **In 1972, Apollo 16 Astronaut John Young Jumped into the Air** — The Times of India
   During the Apollo 16 mission, astronaut John Young performed a salute while jumping on the lunar surface, producing one of the most widely reproduced photographs from the Moon. The low gravity allowed a higher jump than would be possible on Earth, and the image captured the full arc of the motion. The photograph remains a key visual record of human activity on another world. Archival scans continue to reveal fine details of the spacesuit and equipment.

10. **The COSMOSIS Framework Can Ensure Adaptive Space Governance** — SpaceNews
    A new policy paper outlines the COSMOSIS framework as a way for U.S. agencies to manage space activities as a complex adaptive system. The approach draws on recent White House and Office of Space Commerce documents that recognize the interconnected nature of orbital traffic, spectrum use, and debris mitigation. It proposes iterative rulemaking that can adjust as new operators and technologies emerge. The framework aims to avoid rigid regulations that quickly become outdated.

11. **America’s Launch Boom Could Reshape Texas** — Corpus Christi Caller-Times
    Increasing launch activity along the Texas coast is prompting local governments to assess infrastructure and environmental planning needs. SpaceX Starship development at Boca Chica continues to drive the largest share of activity, with additional operators expressing interest in the region. State officials are evaluating road, port, and power upgrades required to support higher flight rates. The changes could alter the economic profile of several coastal counties over the next decade.

12. **Rocket Launches Are Increasing in US** — El Paso Times
    The national rise in orbital launch attempts is concentrating in established ranges while new sites in Texas and elsewhere prepare to enter service. Data show the United States conducted more launches in the past year than any previous comparable period. Local communities near active ranges are updating emergency and environmental response plans. Industry analysts expect the trend to continue as both government and commercial missions multiply.

13. **QPS-SAR-18 Cataloged in Expected Orbit** — Jonathan McDowell (orbital launch tracker)
    The Japanese QPS-SAR-18 satellite has been tracked in the 569 by 582 kilometer orbit at 42 degrees inclination that Rocket Lab targeted. The cataloging confirms the Electron launch delivered the spacecraft to the planned parameters without post-deployment maneuvers. The satellite joins a growing constellation of private synthetic-aperture radar imagers.

14. **2025-313C and D Possibly Marafon Satellites** — Jonathan McDowell (orbital launch tracker)
    Two objects from a recent launch have settled into 494 by 703 kilometer orbits at 97.6 degrees and have not performed maneuvers. Analysts identify them as likely candidates for the Russian Marafon communications satellites. The identity of a third object from the same launch remains unclear pending further tracking.

15. **2025-276EE and EF in Non-Sun-Synchronous Orbits** — Jonathan McDowell (orbital launch tracker)
    Two additional objects from an earlier mission are now confirmed in 532 by 1213 kilometer and 526 by 706 kilometer orbits at 97.4 degrees. Their local-time planes sit near 07:40 and 09:29 but fall short of true sun-synchronous conditions. The measurements help refine models of upper-stage disposal and payload separation dynamics.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Laboratory Plasmas Reveal Stellar Eruption Suppression
Picture a magnetic field strong enough to thread an entire star yet generated within a volume no larger than a city block; that is the scale laboratory teams now recreate to study other suns. Inside the experimental chamber, a pulse of current first ionizes a low-density gas, then rapidly ramps an external coil to tens of tesla, squeezing the plasma into thin filaments that mimic a star’s corona. When the field crosses a critical threshold, any budding ejection is pinched off before it can expand, exactly as the models predicted. The surprising number is the measured cutoff: once the field reaches roughly 20 tesla at the surface, the ejection mass drops below detectable limits within milliseconds. Riding alongside one of those filaments, an observer would see the outward velocity reverse in less than a microsecond as magnetic tension overwhelms thermal pressure. Yet the same apparatus shows that slightly weaker or more tangled fields allow partial eruptions, leaving open the question of why real stars with measured fields near this boundary still display occasional flares while others remain silent.
