### Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **ExoMars spots hydrogen chloride over Martian fissures — European Space Agency**
   The ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter detected hydrogen chloride above volcanic fissures in the Alba Mons region. The gas appeared in early northern summer, outside the usual seasonal pattern tied to dust and water cycles. Fresh narrow fissures hundreds of meters long showed color changes consistent with deposits from escaping gases. Researchers link the release to hot magma beneath the surface. The finding suggests Mars retains enough internal heat for volcanic activity until recently or still today. An international team led by Alexander Trokhimovskiy and co-lead author Daniel Mège analyzed data from the Atmospheric Chemistry Suite infrared spectrometers and the CaSSIS camera. The team ruled out atmospheric chemistry cycles after examining all possible chemical processes. They concluded the seepage connects to a dormant magma chamber beneath the surface. The discoloration around the fissures matches deposits seen around fumaroles on Earth. The Trace Gas Orbiter spotted the handful of molecules while continuously monitoring the whole Martian atmosphere from orbit. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/esa/status/2118263144996905025) Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-european-orbiter-fresh-hints-volcanic.html)

2. **NASA’s Swift observatory reenters over Indonesia — Qazinform**
   The 1,450-kilogram Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory reentered at 7:39 a.m. EST over northern Sumatra. The spacecraft ended 22 years of gamma-ray burst and supernova observations after an attempted orbit boost failed. Most of the craft was expected to burn up, with debris injury risk estimated at one in 2,100. Its final observation occurred October 20. The observatory was launched in November 2004 for an initial two-year mission but operated nearly two decades beyond that. It was renamed in 2018 to honor principal investigator Neil Gehrels. Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of NASA’s Astrophysics Division, noted that Swift delivered an incredible return on investment. The reentry followed a private spacecraft attempt in July that lost control and could not raise the orbit. Source: [qazinform.com](https://qazinform.com/news/nasas-swift-observatory-falls-back-to-earth-after-2-decades-in-space-234715)

3. **NASA unveils X-70 Fractal experimental aircraft — The Aviationist**
   The crewed X-70 Fractal will test rapidly reconfigurable flight-control technology. It cruises at Mach 0.75, reaches 45,000 feet, and offers more than 1,500 nautical miles range. Takeoff weight is 10,000 pounds, with up to 2,000 pounds for internal or external payloads. First flight is expected within two years. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman announced the aircraft at the UP.Summit at Kennedy Space Center. The modular design allows rapid swaps of internal and external payloads. Laurie Grindle, director of the Aeronautics Division, said the X-70 joins a storied legacy of X-planes that advanced American aviation. The aircraft will explore maneuverability in ways never before demonstrated in crewed flight. Isaacman noted the science fiction future imagined as children is ours to build. Source: [theaviationist.com](https://theaviationist.com/2026/11/05/nasa-announces-x-70-fractal/) Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-announces-x-70-experimental-aircraft-to-advance-flight-research/)

4. **NASA awards Ka-band communications contracts — Aviation Week**
   Three firms received contracts worth up to $1.065 billion to develop commercial Ka-band relay services. Astranis was awarded up to $264.3 million, Global Enterprises Solutions up to $340.4 million, and Viasat up to $460.9 million. The work will replace the aging Tracking and Data Relay Satellite fleet. Each firm-fixed price contract gives NASA options to advance from concept development to orbital flight demonstration. The initial performance period extends through May 2027, with further options through 2029. The selections support NASA’s decision to retire the TDRS network and adopt commercial services. Seven TDRS satellites remain active supporting the International Space Station and Hubble until decommissioning. 6. **Starfish Otter spacecraft loses contact — Aviation Week**
   The Otter spacecraft lost its communications link during commissioning. The loss was traced to an issue in the fault protection system. The mission was testing propulsion, navigation, and inspection technologies for orbital debris under a $15 million NASA SBIR Phase III agreement. The spacecraft launched October 1 from Vandenberg Space Force Base. Technologies integrated included the Manta robot arm, Nautilus docking mechanism, Cetacean navigation software, and Cephalopod guidance software. NASA and Starfish determined the two-way link could not be re-established. Future Otter missions will continue gathering data on in-space servicing technologies. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Backlit Imaging Reveals Hidden Activity on Icy Moons
Imagine standing on the night side of Saturn and looking toward the Sun with a 500-kilometer ball of ice hanging between you and the light. That is the perspective Cassini had on November 1, 2009 when it captured Enceladus in silhouette. The geometry turns the moon into a dark disk edged by brilliant sunlight, and any material leaving its surface becomes visible as bright streaks against the black of space. Enceladus is only 500 kilometers across, yet its south-polar plumes stretch hundreds of kilometers into the vacuum, feeding particles directly into Saturn’s outermost E ring. The same image shows 80-kilometer Pandora glowing faintly from Saturnlight reflected off the planet’s icy rings, a reminder that even small moons can be lit by their parent world when the Sun is behind them. Cassini’s cameras recorded the scene while the spacecraft itself moved at several kilometers per second, yet the long exposure still froze the plumes in place because the particles were moving outward at roughly the escape speed from Enceladus’ weak gravity. The surface itself appears as reflective as fresh snow, an effect produced by the constant rain of tiny ice grains falling back from the plumes. Scientists first spotted these jets in 2005 and quickly realized they could not be explained by sunlight alone; the only plausible source was liquid water beneath the ice shell kept warm by tidal flexing from Saturn. The 2009 backlit view remains one of the clearest demonstrations that the activity is ongoing and localized at the south pole. What still puzzles researchers is why the plumes appear in discrete jets rather than a uniform sheet, and whether the fractures that feed them open and close with Enceladus’ orbital cycle around Saturn. Future missions equipped with mass spectrometers and higher-resolution imagers could sample the plume material directly and map the fractures in three dimensions, but until then the silhouette images remain the most direct evidence that this small moon is venting its interior ocean into space.
