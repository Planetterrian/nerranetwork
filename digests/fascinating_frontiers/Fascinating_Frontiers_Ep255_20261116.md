1. **Aging stars show a sudden luminosity dip during red-giant expansion** — Phys.org Space
   Low-mass stars like the Sun exhaust core hydrogen and ascend the red-giant branch, growing brighter while cooling and expanding.
   A temporary decrease in luminosity interrupts this steady rise and appears in color-magnitude diagrams of stellar clusters as the RGB luminosity bump.
   In RGB stars the inert helium core contracts while the hydrogen-burning shell advances outward and the convective envelope deepens.
   When the base of the convective zone reaches layers whose composition was altered by earlier burning, dredge-up mixes that material and changes the envelope’s entropy profile.
   The entropy difference across the boundary layer eventually drops below a critical value, triggering the observed luminosity dip.
   Models place the bump at higher luminosities than observations record, so the entropy mechanism is being tested to reconcile the timing mismatch. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-aging-stars-sudden-luminosity-dip.html)

2. **Euclid satellite uncovers 15,000 new strong gravitational-lens candidates** — Phys.org Space
   The first major data release from ESA’s Euclid telescope covers 1.7 million gigabytes and maps almost 5 percent of the sky.
   Analysis by more than 2,000 scientists identified over 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasars.
   Strong gravitational lenses form when a foreground galaxy bends light from a more distant background galaxy into arcs or Einstein rings.
   Before Euclid fewer than 1,000 confirmed strong lenses were known; the new catalog adds 15,000 candidates, the largest collection assembled.
   The lenses act as natural magnifying glasses that reveal the distribution of invisible dark matter in the foreground galaxies.
   Citizen scientists and machine-learning pipelines helped refine the candidate list before expert verification with follow-up observations. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-dark-matterhunting-satellite-euclid-reveals.html)

3. **Reanalysis casts doubt on an outsize early-universe black hole in galaxy UHZ1** — Phys.org Space
   Earlier claims suggested UHZ1 hosted a heavily dust-obscured black hole formed by direct collapse and already massive when the universe was young.
   New X-ray data combined with fresh infrared observations show no detectable hidden black-hole activity.
   The galaxy is now interpreted as a low-mass, metal-poor, star-forming system rather than a quasar host.
   The reanalysis appears in The Astrophysical Journal. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-evidence-outsize-black-hole-early.html)

   The data reveal physical and chemical differences among the cores that trace the progression of massive-star formation. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-alma-reveals-star-cores-stages.html)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Why aging stars briefly dim
Low-mass stars like the Sun spend billions of years on the main sequence steadily fusing hydrogen in their cores. Once that fuel is exhausted, the core contracts, the outer layers expand, and the star ascends the red-giant branch, growing larger, cooler, and more luminous. Observations of star clusters show that this steady brightening is interrupted by a short-lived dip known as the RGB luminosity bump. The dip appears because the deepening convective envelope eventually reaches layers whose composition was altered by earlier nuclear burning. When that material is mixed outward, the specific entropy difference across the base of the convective zone shrinks. Once the entropy jump falls below a critical threshold, the star’s luminosity temporarily decreases before resuming its climb. Models place the bump at higher luminosities than real clusters display, so the entropy mechanism is now being examined to explain why the observed feature occurs earlier than predicted. The process is entirely internal; no external trigger is required. What remains unknown is the precise entropy value at which the boundary layer becomes unstable and how that value changes with stellar mass and metallicity. Measuring the depth and timing of the bump in large samples of clusters with known ages would pin down that threshold and bring models into closer agreement with the data. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-aging-stars-sudden-luminosity-dip.html)
