Now we turn from active orbital operations to how we discover distant worlds.

Archived data from NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite revealed a Jupiter-mass world whose gravity briefly magnified light from a background star.

The event marks the spacecraft’s initial use of microlensing rather than its usual transit method.

The planet, designated HD 126105 b in related catalog entries, orbits at 1.36 AU from its K-type host.

The technique opens a new search channel for worlds that do not cross their stars from TESS’s line of sight.

Follow-up observations from ground telescopes are planned to refine the mass and orbit.

If the Sun were replaced by a bowling ball on a vast frozen lake, a passing star would bend the surrounding ice just enough to focus light from a distant object into a brief, bright flash visible from shore.

That flash is gravitational microlensing: the foreground mass warps spacetime so that light rays from a background source converge toward the observer for a few hours or days.

In the TESS case the lens was a Jupiter-mass planet roughly 1.4 AU from its host star, and the brightening lasted long enough for the satellite’s cameras to record it in archived frames.

The probability of catching such an alignment is tiny, roughly one in a million for any given background star, yet the survey’s wide field and repeated visits made the first detection possible.

The same physics that lets us weigh planets we cannot see also lets us measure the mass of free-floating worlds and even rogue black holes, provided the alignment is caught.

The open question is whether the current archive already contains dozens more events that simply have not been searched with the right algorithms.
