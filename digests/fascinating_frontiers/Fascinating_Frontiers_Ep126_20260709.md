### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Falcon 9 Completes Record 36th Flight and Landing — Spaceflight Now**
   The booster B1067 lifted off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 0925 UTC carrying the Starlink Group 10-42 mission. It touched down on the droneship A Shortfall of Gravitas after delivering its payload. This marks the first time any orbital-class booster has reached 36 flights and landings. The launch continues the steady cadence of constellation replenishment flights. In the ongoing Commercial Spaceflight arc, this flight advances the open question of rapid reuse cadence by demonstrating sustained high flight counts on a single vehicle. Next, teams will inspect the booster for any signs of wear before assigning it to a future mission.

2. **Exoplanet Survives Host Star’s Death — The News International**
   Astronomers are examining how a planet remained intact after its star evolved into a white dwarf. The system shows the planet orbiting at a distance that allowed it to avoid engulfment during the star’s red-giant phase. Observations reveal unusual atmospheric or orbital properties that challenge standard models of post-main-sequence planetary survival. Follow-up spectroscopy is planned to determine the planet’s current composition and temperature.

3. **New Horizons Nears Solar System’s Outer Boundary — r/space**
   NASA’s Pluto probe is approaching the region where the solar wind gives way to interstellar space. The spacecraft will become only the third human-made object to cross this boundary after Voyager 1 and 2. Instruments are already recording changes in particle populations and magnetic fields. Data from this crossing will refine understanding of the heliopause structure. Mission controllers expect several months of measurements before the craft moves deeper into interstellar space.

4. **Simulation Guides Lunar Outpost Crew Size — Space.com**
   Researchers ran scenarios testing how different numbers of astronauts affect mission success on the Moon. The study found that operational design choices, such as task scheduling and habitat layout, matter as much as psychological factors. Results suggest an optimal crew size balances workload distribution with available resources. NASA can apply these findings when finalizing Artemis base requirements. Additional simulations will test longer-duration stays.

5. **Artemis II Crew Returns to Orion Capsule — Phys.org**
   The four Artemis II astronauts reunited with their spacecraft three months after completing the mission’s record-setting lunar flyby. The capsule had traveled farther from Earth than any crewed vehicle in history. Engineers are now reviewing post-flight data and hardware performance. This reunion allows the crew to inspect systems firsthand before the next phase of vehicle processing. The experience feeds directly into preparations for the first crewed lunar landing.

6. **South Pole Telescope Catalogues 7,000 Galaxy Clusters — Universe Today**
   A five-year survey has produced the largest catalogue yet of massive galaxy clusters, some dating back nearly eight billion years. The data set maps the universe’s largest structures in unprecedented detail. Hidden within the measurements is evidence that star formation rates across cosmic time differed from earlier predictions. Teams are now cross-checking the catalogue against optical and X-ray observations. Future papers will examine how these clusters grew through mergers.

7. **Beta Pictoris b’s Formation Remains Unclear — Phys.org**
   The 11-Jupiter-mass planet orbits its 23-million-year-old star on a 23-year path. Direct imaging since 2008 has tracked its motion within the system’s iconic dust disk. Current data cannot yet distinguish between formation by core accretion or disk instability. Additional observations with next-generation instruments aim to measure the planet’s atmospheric composition and any remaining heat from formation.

8. **Venus Passes Close to Regulus in Evening Sky — Astronomy Magazine**
   The planet stands 1.1 degrees north of the bright star in Leo after sunset. Observers can watch the pair slowly descend together in the western sky. The conjunction offers a simple naked-eye alignment visible from most locations. No special equipment is required beyond clear western horizons.

9. **Spaceflight Triggers Liver Changes Resembling Ageing — Universe Today**
   University of Central Florida researchers exposed liver cells to combined radiation and microgravity conditions. The resulting gene-expression patterns matched those seen in blood samples from actual astronauts. The work identifies specific pathways that could be targeted to protect crews on long-duration missions. Follow-up studies will test whether the same signatures appear after shorter flights.

10. **Infrasound and Seismic Sensors Track Daytime Fireball — Universe Today**
    A bright meteor crossed Alaska skies while the Sun was still up, rendering optical cameras ineffective. Scientists instead used low-frequency sound waves and ground tremors recorded by earthquake stations to reconstruct the object’s trajectory and breakup. The method captured the final seconds of atmospheric entry in detail. This approach could provide routine detection of daytime or cloudy-sky events worldwide. Researchers are now refining algorithms to distinguish fireballs from other acoustic sources.

11. **Vanguard 1 Remains Oldest Active Satellite — Yahoo Tech**
    Launched in 1958, the small sphere continues circling Earth more than six decades later. Its simple design and high orbit have allowed it to outlast every other early spacecraft. Radio tracking still confirms its position and slow orbital decay. No other artificial satellite from the first decade of spaceflight remains operational.

12. **Singapore Forms New Space Agency — SpaceNews**
    The agency signed a cooperation agreement with Japan’s space agency to support industry growth. Officials aim to develop local capabilities in satellite manufacturing and data services. The agreement includes technology exchanges and joint project planning. Early focus areas include Earth observation and communications applications.

13. **Device Turns Air into Fertilizer for Lunar Soil — Universe Today**
    Japanese researchers built a low-power system that extracts nitrogen directly from the atmosphere and converts it into plant-usable form. Tests in simulated lunar regolith successfully grew rice plants. An unexpected side effect improved nutrient uptake in the crops. The technology could support future lunar greenhouses while also offering applications for terrestrial farming in nutrient-poor soils.

14. **Primordial Mini-Moons Explain Meteorite Grains — Phys.org**
    A Southwest Research Institute study proposes that tiny moonlets once orbited early asteroids and concentrated millimeter-sized spherical particles. These grains later became the building blocks of the most common meteorite types. Computer models show how gravitational interactions could sort and preserve the material. Laboratory analysis of meteorites will test the predicted grain-size distributions.

15. **Milky Way’s Outer Arms Extend Farther Than Expected — Universe Today**
    Chandra X-ray Observatory data reveal spiral-arm structures reaching beyond previous mapped limits. The extended arms contain hot gas and young stars at greater distances from the galactic center. Adjusting models of Milky Way structure will affect estimates of total stellar mass and rotation curve. Additional observations are scheduled to trace the arms in other wavelengths.

### Cosmic Spotlight
Infrasound and seismic networks turned an otherwise invisible daytime fireball over Alaska into a fully reconstructed event. Low-frequency sound waves traveled hundreds of kilometers through the atmosphere while faint ground tremors provided timing and trajectory data. The combined signals revealed the object’s breakup altitude and energy release without any useful camera footage. This technique opens a path to continuous monitoring regardless of weather or daylight. How many similar events have already occurred undetected above populated regions?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Infrasound Reveals Invisible Fireballs
If you could shrink Earth’s atmosphere to the size of a football stadium, the thin shell where most meteors burn up would be only a few rows of seats thick. A daylight fireball releases energy equivalent to hundreds or thousands of tons of TNT, yet its optical flash can be lost against the Sun. Instead, the event generates powerful pressure waves that propagate as infrasound—frequencies below 20 hertz—traveling thousands of kilometers with little attenuation. These waves reach ground stations hours later, while the same shock also couples into the solid Earth, registering as tiny seismic signals on earthquake sensors. One surprising number stands out: a single station can sometimes locate the source to within tens of kilometers using arrival-time differences across a sparse network. The remaining mystery is why some fireballs produce unusually strong seismic coupling while others of similar size remain nearly silent on the ground, leaving researchers without a reliable way to predict which events will be easiest to study.
