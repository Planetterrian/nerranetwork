1. **Artemis II Crew Receives Stephen Hawking Medal** — Space.com
   The four Artemis II astronauts accepted the Stephen Hawking Medal at the Starmus VIII festival in Tenerife on October 17. NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman and Victor Glover joined Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen on stage, with Christina Koch also recognized even though she could not attend. The award honors their live communications, interviews, and photos that let people worldwide follow the mission in real time. Wiseman described seeing Earth the size of a thumb from 400,000 kilometers away and feeling only love, peace, hope, and unity. He closed by telling the audience that everyone can choose to make tomorrow better. Previous medal recipients include Buzz Aldrin, Jane Goodall, and David Attenborough. The festival runs through October 22 under the theme "The Search for Truth." Starmus co-founder Brian May noted the special thrill of honoring the Artemis II crew in the same way the Apollo astronauts were honored more than fifty years earlier. Wiseman emphasized that the view from lunar distance made the planet appear tiny yet underscored a shared sense of connection among all people. The crew's outreach turned the technical test flight into an experience that reached audiences far beyond mission control. Festival organizers highlighted how the astronauts' public engagement aligned with the medal's goal of making science accessible and inspiring. Source: [space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/artemis/artemis-ii-astronauts-honored-with-stephen-hawking-medal-at-starmus)

2. **Study Simulates How Microgravity Changes Human Cell Nuclei** — Universe Today
   Researchers at New York University built a Random Positioning Machine to rotate living cell cultures and remove the constant pull of gravity. After 24 hours the nuclei in the simulated microgravity samples grew larger than those kept under normal conditions. The nuclear envelope thickness stayed the same, showing that gravity mainly affects nuclear volume rather than envelope structure. The team also developed 3D algorithms that reduced unwanted fluid flows during the rotation, allowing clearer observation of genome organization changes. Alexandra Zidovska noted that the work helps explain how space travel affects humans at the genetic level. The experiment used advanced imaging to track cell and nuclear responses under each condition and recorded streams of images for detailed analysis of genome dynamics and possible DNA damage. The study built on earlier findings from NASA's Twins Study that showed genetic-level effects in astronauts. Zidovska explained that gravity acts as a constant mechanical stress whose removal reveals how the genome organizes itself without that force. The Random Positioning Machine followed a three-dimensional path to create the microgravity simulation while the team minimized cell aggregation that had obscured results in prior work. Researchers recorded that the nuclear envelope remained unchanged in thickness even as overall nuclear volume increased, pointing to a selective influence of gravity on internal cell structures. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/new-research-reveals-how-long-stays-in-space-affects-the-human-genome)

3. **Three-Dimensional Models Test Nuclear Asteroid Disruption** — Phys.org Space
   Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory researchers ran more than 1,500 one-dimensional Spheral simulations to match results from their established Ares code. They then modeled three disruption scenarios for a 160-meter irregular asteroid, varying detonation distance and material fracture strength. In the longest run, 98 percent of the asteroid material became fully damaged and 97 percent exceeded escape velocity. The simulations showed that energy deposited over a larger area can produce more widespread damage even when total energy is lower. The work highlights how 3D modeling captures fracture and fragment motion that one-dimensional codes miss. The longest simulation used 1,680 processors for 59 days yet advanced only 145 milliseconds of physical time, underscoring the computational challenge of following fragments to their final sizes and trajectories. The team validated the Spheral approach by comparing outputs against established deflection models before moving to full disruption cases. Isaiah Santistevan, who led the study, noted that the three-dimensional view reveals how internal strength variations and detonation geometry alter fragment outcomes. Two of the three scenarios produced strong disruption signatures, with material moving in opposing directions after the simulated nuclear event. The models track damage, fracture, and material motion simultaneously, providing a more complete picture than earlier one-dimensional runs. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-simulations-explore-nuclear-mitigation-asteroid.html)

4. **Salt Crystals in Enceladus Plumes May Carry Ocean Chemistry** — Phys.org Space
   Jets near Enceladus's south pole spray water vapor and icy particles from the subsurface ocean through kilometers of ice. Salt crystals in those particles reach the surface and could preserve chemical clues about the hidden ocean without any drilling. The particles offer a direct sample of material that has interacted with the moon's interior. The ocean lies beneath an ice shell that prevents direct access, yet the plumes provide a natural pathway for material to escape. Scientists can examine the salt crystals for dissolved minerals that record conditions in the water below. This approach avoids the need for a lander or drill and instead uses the moon's own activity to deliver samples. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-salt-crystals-clues-life-beneath.html)

5. **Compact Hydrogen Cloud Could Be a Dark Galaxy** — Phys.org Space
   China's FAST radio telescope detected a compact cloud of neutral hydrogen with no visible stars. The cloud contains nearly 500,000 times the Sun's mass in hydrogen and shows no optical counterpart. The discovery paper appeared on the arXiv preprint server on September 24. The object stands out because it lacks any detectable stars or galactic structure despite its substantial hydrogen content. FAST's sensitivity allowed astronomers to identify the isolated cloud against background emissions. The finding adds to ongoing searches for dark galaxies that may contain large amounts of unseen matter.

### Cosmic Spotlight
The three-dimensional asteroid disruption runs at Lawrence Livermore show that placing the energy deposit farther from the surface can spread damage across more of the body even when total energy delivered is lower. In the longest simulation 98 percent of the material lost structural integrity while 97 percent moved faster than escape velocity, indicating the body was actively breaking apart. Because the models track fracture and fragment trajectories in full three dimensions, they reveal how different internal strengths and impact geometries change the final fragment sizes and velocities. The next modeling step is to follow those fragments for longer times so their ultimate paths and sizes can be predicted. Santistevan emphasized that the high-fidelity approach improves evaluation of planetary defense options by showing realistic material responses. What size threshold would make disruption preferable to deflection for a given warning time?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Jets Sample a Hidden Ocean
Enceladus presents a striking case where an entire ocean hides beneath a thick ice shell yet still communicates with the surface through dramatic jets. The moon itself measures only a few hundred kilometers across, yet the subsurface reservoir holds liquid water kept from freezing by tidal heating generated during its orbit around Saturn. That same flexing opens fractures at the south pole, allowing pressurized water to erupt outward in plumes that climb hundreds of kilometers into space before the material falls back as fine ice grains and vapor. Because the spray originates directly from the hidden sea, each particle carries a chemical signature of the water it left behind. Salt crystals form inside the grains when liquid evaporates or freezes rapidly, trapping dissolved minerals that record temperature, acidity, and the possible presence of organic compounds. Earlier spacecraft measurements already detected sodium, potassium, chlorine, and simple organics within the plume material, though those readings averaged many particles over short fly-through windows. Future collectors could separate individual grains, dissolve the salts, and run them through mass spectrometers or microscopes to reconstruct the ocean's full chemical profile. The jets therefore function as a ready-made sampling system that delivers interior material to any spacecraft passing overhead without the need for drilling or landing. The open question remains whether the salts and organics preserve active chemical gradients that might support life or whether they reflect only ancient, unchanging conditions sealed away long ago. Observers can watch the plumes erupt in real time from orbit, yet the duration of the ocean's exchange with the surface and whether that exchange ever created conditions suitable for biology to start are still unknown. The process shows how a small icy body can maintain a dynamic connection between its hidden interior and the surrounding space environment over long timescales.
