2. **NASA awards $10 billion lunar cargo contract and moves forward with PROMISE rover** — NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)
   Eight companies including Blue Origin, Firefly Aerospace, Intuitive Machines, ispace-U.S., Lockheed Martin Space, Northrop Grumman Space Systems, SpaceX and Voyager Technologies are eligible to compete for future lunar surface deliveries under the new CLPS 2 framework. The contract has a combined maximum value of $10 billion over ten years. NASA increased the value of the existing CLPS 1.0 contract from $2.6 billion to $4.2 billion to support initial Moon Base objectives. The original CLPS contract remains in place through November 2028 with an additional five-year period to complete deliveries through November 2033. NASA is also advancing the nuclear-powered PROMISE rover, which will use spare components from the Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers to cut costs and speed development. Blue Origin received a $94 million base contract, with a possible additional $140 million, to deliver the rover to the lunar south pole using its Mark 1 lander. The six-wheeled rover will carry a drill to sample material roughly one meter below the surface along with spectrometers, temperature and radiation sensors, and cameras. It will demonstrate a radiation-tolerant computing system and advanced autonomy sensors. Launch is targeted for late 2028 with an 18-month mission. The rover can operate during long lunar nights and inside permanently shadowed regions. NASA shared the announcements at the MAX POWER aerospace expo at Kennedy Space Center. 4. **How nuclear rockets could speed deep-space travel** — Interesting Engineering
   Nuclear propulsion systems offer higher efficiency than chemical rockets for long-duration missions. The approach could shorten travel times to Mars and beyond while carrying heavier payloads. Nuclear thermal designs heat propellant to higher temperatures than chemical combustion allows. This produces greater exhaust velocity and reduces the mass of propellant needed. Engineers note that the technology builds on decades of reactor research originally developed for terrestrial power. Integration with existing launch vehicles would require new shielding and safety protocols. The higher specific impulse opens routes to outer planets that chemical stages cannot reach within reasonable crew timelines. Source: [interestingengineering.com](https://interestingengineering.com/space/nuclear-rockets-offer-nasa-high-speed)

5. **Great Comet of 1882 transformed astrophotography** — Astronomy
   David Gill captured the first successful wide-field photograph of a comet from the Cape of Good Hope observatory on November 7, 1882. The image demonstrated that long exposures could record faint detail invisible to the eye. The comet had reached naked-eye visibility during daylight at perihelion two months earlier. Gill used a portrait lens mounted on an equatorial telescope to track the object across several hours. The resulting plate showed the comet's tail structure and surrounding star field in a single frame. This success encouraged other observatories to adopt photographic methods for systematic sky surveys. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/nov-7-1882-a-great-comet-transforms-astrophotography/)

### Cosmic Spotlight
**Supernova 2026ahvu appears in barred Seyfert galaxy NGC 1433: r/space** The Type Ia supernova was discovered November 2 by the ATLAS survey and sits in a galaxy 46 million light-years away that shows active star formation along its spiral arms. Type Ia events occur when a white dwarf reaches the Chandrasekhar limit and detonates, providing astronomers with a standard candle whose peak brightness is consistent enough to measure cosmic distances. The same process also seeds the universe with iron-peak elements, including much of the iron now found in human blood. The galaxy NGC 1433 features a double-ring central structure formed by tightly wound spiral arms along its bar. ATLAS uses four 0.5-meter telescopes to image the entire sky multiple times each night. Once confirmed as a Type Ia event, the supernova's light curve can refine the distance to NGC 1433 without relying solely on other indicators. The explosion leaves no compact remnant because the runaway fusion consumes the entire star. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Big Bang Nucleosynthesis Tests Cosmological Models
The first few minutes after the Big Bang set the chemical recipe for the entire universe, locking in ratios of hydrogen, helium, and trace lithium that astronomers can still measure today. In that brief window the temperature and density allowed protons and neutrons to fuse into light nuclei before expansion cooled everything too much for further reactions. A model that gets these ratios wrong cannot claim to describe the early universe correctly, no matter how well it fits later observations. The CCC+TL cosmology proposes a universe 26.7 billion years old without invoking dark matter or dark energy, yet it must still reproduce the same primordial abundances that standard cosmology matches.

Imagine watching the universe's first three minutes unfold at a rate where every second corresponds to a million years of later cosmic time. Within that compressed interval the neutron-to-proton ratio freezes, deuterium forms, and almost all helium is created. The resulting mix leaves roughly 25 percent helium by mass and trace amounts of lithium-7, numbers that depend sensitively on the expansion rate and baryon density at that epoch. Any alternative model changes either the expansion history or the particle content and therefore predicts different yields. When researchers plug the CCC+TL parameters into nucleosynthesis calculations, the predicted helium and deuterium fractions line up with values measured in the oldest stars and in distant gas clouds whose light has traveled for billions of years.

The University of Ottawa team compared these predictions directly against spectroscopic data and found consistency across multiple light-element channels. That agreement is surprising because the model alters the overall age and energy budget of the cosmos yet still satisfies a constraint fixed when the universe was only seconds old. The test does not prove the model correct, but it shows the framework survives one of the most precise early-universe benchmarks available.

What remains unsettled is whether the same parameters can simultaneously satisfy later observations such as the cosmic microwave background power spectrum and the growth of large-scale structure. Those datasets probe epochs hundreds of thousands of years after nucleosynthesis and could reveal tensions not yet visible in the light-element data. If future measurements tighten the allowed range for the baryon density or the expansion rate, the CCC+TL model may need adjustment or may be ruled out. We already know the universe produced helium and deuterium in specific proportions; the open question is which complete description of cosmic history can explain both those proportions and everything that came afterward.
