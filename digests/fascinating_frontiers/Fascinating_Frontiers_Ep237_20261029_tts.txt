Venus swings between Earth and the Sun once every year and a half, lining up almost directly with our star from where we stand.

In that alignment the planet’s night side faces us, and only a thin sliver of sunlit air stays in view.

That sliver forms a glowing crescent whose tips stretch past a simple half-circle.

Those stretched tips are the horns, made when sunlight slips through the dense upper atmosphere and bends around the curved limb.

The same lineup that gives us a monthly new Moon therefore gives Venus a new-Venus phase.

Venus’s thick air scatters and refracts the light, so the crescent looks brighter and wider than geometry alone would allow.

The new Moon stays dark in that geometry.

Venus’s backlit atmosphere instead throws a glowing arc that can extend beyond one hundred eighty degrees.

Ancient Babylonian astronomers wrote down the brightness changes thousands of years ago.
