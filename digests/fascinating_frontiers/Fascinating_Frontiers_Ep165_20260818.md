### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Gaia and Hubble trace Milky Way’s first major merger — ESA**
   The two observatories combined proper-motion data from Gaia with deep imaging from Hubble to identify stars that once belonged to a dwarf galaxy accreted early in the Milky Way’s history. The remnant stars now occupy the galactic halo and show distinct chemical patterns and orbital energies that set them apart from later-accreted populations. This event is the earliest large-scale merger yet confirmed and occurred when the Milky Way was still assembling its disk. The combined dataset also reveals how the incoming galaxy’s stars were dynamically heated and mixed over time. Follow-up spectroscopy of additional halo stars is planned to refine the mass and orbit of the original dwarf. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Hubble_Gaia_solve_our_galaxy_s_merger_mystery)

2. **MTG-I2 completes fuelling ahead of August 27 launch — ESA**
   The second Meteosat Third Generation imager satellite finished propellant loading and moved into final integrated testing at Europe’s Spaceport. Once in geostationary orbit the satellite will deliver higher-resolution imagery and lightning mapping for European weather services. Liftoff remains scheduled for 27 August aboard an Ariane 6. Ground teams are now verifying thermal and electrical interfaces before encapsulation. The mission continues the transition to the MTG constellation that will operate through the 2030s. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/esa/status/2089355955905462331)

3. **Roman Space Telescope launch targeted in two weeks — NASA**
   NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is scheduled to lift off within the next fourteen days on a Falcon Heavy. The observatory carries a wide-field infrared instrument designed to conduct a large-scale exoplanet census and dark-energy survey. A new podcast episode outlines how Roman will detect thousands of planets via microlensing and directly image young Jupiters around Sun-like stars. Integration and testing at Kennedy Space Center are complete. Launch preparations continue at Cape Canaveral.

4. **Starship returns to calmer waters after 24 days at sea — SpaceX**
   Recovery teams guided the Starship vehicle to a location off Christmas Island following nearly a month adrift in the Indian Ocean. Engineers are now boarding to inspect thermal protection tiles and structural integrity before the vehicle is towed back to Starbase. The extended sea trial provides data on long-duration exposure to saltwater and wave loading. Analysis will inform future recovery procedures for reusable upper stages. No immediate timeline for return to Texas has been released. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/SpaceX/status/2089685256085344560)

5. **Webb captures clearest image yet of the Lion Nebula — Phys.org**
   The James Webb Space Telescope observed NGC 2392 in near- and mid-infrared wavelengths, revealing fine dust structures and ionized gas in the nebula’s face-like appearance. Earlier Hubble images showed the outer envelope; Webb’s data penetrate deeper to expose previously unseen knots and filaments. The observations help refine models of how planetary nebulae disperse heavy elements into the interstellar medium. The data were released as part of an ongoing program studying evolved stars. Additional filters are scheduled for later this year. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-webb-captures-clearest-image-lion.html)

6. **Mars rover detects ruby-forming mineral in impact-altered rock — Phys.org**
   Perseverance identified corundum, the mineral that forms rubies and sapphires, within a rock whose texture suggests shock metamorphism from ancient impacts. Most previously sampled Martian minerals trace to volcanic or aqueous processes; this find points to a separate high-pressure history. The detection was made with the rover’s PIXL and SHERLOC instruments during a traverse across the crater rim. Scientists will compare the composition with laboratory-shocked terrestrial samples. Further drilling is planned to assess how widespread such minerals may be. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-08-ruby-mineral-mars-rover-hints.html)

7. **Hera flyby supplies new data on Deimos impact history — ESA**
   During its March 2025 Mars encounter the Hera spacecraft obtained high-resolution images and spectra of Deimos that are now being used to model possible impact events on the moon. The data show surface features consistent with ejecta from larger impacts on nearby Phobos or Mars itself. Mission teams are integrating these observations with numerical simulations to constrain the timing and energy of past collisions. The results will feed into planning for future Phobos-Deimos sample-return concepts. Additional analysis is expected in the coming months. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Hera/Hera_s_Mars_flyby_guided_impact_study_of_Deimos_moon)

8. **Astronomers document breakup of a split comet — Sky & Telescope**
   Ground-based telescopes tracked a comet that divided into two components over six months, recording changes in brightness, separation velocity, and dust production. The observations allow reconstruction of the fracture event and subsequent outgassing that widened the pair’s orbit. Fragmentation models suggest the split occurred near perihelion when thermal stresses peaked. Continued monitoring will determine whether either piece survives the next orbit. The study provides a rare real-time view of cometary disruption mechanics. Source: [skyandtelescope.org](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/spying-on-a-split-comet/)

9. **Helium discovery marked on 1868 eclipse anniversary — Astronomy**
   On 18 August 1868 astronomers Pierre Janssen and Norman Lockyer independently recorded a bright yellow line in the Sun’s spectrum during totality that did not match any known element. Lockyer proposed the line belonged to a new element he named helium after the Greek Sun god. The identification remained the only noble gas first found in space rather than on Earth. Laboratory confirmation came decades later. The anniversary highlights how eclipse spectroscopy opened the study of stellar composition. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/aug-18-1868-helium-is-discovered/)

10. **Study weighs nuclear deflection for short-warning asteroids — Phys.org**
    Researchers modeled the use of a nuclear device to alter the trajectory of an asteroid detected only months before impact. The simulation shows that a standoff detonation could impart sufficient impulse while minimizing fragmentation risk if the body is larger than a few hundred metres. The work examines variables including yield, standoff distance, and asteroid rotation state. Current planetary-defense roadmaps still prioritize kinetic impactors for longer warning times. Further studies will assess detection thresholds needed to enable such options.

### Cosmic Spotlight
Gaia and Hubble have identified the Milky Way’s earliest known major merger by matching chemical fingerprints and orbital energies of halo stars to a single dwarf galaxy accreted nearly 12 billion years ago. The combined dataset shows how the incoming galaxy’s stars were dynamically heated and redistributed, leaving detectable streams that persist today. This event occurred while the Milky Way was still forming its disk, offering a timestamp on the galaxy’s assembly sequence. Follow-up spectroscopy of additional halo members will tighten the mass and trajectory estimates. The work demonstrates how precision astrometry and photometry together can reconstruct galactic history from stellar fossils.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Ancient Mergers Built the Milky Way’s Halo
The Milky Way’s stellar halo stretches across roughly 150,000 light-years, yet most of its stars arrived in a handful of ancient mergers rather than forming in place. One dwarf galaxy swallowed 12 billion years ago contributed a population now spread over a volume comparable to the distance between Earth and the galactic center, yet those stars occupy only a few percent of the halo’s total mass. When the dwarf fell in, its orbital energy was converted into random motions that puffed the stars into the extended halo we see today, much like dropping a compact ice cube into a swirling pot of soup and watching the fragments disperse. The chemical signatures—lower iron and higher magnesium relative to Milky Way disk stars—still tag these interlopers even after billions of years of mixing. We can map their orbits with Gaia’s micro-arcsecond precision, but the exact mass and internal structure of that original dwarf remain uncertain because later dynamical heating has erased the cleanest orbital clues. The open question is whether similar early mergers delivered the seeds of the galaxy’s oldest globular clusters or whether those clusters formed separately before being captured.
