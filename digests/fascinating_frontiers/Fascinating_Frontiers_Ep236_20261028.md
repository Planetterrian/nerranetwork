### Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Hera spacecraft resolves Didymos binary for first time — European Space Agency**
   The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft captured its first resolved images of the Didymos-Dimorphos pair on October 18 at a distance of 1,280 kilometres. Dimorphos appears as roughly one pixel, clearly separated from the larger parent body. The image followed two braking manoeuvres that slowed the spacecraft to 20 metres per second relative to the asteroids. Didymos measures 780 metres across while Dimorphos spans 151 metres, orbiting an average 1.15 kilometres apart. Mission manager Ian Carnelli noted the team will obtain the first detailed images of Dimorphos on November 25. Those observations will examine the effects of the 2022 DART impact and refine models of asteroid deflection. The DART collision sent debris across 10,000 kilometres of space and shortened Dimorphos’s orbital period by 33 minutes. Hera will later deploy two CubeSats for closer surveys before attempting a landing on the smaller body. The mission’s data will clarify whether the impact left a crater or reshaped the asteroid entirely. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Hera/First_light_Hera_resolves_two_asteroids_instead_of_one)

2. **CHAPEA crew completes 378-day Mars simulation — NASA Breaking**
   Four volunteers will exit the Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog habitat at Johnson Space Center on October 31 after 378 days of isolation. The crew conducted simulated spacewalks, grew plants, performed habitat maintenance and operated under communication delays and resource limits. Data collected will inform food-system design, crew-time efficiency and behavioural support for future Artemis and deep-space missions. Live coverage of the exit begins at 3 p.m. EDT on NASA Johnson’s YouTube channel. Johnson Center Director Vanessa Wyche and NASA astronaut Jack Hathaway will provide remarks during the ceremony. The four crew members—Ross Elder, Ellen Ellis, James Spicer and Matthew Montgomery—will also speak after emerging. The 1,700-square-foot 3D-printed facility simulated stressors expected on long-duration lunar and Mars flights. Health and performance measurements from the mission will help optimise medical care and psychological support practices. Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-invites-media-to-crew-completion-of-yearlong-mars-simulation/)

3. **NASA details early Habitable Worlds Observatory designs — Universe Today**
   A June 2026 study in the Journal of Astronomical Telescopes, Instruments, and Systems outlines three Exploratory Analytic Cases for the Habitable Worlds Observatory. EAC1 uses a 6-metre inscribed, 7.2-metre circumscribed off-axis mirror. EAC2 adopts a 6-metre round off-axis design. EAC3 specifies an 8-metre outer-diameter on-axis mirror. The Systems Engineering Team favoured off-axis architectures for unobstructed light collection and larger apertures to improve coronagraph performance. End-to-end modelling has been completed for EAC1 and shared with industry partners. The coronagraph instrument blocks starlight to enable direct imaging of Earth-like exoplanets. Temperature control properties were also examined because they strongly affect light-collecting efficiency. The Habitable Worlds Observatory remains scheduled for launch in the 2040s and is intended to succeed the James Webb Space Telescope as NASA’s flagship observatory. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nasa-systems-engineering-team-details-hwo-mission-architecture)

4. **Radio observations detect ambipolar diffusion in prestellar core — Universe Today**
   Researchers from Kyushu University and the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics observed the L1544 prestellar core in the Taurus Molecular Complex, 140 parsecs away. They measured a 0.05 kilometre-per-second velocity difference between the ion tracer N2D+ and the neutral tracer para-NH2D. The difference indicates neutral particles are decoupling from magnetic field lines and falling inward faster than ions. This ambipolar diffusion process weakens the magnetic field and allows gravitational collapse to begin. The team used the IRAM 30-metre telescope for the spectral-line observations. Prestellar cores in the study region reach temperatures between 10 and 30 kelvin, cold enough for complex molecules to form on dust grains. Lead researcher Doris Arzoumanian explained that strong magnetic fields can otherwise delay collapse. The velocity offset provides the first direct measurement of ion-neutral drift on the scales where stars actually form. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/unveiling-starbirth-mysteries)

5. **Curiosity rover prepares to drill at ‘Nazca’ — NASA Breaking**
   Curiosity has driven a few metres past the “Chiu Chiu” block to target the newly named “Nazca” site for its next drill campaign. Post-drive imaging revealed abundant disc- and needle-shaped crystal forms at the location. The rover will perform a Quantitative Relief Model observation with MAHLI and a 150-shot depth-profile LIBS measurement on a nearby target called “Toucan.” Additional LIBS, DRT and APXS observations will characterise the surface before the preload test. The crystal forms may represent pseudomorphs whose original mineral composition has been replaced. A finely layered texture on a nearby block named “Condor” will also receive LIBS analysis. The campaign follows the earlier Basque Lakes drill and aims to understand how the distinctive shapes formed under ancient Martian conditions. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-5043-5049-preparing-to-drill-at-nazca/)

6. **Mutual occultation of Ganymede and Io captured on video — NASA Breaking**
   On October 20, 2026, Ganymede passed in front of Io as seen from Earth, producing a mutual occultation recorded by astronomer Michael Wong. The event occurs when Jupiter reaches equinox and its Galilean moons appear edge-on from our viewpoint, a geometry that repeats every six years. Ganymede’s shadow also falls across Jupiter’s cloud tops, creating a solar eclipse for any hypothetical observers on the planet. Jupiter currently has 115 known moons, second only to Saturn. The video shows the precise moment Ganymede blocks Io from view. Mutual occultations of the Galilean moons remain rare because they require Earth’s precise alignment with the orbital plane. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2026-october-28-the-mutual-occultation-of-ganymede-and-io/)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Ambipolar Diffusion Lets Stars Form
A prestellar core the size of a modest city block can contain several solar masses of gas and dust, yet magnetic fields threading the larger cloud often act like rigid scaffolding that prevents collapse. In the Taurus Molecular Complex, 140 parsecs from Earth, one such core named L1544 has now revealed the precise mechanism that lets gravity finally win. Researchers from Kyushu University and the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics used the IRAM 30-metre telescope to track two molecular tracers inside the core. The ion N2D+ remained tightly coupled to the magnetic field lines, while the neutral molecule para-NH2D drifted ahead at 0.05 kilometres per second. That small velocity offset marks the moment when neutral particles begin to slip past the ions and carry mass inward.

The process begins once the core grows dense enough to block external starlight. With fewer ionising photons reaching the interior, the fraction of charged particles drops sharply. Neutral molecules no longer feel the magnetic field directly; they only collide occasionally with the sparse ions that remain tied to the lines. Over thousands of years those gentle collisions transfer momentum, but the neutrals still pull ahead. As they accumulate at the centre they bend the field lines outward, reducing the magnetic pressure that once supported the core against gravity. Temperatures inside L1544 already sit between 10 and 30 kelvin, cold enough for complex chemistry to proceed on dust-grain surfaces even while the magnetic support slowly erodes.

The same spectra that recorded the velocity difference also show the core is chemically rich, with molecules that could serve as precursors to prebiotic compounds. Lead researcher Doris Arzoumanian noted that if the magnetic field were too strong it would delay collapse indefinitely, yet the measured drift demonstrates that ambipolar diffusion can weaken that grip on timescales relevant to star formation. What remains unknown is whether every prestellar core experiences this decoupling or whether some collapse through turbulence or external compression alone. Future high-resolution maps of additional cores will reveal whether the 0.05 kilometre-per-second signature is universal or whether stronger initial fields can suppress the process entirely.
