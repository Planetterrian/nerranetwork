1. **Lunar Farside Mosaic Reveals Thicker Crust** — NASA
   The image is a mosaic from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter’s wide angle camera centered on the lunar farside. It combines over 15,000 images acquired between November 2009 and February 2011. The highest resolution version shows features at a scale of 100 meters per pixel. The rough and battered surface of the farside looks very different from the nearside covered with smooth dark lunar maria. A likely explanation is that the farside crust is thicker, making it harder for molten material from the interior to flow to the surface and form dark, smooth maria. The mosaic covers the entire farside hemisphere without any contribution from Earth-based observations. Credit for the composite goes to NASA, GSFC, Arizona State University, and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter team. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2026-october-10-lunar-farside/)

### Cosmic Spotlight
The SPHEREx telescope’s survey of brown dwarfs opens a new window on objects that sit between stars and planets. These worlds show atmospheric chemistry rich in water and methane, mirroring conditions in our own giant planets. The data come from systematic mapping that captures objects too faint for earlier instruments. Scientists can now compare how these atmospheres evolve across a range of temperatures and ages. One open question is whether similar chemistry appears on free-floating planets that formed the same way. The survey adds dozens of new targets for follow-up spectroscopy.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Why the Moon’s Farside Looks So Different
The Moon is about one quarter the diameter of Earth yet its two faces could hardly be more unlike each other. Picture a dinner plate the size of a city block; the nearside would show dark patches the size of several football fields while the farside would look like a single unbroken expanse of lighter, cratered terrain. That difference comes down to crust thickness. The farside crust runs several kilometers deeper on average, enough to keep molten rock from punching through and forming the smooth dark plains we see from Earth. Spacecraft like the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter measure this by counting craters and mapping elevation at one hundred meter resolution. The result is a surface that records four billion years of impacts without the resurfacing events that reset the nearside clock. We still do not know exactly why the crust grew thicker on one side in the first place.
