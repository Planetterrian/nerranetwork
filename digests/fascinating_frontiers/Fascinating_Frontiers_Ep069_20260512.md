### Cosmic Spotlight
Artemis II astronauts released and shaped a single drop of water inside their spacecraft, watching it form a perfect sphere that hovered until deliberately touched. Surface tension alone held the liquid together, allowing the crew to poke, stretch, and split it with surprising control. The footage underscores how everyday materials behave differently once freed from gravity, directly informing designs for fuel tanks, life-support systems, and medical tools on future missions. Engineers are already using similar observations to refine droplet management in closed-loop water recyclers. What remains unclear is how long-term exposure to radiation and cabin materials might subtly alter these same surface-tension properties over months in deep space.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Liquids Form Spheres in Orbit
Picture the International Space Station as a sealed room the size of a large house, yet every loose droplet inside behaves as though it were the only thing that matters. On Earth a milliliter of water collapses under its own weight into a puddle; in orbit the same volume is pulled inward from all directions by surface tension until it becomes a near-perfect sphere roughly the diameter of a grape. That sphere can grow or shrink simply by adding or removing tiny amounts of liquid because nothing is pressing it against a surface. Astronauts have measured these droplets reaching several centimeters across before they merge or split, all while floating at speeds matching the station’s 28,000 kilometers per hour around the planet. The same physics governs how fuel settles inside rocket tanks and how sweat behaves on an astronaut’s skin during a spacewalk. Yet researchers still cannot fully predict how trace contaminants or prolonged radiation exposure will change the surface tension of these droplets over the course of a six-month mission.
