3. **Vibration scans could reveal buried rocks on other worlds** — Phys.org Space
   Virginia Tech researchers buried 18 concrete blocks of varying sizes and depths in sand. They struck each exposed rock with an instrumented hammer and measured vibrations with a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer. A neural network with two hidden layers was trained on the vibration data and visible geometry to estimate burial depth. The method aims to help robotic excavators on Mars or the Moon avoid wasting effort on immovable boulders. Erik Komendera, an assistant professor of mechanical engineering who previously worked at NASA Langley, led the work with Ph.D. candidate Yiyan Ruan. The team found that soil stiffness, contact angle, and rock shape all affect the returning signal. The approach was published in IEEE Sensors Journal with DOI 10.1109/jsen.2026.3733381. On Earth the same tools could assess unstable rock formations, measure underground caverns, or assist disaster cleanup. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-vibrations-reveal-beneath-planets-surfaces.html)

### Cosmic Spotlight
Astronauts aboard the International Space Station cast midterm ballots electronically from orbit. The three U.S. crew members used secure transmission through NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System to reach county election officials in Texas. Americans have voted from space since 1997 under a Texas law that first enabled David Wolf to vote from Mir. The process routes the encrypted ballot from the station through White Sands Test Facility in New Mexico and then to the Mission Control Center at Johnson Space Center before final delivery to the county clerk. Anil Menon, Luke Delaney, and Jessica Watkins each completed an electronic form inside their station compartments and sent it to Harris County. Source: [space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/international-space-station/voting-in-space-nasa-astronauts-cast-ballots-from-international-space-station)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How vibrations reveal what lies beneath planetary surfaces
Picture a rover on Mars that must decide in minutes whether a boulder blocking its path is a loose rock the size of a suitcase or a buried slab the size of a truck. The difference determines whether the machine wastes hours chipping at immovable stone or simply drives around it. On Earth we make that judgment by eye and a quick kick; on another world the rover has no such intuition. Virginia Tech engineers are teaching machines to listen instead. Their method starts with a simple tap. An instrumented hammer strikes the visible part of a rock while a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer records the vibrations that travel through the buried portion and back out. Eighteen concrete blocks of different sizes and burial depths were planted in sand to create a training set. The team measured vibrations at multiple points on each exposed surface, then fed the frequency patterns and visible geometry into a compact neural network with only two hidden layers. Once trained, the model could estimate burial depth from new vibration readings. The approach works because vibrations travel differently through loose sand than through solid rock; a buried boulder returns a distinct signature compared with a surface pebble. On Mars or the Moon the same principle could let a robotic excavator know when further effort is pointless. Erik Komendera, who once worked as an engineer at NASA Langley Research Center, led the project with Ph.D. candidate Yiyan Ruan. Their results appear in the IEEE Sensors Journal under DOI 10.1109/jsen.2026.3733381. The researchers note that estimating burial depth remains tricky because feedback is affected by rock geometry and the way soil holds the object. By carefully selecting features from vibration measurements and exposed geometry they produced reliable data with a small neural network. Those models could one day guide a robotic excavator on Mars to handle obstacles correctly in the terrain. For now the team plans to continue building datasets and testing their approach on Earth. The same vibration technique could prove useful for determining the components of unstable rock formations, measuring underground caverns, and even helping with disaster cleanup after events that leave hazards buried. The core question remains open: how well will the trained models perform when the “soil” is actually Martian regolith whose grain size, moisture, and temperature are only partly known. Field tests on analog terrains will decide whether the method graduates from laboratory sandboxes to actual planetary surfaces. The work shows that a compact sensor suite and modest computing power can turn a simple hammer strike into actionable knowledge about what lies hidden beneath an alien surface.
