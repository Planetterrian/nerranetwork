Neutron stars form from the cores of exploded high mass stars and squeeze roughly 1.4 solar
masses into a ball only about 20 kilometers across. Black holes by contrast usually start
at 5 solar masses and go up. Between those two populations sits a thinly occupied band
from roughly 2.5 to 5 solar masses, with few objects in it. That band is where the equations
for matter under crushing pressure stop holding up a stable neutron star and allow a collapse
into a black hole. No precise masses in hand for that transition, because the physics inside
these objects is still poorly understood. A recent study published in Science Advances flagged a
candidate that could help fix that limit. The open question is whether bodies in this range are
always neutron stars born unusually heavy, or whether some are the lightest black holes. One more

the object can hold itself up against further collapse. Until then, every new fine trims the set
