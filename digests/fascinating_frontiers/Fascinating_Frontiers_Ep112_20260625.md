The work used data from multiple ground-based telescopes. Follow-up campaigns will search for the same signatures in other systems. ### Cosmic Spotlight
The first detection of event-horizon fingerprints comes from careful analysis of light emitted by gas spiraling near the boundary. Telescopes recorded tiny, repeating brightness changes that match computer models of photons skirting the point of no return. Because the signal is so faint, the team combined data from several observatories to pull it above the noise. The result gives astronomers a new way to test whether real black holes behave exactly as predicted. What other subtle signatures might still be hiding in existing observations of accreting systems?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Gravity Traps Light at an Event Horizon
If you could shrink Earth’s mass into a sphere just 1.8 centimeters across, its surface gravity would equal the speed of light and nothing could climb back out. Photons emitted from that surface would lose all their energy trying to escape, stretching into infinite redshift. The actual event horizon of a stellar-mass black hole sits only a few kilometers from its center, yet the same rule applies. We can watch gas orbit just outside this boundary and see its light dim and stretch, but we still cannot explain exactly how information about what fell in is preserved or lost.
