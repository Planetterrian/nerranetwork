From the human body we turn to the final stages of Sun-like stars, where another kind of gradual change produces surprising results.

Asymmetric mass loss during the final stages of stellar evolution can impart small recoil impulses.

Over hundreds of thousands of years these impulses accumulate and give the resulting white dwarf a noticeable velocity.

The mechanism helps explain why many wide binary systems separate once one star becomes a white dwarf.

The model predicts typical speeds reached after roughly ten thousand individual ejection events.

Observers can now search for matching velocity signatures in nearby white dwarf populations.

If you could watch a Sun-like star in its final million years, you would see it shed mass in sudden, uneven bursts rather than a smooth wind.

Each burst carries away a fraction of the star’s outer envelope in one direction, and the remaining core receives a tiny opposing shove.

Over hundreds of thousands of years these thousands of small recoils add up.

After roughly ten thousand events the white dwarf can reach speeds of several kilometers per second.

The mechanism operates on timescales far longer than any single human lifetime yet leaves an observable signature in the motions of white dwarfs today.

We can measure the resulting velocities with precise spectroscopy, but no one yet knows exactly what fraction of the mass must leave asymmetrically to match the observed speeds.

The same process may also explain why some apparently single white dwarfs show chemical traces of a former companion that was flung away.
