Picture a world the size of Neptune sitting so close to its star that a round-trip light signal would take less than a day to complete, the same distance a commercial jet covers between Vancouver and Tokyo.

Standard formation theory says giant planets must grow far from their stars where ice can help build solid cores, then migrate inward.

Yet this backward-orbiting Neptune appears to have arrived in the wrong direction without leaving the usual trail of aligned companions.

The mechanism involves early disk interactions that can flip orbital direction when a planet scatters off a sibling or when the disk itself warps under uneven magnetic torques.

One concrete measurement shows the planet completes its reversed orbit in roughly eleven days at a separation of only 0.06 astronomical units.

A distance where temperatures exceed one thousand degrees and any original atmosphere should have been stripped.

But nobody knows why the migration reversed instead of simply shrinking the orbit while keeping the original direction.

The unanswered question is whether such flipped giants are common or whether we are seeing the rare survivors of chaotic early-system rearrangements.
