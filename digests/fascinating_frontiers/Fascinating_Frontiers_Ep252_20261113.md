1. **Euclid Releases First Major Dataset of 900 Million Galaxies** — European Space Agency
   The Wide and Deep Surveys cover an area of sky equivalent to 9500 full moons.
   The release contains images with roughly 2.5 trillion pixels and occupies 1.7 million gigabytes.
   Spectra from 160 million sources are included along with 900 million galaxies and 60 million quasar candidates.
   Some galaxies in the data existed when the universe was only 700 million years old.
   The observations allow astronomers to map cosmic structures and measure gravitational lensing distortions.
   Euclid maps one third of the sky over a six-year mission and produces around 20 new images every day. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/science/euclid/euclid-lays-the-foundation-for-its-best-ever-3d-map-of-the-universe)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Prebiotic Molecules Record Conditions Across the Protoplanetary Disk
Asteroid samples preserve chemical fingerprints that formed billions of years ago in the swirling disk of gas and dust around the young Sun. The Bennu grains contain the amino acid β-alanine, the same molecule found in the Murchison meteorite that fell in Australia in 1969. By measuring isotopic ratios at the level of individual atoms within single molecules, researchers can distinguish formation pathways that operated at different distances from the Sun and under different temperature and radiation conditions.

The comparison shows that some β-alanine molecules carry isotopic signatures consistent with cold, outer-disk chemistry while others match warmer, inner-disk environments. These differences survive in the returned samples because the parent bodies never experienced the heating that would have erased them. The measurements therefore act as time capsules that map chemical diversity across the solar nebula at the moment when planets and asteroids were assembling.

What remains unknown is exactly how far apart these two formation zones were and whether similar variations appear in other amino acids or in samples from different asteroids. Future laboratory work that pairs the isotopic data with dynamical models of the disk will test whether the observed patterns require radial mixing of material or can be explained by static formation zones alone.
