From Europe’s strategic planning we turn to a concrete mission already nearing its destination.

The joint ESA-JAXA spacecraft known as BepiColombo is completing its final approach to Mercury after an eight-year journey.

Two separate orbiters on board will examine the surface makeup, the magnetic field and the thin exosphere once they reach the target.

Measurements are expected to shed light on why the planet carries an oversized core and what that reveals about conditions in the early solar system.

After a series of flybys the two orbiters will separate and settle into different paths around the planet.

Information on how the exosphere interacts with the solar wind should help explain the large core fraction and how heat and magnetic activity evolved on terrestrial worlds.

Mission teams will also look for signs of ancient volcanic activity that might connect Mercury's surface history to the first epochs of the inner solar system.

A new electromagnetic launch system under development would accelerate small satellites to orbital velocity from the ground. When merging black holes settle into a final state they emit a fading ringdown signal in gravitational waves. Researchers are exploring whether precise measurements of that signal could reveal deviations from the simple no-hair theorem. The approach focuses on the frequency and damping times of the dominant ringdown modes.

The NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory has produced an exceptionally deep image of the well-known COSMOS field.

While large surveys map galaxies, precise stellar measurements are revealing hidden black holes.

Data from the Gaia mission have identified three stellar-mass black holes each paired with a small companion star.

In two of the systems the companion orbits at a distance tighter than current formation models normally allow.

The close separations raise questions about how such pairs remain bound through the stages that usually disrupt binaries.

From quiet black holes we move to a proposed mechanism that may have helped spark life on early Earth.

Researchers have examined whether powerful ion beams tied to ancient auroras could have concentrated energy in Earth's early atmosphere.

Laboratory work indicates these beams can generate organic molecules at rates far above what steady sunlight achieves because the energy arrives in repeated intense pulses.

The beams would have operated between eighty and one hundred fifty kilometers altitude where charged particles break apart neutral molecules and send reactive fragments downward.

The process tends to narrow as ionization increases, focusing its effect in a manner similar to a natural particle accelerator.

One remaining uncertainty is whether the beams persisted long enough or across enough of the planet's surface to influence the rise of self-replicating chemistry.
