1. **ESA Astronaut Sophie Adenot Begins Return From Station** — European Space Agency
   ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot and her Crew-12 crewmates undocked from the International Space Station at around 12:05 GMT on 7 October 2026. 2. **Hubble Data Reveals Planet Forming After Star Death** — Starlust.org
   Doctoral candidate Jamie Williams at the University of Warwick identified niobium signatures in archival Hubble observations of white dwarf HS 0209+0832. The element was first recorded in 1999 among roughly 100 unidentified chemical features. Far Ultraviolet Spectroscopic Explorer data confirmed the niobium abundance. The material formed after the star became a white dwarf rather than during its main-sequence phase. Williams noted the white dwarf stage may represent only the first chapter of a longer planetary story. Source: [starlust.org](https://starlust.org/breakthrough-hubble-discovery-shows-signs-of-a-planet-forming-after-its-host-stars-death/)

3. **Arctic Sea Ice Reaches 2026 Annual Minimum** — NASA Breaking
   Sea ice extent hit its lowest point on 12 September 2026 at 4.6 million square kilometers. This value ties 2008, 2010, and 2025 for the tenth-lowest minimum in the satellite record. Every summer from 2007 through 2026 ranks among the twenty lowest extents observed since 1978. The 2026 minimum occurred earlier than the long-term average. NASA and the National Snow and Ice Data Center produced the measurements. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/arctic-sea-ice-shrinks-to-its-2026-minimum/)

5. **First Direct Radio Emission Detected From Exoplanet** — Trak.in
   Astronomers recorded repeating radio bursts from Beta Pictoris b using the MeerKAT array in South Africa. The planet is roughly twelve times more massive than Jupiter and orbits a star about twenty-three million years old. The bursts are attributed to auroras generated by the planet’s magnetic field. The field strength is estimated at least two hundred times stronger than Earth’s. Observations distinguished the planetary signal from emission by the host star. Source: [trak.in](https://trak.in/stories/first-ever-radio-signal-directly-detected-from-exoplanet-63-light-years-away/)

6. **Wandering Black Holes Traced Through Cosmological Simulation** — Universe Today
   Researchers used the ASTRID simulation to follow black holes across twelve point six billion years of cosmic evolution. The model tracks galaxies with stellar masses from ten million to one trillion solar masses. Wandering black holes were found to be more common in lower-mass galaxies where central gravity is weaker. The study was led by Yale PhD candidate Emma Jane Weller. Results appear in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/wandering-black-holes-may-hold-clues-to-the-evolution-of-the-universe)

7. **Black Hole Jets Heat Circumgalactic Gas And Trigger Star Formation** — Universe Today
   Observations combined DESI survey spectra with LOFAR radio jet data from hundreds of active galaxies. Ionized hydrogen emission aligned strongly with the axes of radio jets. Jets heat gas in the circumgalactic medium out to distances ten to twenty times the visible galaxy radius. The process can both quench and stimulate star formation depending on location. Sanchateeta Borthakur of Arizona State University and Namrata Roy of the Raman Research Institute led the work. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/galaxy-evolution-can-be-affected-by-black-hole-jets)

### Cosmic Spotlight
The Hubble detection of niobium around white dwarf HS 0209+0832 points to material synthesized after the star’s death. Niobium does not form through standard stellar fusion yet appears in high abundance in the archival spectra. Williams and colleagues traced the element to processes that occurred once the star had become a compact remnant. The finding suggests some planetary systems continue assembling long after their host stars leave the main sequence. Follow-up ultraviolet observations could map additional heavy elements and test whether the material is actively accreting onto a forming world.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Post-Main-Sequence Planet Formation
A typical white dwarf packs the mass of the Sun into a sphere the size of Earth, creating surface gravity two hundred thousand times stronger than what we feel here. Any planet that forms afterward must draw from gas and dust released during the star’s final red-giant phase or from debris scattered by earlier planetary migration. In the case of HS 0209+0832, the niobium signature implies the material condensed and accreted after the star had already shed its outer layers, a process that can take hundreds of millions of years. The same gravitational settling that pulls heavy elements downward in the white dwarf’s atmosphere also concentrates them in observable layers, turning the star into a natural spectrometer. Yet no model fully explains how enough mass survives to build a detectable planet rather than simply falling inward or being ejected. The open question is whether such second-generation worlds are rare curiosities or a common endpoint for systems whose first planets were destroyed during the star’s expansion.
