Now, here's something that blew my mind when I looked into it.

Earth’s magnetic field stretches into space far enough to enclose a volume larger than the planet itself, yet the solar wind constantly presses against it like an invisible river.

If you could ride outward with a packet of charged particles leaving the Sun, you would cover the distance to Earth in roughly four days while traveling at speeds between four hundred and eight hundred kilometres per second.

At that velocity the wind carries enough momentum to compress the dayside magnetosphere to about sixty thousand kilometres from Earth’s centre on average, while stretching the nightside into a long tail that reaches well beyond the Moon.

The boundary itself is not a solid wall; it is a thin current sheet where magnetic fields from the Sun and Earth reconnect, allowing some particles to leak inward and power the aurora.

Scientists can measure the exact location of this boundary with spacecraft, but they still cannot explain why reconnection events sometimes trigger global storms and sometimes fizzle with little effect.
