The European Space Agency’s Hera spacecraft captured its first resolved images of the Didymos-Dimorphos pair on October eighteenth at a distance of one thousand two hundred eighty kilometres.

Dimorphos appears as roughly one pixel, clearly separated from the larger parent body.

The image followed two braking manoeuvres that slowed the spacecraft to twenty metres per second relative to the asteroids.

Didymos measures seven hundred eighty metres across while Dimorphos spans one hundred fifty-one metres, orbiting an average one point one five kilometres apart.

Mission manager Ian Carnelli noted the team will obtain the first detailed images of Dimorphos on November twenty-fifth, examining the twenty twenty-two dart impact that sent debris across ten thousand kilometres of space and shortened Dimorphos’s orbital period by thirty-three minutes.

Hera will later deploy two CubeSats for closer surveys before attempting a landing on the smaller body, and those November observations will examine whether the impact left a crater or reshaped the asteroid entirely.

Four volunteers will exit the Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog habitat at Johnson Space Center on October thirty-first after three hundred seventy-eight days of isolation, NASA reports.

Data collected will inform food-system design, crew-time efficiency, and behavioural support for future Artemis and deep-space missions.

A June twenty twenty-six study in the Journal of Astronomical Telescopes, Instruments, and Systems, covered by Universe Today, outlines three Exploratory Analytic Cases for the Habitable Worlds Observatory.

EAC one uses a six-metre inscribed, seven point two-metre circumscribed off-axis mirror.

EAC two adopts a six-metre round off-axis design, and EAC three specifies an eight-metre outer-diameter on-axis mirror.

The Systems Engineering Team favoured off-axis architectures for unobstructed light collection and larger apertures to improve coronagraph performance.

End-to-end modelling has been completed for EAC one and shared with industry partners.

The coronagraph instrument blocks starlight to enable direct imaging of Earth-like exoplanets, and temperature control properties were also examined because they strongly affect light-collecting efficiency.

The Habitable Worlds Observatory remains scheduled for launch in the twenty forties and is intended to succeed the James Webb Space Telescope as NASA’s flagship observatory.

Researchers from Kyushu University and the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics observed the L1544 prestellar core in the Taurus Molecular Complex, one hundred forty parsecs away.

They measured a velocity difference between the ion tracer N two D plus and the neutral tracer para-N H two D using the IRAM thirty-metre telescope.

Neutral particles are decoupling from magnetic field lines in a process called ambipolar diffusion, falling inward faster than ions.

Prestellar cores in the study region reach temperatures between ten and thirty kelvin, cold enough for complex molecules to form on dust grains.

Lead researcher Doris Arzoumanian explained that strong magnetic fields can otherwise delay collapse.

The velocity offset provides the first direct measurement of ion-neutral drift on the scales where stars actually form.

A prestellar core the size of a modest city block can contain several solar masses of gas and dust, yet magnetic fields threading the larger cloud often act like rigid scaffolding that prevents collapse.

In that Taurus core, the ion tracer stayed tightly coupled to the field lines, while the neutral gas slipped ahead at zero point zero five kilometres per second.

That small offset is the moment gravity starts to win.

The process begins once the core grows dense enough to block external starlight.

With fewer ionising photons reaching the interior, the fraction of charged particles drops sharply.

Neutral molecules no longer feel the magnetic field directly; they only collide occasionally with the sparse ions that remain tied to the lines.

Over thousands of years those gentle collisions transfer momentum, but the neutrals still pull ahead.

As they accumulate at the centre they bend the field lines outward, reducing the magnetic pressure that once supported the core against gravity.

Even while that support slowly erodes, the interior stays cold enough for complex chemistry to proceed on dust-grain surfaces.

The same spectra that recorded the drift also show the core is chemically rich, with molecules that could serve as precursors to prebiotic compounds.

What remains unknown is whether every prestellar core experiences this decoupling, or whether some collapse through turbulence or external compression alone.

Future high-resolution maps of additional cores will reveal whether that velocity signature is universal, or whether stronger initial fields can suppress the process entirely.

Ganymede passed in front of Io as seen from Earth on October twentieth, twenty twenty-six, producing a mutual occultation recorded by astronomer Michael Wong.

The event occurs when Jupiter reaches equinox and its Galilean moons appear edge-on from our viewpoint, a geometry that repeats every six years.

Ganymede’s shadow also falls across Jupiter’s cloud tops, creating a solar eclipse for any hypothetical observers on the planet.

Jupiter currently has one hundred fifteen known moons, second only to Saturn.

Mutual occultations of the Galilean moons remain rare because they require Earth’s precise alignment with the orbital plane.

Observations with ESO’s Very Large Telescope reveal a rare double debris disk around a white dwarf, with the two components separated by a clear gap.

The disk consists of material from planets destroyed during the star’s red-giant phase.

The gap suggests ongoing dynamical clearing by surviving planets that avoided engulfment.

This configuration offers a glimpse of what may happen to our own solar system once the Sun becomes a white dwarf.
