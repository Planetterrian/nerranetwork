An international team spotted the earliest known seed of a galaxy supercluster through the ODIN survey.

Narrowband imaging isolated Lyman-alpha light from young galaxies inside the structure.

The object already held substantial mass when the universe was under two billion years old.

The result lines up with models that place the first large knots of the cosmic web at high redshift.

From the universe’s largest structures forming early, we turn to how individual galaxies and their central engines sometimes grow at very different rates.

Observations show some supermassive black holes pull in mass faster than their host galaxies build stars.

Large supplies of cold gas lose angular momentum and stream straight to the center.

In the strongest cases the light from this accretion outshines the galaxy’s total starlight by several times.

The trigger that lets so much gas reach the black hole without first forming stars remains unclear.

While black holes and galaxies show mismatched growth, closer to home new telescope pairings are revealing unexpected patterns among the solar system’s smallest members.

The James Webb Space Telescope and Hubble worked together for the first time on faint trans-Neptunian objects.

The combined data turned up fewer small bodies than earlier models expected.

Colors stayed consistent from the smallest detected objects up through larger ones.

The pattern points to formation conditions that stayed stable across the early outer solar system.

From the outer solar system’s preserved building blocks, we move to a dramatic new image of a stellar nursery right next door.

Hubble photographed nebula N44 and its central cavity measuring two hundred ten by one hundred forty light-years.

Winds and supernovae from young massive stars carved out the void.

Thick shells of gas and dust now border the cavity and host fresh star formation.

While Hubble shows star formation in action nearby, another spacecraft just proved a new navigation technique at an asteroid.

Hayabusa2 carried out the first laser ranging test during a July flyby of an asteroid.

The instrument returned precise distance readings as the spacecraft passed the target.

The successful run shows the method works at asteroid range and widens options for future navigation.

Reusable launch progress on Earth contrasts with long-term questions about how our own Moon came to be.

Updated simulations test whether a giant impact could produce the Earth-Moon isotope match within hours.

Temperature and material strength during the collision stand out as the main variables.

Moon formation models remind us that planetary systems can lose moons as well as gain them.

From lost moons we shift to water delivery in young planetary systems still under construction.

Astronomers at Lund University found signs of exocomets around the young star PDS 70.

The comets appear to carry water ice inward toward the inner disk.

The system serves as a nearby example of the processes that may have supplied early Earth with water.

Water delivery in other systems raises questions about what happens when giant impacts strip oceans from icy moons.

Simulations at Southwest Research Institute show large collisions remove or thin subsurface oceans on icy moons.

The models indicate impacts destroy existing water rather than create new oceans.

The findings apply to outer solar system moons that went through early heavy bombardment.

Ocean loss on moons leads naturally to efforts to make fuel from planetary atmospheres.

Researchers at the University of Mississippi are testing catalysts that turn Martian carbon dioxide into methane.

The work aims at lower byproduct levels under simulated Martian conditions.

The goal is in-situ propellant production for future return missions.

Propellant ideas on Mars connect to a rover still climbing and exploring the real Martian surface.

While one rover keeps climbing, another satellite keeps moving in Earth orbit with no announced reason.

The Hylas 2 satellite began its third major longitude shift since twenty twenty-four.

Tracking shows the spacecraft continues to change position, though no official explanation has been given.

Fast radio bursts offer another way to map matter across vast distances.

Dispersion measures from bursts traveling billions of light-years reveal gas density in galactic halos and the intergalactic medium.

Researchers are checking whether the signals can separate feedback effects from dark matter distributions.

The ODIN survey’s detection of the most distant galaxy proto-supercluster relied on narrowband imaging that isolates Lyman-alpha emission from forming galaxies.

This technique picks out structures still assembling when the universe was less than two billion years old.

The find strengthens the picture that the cosmic web’s largest knots began growing very early.

It also supplies a benchmark for simulations that must now reproduce such massive overdensities at high redshift.

How many more of these seeds remain hidden at even greater distances?

That is the scale difference astronomers now measure in some galaxies.

The central black hole can double its mass while the galaxy’s stars increase by only a modest fraction.
