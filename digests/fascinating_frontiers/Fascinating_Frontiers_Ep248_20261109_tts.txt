Hubble, according to Universe Today, measured both the oxygen thirty-seven nearby dwarf galaxies still hold and the oxygen they have lost across their lifetimes.

What remains sits mostly in gas, with smaller shares locked in stars and in dust, while radiation from massive stars and core-collapse supernovae have driven the rest into the circumgalactic medium and beyond.

The GLOW project folds new and archival data from Hubble, Spitzer, the Very Large Array, and the Australian Telescope Compact Array to follow gas and heavy elements among low-mass galaxies, their halos, and the intergalactic medium.

Grace Telford, second author on the paper and an assistant professor of physics and astronomy at the University of Utah, noted that the light and winds of heavy stars, and the supernovae that finish them, push oxygen-rich gas out of these galaxies as time goes on.

Most of that oxygen is forged in core-collapse supernovae, the explosive deaths of massive stars, though a very small fraction also comes from other supernovae when remnants of low-mass stars become unstable or merge.

GLOW is a large-scale effort jointly funded by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, and the National Science Foundation, and massive galaxies like the Milky Way have had lost oxygen tallied in similar ways; computer models of galaxy growth still fall short of the loss seen in these dwarfs, a gap tied to gravity too weak to keep the gas at home.

A dwarf galaxy may span only a few thousand light-years from edge to edge and still form stars at a steady rate.

That is a small island of stars, and the whole system is light enough that the energy from a handful of supernovae can blow material clean out.

Set that island beside a familiar yardstick: the continental United States runs roughly three thousand miles from coast to coast.

A dwarf galaxy is thousands of times larger in linear extent than that coast-to-coast run, yet its gravitational grip stays fragile enough that a few energetic events can unbind its gas.

A few thousand light-years is modest next to a giant spiral, and that modest size is why a handful of explosions can empty so much of the reservoir.

In the Milky Way you get about one oxygen atom mixed into two thousand atoms of hydrogen.

In these dwarfs that count falls to one oxygen among ten thousand to twenty thousand hydrogen atoms. [pause]

The drop comes because the weaker pull cannot hold the heated, metal-loaded gas that starlight pressure and supernova shock waves throw outward.

Bigger galaxies lose metal-rich gas too, but dwarfs sit in shallower gravitational wells, so the gas can leave more easily and more thoroughly than it can from a heavy spiral.

Massive stars couple their energy to the surrounding gas through more than one route.

Radiation is one channel. Cosmic rays are another. Supernovae going off in clusters are a third.

Which of those channels does the most efficient job of driving gas out is not settled, and it is not settled whether a bursty history of star formation, or a quieter one, changes how well that driving works.

The exact mix of forces that sets how much gas leaves any one dwarf is still not known.
