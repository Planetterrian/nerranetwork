1. **Fermi Closes the MeV Gap with Fourteen-Year Map — Phys.org**
   The Fermi Large Area Telescope Collaboration has produced the clearest view yet of low-energy gamma rays by identifying four hundred fifty cosmic sources between twenty and two hundred megaelectronvolts. This range had remained a persistent blind spot because earlier instruments lacked the sensitivity to measure it reliably. The new catalog supplies the first systematic bridge across what astronomers call the MeV gap. The work appears in The Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series. ### Cosmic Spotlight
A team at the Trottier Institute for Research on Exoplanets discovered an unusual triple-star system just twenty-eight light-years away while they were actually searching for planets. The system, named Welopōʻai, contains two white dwarfs and one red dwarf—an arrangement researchers say should not exist because the white dwarfs would have disrupted the red dwarf during their earlier evolution. The find came from precise radial-velocity measurements that revealed velocity changes far larger than those expected from planets alone. Professor Patrick Dufour noted the configuration challenges current models of how stars interact in close multiples. The discovery offers a nearby laboratory for testing binary and triple-star formation theories. Source: [inshorts.com](https://inshorts.com/en/news/astronomers-discover-triple-star-system--that-shouldn-t-exist--1795262562000)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Closing the MeV Gap
Gamma rays carry the highest energies in the electromagnetic spectrum, yet a wide slice of that range has stayed stubbornly difficult to map. Between roughly twenty and two hundred megaelectronvolts lies the so-called MeV gap, a band where photons are too energetic for conventional X-ray telescopes and too soft for the large-area detectors built for higher-energy gamma rays. Instruments simply could not collect enough clean signals to build reliable sky maps, leaving a blind spot right where many astrophysical processes are expected to shine brightest.

The Fermi Large Area Telescope has now changed that picture. Over fourteen years the instrument recorded enough photons to identify four hundred fifty distinct sources across the twenty-to-two-hundred megaelectronvolt window. The long integration time allowed the team to separate faint celestial signals from instrumental and cosmic-ray background noise that had previously swamped the data. Because the telescope orbits above the atmosphere, it avoids the absorption that blocks these photons from reaching ground-based detectors, giving it a unique vantage on the gap.

Even with this advance, important questions remain open. The catalog shows where the sources are, but astronomers still need to determine what physical mechanisms dominate at these energies and how the sources evolve over time. Follow-up observations with future instruments that can measure polarization or rapid variability in the same band would help pin down whether the emission comes from pulsar magnetospheres, active galactic nuclei, or something entirely unexpected. Until those measurements arrive, the MeV sky stays only partially charted.
