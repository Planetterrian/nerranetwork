Japan's Fugaku supercomputer ran high-resolution models of low-mass neutron-star binaries, according to Phys.org.

Those stars started with magnetic fields around three trillion gauss, already in the upper range for ordinary neutron stars.

The study is posted on the arXiv under the title "A magnetar formation in binary neutron star merger."

Most neutron stars sit above the Chandrasekhar limit that caps white dwarfs, yet remain below the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit near two point six solar masses.

That mass window lets some mergers leave a single neutron star instead of a black hole.

The calculation opened just before the stars touched and stopped shortly after contact, while the mixed fluid still had time to change the remnant.

Imagine two spheres the size of cities, each denser than an atomic nucleus, spiraling in at hundreds of kilometers per second.

Contact does not flatten them into one lump.

The interface shears the way two rivers smash together at right angles.

The shear sets off Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, those rolling billows you get when wind lifts candle smoke.

Inside the neutron fluid the rolls stretch and fold embedded magnetic field lines the way taffy is pulled, packing more lines into the same volume and driving the field up.

That folding finished soon enough for the new field to steer the merger's later evolution before the remnant could settle.

Fields like these already sit thousands of times stronger than anything built on Earth.

The gain is not even.

It starts in a thin turbulent sheet and then spreads as the mixed fluid circulates outward.

The burst is fast enough to alter internal pressure support, and it may decide whether the object stays intact or collapses further.

The runs treated the two stars as equal in mass and covered the stretch from just before contact until the instability had finished its first work.

How far that turbulence can shove the field before it saturates or reconnects is still open.

The calculation halted once the first mixing burst ended.

Longer runs, or different mass ratios, might show extra gain or a field that starts to resist more stretching.

No telescope can yet read the internal field of a freshly merged neutron star, so these models are the clearest forecast we have for how at least some magnetars are born.

The same folding still operates when the starting fields are already intense, which hints the process is common among mergers that leave a neutron star behind.

Runs that continue past that first millisecond are needed to test whether the field keeps climbing or whether reconnection caps the gain.

The models show the rolls forming, yet no one knows exactly how the amplified field redistributes angular momentum inside the remnant, or whether it changes the odds of a later collapse to a black hole.
