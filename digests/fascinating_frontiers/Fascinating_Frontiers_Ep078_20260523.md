### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Starship V3 Completes First Flight Test Despite Engine Issue — Space.com**
   The vehicle lifted off from Texas carrying six third-generation Raptor engines on the upper stage. One vacuum-optimized engine shut down early, yet the flight computer extended burn time on the remaining five to reach a usable suborbital path. The upper stage executed a banking maneuver during descent that previews the profile needed for a future tower catch at Starbase. Heat-shield performance held up well enough for a controlled splashdown in the Indian Ocean. The booster stage experienced an uncontrolled hard landing in the Gulf of Mexico after separate engine problems. Next steps include post-flight data review before any decision on the following test flight.

2. **Mars Fungi Could Turn Regolith into Crop Soil — Universe Today**
   Researchers are testing beneficial fungi that break down Martian regolith and improve its ability to support plant growth. The approach would let future crews grow food locally instead of shipping every calorie from Earth. Early experiments focus on how the fungi interact with the mineral-heavy soil and whether they can sustain repeated crop cycles. A single successful demonstration could reduce the mass of supplies needed for long-duration surface stays. Follow-up work will examine how the same microbes behave under actual Martian radiation and temperature swings.

3. **Olympic Rower Tests Prototype Space Gym in Weightlessness — BBC**
   British Olympic rower Helen Glover used a parabolic flight to float through a compact exercise device designed for future space stations. The test checked whether rowing motions can provide effective resistance training when there is no gravity to work against. Engineers recorded muscle activation and machine stability during repeated zero-g intervals. Data from the session will guide refinements before the hardware is considered for longer missions. The project aims to keep crews healthy on trips that last months rather than days.

4. **JPL Management Contract Opens to Competition for First Time Since 1930s — Space Daily**
   NASA has released a draft solicitation for the contract that has run Caltech’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory for decades. The agency’s language emphasizes performance metrics and cost controls that go beyond routine renewal language. Current work at JPL includes multiple Mars missions, the Europa Clipper spacecraft, and technology development for future astrophysics observatories. Any change in operator would require an extensive transition plan to avoid disrupting ongoing projects. Comments on the draft are due before NASA issues the final request for proposals.

5. **First Quarter Moon Highlights Vallis Alpes This Weekend — Astronomy Magazine**
   Observers can target the Alpine Valley, a 130-kilometer-long cleft cutting across the lunar Alps, while the Moon is at first quarter phase. The feature is best seen when sunlight strikes it at a low angle, casting long shadows that reveal its depth and rilles. Nearby craters and the Montes Alpes mountain range provide additional detail for modest telescopes. The same lunar phase also places Jupiter’s moons in a compact grouping that changes noticeably over a single evening. Sky This Week columns list additional events through the end of the month.

6. **Hong Kong’s First Astronaut Selected for Shenzhou-23 Mission — South China Morning Post**
   Lai Ka-ying will become the first person from Hong Kong to fly on a Chinese space station mission. She was chosen after a national selection process that included medical and technical evaluations. The Shenzhou-23 flight is scheduled to dock with the Tiangong station and conduct a series of science experiments. Her selection marks a milestone in expanding China’s astronaut corps beyond the original cohort. Training will focus on station systems and the specific research tasks assigned to the crew.

7. **Musk Calls Starship Flight 12 an “Epic” Achievement — Spaceflight Now**
   The CEO noted that the upper stage reached its intended trajectory even after losing one engine early in the ascent. Five remaining engines ran longer than planned to compensate, demonstrating the vehicle’s ability to adapt in real time. The flight also tested a descent profile that mimics the final approach for a future booster catch. Post-flight analysis will examine how the heat shield and control surfaces performed under flight conditions. The company views the test as a step toward rapid reusability goals.

8. **Biggest Starship Yet Splashes Down in Indian Ocean — San Antonio Express-News**
   The upper stage completed a controlled descent and landed in the target zone after traveling thousands of kilometers downrange. Onboard cameras captured the final approach and confirmed the vehicle remained intact through peak heating. The booster stage did not achieve a soft landing after engine issues developed during its return. Teams are already reviewing telemetry to identify the specific hardware or software adjustments needed for the next vehicle.

9. **Russian Radar Satellites Maneuver Near Commercial Spacecraft — r/space**
   Four Russian satellites have moved into orbits that bring them within close range of an ICEYE synthetic-aperture radar satellite. The proximity is described as unusual for satellites on standard operational missions. Observers are tracking the objects to understand whether the moves represent routine station-keeping or something more deliberate. No immediate impact on the commercial satellite’s data collection has been reported.

10. **Amateur Astronomer Shares Jupiter Moon Timelapse — r/astronomy**
    A 2.5-hour sequence captured with a Takahashi CN-212 Cassegrain shows Europa, Ganymede, and Io crossing the face of Jupiter. Each frame combines 600 short exposures that were later stacked and processed. The video reveals the moons’ changing positions relative to the planet’s cloud bands over a single night. Observers can replicate similar sequences with mid-sized telescopes when Jupiter is well placed.

11. **China Continues Crewed Space Station Operations — The Killeen Daily Herald**
    Recent reports outline ongoing activity at the Tiangong complex, including crew rotations and experiment handovers. The station supports a growing set of life-science and materials research projects. International partners have expressed interest in future collaboration opportunities. Details on the next crewed launch remain tied to the Shenzhou schedule.

12. **Meteor Shower Calendar Created for Public Use — r/astronomy**
    A new shared calendar lists upcoming major showers and other notable sky events with automatic timezone conversion. Users can import the file into Google Calendar, iCal, or Outlook. The creator built the tool after missing a shower due to lack of advance notice. The calendar will be updated as new events are confirmed by professional networks.

### Cosmic Spotlight
SpaceX’s Starship V3 upper stage reached space on its first flight even after losing one of six Raptor engines shortly after ignition. The flight computer automatically extended the burn on the remaining engines to protect the trajectory, then guided the vehicle through a banking maneuver that previews future landing profiles. Heat-shield tiles survived the peak-heating phase well enough for a precise splashdown in the Indian Ocean. The test also revealed that the booster stage still needs further work after an uncontrolled landing in the Gulf of Mexico. What remains unclear is how quickly the team can close the remaining performance gaps before attempting a booster catch on a subsequent flight.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Heat Shields Survive Reentry
If you scaled a typical reentry speed to everyday distances, a spacecraft would cross the length of a football field in less than a millisecond while the air in front of it is compressed to temperatures above 1,600 °C. The shield does not try to absorb that heat; instead it sheds most of it by ionizing the air into a plasma that carries energy away from the vehicle. Starship’s tiles must withstand not only the temperature spike but also the mechanical stress of a banking turn at hypersonic speeds, where even small gaps can let superheated gas reach the underlying structure. Engineers still cannot predict exactly how the tiles will erode after dozens of flights because the interaction between material microstructure, plasma chemistry, and vibration remains only partly modeled. The open question is whether any current tile formulation can survive the cumulative damage of repeated high-energy entries without requiring replacement after every mission.
