### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **BepiColombo Turns Off Ion Engines for Mercury Arrival — European Space Agency**
   The ESA/JAXA spacecraft has switched off its solar electric propulsion system after operating it for eight years across the inner solar system. The mission now begins its final approach sequence, with critical arrival maneuvers scheduled to start in September. The blue glow from the engines marked one of the longest continuous uses of ion propulsion on a planetary mission. Engineers will rely on chemical thrusters and gravity assists for the remaining trajectory adjustments. The next milestone watchers are tracking is the September start of the arrival phase. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Operations/End_of_the_blue_glow_BepiColombo_turns_off_solar_electric_propulsion_for_Mercury_arrival)

2. **Oxford Team Finds Evidence of Ancient Magma Systems on Mars — Phys.org**
   Researchers at the University of Oxford have identified signs of enormous, Earth-like magmatic systems that once existed deep beneath the Martian surface. The findings challenge the long-held view that plate tectonics are required for such complex geology. Data from orbital instruments revealed layered structures consistent with repeated episodes of large-scale melting and intrusion. These hidden systems would have shaped the planet’s crust and mantle over billions of years. Follow-up studies will examine whether similar features appear in other regions of Mars. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-scientists-evidence-vast-hidden-magma.html)

3. **Uranus and Neptune May Contain Magma Oceans Instead of Icy Mantles — Universe Today**
   New interior models suggest the two outer planets could host hot, rocky magma layers rather than the traditional water-ammonia ices. Voyager 2 flyby data from 1986 and 1989 remain the only direct measurements, leaving room for alternative compositions. Revised equations of state show that silicate mantles under high pressure and temperature could match the observed densities and magnetic fields. The shift would change how scientists interpret heat flow and atmospheric dynamics on both worlds. Future orbiter missions will test these predictions through gravity and magnetic measurements. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/uranus-neptune-may-be-magma-worlds-not-ice-giants)

4. **Euclid Captures Detailed View of Milky Way’s Central Bulge — Universe Today**
   The ESA telescope spent 26 hours collecting a high-resolution image of the crowded stellar field near the galactic center. Although this observation falls outside the mission’s primary cosmology program, the data will support gravitational microlensing searches planned for NASA’s Roman Space Telescope. The image reveals millions of stars packed into a region only a few degrees across. Processing the crowded field required new algorithms to separate overlapping sources. Astronomers expect the catalog to aid studies of stellar populations and dark matter distribution in the inner galaxy. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/euclids-new-portrait-of-the-milky-ways-crowded-bulge)

5. **Ancient Stellar Flyby May Still Influence Long-Period Comets — Phys.org**
   Gaia data allowed researchers to trace a star that passed close enough to disturb the Oort cloud several million years ago. The gravitational perturbation is thought to have increased the number of comets sent toward the inner solar system. Nathan Kaib of the Planetary Science Institute and Sean Raymond of Université de Bordeaux presented the work at the American Astronomical Society Division on Dynamical Astronomy meeting. Models show some of those altered orbits could still be delivering comets today. Continued monitoring of new long-period comets will help test the connection. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-ancient-stellar-flyby-period-comets.html)

6. **Hubble Spots Tiny Galaxy Clearing Early Universe Fog — Universe Today**
   The galaxy, observed as it appeared 1.4 billion years after the Big Bang, is emitting intense ultraviolet radiation capable of ionizing surrounding hydrogen. Hubble’s ultraviolet sensitivity captured the object during its brief window of activity. This detection supports the idea that small, starburst galaxies contributed significantly to reionization. The galaxy’s compact size and high star-formation rate make it an efficient source of ionizing photons. Additional observations with JWST are planned to measure its exact contribution to clearing the intergalactic medium. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/hubble-details-early-galaxy-transforming-neighborhood)

Its microwave radiometer measures temperature and moisture profiles through clouds. Early imagery confirms the instrument is meeting performance expectations for weather forecasting applications. The Boulder-based company plans to expand the constellation with additional satellites. Data validation against ground stations is the next step before operational use. Source: [spacenews.com](https://spacenews.com/weather-stream-releases-first-light-imagery-from-gems2-microwave-radiometer/)

The contract covers construction of satellites designed to provide secure voice and data links for mobile forces. Integration of the 702MP’s electric propulsion and high-power payload capabilities is underway. Launch dates for the first satellites have not yet been announced. The selections mark another step in NASA’s use of small launch providers for dedicated science missions. Mission integration schedules are now being finalized. Copernicus services are using the data to assess heat-stress impacts on populations and agriculture. Additional passes are scheduled as the heatwave continues. Farmers are drawing on groundwater to support the expansion. The time series from Sentinel-2 allows monitoring of field growth and water-use patterns. Similar developments are being tracked in other arid regions.

### Cosmic Spotlight
BepiColombo’s decision to shut down its ion engines marks the transition from cruise to arrival operations at Mercury. The solar-electric system provided the gentle, continuous thrust needed to reach the innermost planet despite limited launch mass. With the engines off, the spacecraft will rely on a series of gravity-assist flybys and short chemical burns to enter orbit. The long propulsion phase demonstrated how electric systems can stretch the reach of modest launch vehicles. What remains unclear is how much residual xenon propellant will be left for any extended mission operations once the primary science phase begins.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Solar Electric Propulsion Enables Long Interplanetary Journeys
If the chemical rockets that lifted BepiColombo from Earth had to carry all the propellant needed for the entire trip, the spacecraft would have been impossibly heavy. Instead, its ion engines sip xenon and accelerate it to 40 kilometres per second, producing a thrust measured in millinewtons yet sustained for years. Over eight years the cumulative velocity change reached tens of kilometres per second while consuming only a few hundred kilograms of fuel. The journey itself becomes a slow spiral inward, trading time for mass efficiency in a way chemical stages cannot match. The unanswered question is how far this gentle push can be scaled before power limits or thruster lifetime become the next barrier.
