### Cosmic Spotlight
NASA’s Perseverance rover has captured an odd stack of rocks on the Martian surface that looks almost deliberately arranged. The formation stands out against the surrounding terrain and immediately raises questions about how such a pile could form naturally on Mars. Rovers and orbiters have documented many rock arrangements before, yet this one’s balanced appearance makes it especially intriguing for geologists studying wind, water, or impact processes. Close-up images and future spectroscopy could reveal whether the rocks share the same composition or arrived through different mechanisms. What process on Mars could stack stones so neatly without human help?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Space Interferometry Creates Giant Virtual Telescopes
If two telescopes sit a kilometer apart, they can together resolve details as sharply as a single dish that wide, turning modest hardware into an instrument the size of a city block. Light waves from a distant star or galaxy reach each telescope at slightly different times, and precise electronics measure those tiny delays before computers combine the signals into one high-resolution image. The technique demands keeping the path lengths matched to within a fraction of a wavelength, which is why space-based versions must control vibrations and temperature changes at the nanometer level. A baseline of just 100 meters already delivers resolution equivalent to reading a license plate from hundreds of kilometers away. Yet even with these gains, astronomers still cannot directly image the surfaces of most exoplanets because the required baselines would stretch across thousands of kilometers. How do we maintain perfect synchronization across such distances when the only reference is the faint starlight itself?
