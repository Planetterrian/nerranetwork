1. **ispace-EUROPE and ALTEC Sign Memorandum of Understanding to Collaborate on Lunar Rover Testing** — ispace
   ispace-EUROPE S.A. and ALTEC S.p.A. signed a memorandum of understanding on 12 October to explore collaboration on lunar rover testing, mission simulations and operational training. The signing took place at the 77th International Astronautical Congress in Antalya, Türkiye, with Vincenzo Giorgio, CEO of ALTEC, and Julien-Alexandre Lamamy, CEO of ispace-EUROPE, participating in the ceremony. Under the agreement the two organisations will evaluate opportunities for ispace-EUROPE to use ALTEC facilities for simulating rover platform operations inside lunar analogue environments. Planned activities include functional testing of lunar rover platforms and ancillary devices, mission simulations, operator rehearsals, subsystem and system-level verification and validation, plus targeted training and certification for ispace-EUROPE’s lunar mission operations team. The MoU also opens discussion of broader cooperation on lunar exploration technologies, lunar mobility systems and space resource utilisation. ALTEC is a public-private company based in Turin and owned by Thales Alenia Space and the Italian Space Agency. Since 2019 ALTEC has run the Rover Operations Control Centre that supports ground testing for ESA’s ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover. The company is now building a new Lunar Simulation Centre funded by the Italian Space Agency to provide analogue environments for testing robotic lunar exploration technologies and operational concepts. For ispace-EUROPE the partnership increases access to specialised European testing and operations infrastructure while the company develops its family of lunar rover systems and future surface mobility missions. ### Cosmic Spotlight
The ispace-EUROPE and ALTEC agreement opens access to a dedicated Lunar Simulation Centre that will replicate lunar surface conditions for rover hardware and operations teams. ALTEC already runs the control centre for the ExoMars rover, giving it direct experience translating simulation results into flight procedures. The partnership could shorten the time needed to validate mobility systems and train operators before any flight hardware reaches the Moon. Future work may also examine technologies for lunar resource utilisation once the simulation infrastructure is complete. The new centre adds to Italy’s growing role in planetary exploration through ASI funding and existing operational heritage with ESA missions.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Spacecraft Match Orbits During Rendezvous
Picture two cars on a circular highway where one vehicle travels at 28 000 kilometres per hour; closing the gap requires the trailing craft to speed up, climb into a higher lane, then slow down again so both vehicles end up side by side at exactly the same speed. That sequence is what happens every time a capsule approaches the International Space Station. A spacecraft first enters a slightly lower orbit, travels faster relative to the station, then performs a series of burns to raise its path until the two trajectories coincide. Each burn must be timed to within seconds because a one-minute error can leave the vehicles hundreds of kilometres apart after a single lap. The final approach occurs at relative speeds of only a few centimetres per second so docking mechanisms can engage without damage. Engineers still cannot predict exactly how small attitude-control thruster firings will interact with the station’s own micro-movements caused by solar-panel flexing and crew activity inside. We can see the geometry work in every successful docking, yet nobody knows precisely how much margin remains when the next generation of commercial vehicles begins flying more frequent, higher-traffic rendezvous.
