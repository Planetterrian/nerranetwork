Researchers have identified a new class of super-puff exoplanets with radii similar to Jupiter yet densities lower than cotton candy.

These worlds orbit close to their stars, where intense radiation should strip atmospheres, yet the envelopes remain inflated.

Transit and radial-velocity measurements supplied the size and mass values that revealed the extreme low densities.

The discovery challenges standard models of how gas giants retain heat and mass during migration.

Follow-up spectroscopy with large telescopes will test whether the low densities come from extended hazy layers or internal heat sources.

A University of Alabama in Huntsville study proposes that charged dust particles near the Sun help transport energy into the corona.

The Astrophysical Journal paper models how these grains interact with the solar magnetic field and plasma waves.

Observations show the corona reaches millions of degrees while the surface stays at roughly five thousand five hundred degrees Celsius, a long-standing puzzle.

Dust trajectories calculated in the new work suggest repeated collisions and electromagnetic interactions could supply the missing heat.

Future Parker Solar Probe passes closer to the Sun will search for direct signatures of these dust-plasma couplings.

Astronomers have compiled rotation periods and activity levels for thousands of stars to improve target selection for habitable-zone planets.

Stars with lower activity produce steadier signals, making small planetary atmospheres easier to detect.

The work directly supports planning for future missions that will measure transmission spectra of rocky worlds.

The next step is cross-matching the catalog with upcoming transit surveys to prioritise the quietest host stars.

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope has examined a planet orbiting the remnant of a Sun-like star that has become a white dwarf.

The data reveal the planet's atmosphere and orbital distance remained intact despite the star's earlier expansion and mass loss.

Spectra show molecular features that survived the intense radiation and dynamical upheaval of the stellar transition.

The system offers a rare glimpse of post-main-sequence planetary evolution.

Additional Webb cycles will map temperature variations across the planet's dayside to refine survival models.

Measurements from the Chandra X-ray Observatory indicate the outer spiral arms of the Milky Way reach wider than earlier maps showed.

Astronomers used precise distances to dust clouds absorbing X-rays to trace arm locations.

The new positions imply the galaxy's disk is more extended than standard models assumed.

Refined arm geometry will affect calculations of the galaxy's total mass and rotation curve.

Follow-up observations with optical and radio telescopes will test the revised structure.

If the Milky Way were scaled to the size of a dinner plate, a passing galaxy the size of a saucer would stretch our outer stars into ribbons hundreds of plate-widths long.

During a close flyby, the gravitational gradient across a galaxy pulls stars on the near side forward while stars on the far side lag behind, creating two opposing streams of material.

These tidal tails can extend tens to hundreds of thousands of light-years and persist for hundreds of millions of years after the encounter.

The process moves stars at speeds of tens of kilometres per second relative to the parent galaxy, enough to fling some into intergalactic space.

We still do not know exactly how much dark matter is needed to keep the tails coherent versus letting them disperse into the cosmic web.

The technique opens a new pathway for characterising wide-orbit giants around bright stars.
