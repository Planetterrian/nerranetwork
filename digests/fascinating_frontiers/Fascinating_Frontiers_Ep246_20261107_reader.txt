China's Chang'e five samples formed only about two billion years ago, yet the rock still carries volcanic textures.

Standard cooling models had the Moon's interior frozen solid well before those lavas could have formed, with the mantle cold and locked. 

As the Moon travels around Earth, Earth's gravity raises tides in the lunar rock that repeat on a monthly cycle.

That pull stretches and squeezes the interior again and again, and friction inside the rock turns that mechanical energy into heat, the way a paperclip grows warm when it is bent back and forth.

Over billions of years that steady input can keep pockets of the mantle above the melting point even while the rest of the body cools.

Tidal heating reaches its peak when the Moon orbits closer to Earth.

Early in lunar history the Moon circled at roughly one-third its present distance, so the gravitational forces were far stronger.

That extra energy likely sustained widespread melting for hundreds of millions of years.

As the Moon receded through tidal interactions, the heating rate declined but never vanished completely.

The Chang'e five basalts indicate that at least some regions stayed warm enough for volcanism until relatively recently in lunar history.

Miao Chen of the Chengdu University of Technology and colleagues published the tidal-heating explanation in the journal Planet.

The cumulative effect of monthly flexing can offset the expected cooling, and the process operates most efficiently in regions where rock properties allow greater dissipation of tidal energy into heat.

We still do not know exactly how that heat was distributed through the Moon's interior.

Current models disagree on whether tidal energy concentrated in a thin layer near the core-mantle boundary or spread more evenly through the mantle.

Resolving the distribution requires new measurements of the Moon's present-day internal temperature profile and the precise rate at which heat continues to escape from the surface today.

Without those data, it is not possible to determine whether tidal heating alone accounts for the observed volcanic activity or whether additional mechanisms also contributed.
