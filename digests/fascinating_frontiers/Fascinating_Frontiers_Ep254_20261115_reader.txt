TOI-2535 b, watched by TESS at the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, according to Mashable, crossed its star repeatedly for seven years. A hot Jupiter is a giant planet that orbits close to its star, and a transit is that planet passing in front of the star as seen from Earth.

Those transits came as much as six hours early or late, then turned shorter and dimmer and ceased altogether earlier this year, as the path drifted from the star's centre out toward the rim.

An unseen companion at least as massive as Saturn, itself on a tilted orbit, is tugging the planet, and the pair will keep pulling on one another. TOI-2535 b is expected to come back into view in thirty to fifty years.

"The planet's orbit is tilting so quickly that its transit disappeared while we were watching," said Samuel Yee of UCLA.

Euclid, the telescope of the European Space Agency, or ESA, according to ScienceDaily, identified thirty-one quasars from when the universe was only six hundred seventy million years old. A quasar is a brilliant core of a young galaxy powered by a black hole.

Redshift is a measure of how much the universe has stretched an object's light. Two of the objects set new redshift records at seven point seven seven and seven point six nine.

Both were as bright as a trillion Suns and sit a little more than thirteen billion light-years from Earth. "This finding more than doubles the number of quasars we know of that are so ancient," said Antonio La Marca of ESA.

Closure invariants were applied, according to Phys.org, to original Event Horizon Telescope data of the M87 black hole. The method folds measurements from telescope triplets so atmospheric and instrumental errors drop out.

A machine-learning imaging approach called GenDIReCT, trained on ordinary photographs, then produced the reconstruction.

The ring's diameter and orientation agree with the twenty nineteen result for a black hole of six point five billion solar masses seen nearly face-on. Width and brightness of the ring differ a little from those earlier pictures.

University of Arkansas researchers used laser powder bed fusion, according to SciTechDaily, on Lunar Highlands Regolith Simulant LHS-1, a stand-in for highland moon dust. The process heats chosen patches of powder until the grains fuse to each other.

They printed blocks a few centimetres across, in cross and H shapes, that lock together like LEGO. The material is more full of pores than prints that use an added binder.

Vacuum and microgravity tests still have to come before the bricks can carry structural loads.

Beyond Pluto, a potentially enormous icy body has been detected, according to ScienceDaily, in a crowded region of the outer solar system and designated twenty twenty-six ED one hundred thirty-eight. The object was hidden for years before its discovery.

Confirmation would expand the solar system's official dwarf-planet count to six. The find also suggests more large worlds may remain undetected beyond Neptune.

Long-period radio transients have been traced, according to Phys.org, since twenty twenty-two to pairs containing a white dwarf and a red dwarf. A white dwarf is a collapsed stellar remnant, and a red dwarf is a small cool star.

The systems produce bright radio flashes from a few minutes apart to a few hours apart. The white dwarf's magnetic field and interaction with the companion star are thought to drive the emission.

More of these objects keep turning up as low-frequency surveys get more sensitive.

NASA's Voyager 1 is closing in on one light-day from Earth as it travels through interstellar space. One light-day is the distance light travels in a day.

The spacecraft still sends back measurements of the heliopause and of the interstellar medium. No other craft built by humans has gone this far.

Supernova SN Ares was split into multiple images by gravitational lensing from a massive galaxy cluster when the universe was four point nine billion years old. A supernova is a star exploding at the end of its life.

The James Webb Space Telescope, or JWST, recorded those distorted views at high resolution. A paper posted to arXiv on October twenty-third forecasts the arrival of the remaining images, roughly seventy years apart.

In brief, Jupiter and Mars, according to Space.com, appear just over one degree apart overnight and will make a close approach visible to the naked eye on the night of November fifteenth and sixteenth. Researchers, according to Phys.org, are evaluating a common food additive to keep solid particles suspended in gelled fuels, addressing a long-standing hybrid-propellant challenge.

Astronaut Koichi Wakata, according to GMA Network, highlighted research, manufacturing, and human-spaceflight prospects for ASEAN partners as commercial access to orbit grows.

Matt Cook, according to the Telegraph and Argus, used to explain galaxy formation on building sites in Bradford and now leads the UK Space Agency's exploration programme.

He heads work on astronauts, lunar missions, and the Rosalind Franklin Mars rover built in Stevenage. He attended the Artemis II launch, which he described as a life highlight.

Cook credits his multicultural hometown with preparing him for international collaboration and urges young people not to self-reject when applying for space roles.

Gravity can send light from a single explosion down several curved paths around a massive galaxy cluster, so each image arrives at a different time because the light has travelled a different distance.

That delay comes from extra path length on the longer routes, which can grow to years or decades even though the blast itself happened once. 

Repeated high-resolution images over the coming decades are still required to check the predicted arrival windows and to see how closely the cluster's mass distribution fits the models. The same future images would test whether small tweaks to the lens model change the expected delays.

Oxygen leaking from dwarf galaxies, gravitational waves from white-dwarf pairs, and Euclid's giant galaxy map were the through-lines of the past seven days. Dwarf galaxies keep only fifteen percent of the oxygen their stars produce, and that is still where that result stands.

Tight white-dwarf pairs could give away hidden exoplanets across the Milky Way through gravitational-wave frequency sidebands that LISA can detect.
