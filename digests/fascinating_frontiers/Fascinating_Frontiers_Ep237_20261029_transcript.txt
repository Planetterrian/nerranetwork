Venus swings between Earth and the Sun once every year and a half, lining up almost directly
with our star from where we stand.
In that alignment, the planet's night side faces us, and only a thin sliver of sunlit
air stays in view.
That sliver forms a glowing crescent whose tips stretch past a simple half-circle.
Those stretched tips are the horns, made when sunlight slips through the dense upper atmosphere
and bends around the curved limb.
The same lineup that gives us a monthly new moon therefore gives Venus a new Venus phase.
Venus's thick air scatters and refracts the light, so the crescent looks brighter and wider
than geometry alone would allow.
The new moon stays dark in that geometry.
Venus' backlit atmosphere instead throws a glowing arc that can extend beyond 180 degrees.
Ancient Babylonian astronomers wrote down the brightness changes thousands of years ago.
The Maya kept similar records in their oldest surviving book, and other cultures logged the
same cycle.
The crescent can be caught with binoculars or a small telescope in twilight, and on rare
air nights people with very sharp eyesight have seen it with no optics at all.
Because the alignment repeats on a clockwork schedule, observers can say in advance when
the horns will show again.
