Planetary scientists have revised their picture of the ice at Mars north pole.

They combined measurements from the OMEGA spectrometer on Mars Express, the CRISM instrument on Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and the Surface Stereo Imager that flew on the Phoenix lander.

The new calculation shows the top layer holds only three percent dust by mass.

Earlier models had assumed twenty five percent dust by mass.

The difference comes from recognizing that the ice sits in distinct layers with thin dust sheets between them.

Seasonal frost that forms in winter carries more dust while summer exposes cleaner older ice beneath.

The study appears in npj Space Exploration.

Next up, a study of astronaut health after long flights.

Researchers reviewed medical records for U.S. astronauts and found higher hip fracture rates after missions longer than ninety days.

They used Bayesian probabilistic modeling to work around the small number of people who have flown in space.

Lead investigator Jean D. Sibonga at NASA Johnson Space Center noted that astronauts often feel recovered and return to demanding activities even though bone loss continues.

Co-author Matthew T. Drake pointed out that prior studies were limited by small sample sizes.

The work is published in Mayo Clinic Proceedings and calls for more attention to long-term skeletal health.

Astronomers have captured the first direct image of a protoplanet still embedded in its birth disk.

The Atacama Large Millimeter submillimeter Array revealed WISPIT two b at fifty seven astronomical units from its star.

The object is roughly five times the mass of Jupiter and lies four hundred thirty light years away.

A second protoplanet, WISPIT two c, orbits closer at fifteen astronomical units with a mass between eight and twelve Jupiter masses.

Lead author Myriam Benisty reported clear signs of both planets carving gaps and cavities while gas swirls appear around the outer body.

Those swirls match structures long predicted by computer models but never seen before.

The image is published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

A different team has found evidence that even a relatively cool lava world can hold an atmosphere.

They observed the secondary eclipse of HD three one six seven b with the James Webb Space Telescope.

The planet is Earth sized, orbits its K type star every zero point nine six Earth days, and lies one hundred fifty four light years away.

Its measured dayside temperature came in cooler than expected, pointing to heat being carried to the night side.

The result challenges the assumption that only the hottest lava worlds can sustain atmospheres.

The paper appears in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
