1. **Unseen companion likely a 2.2-solar-mass neutron star** — Phys.org Space
   Astronomers identified a red giant star with an unseen binary companion using data from LAMOST and APOGEE. The companion's mass falls between 1.8 and 2.8 solar masses, with a best estimate of 2.2 solar masses. The system, called J0008+0128, completes an orbit in about 3.3 years on a near-circular path. No light was detected from the companion, ruling out a regular star. Three hundred thousand POSYDON simulations of isolated binaries failed to reproduce the system's masses and orbital properties. The researchers conclude the companion is most likely a neutron star pushing the upper mass limit. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-supermassive-neutron-star-mass-gap.html)

### Cosmic Spotlight
Gemini astronaut photos from 1966 helped make the case for dedicated Earth-observation satellites. Lovell and Aldrin captured images of the Red Sea and Nile valley during Gemini XII, demonstrating the value of orbital imagery for studying Earth’s surface. Those pictures directly influenced William T. Pecora’s push for what became the Landsat program. The photos showed scientists could monitor crops, flooding, and land use from space. Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/gemini-astronaut-photos-inspired-early-earth-observation-program/)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: The Mass Gap Between Neutron Stars and Black Holes
Neutron stars form from the cores of exploded high-mass stars and pack roughly 1.4 solar masses into a sphere only about 20 kilometers across. Black holes, by contrast, typically begin at five solar masses and climb from there. Between these two populations sits a sparsely populated region from roughly 2.5 to 5 solar masses where few objects have been found. The gap matters because it marks the boundary where the equations describing matter under extreme pressure cease to support a stable neutron star and instead allow collapse into a black hole. Astronomers still lack a precise measurement of that transition mass because the relevant physics inside these objects remains poorly understood.

A recent study published in Science Advances identified a candidate that may help pin down the limit. Using spectra from LAMOST and APOGEE, researchers found a red giant star orbited by an unseen companion whose mass is estimated at 2.2 solar masses, with an upper range reaching 2.8 solar masses. The binary completes an orbit every 3.3 years on a surprisingly circular path, and no light is detected from the companion itself. Three hundred thousand simulations of isolated binary evolution failed to reproduce the observed combination of mass, separation, and circular orbit, suggesting the system formed through an unusual pathway or that current models miss key physics.

What remains unknown is whether objects in this mass range are always neutron stars that formed with unusually high birth masses or whether some represent the lightest black holes. A single additional detection with a measured radius or a clear signature of a solid surface would settle the question by showing whether the object can support itself against further collapse. Until then, each new system like J0008+0128 narrows the range of allowed equations of state for ultra-dense matter.
