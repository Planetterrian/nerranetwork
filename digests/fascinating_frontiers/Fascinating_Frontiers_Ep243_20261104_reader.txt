Virginia Tech engineers, according to Phys.org, are teaching machines to listen for the answer instead.

The method begins with a tap on whatever rock still sticks out of the ground.

A hammer fitted with sensors strikes that exposed face, and a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer records how the vibration runs through the buried mass and comes back out.

To train the system, the team planted eighteen concrete blocks in sand, each a different size and set at a different depth.

They measured vibration at several points on every exposed surface.

Those frequency patterns, together with the visible geometry of each block, went into a neural network built with just two hidden layers.

Once that network was trained, it could estimate burial depth from a fresh set of vibration readings.

Loose sand and solid rock carry vibration differently, so a buried boulder sends back a signature that a pebble sitting on the surface does not.

On Mars or the Moon, a robotic excavator could use that contrast to stop wasting work on a rock it cannot move.

Erik Komendera led the project with Ph.D. candidate Yiyan Ruan.

Komendera is an assistant professor of mechanical engineering who previously worked as an engineer at NASA Langley Research Center.

The results appear in the IEEE Sensors Journal.

Soil stiffness, contact angle, and rock shape all change the signal that comes back.

Burial depth is still hard to read, because the feedback depends on the rock's geometry and on how the soil grips it.

The team selected features from the vibration data and from the exposed shape, and that selection produced reliable estimates even with the small network.

For now they plan to keep building datasets and to keep testing the method on Earth.

The same tools could assess unstable rock formations, measure underground caverns, or help after disasters that leave hazards under debris.
