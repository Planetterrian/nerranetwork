White dwarfs pack the mass of the Sun into a volume the size of Earth, so their atmospheres are thousands of times denser than the Sun's.

In ordinary stars the light we see follows a smooth blackbody curve set only by temperature, yet the new models show that a few percent of white dwarfs deviate from that curve.

Add carbon at the same six thousand kelvin and the tint turns pink or purple because the element absorbs and re-emits light at specific bands.

Models treat the stars as blackbodies whose emission depends only on temperature, yet the added elements shift the observed spectrum.

Dense atmospheres allow trace elements to alter that pattern in ways impossible for main-sequence stars.

The same dense layers also trap debris from destroyed planets, and green tones appear when that material settles into the atmosphere.

Cooler white dwarfs with hydrogen and helium tend toward cyan or blue because those gases suppress certain parts of the visible range.

These effects only appear because the atmospheres are so compressed that even parts-per-million abundances of heavy elements become optically important.

In a normal star the same trace amounts would be invisible against the much lower density.

The team's computer models treat the atmosphere as a stratified layer where pressure, temperature and composition interact at every depth.

Small changes in the abundance of carbon can swing the output from pink to purple depending on the exact temperature.

The five stars in the nearby sample already demonstrate that the phenomenon is repeatable rather than a modeling artifact.

Because the color shift depends on both temperature and composition, observers can now use broadband filters to flag candidates for follow-up spectroscopy instead of examining every white dwarf in detail.

Future observations with larger telescopes could measure the actual spectra of these objects and confirm whether the predicted colors match reality.

If they do, color surveys could become an efficient first filter for identifying white dwarfs that once hosted planets.

The remaining puzzle is whether these colored stars represent a brief transitional phase or a stable end state, and whether the same process operates in white dwarfs that formed through different channels.

We can already see the color difference in the models, but we do not yet know how often the responsible elements arrive from outside versus how often they are dredged up from the star's own interior.

The open question is how long these color signatures persist before the elements sink or are mixed away.

Some white dwarfs may show the effect only during a narrow window after a planetary disruption event.

Others could maintain the color for much longer if the elements remain suspended.

Resolving that timescale would tell us whether the colored population is a steady fraction of all white dwarfs or a transient phase that most stars pass through.
