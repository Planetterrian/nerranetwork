Casey McGrath, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, Goddard Space Flight Center, led two studies, Starlust dot org reports, published in The Astrophysical Journal on November tenth, twenty twenty-six.

One paper charts the conditions that favor a detection, and the other works out how the James Webb Space Telescope can confirm a candidate.

When two white dwarfs circle each other in under an hour, they pour out a gravitational-wave tone that stays nearly constant.

The Milky Way holds roughly ten million of these pairs, and LISA should separate about ten thousand of them as individual sources.

Mary Anne Limbach, an assistant research scientist at the University of Michigan and a co-author on the second paper, stressed that pairing the two observatories is how genuine planets get separated from false positives.

Webb’s infrared reach can pick up a planet of five Jupiter masses at least five hundred light-years away.

A triangle of spacecraft, each side two point five million kilometers, will drift behind Earth, and every tiny change in those distances will be tracked to a precision finer than the width of a helium atom.

When a planet circles the pair, its gravity tugs the white dwarfs into a slight wobble, and that motion writes extra frequencies just above and below the main tone, the same way a radio carrier wave carries audio off to either side of the center frequency.

LISA is also expected to pick out roughly one hundred mixed binaries that pair a white dwarf with a black hole or a neutron star.

Under favorable conditions the sideband pattern can betray a companion as small as sub-Jupiter mass, and a matching infrared detection would show the gravitational-wave signature belongs to a planet rather than an instrumental artifact or an unseen third star.

The method mirrors the radial-velocity trick already used with starlight, except the wobble is read in ripples of spacetime instead of in spectral lines, and those ripples travel through dust and gas without being blocked.

The expected signal strength for a Jupiter-mass companion can already be calculated, yet nobody knows how often such companions actually sit around double white dwarfs, or what that rate would say about worlds surviving the violent evolution that strips stars down to white dwarfs in the first place.

A team of four from CSIRO, Phys dot org reports, developed a new imaging technique that validated the two thousand nineteen Event Horizon Telescope image of the black hole at the center of galaxy M87.

That reconstruction captured the first direct view of a black hole’s shadow.

The two thousand nineteen picture became one of the most recognized scientific images of the modern era, and the CSIRO method offers an independent check on the original reconstruction produced by hundreds of scientists.

The work adds confidence that the observed ring structure accurately reflects the black hole environment rather than processing artifacts.

Uranus and Neptune, Universe Today reports, have interiors unlike the other planets and therefore possess messy, tilted magnetic fields.

Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have flown by them and revealed how irregular those fields are.

New models suggest the fields change on very short timescales and could already differ from their state during the Voyager 2 encounters in the late nineteen eighties.

The research uses updated interior models to explore why the ice giants generate such complex magnetic environments compared with the more orderly fields of Earth and the gas giants.

Atmospheric argon-forty on Uranus and Neptune, Phys dot org reports, can estimate the amount of rock beneath their surfaces and supply clues about where and how the planets formed.

A Southwest Research Institute team led the study published in The Planetary Science Journal.

The argon isotope ratio serves as a tracer for the bulk composition of the planets’ interiors.

The team aims to distinguish between formation scenarios that place the ice giants closer to the Sun before migration, and formation in place farther out.

The supermassive black hole in Centaurus A, twelve million light-years away, hurls two enormous plasma jets at nearly the speed of light.

Plasma simulations now explain why those jets appear brighter at their edges than at their centers, a pattern that had puzzled observers since the jets were first imaged in detail.

The work comes from the Chair of Astronomy at the University of Würzburg and appears in Astronomy and Astrophysics.

The new models reproduce the observed brightness pattern through the interaction of magnetic fields and particle acceleration within the jet structure.
