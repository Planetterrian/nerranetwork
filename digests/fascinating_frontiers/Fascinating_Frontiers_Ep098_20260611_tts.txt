Webb’s observations of GLIMPSE-17775 show a black hole still embedded in a dense gas cocoon 1.8 billion years after the Big Bang. The finding adds one more data point to the puzzle of how supermassive black holes assembled so quickly in the early universe. Next, astronomers will search for similar objects to test whether dense cocoons were common at that epoch.

If the Sun were scaled to the size of a basketball sitting in downtown Toronto, the nearest star would still be another basketball roughly seven thousand kilometres away in Vancouver.

Star colour works the same way a heated metal bar changes hue: hotter objects emit shorter-wavelength light that our eyes register as blue or white, while cooler ones peak at longer red wavelengths.

A star only three thousand kelvin cooler than the Sun appears noticeably orange; one ten thousand kelvin hotter looks blue-white.

The exact shade also shifts as the star ages and its surface temperature changes by hundreds of degrees over millions of years.

We can therefore read a star’s temperature, and roughly its mass and age, from a single glance on a clear night.

Yet the precise mapping between observed colour and interior structure still contains gaps for stars caught in rapid evolutionary phases.
