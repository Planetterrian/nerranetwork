University of Ottawa researchers, in a Phys.org Space report, tested the CCC plus TL cosmology against several major cosmological observations and published the work in the journal Universe.

The study examined Big Bang nucleosynthesis consistency and found the model held up, and the consistency test focused on the production of light elements in the first minutes after the Big Bang.

Results aligned with measured abundances of helium and other primordial nuclei, and the authors compared predicted element ratios directly with spectroscopic data from ancient stars and gas clouds.

The same framework matches observed expansion and structure formation.

Those ratios were locked while protons and neutrons could still fuse into light nuclei, before expansion cooled the mix too far for further reactions, and a model that gets hydrogen, helium, and trace lithium wrong cannot claim to describe the early universe correctly no matter how well it fits later data.

Watch that first stretch of nuclear time at a rate where every second corresponds to a million years of later cosmic history, and inside that compressed interval the neutron-to-proton ratio freezes, deuterium forms, and almost all of the helium is created.

The resulting mix leaves roughly twenty-five percent helium by mass and trace amounts of lithium-seven, numbers that depend sensitively on the expansion rate and the baryon density at that epoch.

Any alternative framework that changes the expansion history or the particle content therefore predicts different yields, and when the CCC plus TL parameters go into nucleosynthesis calculations the predicted helium and deuterium fractions still line up with values whose light has traveled for billions of years.

That agreement holds across multiple light-element channels even though the framework alters the overall energy budget of the cosmos, and it still satisfies a constraint fixed when the universe was only seconds old.

The test does not prove the model correct, but the framework survives one of the most precise early-universe benchmarks available.

What remains unsettled is whether the same parameters can simultaneously satisfy later observations such as the cosmic microwave background power spectrum and the growth of large-scale structure, datasets that probe epochs hundreds of thousands of years after nucleosynthesis and could reveal tensions not yet visible in the light-element data.

If future measurements tighten the allowed range for the baryon density or the expansion rate, the CCC plus TL model may need adjustment or may be ruled out.

The universe produced helium and deuterium in specific proportions; the open question is which complete description of cosmic history can explain both those proportions and everything that came afterward.
