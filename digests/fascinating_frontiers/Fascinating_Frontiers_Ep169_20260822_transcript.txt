While crude exploration also depends on quiet scientific outposts, a small satellite is soon heading to the moon's far side. The UK-built Cosmo Cube is roughly the size of a carry-on suitcase and will operate from the lunar far side.
Its radio antenna will search for the faint 21-centimeter signal emitted by neutral hydrogen during the cosmic dark ages, the 150 million years before the first stars formed.
Placing the satellite behind the moon shields it from Earth's radio noise. Scientists expect the data could reveal how the first structures in the universe began to glow.
The mission targets one of cosmology's longest standing observational gaps. The moon's far side offers a natural radio quiet zone, roughly 3,500 kilometers across. A receiver placed there can detect signals billions of times fainter than anything reachable from our planet's surface.
Cosmo Cube will tune to the 21-centimeter wavelength emitted by neutral hydrogen atoms, aligned stretched by the universe's expansion into the low frequency radio band.
During the 150 million years before the first stars turned on, this hydrogen filled all space at a temperature of only a few Kelvin above absolute zero.
The spacecraft must maintain extreme thermal stability because even a 1-degree shift in receiver temperature can swamp the cosmic signal. Yet the exact timing of when that hydrogen first began to glow remains one of the largest open questions in cosmology.
