### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Japan Tests Reusable Rocket Prototype for Cost Cuts** — Phys.org
   Japan's space agency launched and landed its RV-X experimental vehicle in the first test of reusable technology. The flight lasted only seconds but proved the basic landing sequence works. Engineers aim to reuse first stages repeatedly to compete with lower-cost operators. The prototype uses a hybrid engine design that allows controlled descent. JAXA conducted the test on Saturday to achieve technology key to cutting launch costs in a market dominated by SpaceX. Next, the team plans higher-altitude tests to refine guidance software.

2. **NASA Releases Private Station Planning Document** — Ars Technica
   The document mixes cost-plus requirements with firm fixed-price clauses for commercial space stations. Industry observers note the hybrid structure creates tension between flexibility and accountability. NASA intends the stations to succeed the International Space Station after 2030. The agency has not yet selected operators for the initial commercial modules. The planning document blends all the requirements, deliverables, and clauses of a cost-plus contract into a firm fixed-price framework.

3. **Parabilis Tests Hybrid Engine for Maneuverable Cubesats** — SpaceNews
   The company completed ground tests of a propulsion system sized for small satellites. The Space Force is funding development to give cubesats independent orbit changes. The hybrid engine combines solid fuel with liquid oxidizer for restart capability. Engineers report stable burns across multiple throttle settings. The technology targets maneuverability for cubesats in support of Space Force objectives. Flight demonstrations are scheduled for later this year.

4. **Artemis II Crew Will Use Off-the-Shelf Cameras** — CNET
   Standard digital cameras are being qualified as science instruments for the upcoming lunar flight. Crew members will capture high-resolution images of Earth and the Moon during the mission. The approach reduces development costs while still meeting radiation and vacuum requirements. Data from these cameras will supplement official mission imagery. Building on recent Artemis program progress, the decision shows how commercial hardware can now meet spaceflight standards for the crewed lunar return. Calibration tests are already underway at Johnson Space Center.

5. **FCC Approves First Sun-Reflecting Satellite Test** — SpaceNews
   Reflect Orbital received permission to launch a single demonstration satellite. The spacecraft will attempt to redirect sunlight toward selected ground targets at night. Astronomers have raised concerns about increased sky brightness during observations. The company must still secure launch and orbital slot approvals. A second approval round will be needed before any constellation expansion. The Federal Communications Commission granted the approval despite widespread criticism from the astronomy community.

6. **Nearby Galaxy Hosts Rapidly Growing Black Hole** — Universe Today
   The central black hole is producing an unusual radio flare never seen before in similar systems. Its growth rate matches expectations for black holes in the early universe. Radio observations reveal jets interacting with surrounding gas on short timescales. The galaxy lies close enough for detailed follow-up with current telescopes. Researchers plan multi-wavelength campaigns to track changes in the accretion flow. This object offers a rare local window into processes that shaped the first supermassive black holes.

7. **Theta Eridani Dimmed After Ancient Records** — Phys.org
   Historical catalogs from Ptolemy and al-Sufi listed the star among the brightest in the sky. Modern measurements show it is now far fainter than those rankings imply. Astronomers attribute the change to intrinsic stellar evolution rather than observational error. The star is a binary system whose combined light once reached higher luminosity. Long-term monitoring continues to refine the evolutionary timeline. The discrepancy spans more than two thousand years of recorded observations.

8. **Jean-Louis Pons Discovered First Comet on This Date** — Astronomy Magazine
   In 1801 the Marseille Observatory doorkeeper recorded comet C/1801 N1. Directors had trained Pons in observational techniques after noticing his talent. He went on to discover 37 comets, still the record for visual discoveries. The initial find came during routine sweeps of the evening sky. His logs remain valuable for orbit reconstructions of long-period comets. Pons began his career in 1789 as a concierge at the observatory.

9. **Dione Passes North of Saturn This Morning** — Astronomy Magazine
   Saturn’s tenth-magnitude moon crossed directly above the planet’s north pole. Observers with video equipment recorded the moon’s shadow moving across the northern rings. The event repeats roughly every 2.7 days as Dione orbits. High-resolution imaging can capture fine details in the shadow’s edge. The next similar alignment occurs in early August. The alignment provided a rare chance to study ring structure under the moving shadow.

10. **Museum of Flight Hosts Nuclear Propulsion Talk** — Vintage Aviation News
    The Seattle museum will present research on nuclear electric systems for future Mars missions. Speakers will discuss power levels needed for crewed round-trip trajectories. The event highlights ongoing work at several NASA centers on reactor designs. Attendance is open to the public with registration required. Materials from the presentation will be archived online afterward. The talk focuses on propulsion concepts that could shorten travel times to the red planet.

11. **Southern Pinwheel Galaxy Imaged in 8 Hours** — Reddit r/space
    Over 700 individual exposures were stacked to reveal the barred spiral structure. A convolutional neural network separated stars from the galaxy for separate processing. Color calibration matched stars to the Gaia catalog for accurate hues. The galaxy lies roughly 15 million light-years away. The final image combines RGB, hydrogen-alpha, and foreground data. The Southern Pinwheel is Messier 83, a barred spiral similar in structure to the Milky Way.

12. **Moon, Mars, and Uranus Align in Morning Sky** — Reddit r/space
    The three bodies formed a tight grouping visible before dawn. Unedited images show the planets near the Moon’s limb. The alignment is a line-of-sight effect rather than a true orbital grouping. Observers across mid-northern latitudes could capture the scene with modest equipment. Similar configurations recur on monthly cycles. The conjunction offered photographers a striking composition against the pre-dawn sky.

13. **Cygnus Constellation Photographed Over Dolomites** — Reddit r/space
    Multiple tracked exposures captured the constellation above Lake Limides. RGB and hydrogen-alpha frames were combined to highlight nebulosity. The foreground required separate processing to balance star and landscape brightness. The site offers dark skies with dramatic alpine reflections. The photographer used a Tamron 17-35 mm lens at f/2.8 on a Canon 6D. The image blends three hours of RGB data with additional hydrogen-alpha frames.

14. **Europe Unveils Reusable Launcher Concept** — ScienceDaily
    The design targets heavy-lift capability comparable to current super-heavy vehicles. Engineers emphasize rapid turnaround and reduced production costs. The vehicle would use methalox engines and vertical landing legs. Development timelines remain tied to European Space Agency funding decisions. Wind-tunnel tests of the booster shape are already scheduled. The concept positions Europe to challenge existing dominance in reusable heavy-lift systems.

15. **Historical Comet Discovery Marks 225 Years** — Astronomy Magazine
    Pons’s first find demonstrated that dedicated amateurs could contribute to comet catalogs. His later discoveries helped map the population of long-period objects. Modern surveys continue the same systematic search approach with digital detectors. The anniversary highlights the continuity of visual and CCD comet hunting. Archival plates from Pons’s era are still being digitized. The discovery of C/1801 N1 occurred on July 11, 1801.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How a Star Can Lose Its Rank Among the Brightest
If every star in the night sky were fixed in brightness, ancient catalogs would match modern lists exactly. Theta Eridani shows that assumption fails. Two thousand years ago the star ranked among the thirteen brightest visible to the naked eye; today it sits well down the list. The change happened because the two stars in this binary system evolved at slightly different rates, shifting their combined output over centuries. A single human lifetime is too short to notice the drop, yet the historical record captures it clearly. We still do not know the exact masses of the two components or the precise moment their combined light began to fade. That missing detail keeps the system on observing lists at professional telescopes.
