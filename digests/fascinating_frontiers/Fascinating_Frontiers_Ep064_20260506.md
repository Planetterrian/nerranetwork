### Cosmic Spotlight
The James Webb Space Telescope has achieved the first direct study of an exoplanet surface by capturing and analyzing its emitted and reflected light. Previous observations could only infer bulk properties or atmospheric composition, but this dataset reveals the world as a dark, hot rock with measurable thermal characteristics. The result demonstrates JWST’s ability to move beyond transit spectroscopy and examine solid surfaces on distant rocky worlds. Future targets may show whether such planets share common surface minerals or retain unexpected features shaped by their formation histories. How many more rocky exoplanets will yield usable surface data before the telescope’s instruments reach their limits?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Solar Activity Clears Orbital Debris
If the Sun were scaled to the size of a basketball, the thermosphere would still extend hundreds of kilometers outward during peak activity, enough to reach satellites that normally orbit in near-vacuum. Picture yourself riding alongside a spent rocket stage at 400 kilometers altitude as a surge in solar ultraviolet radiation heats the thin air below, causing it to balloon upward and create measurable friction on every pass. Over 36 years of records, researchers identified a clear threshold: once solar activity climbs past roughly 67 percent of cycle maximum, drag forces shorten orbital lifetimes dramatically. This process can lower a satellite’s altitude by tens of kilometers within weeks, far faster than gravitational perturbations or residual atmospheric density at solar minimum. The same expansion also increases collision risk for active spacecraft that must fire thrusters more often to stay in position. Yet the precise chain of energy transfer—from solar photons to atmospheric molecules to macroscopic drag—still contains gaps that models cannot fully close. We can forecast the overall effect, but the exact timing and strength of individual reentry events remain difficult to pin down.
