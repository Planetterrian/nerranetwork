Researchers from Kyushu University and the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics
observed the L-154 pre-stellar core in the Taurus molecular complex 140-PAR-SEX away.
They measured a velocity difference between the ion tracer N2D-plus and the neutral tracer
para-NH2D using the IRAM 30-meter telescope.
Neutral particles are decoupling from magnetic field lines in a process called ambipolar
diffusion, falling inward faster than ions.
Prestellar cores in the study region reach temperatures between 10 and 30 Kelvin, cold
enough for complex molecules to form on dust grains.
Lead researcher Doris Arzumanian explained that strong magnetic fields can otherwise delay
collapse.
The velocity offset provides the first direct measurement of ion neutral drift on the scales where stars actually form.
A pre-stellar core, the size of a modest city block, can contain several solar masses of gas and dust,
yet magnetic fields threading the larger cloud often act like rigid scaffolding that prevents collapse.
In that tourist core, the ion tracer stayed tightly coupled to the field lines, while the neutral gas slipped ahead at 0.05 km per second.
That small offset is the moment gravity starts to win.
The process begins once the core grows dense enough to block external starlight.
With fewer ionizing photons reaching the interior, the fraction of charged particles drop sharply.
Neutral molecules no longer feel the magnetic field directly.
They only collide occasionally with the sparse ions that remain tied to the lines.
Over thousands of years, those gentle collisions transfer momentum, but the neutral still pull
ahead. As they accumulate at the center they bend the field lines outward, reducing the magnetic
pressure that once supported the core against gravity. Even while that supports slower roads,
the interior stays cold enough for complex chemistry to proceed on dust grain surfaces.
The same spectra that recorded the drift also show the core is chemically rich,
with molecules that could serve as precursors to prebiotic compounds. What remains unknown is
whether every pre-stellar core experiences this decoupling, or whether some collapse through
turbulence or external compression alone.
Future high-resolution maps of additional cores will reveal whether that velocity signature
is universal, or whether stronger initial fields can suppress the process entirely.
