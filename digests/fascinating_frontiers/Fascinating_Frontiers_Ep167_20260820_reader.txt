Europe’s newest eye on the health of Earth’s vegetation has taken another crucial step towards orbit.

The European Space Agency’s FLEX satellite has been fuelled at Europe’s Spaceport in French Guiana.

The spacecraft will measure fluorescence from vegetation to track plant health and photosynthetic activity across Earth.

Launch is set for 15 September at 03:21 CEST, which corresponds to 14 September at 22:21 local time.

FLEX carries a single instrument designed to detect faint light emitted by plants during photosynthesis.

Engineers will now complete final checks before the satellite moves to the launch pad.

The mission will operate in low Earth orbit and deliver daily global maps of vegetation stress.

Now on a completely different note, NASA has confirmed that the LINK spacecraft will not boost the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory to a higher orbit.

An ongoing attitude control issue on the commercial vehicle prevented the planned capture and reboost.

The observatory will remain in its current orbit and is expected to re-enter the atmosphere later this year.

Swift has provided ultraviolet and X-ray observations of gamma-ray bursts and other transients since 2004.

No alternative reboost option is currently available.

The decision follows an extended period during which teams assessed whether the commercial spacecraft could still complete its task.

Swift has operated for more than two decades, delivering rapid ultraviolet and X-ray follow-up of cosmic explosions.

Its loss will remove one of the few instruments capable of immediate multi-wavelength observations of transient events.

That distance equals roughly 26 billion kilometers.

Voyager 1 has been traveling outward since 1977 and currently moves at about 17 kilometers per second relative to the Sun.

Mission teams use the benchmark to illustrate the scale of interstellar space.

No other human-made object has reached this range.

The milestone underscores how far the probe has traveled while still returning data from beyond the heliopause.

One light-day equals the distance light covers in twenty-four hours—roughly 26 billion kilometers, or about 650 times the average Earth-Moon distance.

Imagine riding alongside a photon as it leaves Earth: in one second it has already passed the Moon, in eight minutes it reaches the Sun, yet a full day later it is still only a tiny fraction of the way to the nearest star.

Voyager 1, now traveling at 17 kilometers per second, will need another 20,000 years to cover that same interval at its current speed.

The milestone therefore marks not just distance but the immense gulf between our fastest spacecraft and the rest of the galaxy.

We can calculate the number precisely, yet we still do not know how the solar wind and interstellar medium interact across that final stretch of Voyager’s journey.
