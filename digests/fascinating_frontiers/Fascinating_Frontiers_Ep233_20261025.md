1. **Rare Lunar Meteorites Could Fill Gaps In Moon’s Evolution — Universe Today**
   Only 835 lunar meteorites have been verified, representing 0.7 percent of all meteorites that fall to Earth. Apollo missions returned 382 kilograms of samples while three Soviet Luna missions and China’s Chang’e 5 and 6 missions added roughly 4 kilograms combined. Most lunar meteorites originate from randomly distributed locations across the Moon, including the far side, unlike the Apollo sites that landed in or near the geochemically anomalous Procellarum KREEP Terrane. Researchers use the chemistry and age of these meteorites to test the lunar magma ocean hypothesis that a global ocean of magma covered the Moon early in its history. The first identified lunar meteorites, Yamato-791197 and Yamato-793169, were collected in Antarctica in 1979. Lunar meteorites arrive after spending years to tens of thousands of years in Earth orbit or up to tens of millions of years in solar orbit. Randy Korotev of Washington University in St. Louis noted that Apollo landing sites were chosen without orbital mineralogical data, leaving five of six missions inside one unusual region. Katherine Joy of the University of Manchester emphasized that meteorites supply pieces of ancient crust potentially from the far side, allowing tests of whether near-side and far-side crust formed identically. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/rare-lunar-meteorites-could-fill-gaps-in-moons-evolution)

2. **Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System — Science Daily**
   Observations with ESO’s Very Large Telescope uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body orbiting the brown dwarf in the CD-35 2722 system. The exosatellite has at least the mass of Jupiter while the brown dwarf it circles exceeds 30 Jupiter masses. Lead author Kevin Hoy described the arrangement as super weird compared with our own Solar System. The team applied the radial velocity method with the CRIRES+ instrument to measure tiny changes in the brown dwarf’s motion caused by the orbiting body. If confirmed, the find would mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System and blur traditional definitions of planets and moons. Alice Zurlo noted the satellite is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion several times the mass of Jupiter. The system centers on a star of roughly half the Sun’s mass, with the brown dwarf and its satellite forming a nested hierarchy unlike anything in our Solar System. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261023023736.htm)

4. **How flare ribbons reveal hidden solar explosions on the sun — Phys.org Space**
   Researchers applied a new model to an M6.5-class solar flare observed on June 22, 2015, by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph satellite. The model quantifies how flare ribbon substructure evolves and links those changes to fragmentation in the reconnecting current sheet in the corona. Flare ribbons form when high-energy particles slam back into the chromosphere along magnetic field lines. The team found that energy deposition occurs in irregular bursts rather than a smooth process, with ribbons breaking into fragments of varying sizes. Lead author Marcel Corchado Albelo noted the work provides the first experimental demonstration of a physical relationship between observed ribbon shapes and the structure of the energy conversion region. Co-author Maria Kazachenko stated that simulations had long predicted ribbon boundary behavior but lacked observational confirmation until this quantitative test. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-10-flare-ribbons-reveal-hidden-solar.html)

7. **Oct. 24, 1601: The death of Tycho Brahe — Astronomy**
   Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe died in Prague on October 24, 1601, after constructing large quadrants, an armillary sphere, a sextant, and a large globe for precise pre-telescopic observations. He ranked second only to Galileo in a 2022 Astronomy magazine list of the twenty greatest astronomers. Brahe measured the 1572 supernova and the Great Comet of 1577, finding no parallax and placing both objects beyond the Moon. His meticulous stellar and planetary position data passed to Johannes Kepler, enabling the derivation of the laws of planetary motion that later underpinned Isaac Newton’s work. Brahe had discovered early in his career that existing published positions were grossly inaccurate and devoted his life to correcting them through sustained instrumental measurements. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/oct-24-1601-the-death-of-tycho-brahe/)

### Cosmic Spotlight
The possible exosatellite in the CD-35 2722 system challenges how astronomers classify objects that sit at the boundary between planets and moons. Its mass equals or exceeds Jupiter’s while it orbits a brown dwarf more than thirty times Jupiter’s mass, creating a hierarchy in which a planet-scale body circles another planet-scale body that itself orbits a star. Lead author Kevin Hoy and collaborator Alice Zurlo note the arrangement forces a rethink of Solar-System-based labels because the satellite is clearly massive enough to be considered a planet under some schemes yet does not orbit a star directly. Radial-velocity measurements with CRIRES+ on the Very Large Telescope supplied the key evidence by tracking the brown dwarf’s slight wobble. Confirmation would open a new category of observations for giant satellites around substellar objects and would require updated frameworks for distinguishing moons from planets when both orbit within the same system.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Flare Ribbons Map Magnetic Reconnection in the Solar Corona
A single solar flare can release energy equivalent to the simultaneous detonation of millions of hydrogen bombs, yet the process that converts stored magnetic energy into that outburst remains hidden behind the Sun’s bright surface. Imagine riding a magnetic field line as it twists and shears under the relentless motion of the Sun’s plasma; eventually the line can no longer tolerate the accumulated stress and snaps, reconnecting into a simpler configuration while liberating enormous heat and accelerated particles. Those particles stream downward along newly formed field lines and slam into the denser chromosphere, lighting up bright ribbons whose fine structure records the fragmentation occurring far above in the corona. On 22 June 2015 an M6.5 flare was captured by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph, revealing that the ribbons did not brighten uniformly but instead broke into irregular fragments of varying sizes, depositing energy in discrete bursts rather than a continuous flood. Researchers built a quantitative model that links the observed spatial complexity of those ribbon boundaries directly to the breakup of the reconnecting current sheet, providing the first observational test of three-dimensional flare simulations that had long predicted such substructure. The measurement matters because it turns the ribbons into a remote diagnostic of coronal physics that cannot be observed directly. One persistent mystery is why the fragmentation occurs at the particular scales recorded in the data; the model reproduces the pattern yet leaves open the question of which instability or turbulence process sets the fragment sizes that ultimately control how and where the Sun’s magnetic energy is released.
