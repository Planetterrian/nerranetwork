### Cosmic Spotlight
An astronaut aboard the International Space Station captured a sudden shower of bright streaks while gazing through the Cupola’s windows. The image shows objects streaking across the darkness of Earth’s night side, likely meteors or small pieces of debris heating up as they encounter the thin upper atmosphere. Such views from orbit give scientists a unique vantage point above the weather and city lights that often obscure ground observations. The timing and brightness patterns can help refine models of meteoroid streams and orbital debris behavior. What else might future crews notice when they look down at our planet from this privileged perch?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Human Radio Signals Race Outward
If the Sun were scaled to the size of a basketball sitting in downtown Toronto, the sphere of our strongest radio broadcasts would already stretch past the nearest star and keep expanding at the speed of light every second. Picture riding alongside one of those early television signals as it leaves the ionosphere: once free of Earth’s protective layers it travels in a straight line forever, growing fainter with distance yet never truly disappearing. By the time a 1980s-era broadcast reaches a star 50 light-years away it has spread into a shell so vast that detecting it requires an antenna hundreds of kilometres across. Specific milestones mark the bubble’s growth: signals from the 1930s now form an outer shell roughly 240 light-years in diameter. We can map exactly which nearby stars have already received our earliest transmissions and which ones will not hear us for centuries yet. The lingering puzzle is whether any receiver out there could ever distinguish our faint whispers from the natural radio noise of the galaxy itself.
