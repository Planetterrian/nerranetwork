Samuel Boucher and colleagues at the University of Waterloo used JWST’s NIRSpec instrument to watch the lava planet TOI-561b for thirty-seven hours across nearly four full orbits, according to a report on Reddit.

Phase-curve measurements showed the planet reflects about three-quarters of incoming starlight, comparable to Venus, while heat is redistributed rather than confined to the dayside.

The campaign captured the planet’s full orbit and found that reflected starlight explains the unexpectedly cool dayside.

Climate simulations point to clouds of vaporized silicate minerals such as silicon dioxide and magnesium silicate acting as a reflective layer on the permanent dayside.

Those silicate clouds act like a planetary sunscreen, keeping temperatures low enough for an atmosphere to persist.

The findings suggest the atmosphere is replenished by gases from the planet’s global magma ocean, and the same clouds may seed the thick envelope that ocean continually replenishes.

Boucher began the project as an undergraduate research intern at the Trottier Institute for Research on Exoplanets, and the work is now a published study.

Future observations at longer wavelengths could distinguish whether the atmosphere resembles a runaway-greenhouse Venus or an extreme volcanic Io.

Perseverance collected data on dust particles clinging to spacesuit fabric samples over one thousand six hundred forty-five Martian days, NASA reported.

Compact stellar systems in the young universe, observed by JWST, suggest collisions between stars occurred at higher rates than today, Phys.org reported.

Parker Solar Probe data show braided magnetic structures forming and growing within the solar wind, Universe Today reported.

These features link large-scale plasma flows to the behaviour of individual ions near the Sun.

The twisted magnetic braids appear in the probe’s solar-wind measurements as they form and grow.

Earth’s magnetic field stretches across a volume hundreds of times larger than the planet itself, forming an invisible bubble that reaches tens of thousands of kilometres into space.

Picture a city-sized refrigerator magnet whose field lines extend from one end of a continent to the other; that is the relative scale of the magnetosphere compared with the solid Earth beneath it. 

Inside that bubble, the solar wind arrives as a steady stream of charged particles, and when those particles slam into the outer boundary they set up waves that ripple asymmetrically around the planet.

Researchers identified how incoming solar-wind pressure creates those uneven wave patterns across the magnetosphere.

On the dayside the field is compressed; on the nightside it stretches into a long tail.

The waves that form at the boundary carry information about where the pressure is greatest and where the shield is working hardest.

Those patterns reveal where the magnetic field works hardest to deflect cosmic rays and solar radiation.

The field shields the atmosphere from gradual stripping by the solar wind while also handling radiation from distant supernovas.

Measurements show that the finest dust-sized particles in the solar wind can still leak through at certain latitudes, while larger cosmic-ray nuclei are deflected far more effectively.

The same asymmetry that concentrates pressure on one flank also channels some particles toward the polar cusps, where they spiral down and create the aurora.

Yet the exact efficiency of that filtering remains uncertain.

We can map the waves and count the particles that reach low-Earth orbit, but we still do not know why the leakage rate changes from one solar-wind gust to the next, or how much the shield’s protective power will shift if Earth’s magnetic field continues its slow weakening.
