Uranus orbits at an average distance of 2.9 billion kilometres, yet its magnetosphere experiences the solar wind’s push on a 17-hour cycle because the planet rolls on its side like a barrel.

Imagine standing on the surface while a 400-kilometre-per-second stream of charged particles slams into a magnetic field that itself spins perpendicular to the flow.

The bow shock can shift inward or outward by several planetary radii within one rotation.

Voyager 2’s 1986 flyby recorded only a single moment of this breathing motion, leaving the full daily pattern to be modelled from limited data.

The planet’s magnetic axis is also offset by roughly 60 degrees from its spin axis, so the shock front never settles into the stable shape seen at Earth or Jupiter.

We can calculate the geometry with increasing precision, but the key unanswered question remains how these repeated compressions and expansions influence the rate at which atmospheric ions escape into space and whether any radiation belts can persist under such variable pressure.
