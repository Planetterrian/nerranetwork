HD 158485 turned up as a binary, Universe Today reports, when researchers at the Xinjiang Astronomical Observatory went through Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite, or TESS, observations.

A binary is two stars orbiting each other, and this pair completes an orbit every three hundred eighty-two point eight days, with the companion roughly eighty percent the mass of the primary.

Flux is the amount of light measured from the star, and a two-star atmosphere model showed a two point five percent difference between five and twenty-eight micrometers compared with a single-star model.

An independent orbital solution built from earlier TESS data confirmed the find, and the team placed the first orbital solution and dynamical mass ratio, the mass comparison taken from the orbit, in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

The orbit also creates measurable light-travel-time shifts from one pulsation mode to another, the different ways the star brightens and dims.

University of Liège astrophysicists, Phys.org reports, built the DESTINY model using Markov chains with absorbing states to track molecules on amorphous ice surfaces.

A Markov chain is a step-by-step model that tracks the chance of each next move, and this one assigns binding energies, how tightly a molecule sticks, to individual surface sites instead of a single average value.

Encounters can change that binding strength without a reaction, and methane population trends already differ from earlier single-energy models.

The approach was detailed in Astronomy and Astrophysics.

Molecules arriving from the gas phase can land on a sticky site or a slippery one on that uneven ice.

Each adsorbed species, a molecule stuck on the surface, faces four possible outcomes at every step: staying in place, diffusing to a new site, reacting, or desorbing.

That treatment reproduces the observed competition between adsorption, diffusion, and desorption on realistic disordered ice.

Direct comparison with new James Webb Space Telescope, or JWST, spectra of ice features in dense clouds would test whether the site-by-site treatment is required or whether simpler averages still suffice at the precision now achievable.

What remains unknown is how large the cumulative effect becomes once many species and many grain types are included together.
