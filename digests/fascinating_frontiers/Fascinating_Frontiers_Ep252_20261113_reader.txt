The European Space Agency’s Wide and Deep Surveys hold roughly two point five trillion pixels in one point seven million gigabytes. Spectra from one hundred sixty million sources sit in the release, along with sixty million quasar candidates.

Some galaxies date from an epoch when the universe was only seven hundred million years old. The observations map cosmic structures and gravitational lensing distortions as Euclid covers one third of the sky across a six-year mission, producing around twenty new images each day.

Bennu grains contain the amino acid beta-alanine, Universe Today notes, the same molecule as in the Murchison meteorite, which landed in Australia in nineteen sixty-nine. Those grains still hold chemical fingerprints laid down billions of years ago in the gas-and-dust disk around the young Sun.

Isotopic ratios read atom by atom inside single molecules separate formation pathways that ran at different distances from the Sun, and under different temperatures and radiation.

Some of those beta-alanine molecules carry isotopic signatures that fit cold chemistry in the outer disk, while others fit warmer conditions closer in. 

The contrast survived in the returned samples because the parent bodies never got hot enough to wipe it out, and the measurements work as time capsules of chemical diversity across the solar nebula while planets and asteroids were coming together.

Exactly how far apart the two formation zones sat is still unknown, and it is not yet clear whether other amino acids or grains from other asteroids show the same spread. Lab work that pairs those isotopic readings with dynamical models of the disk will test whether the pattern needs radial mixing, or whether static formation zones can explain it on their own.
