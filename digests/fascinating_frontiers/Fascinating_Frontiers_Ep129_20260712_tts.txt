Researchers from Italy and Germany have modeled a lightweight permanent-magnet array that could deflect solar energetic particles.

The design avoids the power demands of superconducting coils and the mass penalty of water shielding.

Computer simulations show the static field can reduce crew exposure during solar storms by a measurable fraction.

The team's preprint explores field geometry needed to cover a small habitat module.

The magnets would be mounted externally on the spacecraft structure.

No cooling systems or electrical current are required.

Radiation protection matters most on long voyages.

One recent X-ray study shows how uneven forces already shape outcomes in space.

A neutron star squeezes the mass of the Sun into a sphere roughly twenty kilometres across.

A teaspoon of its material would weigh as much as a mountain on Earth.

Permanent magnets proposed for spacecraft create a comparable but far weaker static field that can nudge charged particles away from a crew module without any electrical power.

Picture riding alongside a solar energetic particle as it approaches the habitat.

Instead of a straight path, the proton curves along the field lines and misses the thin shell by tens of metres.

The geometry that works best is a simple dipole whose poles sit outside the living quarters.

Even a modest field of a few tesla can reduce the dose from a typical solar storm by thirty to forty percent according to the new simulations.

The remaining puzzle is whether the same arrangement leaves gaps at the cusps near the magnetic poles.

Dutch astronomers used Chandra X-ray Observatory data to track the shock front of G292.0+1.8.

The remnant, rich in oxygen, shows faster expansion along some axes than others.

The asymmetry suggests the original explosion was not perfectly spherical.

Continued monitoring will test whether the pattern persists as the remnant ages.

The X-ray images resolve the forward shock in multiple energy bands.

Proper-motion measurements over several years reveal the velocity differences.

The remnant lies several thousand light-years away in the southern sky.

Planetary surfaces also reveal ongoing processes that leave no lasting trace.

In the planet's polar regions, carbon dioxide freezes and precipitates as fine crystals that sublimate before reaching the surface.
