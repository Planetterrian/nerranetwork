1. **White Dwarfs Glow Yellow, Pink or Purple in New Models** — Universe Today
   Researchers at the University of Warwick modeled 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs of Earth. They found that five of the stars—roughly 3.3 percent—display non-white colors when carbon, calcium or sodium sits in their extremely dense atmospheres. Carbon produces pink or purple hues while hydrogen and helium at lower temperatures yield cyan or blue tones. Debris from destroyed planets leaves some white dwarfs looking green. The team notes that the median white dwarf across a representative population remains white. Models treat the stars as blackbodies whose emission depends only on temperature, yet the added elements shift the observed spectrum. Dr. Mark Hollands, the study’s lead author, explained that a true blackbody can only appear red, orange, white or pale blue. At 6000 K most radiation spreads across optical wavelengths and the object looks white. Hotter objects shift toward ultraviolet and appear pale blue. The dense atmospheres of white dwarfs allow trace elements to alter this pattern in ways impossible for main-sequence stars. Dr. Boris Gänsicke, a co-author, noted that one in thirty may not sound like a huge fraction, yet across the entire population of white dwarfs that still represents a lot of oddly colored stars. The findings were published in RAS Techniques and Instruments. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/white-dwarfs-are-actuallyyellow-green-pink-and-purple)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Trace Elements Rewrite a Star’s Color
White dwarfs pack the mass of the Sun into a volume the size of Earth, so their atmospheres are thousands of times denser than the Sun’s. That extreme density lets even tiny amounts of carbon or sodium change which wavelengths escape. In ordinary stars the light we see follows a smooth blackbody curve set only by temperature, yet the new models show that a few percent of white dwarfs deviate from that curve. At 6000 K a pure blackbody looks white because its radiation spreads evenly across visible wavelengths; add carbon and the same temperature produces a pink or purple tint because the element absorbs and re-emits light at specific bands. The researchers examined every white dwarf within 20 parsecs—151 objects in total—and found five that matched these altered colors, or roughly one in thirty. Across the galaxy that fraction still means thousands of oddly hued stellar remnants. The same dense layers also trap debris from destroyed planets, turning some white dwarfs green. Because the color shift depends on both temperature and composition, observers can now use broadband filters to flag candidates for follow-up spectroscopy instead of examining every white dwarf in detail. The remaining puzzle is whether these colored stars represent a brief transitional phase or a stable end state, and whether the same process operates in white dwarfs that formed through different channels. We can already see the color difference in the models, but we do not yet know how often the responsible elements arrive from outside versus how often they are dredged up from the star’s own interior. The study also compared the results against the known temperature sequence for true blackbodies. Dr. Hollands pointed out that only red, orange, white and pale blue are possible for an object whose glow depends solely on temperature. Once carbon or sodium enters the picture the spectrum develops absorption and emission features that shift the perceived color. Cooler white dwarfs with hydrogen and helium tend toward cyan or blue because those gases suppress certain parts of the visible range. The presence of planetary debris adds another layer, producing green tones when the material settles into the atmosphere. These effects only appear because white-dwarf atmospheres are so compressed that even parts-per-million abundances of heavy elements become optically important. In a normal star the same trace amounts would be invisible against the much lower density. The team’s computer models therefore treat the atmosphere as a stratified layer where pressure, temperature and composition interact at every depth. Small changes in the abundance of carbon can swing the output from pink to purple depending on the exact temperature. The five stars identified in the 151-object sample already demonstrate that the phenomenon is repeatable rather than a modeling artifact. Future observations with larger telescopes could measure the actual spectra of these objects and confirm whether the predicted colors match reality. If they do, color surveys could become an efficient first filter for identifying white dwarfs that once hosted planets. The open question is how long these color signatures persist before the elements sink or are mixed away. Some white dwarfs may show the effect only during a narrow window after a planetary disruption event. Others could maintain the color for much longer if the elements remain suspended. Resolving that timescale would tell us whether the colored population is a steady fraction of all white dwarfs or a transient phase that most stars pass through. Either answer would reshape how we interpret the final stages of stellar evolution and the fate of planetary systems around dying stars.

A single Falcon 9 flight and fresh models of stellar remnants both remind us how small changes at the right scale can open new views of the universe.
