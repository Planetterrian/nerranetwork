Subaru Telescope mid-infrared instruments, Phys dot org reports, turned on Comet two P Encke and found a nucleus that is not a sphere but an elongated body about seven kilometers along the long axis and about three kilometers along the short axis.

That outline is comparable to a small city pulled into a rough oval, [pause] turning once every few hours and showing different faces to the Sun.

Mid-infrared wavelengths read temperature directly, so as the nucleus spins, the surface warms and cools, and the changing brightness traces the projected area and the thermal properties of the body.

By modeling how the emission varied over a full rotation, the team extracted both axes and found the surface reached three hundred twenty-eight plus or minus seven Kelvin at the time of observation.

That temperature is hot enough to drive the comet’s activity and still fits a dark, low-albedo surface that soaks up sunlight efficiently.

The same wavelengths separate the solid nucleus from the surrounding coma of dust and gas, because visible light scatters off the coma and can hide the true size of the body, while mid-infrared light comes mostly from the warmer nucleus itself.

That separation lets astronomers measure the nucleus even when the comet is active and optically bright, and these data give the first direct constraints on Encke’s shape from ground-based mid-infrared observations.

What is still unknown is how typical that stretched form is among other short-period comets, because repeated close passes by the Sun could grind nuclei toward spheres or break them into irregular shards, and only a handful of nuclei have been measured at this level of detail.
