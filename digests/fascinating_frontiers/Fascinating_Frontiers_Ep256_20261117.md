> **Slower dark matter particles may accumulate inside the sun, Earth, Jupiter and white dwarfs.**

1. **Celestial bodies could trap slow dark matter particles** — Phys.org Space
   Dark matter particles carrying a tiny electric charge or behaving like miniature magnets might lose just enough energy in collisions with ordinary matter to be captured by a planet or star. Over billions of years the particles would collect in the central regions, where annihilations could produce neutrinos or extra heat. Researchers combined theoretical calculations with existing observations of Earth, the sun, Jupiter and white dwarfs. Neutrino searches from IceCube supplied limits for Earth and the sun, while measured heat output ruled out certain mass and interaction combinations for Jupiter and white dwarfs. Earth proved especially useful for testing a tiny electric charge, while white dwarfs constrained other electromagnetic properties. The work, published in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics, rules out some combinations of particle mass and properties that other experiments had left open. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-sun-earth-jupiter-white-dwarfs.html)

2. **Black hole mergers ring in ways that match general relativity** — Universe Today
   More than seventy researchers used computer models and a literature review to examine the ringdown phase of gravitational waves from black hole mergers. Hundreds of observed events produced ringing signals consistent with Einstein’s theory of general relativity. The study advances black hole spectroscopy from a theoretical idea to a practical tool by measuring the gravitational waves emitted after two black holes merge. Current detectors still face measurement limits, but next-generation ground-based instruments are expected to deliver more precise data. The findings appear in Classical and Quantum Gravity. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/ringing-black-holes-offer-precision-tests-of-relativity)

3. **Chaotic accretion may explain rapid early black hole growth** — Phys.org Space
   A new model shows that gas falling onto early supermassive black holes from random directions keeps their spin low. Low spin means only 6 to 7.5 percent of infalling mass is converted to radiation instead of the usual 10 percent, shortening the growth timescale from 45 million years to roughly 27–32 million years. The scenario reproduces the observed masses of all reliably measured supermassive black holes at redshifts above seven, including the quasar J0313-1806, with seeds of about 100 solar masses formed around 100 million years after the Big Bang. The paper, published in Astronomy & Astrophysics, predicts that the most massive early black holes should be slowly spinning. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-early-black-holes-grown-productive.html)

6. **Dune sand grains reveal wind speeds on Earth and Mars** — Phys.org Space

The method turns sand into a wind-speedometer that works for both present-day and ancient dune fields across the solar system. ### Cosmic Spotlight
SpaceX caught the upper stage of Starship 42 with the launch tower’s mechanical arms after a 22.5-hour orbital flight, marking the first recovery of an orbital-class second stage. The 52-meter vehicle deployed 26 Starlink satellites, executed a belly-flop reentry, and was plucked from the sky by the “Mechazilla” arms. The catch followed three prior booster recoveries and brings routine reuse of both stages closer to reality. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman called the achievement “almost magic” and noted it moves the United States one step closer to returning astronauts to the Moon. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Sand Grains Record Planetary Wind Speeds
Sand dunes on Earth and Mars preserve a record of the winds that built them, and researchers have now shown that the size of the sand grains themselves acts as a direct speedometer. The median grain diameter measured in a dune field reflects the typical wind velocity at the time the dune formed. On Earth, dunes built by stronger winds contain coarser grains because only faster winds can move larger particles. The same relationship holds on Mars, where orbital images reveal dune fields whose grain sizes can be compared with terrestrial calibrations. The method works for both active dunes and ancient ones preserved in the rock record, giving scientists a way to reconstruct past wind regimes across the solar system without needing direct wind measurements. Stanford University researchers derived the relationship from field data on Earth dunes and then applied it to Martian dunes observed by spacecraft. The resulting wind-speed estimates match independent atmospheric models, confirming that grain size reliably encodes wind strength. What remains unknown is how the relationship changes when dunes form under very different atmospheric densities or when the sand supply is limited. Future rover or lander measurements of grain-size distributions on other worlds would test whether the same calibration applies universally or needs adjustment for local conditions. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-written-sand-dunes-reveal-secrets.html)
