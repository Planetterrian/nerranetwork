The technique places a mask with several small pinholes in the light path to enable aperture masking interferometry.

Teams used it to observe the massive binary Wolf-Rayet 137, the young star PDS 70, and Jupiter’s moon Io.

The method combines light from separate parts of the telescope mirror to achieve higher resolution than standard imaging.

It reveals fine structures such as volcanic features on Io and dust distributions around forming planets.

Follow-up observations are planned to track changes in these systems over multiple orbits.

This mode on the James Webb Space Telescope adds a new tool for studying protoplanetary disks, directly feeding into ongoing questions about how we characterize exoplanet environments.

Webb’s aperture-masking observations of Io captured surface details at scales previously limited to flyby spacecraft.

The mask turns the telescope into an interferometer by sampling light through discrete pinholes, then recombining the beams to reconstruct finer structure.

This approach bypassed the glare of Jupiter while resolving volcanic hotspots and plume deposits.

The same mode also mapped dust gaps around PDS 70, showing where planets are clearing material.

Next, teams will apply the technique to additional targets during upcoming visibility windows.

Picture a six point five metre mirror reduced, for a few minutes, to a handful of coin-sized openings spaced metres apart.

The light that passes through those openings travels separate paths yet still interferes at the detector as though the entire mirror had been used at once.

Riding alongside one photon, you would see it take one pinhole route while its twin takes another, the two waves arriving with a tiny delay that encodes the exact angular separation of the source.

That delay is measured in fractions of a wavelength, letting the instrument resolve details ten times finer than the telescope’s normal diffraction limit.

On Io the technique isolated individual volcanic vents separated by only tens of kilometres.

On PDS 70 it traced gaps in the protoplanetary disk narrower than the orbit of Jupiter.

Yet the same mask also discards most of the starlight, so exposure times stretch and faint targets remain out of reach.

The open question is whether future masks with hundreds of pinholes, paired with advanced wavefront sensing, can push the resolution even closer to the theoretical maximum set by the mirror diameter itself.
