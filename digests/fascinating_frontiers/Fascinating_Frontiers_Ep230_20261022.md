1. **Stars brewing in a nebulous cauldron** — ESA Activities
   The James Webb Space Telescope captured a turbulent star-forming region named N159 in the Large Magellanic Cloud, 161 000 light-years away. The image spans about 230 light-years across and shows dense clumps of gas where giant molecular clouds collapse under gravity to form new stars. Massive hot stars ionise surrounding hydrogen gas, creating brilliant emission nebulae. Webb’s Near-Infrared Camera reveals fierce stellar winds sculpting ridges and carving cavities, with light orange polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and pale blue H II gas visible. Dark red filaments of cool molecular hydrogen shield young stars from radiation. N159 is less than three million years old and contains thousands of stars at early formation stages. The region’s lower metallicity compared with the Milky Way offers a window into star formation conditions in the early Universe. Data from observing programme 5114 allowed detection of stars less than a tenth the mass of the Sun. The western region shows more young stellar objects in a concentrated area, indicating it is at an earlier stage of collapse. The Papillon Nebula appears as a compact blob of ionised hydrogen on the left side of the image. Source: [esa.int](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2026/10/Stars_brewing_in_a_nebulous_cauldron)

2. **The First Green Star, WD J1330+6435** — NASA Breaking
   Astronomers identified white dwarf WD J1330+6435 as one that would appear green due to trace elements accreted from an orbiting disk interacting with its atmosphere. White dwarfs are small faint remnants left when Sun-like stars exhaust nuclear fuel. The illustration shows the star surrounded by gas and rocks in an accretion disk. Trace elements shift the normally white colour to yellow, green, cyan, pink or purple in some cases. These objects remain too faint for direct observation tonight. The star is surrounded by an accretion disk containing gas and rocks orbiting the remnant. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2026-october-22-the-first-green-star-wd-j13306435/)

3. **Faster to Science: A Letter to the Science Community** — NASA Breaking
   NASA Science is introducing Competition Model 2.0 to shorten the mission selection process. Proposals will now focus first on the science case, science implementation plan and proof-of-concept mission design, removing roughly half the material previously required. The change aims to reduce time from announcement of opportunity to step-two selection by up to 45 percent, from 35 months to about 19.5 months. Flagship missions retain their existing reformed process. Astro-Probes, New Frontiers and Discovery missions will use the accelerated two-step approach with implementation studies at Goddard or JPL. MIDEX and Earth Explorer missions will evaluate implementing organisations on past performance before selection. The new model allows principal investigators without major university or commercial backing to receive fair consideration. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/directorates/smd/faster-to-science-a-letter-to-the-science-community/)

4. **NASA Conducts RS-25 Engine Test in Support of Future Artemis Missions** — NASA Breaking
   Test teams fired developmental RS-25 engine No. 10001 for almost eight-and-a-half minutes at NASA’s Stennis Space Center on October 21. The engine reached the 111 percent power level required for new production engines on the Space Launch System. The test validated a new thermal protection system for the nozzle and acceptance-tested flight components for future Artemis missions. Four RS-25 engines together produce up to 2 million pounds of thrust for each SLS launch. The developmental engine will not fly but supplies data to improve performance and reliability. The hot fire occurred on the Fred Haise Test Stand near Bay St. Louis, Mississippi. Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/rs-25-engine-test-in-support-of-future-artemis-missions/)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Limits of the Observable Universe
The universe is expanding so rapidly that light from some regions will never reach us, no matter how powerful our telescopes become. Imagine standing on a moving walkway that accelerates: friends walking away from you eventually move faster than any message you could send. In cosmic terms, entire galaxies now lie beyond a horizon set by the expansion rate, and that horizon grows with time. Light from the Andromeda galaxy left its stars 2.5 million years ago, so any telescope pointed there sees the galaxy as it existed before modern humans appeared. A civilisation in Andromeda looking back at the Milky Way would see Earth as it was millions of years earlier, populated only by our distant ancestors. The same expansion that stretches space also stretches the light itself, shifting distant signals out of reach. We can still map the cosmic microwave background from when the universe was 380 000 years old, yet we cannot see what lies beyond the current particle horizon. The question that remains is whether those unreachable regions contain other observers asking the same questions, or whether the geometry of space-time simply leaves them forever silent to one another. There could be entire galaxies filled with stars, planets, oceans, and perhaps civilizations looking up at their own night skies, wondering the exact same things we are. They could be asking whether they're alone in the universe. They could be developing technology, discovering physics, searching for life. And we might never know they exist. Not because they're dead. Not because they've disappeared. But because the universe itself may have placed them beyond the reach of any information we could ever receive. There could be an entire reality unfolding beyond our ability to witness it. And we wouldn't even know what we were missing. Every time we look into space, we're looking into the past. The light from the Moon takes about 1.3 seconds to reach us. The light from the Sun takes about eight minutes. When we look at Andromeda, we're seeing it as it was approximately 2.5 million years ago. We're not looking at the universe as it exists right now. We're looking at the information the universe has sent us, sometimes across billions of years. Imagine a civilization in Andromeda developing a telescope powerful enough to observe Earth. The light reaching them today would show them a version of Earth from roughly 2.5 million years ago. They wouldn't see our cities, our technology, or our modern civilization. They'd see an ancient Earth, populated by our distant ancestors. Meanwhile, we could be looking at their galaxy at the exact same moment, seeing a version of their world from millions of years in their past. Both realities could be unfolding simultaneously, yet neither civilization would be witnessing the other as it currently exists. And that makes me wonder: how much of what exists right now will never be seen by anyone? How many stars have already died whose light hasn't reached us? How many planets have formed, changed, or been destroyed while their light is still traveling through space? The universe has a history that we can observe, but we can never experience all of it at once. We're surrounded by evidence of what was, trying to understand what is. What if we're not alone, but the universe makes it impossible for us to find out? This is the thought that really gets me. We search for extraterrestrial life, but we tend to imagine discovery as a matter of building a powerful enough telescope, developing faster spacecraft, or listening carefully enough for the right signal. But what if the problem isn't simply that we haven't looked hard enough? What if distance and time are fundamental barriers that no amount of technological progress can overcome? Suppose a civilization exists on a planet in Andromeda right now. Suppose they've evolved, developed intelligence, built cities, discovered mathematics, and begun asking whether life exists beyond their world. Perhaps they've been searching for millions of years. Perhaps they've sent signals into space. Perhaps they've looked at our galaxy and wondered whether any of those distant stars host life. But the light we're receiving from their galaxy began its journey millions of years ago. And any signal we send today would take another 2.5 million years to arrive. Even if we discovered undeniable evidence of their existence tomorrow, we wouldn't be discovering who they are today. We'd be discovering who they were millions of years ago. They could have advanced far beyond anything we can imagine. They could have gone extinct. They could have never existed in the first place by the time our information reaches them. We might discover a civilization's ancient history without ever knowing whether anyone is still there to tell it. And that's assuming communication is possible at all. Now put those three thoughts together. There may be civilizations we can never observe because the expansion of space permanently separates us. There may be civilizations whose existence we could eventually discover, but only through light that left their planets millions or billions of years ago. And there may be civilizations so distant that, even if we knew they existed, the time required to exchange a single message would make any meaningful conversation almost impossible. Somewhere out there, life could be looking back at us. But depending on the distance, they might be seeing Earth before humans even existed. Or they might be beyond the point from which any information could ever reach us. And we could be doing exactly the same thing to them. We could be looking at the same universe, asking the same questions, yet forever separated by the very laws that make the universe what it is. And then there's the possibility that none of this is special to us. Earth is one planet orbiting one star in one galaxy. Our galaxy is one among an almost incomprehensible number of galaxies in the observable universe. We exist for a tiny fraction of cosmic history. Our civilization has existed for an almost negligible amount of time compared with the age of the universe. Our ability to observe the cosmos is limited by the technology we've developed, the time we've been here, and the physical laws we can barely begin to understand. We have no definitive evidence that intelligent life exists elsewhere. But we also haven't searched every star, every planet, or every possible way life might exist. So what if we're alone? And what if we're not? Both possibilities are terrifying in completely different ways. If we're alone, then perhaps consciousness is an extraordinarily rare event in a universe that may otherwise be indifferent to it. Perhaps, for all we know, the ability to look at the stars and wonder why we're here is something that has happened only once. And if we're not alone, then there could be countless other forms of consciousness scattered across the cosmos, each experiencing its own little corner of reality, each wondering whether it's alone, and each potentially separated from the others by distances that make discovery impossible. Perhaps the universe is full of life, but the universe is so vast that most life will never know about any of the rest. Perhaps being alive isn't the rarest thing. Perhaps being able to find one another is. And here's the thought I can't get away from tonight: The universe doesn't seem to owe us answers. It doesn't promise that everything that exists can be discovered. It doesn't promise that every question has an answer we'll ever be capable of knowing. It doesn't promise that the truth is within our reach simply because we're curious enough to seek it. There may be things that are real but permanently unknowable to us. There may be events happening right now that no observer on Earth will ever witness. There may be questions we can formulate but never answer, not because we're unintelligent, but because reality itself imposes limits on what we can know. And the strangest part is that we're conscious beings inside this enormous system, capable of recognizing at least some of those limits. We're pieces of the universe that somehow evolved the ability to question the universe. Matter that became aware of itself. A species on a small planet, staring into the darkness and trying to understand something vastly larger than itself. I lie here thinking about all of this, and suddenly the ceiling above me feels almost irrelevant. The room, the house, the planet beneath me—all of it is real, all of it matters to us, yet it's such a tiny part of something so much larger that I struggle to comprehend it. I don't know whether the universe is infinite. I don't know whether we're alone. I don't know how much of reality exists beyond what we can ever observe. And maybe that's what keeps my mind going. It's not that I think I can solve these questions tonight. It's that I can finally begin to understand how enormous they really are. Somewhere, right now, the universe is doing things we'll never witness. Somewhere, light is traveling toward places that may never receive it. Somewhere, there may be another mind looking into the darkness, asking whether anyone else is out there. And we may never know. Not tomorrow. Not in a thousand years. Maybe not ever. So here I am, awake at 3:09 in the morning, on a planet spinning through space around an ordinary star, thinking about things so enormous that my brain can barely hold them. And I keep coming back to one question: What if the most unsettling thing about the universe isn't how much we don't know, but how much we might never be able to know?

That's today's snapshot of the cosmos. Keep looking up.
