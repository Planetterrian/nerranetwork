Cosmic web filaments are the largest structures in the universe, vast bridges of dark matter and gas linking galaxy clusters across hundreds of millions of light-years.

Research newly out from the American Astronomical Society sharpens how dark matter sits inside those filaments and finds a universal density pattern that had been hidden.

Galaxy surveys and weak-lensing measurements have long traced the web, but pinning down how mass piles up along the filaments has been hard because they are so diffuse and far away.

The new work sorts out clashing signals from earlier studies by stacking density profiles from several filament samples and simulation runs, and it finds that central density follows a consistent scaling relation after projection effects and galaxy bias are stripped out. 

Observational catalogs and large-scale cosmological simulations were both used to isolate the dark-matter distribution underneath.

It is still unknown whether that relation still applies at the highest redshifts or inside the heaviest filaments, and whether one physical mechanism sets the pattern in every environment; mapping a larger sample at higher resolution with coming surveys would show if the relation is truly universal or shifts with cosmic time and with how dense the surroundings are.
