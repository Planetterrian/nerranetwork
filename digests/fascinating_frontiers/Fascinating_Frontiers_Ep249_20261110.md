**First known disappearing planet wobbles under neighbor’s pull — Phys.org Space**
TOI-2535 b, a hot Jupiter orbiting its star every 8.25 days, stopped transiting after its orbit tilted. Gravity from an unseen neighbor makes the two planets wobble like tops, swinging each out of alignment with our line of sight. The planet should next transit sometime between 2056 and 2076. Researchers observed the system with TESS between 2019 and 2026 and noted transit times shifting by up to six hours. Follow-up measurements from the WIYN 3.5-meter telescope confirmed the planet’s size and temperature. The discovery suggests hot Jupiters can form through violent pathways that leave tilted orbits rather than flat systems. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-planet-neighbor.html)

**Subaru Telescope measures Comet Encke’s nucleus shape and temperature — Phys.org Space**
Mid-infrared observations showed the nucleus is elongated, with a long axis of about 7 km and a short axis of about 3 km. At the time of observation the surface temperature reached 328 ± 7 Kelvin. The team tracked how thermal emission changed as the nucleus rotated to derive both dimensions and temperature. These measurements provide the first direct constraints on Encke’s shape from ground-based mid-infrared data. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-subaru-telescope-reveals-temperature-comet.html)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Mid-Infrared Light Reveals the True Shape of a Comet Nucleus
A comet nucleus only a few kilometers across can swing through the inner solar system at tens of kilometers per second, yet from Earth it often appears as nothing more than a fuzzy point of light. Mid-infrared observations change that picture by capturing the faint heat the nucleus itself radiates. When the Subaru Telescope turned its mid-infrared instruments on Comet 2P/Encke, the data showed the nucleus is not a sphere but an elongated body roughly 7 kilometers long and 3 kilometers wide. That shape is comparable to a small city stretched into a rough oval, rotating once every few hours and exposing different faces to the Sun.

The measurement works because mid-infrared wavelengths sense temperature directly. As the nucleus spins, its surface warms and cools, and the changing brightness at these wavelengths traces the projected area and thermal properties. By modeling how the emission varied over a full rotation, researchers extracted both the long and short axes and determined that the surface reached 328 ± 7 Kelvin at the time of observation. That temperature is hot enough to drive the comet’s activity yet still consistent with a dark, low-albedo surface that absorbs sunlight efficiently.

The same technique also distinguishes the nucleus from the surrounding coma of dust and gas. Visible light is scattered by the coma and can mask the true size of the solid body, but mid-infrared light comes mostly from the warmer nucleus itself. This separation lets astronomers measure the nucleus even when the comet is active and bright in optical wavelengths.

What remains unknown is how representative Encke’s elongated shape is of other short-period comets. Repeated close passages to the Sun could grind nuclei into more spherical forms or fracture them into irregular shapes, yet only a handful of nuclei have been measured at this level of detail. Future mid-infrared observations of additional comets will show whether most nuclei share this stretched geometry or whether Encke is an outlier shaped by its particular dynamical history.
