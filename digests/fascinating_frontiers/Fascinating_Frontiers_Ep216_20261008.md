### Cosmic Spotlight
The confirmation of TXS 2354+015 at redshift four point nine four six places it among the earliest known radio galaxies. Its extreme power output at such distance implies rapid black hole growth shortly after the Big Bang. The source was first identified through a characteristic drop in optical light before spectroscopy locked in its distance. Follow-up work will test whether similar objects exist at even higher redshifts. This detection adds a concrete data point to models of early supermassive black hole formation. The galaxy’s radio luminosity exceeds that of most known high-redshift sources by a significant margin.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Barycentric Time Corrections in Pulsar Timing
Imagine a city-sized radio dish on Earth turning toward a distant pulsar while our planet races around the Sun at thirty kilometers per second. The distance between the telescope and the solar system’s center of mass changes by up to one hundred fifty million kilometers over a year. That motion stretches or compresses the arrival time of each radio pulse by several milliseconds, enough to mask or mimic the tiny signals astronomers hunt. Correcting for this effect requires shifting every measurement into a common reference frame called TDB, or barycentric dynamical time. Without that step, a perfectly steady pulsar appears to speed up and slow down once per orbit. The correction itself rests on general relativity because clocks run slightly faster or slower depending on their position in the Sun’s gravity well. We still lack a complete map of how these timing residuals behave across the full sky at the nanosecond level that next-generation arrays demand.
