3. **The Ice Giants' Magnetic Fields are Very Strange Compared to Earth's** — Universe Today
   Uranus and Neptune have interiors unlike the other planets and therefore possess messy, tilted magnetic fields. Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have flown by them and revealed how irregular those fields are. New models suggest the fields change on very short timescales and could already differ from their state during the Voyager 2 encounters in the late 1980s. The research uses updated interior models to explore why the ice giants generate such complex magnetic environments compared with the more orderly fields of Earth and the gas giants. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/the-ice-giants-magnetic-fields-are-very-strange-compared-to-earths)

4. **Argon on Uranus and Neptune may reveal more about their origins than their interiors** — Phys.org Space
   Measurements of atmospheric argon-40 on the ice giants can estimate the amount of rock beneath their surfaces and provide clues about where and how the planets formed. A Southwest Research Institute team led the study published in The Planetary Science Journal. The argon isotope ratio serves as a tracer for the bulk composition of the planets' interiors. Researchers hope the measurement will distinguish between formation scenarios that place the ice giants closer to the Sun before migration or formation in place farther out. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-argon-uranus-neptune-reveal-interiors.html)

The work comes from the Chair of Astronomy at the University of Würzburg and appears in Astronomy & Astrophysics. The bright edges had puzzled observers since the jets were first imaged in detail. The new models reproduce the observed brightness pattern through the interaction of magnetic fields and particle acceleration within the jet structure. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Gravitational Waves Can Reveal Exoplanets Around White Dwarfs
Picture a triangle of spacecraft with sides 2.5 million kilometers long drifting behind Earth, each change in separation measured to a precision finer than the width of a helium atom. That is the scale of the LISA mission, and it turns ordinary stellar pairs into tools for finding planets no other telescope can easily reach. Double white dwarfs orbiting each other in less than an hour emit steady gravitational waves at frequencies LISA is built to hear. When a planet circles the pair, its gravity tugs the white dwarfs into a slight wobble, imprinting sidebands—extra frequencies above and below the main signal—exactly as a radio carrier wave carries audio sidebands. Roughly ten million such double white dwarfs exist in the Milky Way, and LISA is expected to resolve about ten thousand individual systems plus roughly one hundred mixed binaries involving black holes or neutron stars. Two studies led by Casey McGrath at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County and NASA Goddard show that these sidebands can betray planets as small as sub-Jupiter mass under favorable conditions. The same planets would also be detectable in infrared by the James Webb Space Telescope from distances of at least 500 light-years, where a five-Jupiter-mass world already stands out against the white dwarfs’ glow. Verification between the two observatories would confirm that the gravitational-wave signature truly belongs to a planet rather than an instrumental artifact or an unseen third star. The first of the two papers maps the regions of parameter space where the gravitational-wave method works best, while the second paper works out the practical steps for confirming any candidate with Webb’s infrared capabilities. Mary Anne Limbach, an assistant research scientist at the University of Michigan and co-author on the verification study, emphasized that joint detections will be essential to rule out false positives. The technique mirrors the radial-velocity method already used with starlight, except the “wobble” is measured in spacetime ripples instead of spectral lines. Because gravitational waves pass through dust and gas unimpeded, the method could eventually probe systems hidden from optical telescopes. Yet the method still leaves one open question: how common are planets in these tight, ancient systems, and what does their presence or absence tell us about whether worlds can survive the violent evolution that creates white dwarfs in the first place. We can already calculate the expected signal strength for a Jupiter-mass companion, but nobody yet knows how often such companions actually exist around double white dwarfs or what that frequency would imply for planet formation after the stars have shed their outer envelopes.
