Webb caught a stellar merger in Andromeda, engineers modeled how the Moon shakes, then a Cosmic Deep Dive on lunar seismic waves.

Aymeric Goyer of Oregon State University's Department of Botany and Plant Pathology, in Phys.org Space coverage, said, "We found that potatoes can complete their entire life cycle in simulated lunar soil when supplemented with organic matter," and the harvest is safe to eat.

Plants grown without that amendment were severely stunted. That added organic matter locks up heavy metals and cuts how much of those harmful compounds the plants take in.

The trials ran on five simulants of lunar mare and highland ground, plus soil from Hermiston, Oregon. The team picked potatoes for packing more calories into a plot than many other crops can, and a later paper will test ten potato varieties in those same simulants.

A stellar merger at the outskirts of the Andromeda galaxy, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, or NASA, reported, produced a remnant nicknamed Bao.

A star slightly less massive than the Sun, approximately zero point seven solar masses, spiraled into a supergiant after only seven orbits lasting about seven years. Before the merger the primary held seven times the Sun's mass, stretched three hundred fifty times the Sun's radius, and shone eighteen thousand times brighter.

Webb, a space telescope, looked at the system forty-seven days after the eruption peaked, the earliest follow-up of this kind of event.

Mid-infrared readings let the team measure how much mass was thrown off during common-envelope evolution, the phase when two stars share their outer layers as they spiral together, and they checked those readings against ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared observations from multiple telescopes.

Civil engineers at Slate Geotechnical Consultants created, Phys.org Space noted, the first ground-motion model tailored to shallow moonquakes from Apollo seismic recordings and physics-based simulations.

A ground-motion model describes how shaking travels through the ground after a quake. It forecasts much stronger shaking far from the source than Earth-based models do.

At two hundred kilometres, shaking on the Moon still counts as a short-to-moderate distance by lunar standards, while that same span on Earth would already show mostly attenuated motion, and strong shaking still shows up past one thousand kilometres on the Moon, even after small-magnitude events.

A colder, more solid interior lets seismic waves hold their energy, and a fractured surface scatters that energy into long codas. Apollo records put average strong-shaking at fifty-four minutes, compared with under one minute for shallow crustal earthquakes on Earth.

Apollo's four-station data set carried lunar values for temperature, rigidity and surface roughness into the simulations.

Limited station geometry from the Apollo era makes it harder to pinpoint the exact source location.

Broadband instruments set at varied depths and distances would record a wider frequency range and measure directly how wave energy fades with distance and time. That would tighten the model and hand engineers the figures required to design structures that can take prolonged lunar shaking.

Korea Aerospace Administration, or KASA, in Asiae reporting, held a workshop on numerical models of astrophysical phenomena and artificial intelligence, or AI, with about fifty researchers from universities and institutes.

Participants presented simulations of galaxy formation, gravitational waves, planetary collisions and the effects of lunar-lander exhaust plumes on regolith, and discussed using these models to pre-validate lander safety and observation strategies for space telescopes and lunar surface instruments.

The Korea Institute of Science and Technology Information and the Institute for Basic Science shared updates on national supercomputing resources, while the Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute reported progress on an AI collaboration platform. KASA plans to incorporate the proposals into future mission planning and policy.

Tufts students joined SpaceVision twenty twenty-six at NASA's Space Center Houston, The Tufts Daily reported, and met astronauts including Jim Wetherbee and Dan Burbank plus industry leaders involved in rocketry clubs and lunar-lander design contests.

Twelve students attended the two-and-a-half-day gathering, which ran networking, project-team presentations, and a keynote from former astronaut Joe Acaba who told students to stay curious and take pride in their work.

People from schools that lack strong aerospace programs called it a rare chance to meet peers and professionals they would not otherwise encounter.

Dragon's departure from the International Space Station, or ISS, SpaceX said, has a new target.

The company is aiming for no earlier than Friday, November twentieth, and teams continue monitoring weather at the planned splashdown locations.

United Launch Alliance, Spaceflight Now reported, said preliminary data from the November fifteenth Leo 1 launch show the booster's low thrust came from too little fuel reaching the BE-4 engines, not from the engines themselves.

All six solid rocket boosters and the Centaur upper stage behaved as expected after earlier nozzle improvements. The Federal Aviation Administration, or FAA, is overseeing the investigation still under way.

Empire State Building glowed gold, NASA said, on November seventeenth to mark Voyager 1 reaching one light-day from Earth, a distance no other human-made object has achieved. A light-day is the distance light travels in a day.

Voyager 1 launched in nineteen seventy-seven and still sends back data from interstellar space.
