Next, infrasound and seismic networks turned an otherwise invisible daytime fireball over Alaska into a fully reconstructed event.

A bright meteor crossed Alaska skies while the Sun was still up, rendering optical cameras ineffective.

Scientists instead used low-frequency sound waves and ground tremors recorded by earthquake stations to reconstruct the object’s trajectory and breakup.

The method captured the final seconds of atmospheric entry in detail.

This approach could provide routine detection of daytime or cloudy-sky events worldwide.

Researchers are now refining algorithms to distinguish fireballs from other acoustic sources.

If you could shrink Earth’s atmosphere to the size of a football stadium, the thin shell where most meteors burn up would be only a few rows of seats thick.

A daylight fireball releases energy equivalent to hundreds or thousands of tons of TNT, yet its optical flash can be lost against the Sun.

Instead, the event generates powerful pressure waves that propagate as infrasound—frequencies below twenty hertz—traveling thousands of kilometers with little attenuation.

These waves reach ground stations hours later, while the same shock also couples into the solid Earth, registering as tiny seismic signals on earthquake sensors.

One surprising number stands out: a single station can sometimes locate the source to within tens of kilometers using arrival-time differences across a sparse network.

The remaining mystery is why some fireballs produce unusually strong seismic coupling while others of similar size remain nearly silent on the ground, leaving researchers without a reliable way to predict which events will be easiest to study.
