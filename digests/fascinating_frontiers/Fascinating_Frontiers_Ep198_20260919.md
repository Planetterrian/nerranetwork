1. **Astronomers Question Space Mirror Plan** — Science News
   Astronomers including Jonathan McDowell are skeptical of ReflectOrbital's plan to launch satellites that would reflect sunlight and brighten the night sky. The proposal would destroy the natural darkness of the night for observers on the ground. Concerns center on increased light pollution from orbit and interference with astronomical observations. The Science News article outlines the technical and environmental objections raised by the community. McDowell and colleagues highlight how the satellites would alter the visibility of stars for both professional and amateur observers. Ground-based telescopes would face new challenges in calibration and data collection as a result. Source: [sciencenews.org](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/reflect-orbital-space-mirrors-satellite)

6. **BepiColombo Measures Mercury Particle Bombardment** — Universe Today
   BepiColombo recorded particle bombardment at Mercury during a close flyby. The measurements will help assess how space storms affect Mercury’s surface and Earth’s atmosphere. Data come from instruments aboard the joint ESA-JAXA mission. The spacecraft passed through regions where solar wind interacts directly with the planet’s exosphere. Scientists will compare these readings with models of surface sputtering and atmospheric loss. Similar processes occur at Earth but on a different scale due to Mercury’s lack of a global magnetic field. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/bepicolombo-makes-up-close-measurement-of-mercurys-solar-bombardment)

8. **XRISM Observatory Captures Stellar Wind** — NASA
   The NASA-JAXA XRISM mission directly observed a giant star’s stellar wind being captured by its compact companion. The captured material powers strong X-ray flares from the system. The observation provides new insight into accretion processes around compact objects. XRISM’s high-resolution spectrometer isolated the velocity structure of the inflowing gas. The data show how the wind is funneled toward the compact object before releasing energy as X-rays. This marks one of the clearest views yet of wind capture in a high-mass X-ray binary. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/xrism/xrism-sees-pulsar-gathering-companions-wind/)

10. **Planets May Scatter in Warm Jupiter Systems** — AAS Nova

Simulations show how initial disk conditions influence final planet positions. The work focuses on systems where giant planets orbit closer to their stars than Jupiter does to the Sun. Scattering events can eject smaller bodies or shift orbits into resonant configurations. The models incorporate both gas-disk interactions and gravitational encounters between planets. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Light Travels Across the Universe
Light from the most distant galaxies we can see has been traveling for over thirteen billion years, a span that would take a commercial jet roughly one hundred fifty million years to cross at typical cruising speed. That same photon left its source when the universe was only a few hundred million years old and has been stretched by cosmic expansion into the infrared wavelengths our telescopes now detect. Along the way it passed through regions where neutral hydrogen absorbed certain colors, creating the absorption lines we use to map the early intergalactic medium. We can measure the exact stretch factor by comparing the observed wavelength to the wavelength the light had when it was emitted, giving us a direct record of how much the universe has grown since then. Yet we still cannot explain why the expansion rate measured from these ancient photons differs slightly from the rate measured in the nearby universe using supernovae and Cepheids. The mismatch leaves open the question of whether an unknown form of energy or a revision to our understanding of gravity is required to reconcile the two measurements.
