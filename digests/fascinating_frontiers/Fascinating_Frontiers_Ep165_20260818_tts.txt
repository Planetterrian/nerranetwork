The two observatories combined proper-motion data from Gaia with deep imaging from Hubble to identify stars that once belonged to a dwarf galaxy accreted early in the Milky Way’s history.

The remnant stars now occupy the galactic halo and show distinct chemical patterns and orbital energies that set them apart from later-accreted populations.

This event is the earliest large-scale merger yet confirmed and occurred when the Milky Way was still assembling its disk.

The combined dataset also reveals how the incoming galaxy’s stars were dynamically heated and mixed over time.

Follow-up spectroscopy of additional halo stars is planned to refine the mass and orbit of the original dwarf.

The Milky Way’s stellar halo stretches across roughly one hundred fifty thousand light-years, yet most of its stars arrived in a handful of ancient mergers rather than forming in place.

One dwarf galaxy swallowed 12 billion years ago contributed a population now spread over a volume comparable to the distance between Earth and the galactic center, yet those stars occupy only a few percent of the halo’s total mass.

When the dwarf fell in, its orbital energy was converted into random motions that puffed the stars into the extended halo we see today, much like dropping a compact ice cube into a swirling pot of soup and watching the fragments disperse.

The chemical signatures, lower iron and higher magnesium relative to Milky Way disk stars, still tag these interlopers even after billions of years of mixing.

We can map their orbits with Gaia’s micro-arcsecond precision, but the exact mass and internal structure of that original dwarf remain uncertain because later dynamical heating has erased the cleanest orbital clues.

The open question is whether similar early mergers delivered the seeds of the galaxy’s oldest globular clusters or whether those clusters formed separately before being captured.

Laboratory confirmation came decades later.

The anniversary highlights how eclipse spectroscopy opened the study of stellar composition.
