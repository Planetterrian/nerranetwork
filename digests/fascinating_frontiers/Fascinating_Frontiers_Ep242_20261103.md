1. **Planetary system in perfect harmony discovered — Universe Space Tech**
   Astronomers confirmed six planets orbiting HD110067, roughly 100 light-years away, all locked in orbital resonance. The innermost completes an orbit in 9.11 days, the next in 13.67 days, and the third in 20.52 days. ESA’s CHEOPS telescope supplied the data that revealed the pattern after TESS had spotted initial transits in 2020. Researchers used the resonance to predict and then verify three additional outer planets whose orbits matched earlier anomalies in the star’s brightness. Only about one percent of planetary systems retain this original resonant state after billions of years. Each world is smaller than Neptune and carries a thick gaseous envelope resembling those of Uranus or Neptune. The system’s brightness makes it the most prominent known example containing four or more planets, positioning it as a prime target for atmospheric study with the James Webb Space Telescope. Astronomers want to determine whether these planets are rocky worlds with exceptionally thick atmospheres or whether solid surfaces with liquid-water oceans exist beneath the clouds. Source: [universemagazine.com](https://universemagazine.com/en/a-six-planet-system-baffles-astronomers-with-its-perfection/)

2. **Scientists develop an advanced whole-Earth simulation to understand how planets evolve without life — The Times of India**
   University of Washington researchers built a virtual Earth model that runs geochemical exchanges among core, mantle, crust, surface and atmosphere for five billion years while excluding any biological influence. The simulation reproduces nineteen observed planetary properties, including mantle heat flow. Lead author Samantha Gilbert-Janizek, who completed the work as a doctoral student and is now a postdoctoral fellow at UC Riverside, added surface and atmosphere modeling to an existing framework developed in senior author Rory Barnes’s lab. The model can now be applied to any rocky exoplanet to test whether an observed atmosphere could arise without life. If the model fails to match the data, the mismatch strengthens the case for biological activity. The work was published in The Planetary Science Journal on September 18. Gilbert-Janizek noted that the calibration gives when future simulations of exoplanets are run. Source: [timesofindia.indiatimes.com](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/science/discovery/scientists-develop-an-advanced-whole-earth-simulation-to-understand-how-planets-evolve-without-life-and-help-astronomers-identify-distant-worlds-whose-atmospheres-could-reveal-signs-of-extraterrestrial-life/articleshow/135488350.cms)

4. **November Night Sky Highlights: Meteor Showers, Jupiter-Mars Meetup, Supermoons — [@NASA](https://x.com/NASA)**
   The Southern Taurids peak around November 5 and the Northern Taurids around November 12, each offering a chance of bright fireballs though producing only a handful of meteors per hour under ideal conditions. Mars and Jupiter appear 1.2 degrees apart in the predawn sky on November 15 and 16, with Jupiter the brighter point and reddish Mars nearby. Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth on November 17, meaning a signal at the speed of light takes 24 hours to reach the spacecraft and another 24 hours for a reply. The Leonids peak on November 17–18 after moonset, potentially producing up to 15 meteors per hour under ideal conditions. A supermoon rises on November 24, the first of three consecutive supermoons, and may appear larger near the horizon due to the Moon illusion. NASA’s Raquel Villanueva from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory presented the monthly skywatching tips. 8. **Martian polar crater ice shifts with seasonal frost — Phys.org Space**
   HiRISE images show that water-ice frost in Martian polar craters changes location and thickness with the seasons, behaving differently even in neighboring craters because of local slopes and rim heights. Mars shares many landforms and weather patterns with Earth, including changing seasons, yet its north polar ice cap is composed only of water while the southern cap is carbon dioxide. These rocks are considered chemically primitive because they preserve the chemical composition of the early solar system remarkably well while also reacting extensively with water after formation. ### Cosmic Spotlight
The six-planet system around HD110067 offers a rare window into the earliest stages of planetary formation because its resonant chain has survived intact. Most systems lose this delicate balance when giant planets form, neighboring stars tug on orbits, or collisions scramble the arrangement. Here the planets apparently avoided every one of those disruptions, leaving their original spacing frozen in place. Astronomers can now use the known periods to refine mass estimates and test whether any of the worlds retain substantial rocky cores beneath their thick envelopes. The next step is targeted JWST spectroscopy to measure atmospheric composition and search for signs of the kind of chemical disequilibrium the new whole-Earth simulation is designed to flag. The system’s brightness and the precise timing provided by the resonance make it an especially efficient target for follow-up observations that could distinguish between formation scenarios.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How resonant chains record a planetary system's birth
Six metronomes placed on a single table can swing at different speeds yet stay locked so that every sixth swing of the slowest exactly matches the fastest one’s count. That coordination mirrors the orbital periods astronomers have measured around HD110067, where six planets form an unbroken chain of simple whole-number ratios across more than 100 light-years. The pattern is the surviving signature of the gas-and-dust disk in which the planets grew. While the disk was still present, the planets migrated inward together, their mutual gravitational tugs keeping the resonances tight. Once the gas vanished, the chain froze in place. Any later giant impact or passing star would have broken the links, so the continued existence of the resonance tells us the system has experienced almost no violence since the disk dispersed billions of years ago.

The same architecture gives observers a practical advantage. Because the periods are known to high precision, future transit observations can be timed exactly, allowing telescopes to catch every planet passing in front of the star without wasted pointings. Small deviations from perfect commensurability reveal the planets’ masses through their gravitational influence on one another. Those masses, combined with radius measurements from the transit depths, yield bulk densities that already hint at substantial atmospheres rather than bare rock. The resonance therefore functions as a natural scale that converts timing data into physical properties without requiring additional assumptions.

Yet the very stability that makes the system valuable also raises a sharper question. Models show that resonant chains are common at formation but rare today; only about one percent survive. What protected this particular chain for billions of years while thousands of others were disrupted? The answer may lie in the absence of a gas giant that could have scattered the inner planets, or in the quiet stellar environment around HD110067. Either way, the system stands as a benchmark: any theory of planet formation must now explain not only how resonances arise, but how a few of them endure across cosmic time.
