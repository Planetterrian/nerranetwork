### Cosmic Spotlight
A rare meteorite recovered from the Sahara contains chemical fingerprints that match no known asteroid or planet. Its oxygen and chromium isotopes point to a parent body that melted and differentiated within the first few million years after the solar system formed. Laboratory measurements show the fragment once sat inside a moon-sized world whose core and mantle separated before the object was destroyed. Researchers are now comparing the sample against other primitive meteorites to map where this lost world orbited. The find raises the question of how many such early bodies existed and why none survived intact.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Low Gravity Fluffs Asteroid Surfaces
If Earth’s gravity were scaled down to that of a 10-kilometer asteroid, a person could jump off the surface and never return. On such tiny worlds the weak pull allows loose grains to sit almost weightless, so even gentle vibrations or micrometeorite impacts can make the top layer expand like foam. Experiments in a 100-meter drop tower showed that the same dry powder used to simulate asteroid dirt increased in volume by more than 30 percent once free-fall removed its weight. That extra fluff changes how a spacecraft footpad sinks or how a drill bit grips the surface. Yet no one has measured the exact porosity of the uppermost centimeters on any visited asteroid, leaving open the question of whether real surfaces are even lighter than the lab results suggest.
