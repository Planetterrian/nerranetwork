1. **Lost Ice Giant May Explain Jupiter and Uranus Moons — Space.com**
   New research points to a vanished third ice giant that once orbited between Jupiter and Uranus. Its gravitational influence could have scattered material that later coalesced into the moons we see today. The study draws on orbital simulations comparing current moon systems with scenarios that include an extra giant planet. Evidence comes from the similar compositions and orbital patterns of several irregular satellites around both worlds. The work suggests this lost body was ejected early in solar system history. Follow-up dynamical models will test whether the same event also shaped the Kuiper Belt. Source: [space.com](https://www.space.com/astronomy/solar-system/a-lost-planet-may-have-given-jupiter-and-uranus-their-moons)

2. **Dusty Fingerprints Reveal Newborn Planet Masses — r/astronomy**
   Researchers at the University of Warwick developed a method to weigh forming planets by measuring gaps they carve in surrounding dust disks. The technique uses high-resolution images from ALMA to map how dust is cleared at specific orbital distances. Gap width and depth correlate directly with the planet’s mass through hydrodynamic models. The approach works on systems still embedded in their natal gas, offering a window into the first few million years of planet growth. It avoids the need for direct imaging of the planets themselves. Next steps involve applying the method to a larger sample of disks observed by ALMA and JWST. Source: [reddit.com](https://www.reddit.com/r/Astronomy/comments/1tqxkyh/weighing_newborn_planets_using_their_dusty/)

3. **China Builds Telescope With 20,000 Spectroscopic Eyes — Universe Today**
   A new facility under construction in western China will obtain spectra for thousands of galaxies each night. The instrument combines a wide-field telescope with thousands of optical fibers feeding individual spectrographs. It addresses the current imbalance where billions of images exist but spectra remain scarce. The design targets faint objects across large sky areas to measure redshifts, compositions, and velocities in one pass. Once operational it will map cosmic structure on scales previously limited by spectroscopic coverage. Data releases are expected to begin within two years of first light. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/20000-eyes-on-the-universe)

4. **Ferroelectric Memory Chip Survives Extreme Radiation — Universe Today**
   A new memory technology uses ferroelectric materials that retain data without power and resist radiation damage. Laboratory tests exposed the chips to radiation doses equivalent to 100 million X-rays with no loss of stored information. Conventional flash memory fails at far lower levels, limiting deep-space missions. The chips could store science data on probes traveling to Jupiter or beyond without heavy shielding. The design exploits the spontaneous electric polarization in certain crystals that remains stable under particle bombardment. Flight hardware development is now under discussion with several agencies. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/the-flash-memory-that-space-cant-destroy)

5. **Pulsars Weigh Nearby Galaxies and Their Dark Matter — Universe Today**
   Astronomers at the University of Alabama in Huntsville used 54 millisecond pulsars as precise clocks to detect gravitational perturbations. Tiny timing shifts revealed the mass of the Large Magellanic Cloud and the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy, including their dark matter halos. The method measures the galaxies’ gravitational pull as they orbit the Milky Way. It provides direct mass estimates without relying on visible stars or gas alone. The same network of pulsars could eventually map the Milky Way’s dark matter distribution. Additional observations will refine the measurements and extend the technique to more distant satellites. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/we-can-now-weigh-galaxies-using-dead-stars-as-scales)

### Cosmic Spotlight
Pulsar timing now lets astronomers weigh entire galaxies by detecting the tiny gravitational tugs those galaxies exert on the Milky Way. Fifty-four precisely timed millisecond pulsars act as a network of cosmic clocks spread across our galaxy. When the Large Magellanic Cloud or Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy passes nearby, their mass slightly advances or delays the pulses reaching Earth. The measured shifts directly yield the galaxies’ total mass, including dark matter that emits no light. The same network could eventually trace the Milky Way’s own dark matter distribution on kiloparsec scales. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: Weighing the Invisible With Pulsar Clocks
If the Sun were compressed into a sphere the size of Manhattan, a single teaspoon of its material would weigh about a billion tonnes. Millisecond pulsars are neutron stars spinning hundreds of times per second, sending out radio beams that sweep past Earth like lighthouse flashes. When a passing galaxy tugs on the Milky Way, the distance between us and the pulsar changes by mere metres, enough to advance or delay the pulse arrival by microseconds. A network of 54 such clocks spread across the sky can isolate these minute shifts from the galaxy’s overall motion. The surprising result is that the mass of the Large Magellanic Cloud, including its dark matter halo, can be read directly from the timing residuals without ever seeing the dark matter itself. The remaining mystery is why the measured masses sometimes differ from those inferred from stellar motions alone.
