2. **Ancient Solar Eclipse Prompted Spartan Retreat in 480 B.C.E.** — Astronomy
   On October 2, 480 B.C.E., a solar eclipse darkened the sky during a standoff near Corinth between Spartan forces under Cleombrotus and Persian troops led by Xerxes I. Herodotus recorded that Cleombrotus interpreted the sudden darkening as an unfavorable omen while preparing to sacrifice. The Spartan commander ordered a full retreat rather than engage. Modern astronomers have dated the event to that specific day based on historical and orbital calculations. The account provides one of the earliest written records linking celestial events to military decisions. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/oct-2-480-b-c-e-a-eclipse-causes-a-retreat/)

4. **Lowest-Mass Double Neutron Star System Detected** — Phys.org Space
   Astronomers identified the lowest-mass double neutron star system observed to date through radio pulsar timing. Neutron stars in the system are the dense remnants of massive stars that ended in supernovae. Some neutron stars appear as pulsars when their beams sweep past Earth. The discovery adds a new data point for models of binary stellar evolution and supernova mass loss. Timing measurements allow precise mass determinations that refine understanding of core-collapse outcomes. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-09-astronomers-lowest-mass-neutron-star.html)

This system sits below previously known examples, tightening the allowed range for remnant masses after iron-core collapse. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: Neutron Star Binary Formation
Imagine a pair of stars each eight times the Sun's mass orbiting only a few astronomical units apart. When the first star exhausts its fuel and explodes, the surviving companion must remain bound despite losing several solar masses in seconds. The second star later follows the same path, leaving two ultra-dense remnants whose combined mass is less than three solar masses. Radio telescopes detect the pulsar's regular pulses and measure tiny shifts in arrival times caused by the companion's gravity. Those timing residuals yield masses accurate to a few percent. The puzzle is why so little mass remains: standard models predict more material ejected, yet this system shows the opposite outcome. No one yet knows whether the explosions were unusually symmetric or whether the stars began with lower initial masses than theory assumes.
