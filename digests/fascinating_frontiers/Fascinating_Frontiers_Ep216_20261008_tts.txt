Point a city-sized radio dish on Earth toward a distant pulsar while our planet races around the Sun at thirty kilometers per second.

The distance between the telescope and the solar system’s center of mass changes by up to one hundred fifty million kilometers over a year.

That motion stretches or compresses the arrival time of each radio pulse by several milliseconds.

Correcting for this effect requires shifting every measurement into a common reference frame called TDB, or barycentric dynamical time.

Without that step, a perfectly steady pulsar appears to speed up and slow down once per orbit.

The correction itself rests on general relativity because clocks run slightly faster or slower depending on their position in the Sun’s gravity well.

We still lack a complete map of how these timing residuals behave across the full sky at the nanosecond level that next-generation arrays demand.
