### Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Nearby Dwarf Galaxies Retain Just 15 Percent of Produced Oxygen — Universe Today**
   Researchers using Hubble studied 37 nearby dwarf galaxies and measured both retained and lost oxygen across their lifetimes. Only about 15 percent of the oxygen produced by stars remains inside these galaxies today, mostly in gas with smaller fractions in stars and dust. The rest has been driven out by radiation from massive stars and core-collapse supernovae into the circumgalactic medium and beyond. In these low-mass systems the oxygen-to-hydrogen ratio reaches one atom per 10,000 to 20,000 hydrogen atoms, compared with one per 2,000 in the Milky Way. The GLOW project combines new and archival data from Hubble, Spitzer, the Very Large Array, and the Australian Telescope Compact Array to track how gas and heavy elements move between galaxies, their halos, and the intergalactic medium. The results show low-mass galaxies lose oxygen more readily than current simulations predict because of weaker gravity. Most of the oxygen comes from core-collapse supernovae, the explosive deaths of massive stars, though a very small fraction is also produced in other types of supernovae caused by the remnants of low-mass stars becoming unstable or merging. Grace Telford, the paper’s second author and an assistant professor at the University of Utah’s Dept. of Physics & Astronomy, noted that energetic radiation from massive stars and their deaths as supernovae drive oxygen-rich gas out of the galaxies over time. The missing oxygen is likely in the gaseous halos surrounding the galaxies, that is, the Circumgalactic Medium, and has also likely traveled even farther into the intergalactic medium. The GLOW project is jointly funded by NASA and the National Science Foundation and is a large-scale effort to understand how gas and heavy elements move between low-mass galaxies, their CGM, and the intergalactic medium. Similar measurements of oxygen lost from galaxies have been made for massive galaxies like the Milky Way, but low-mass galaxies are expected to lose oxygen more easily due to their smaller gravity, and the paper demonstrates that this is indeed the case. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/nearby-dwarf-galaxies-show-surprising-lack-of-oxygen-says-paper)

### Cosmic Spotlight
**Scientists simulate an Earth without life to help hunt for aliens and inhabited worlds: Space.com** Astronomers ran atmospheric models of a lifeless rocky planet to learn how its chemistry would evolve without biological activity. The simulations produce distinct spectral signatures that differ from those expected on worlds with life, giving observers clearer criteria for interpreting future telescope data on exoplanet atmospheres. The work focuses on gases that life would alter or produce, allowing researchers to flag worlds whose atmospheres look too inert to support biology. One open question is how varying stellar types would change the baseline lifeless atmosphere and whether some false positives could still mimic biological signals. Source: [space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/search-for-life/scientists-simulate-an-earth-without-life-to-help-hunt-for-alien-and-inhabited-worlds)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Galactic Winds Remove Heavy Elements from Dwarf Galaxies
Picture a dwarf galaxy no larger than a few thousand light-years across yet still forming stars at a steady clip. Its total mass sits so low that the energy from a handful of supernovae can literally blow material clear out of the system. Compare that scale to the distance across the continental United States, roughly three thousand miles from coast to coast; a dwarf galaxy is thousands of times larger in linear extent, yet its gravitational grip remains fragile enough that a few energetic events can unbind its gas. In our own Milky Way the ratio of oxygen to hydrogen atoms hovers near one oxygen atom for every two thousand hydrogen atoms. In the dwarf galaxies studied by the GLOW team that ratio drops to one oxygen atom for every ten thousand to twenty thousand hydrogen atoms. The difference arises because the smaller gravitational pull cannot hold onto the hot, metal-rich gas blasted outward by radiation pressure and supernova shocks. Once the gas leaves the galaxy it joins the circumgalactic medium and, in many cases, travels even farther into the space between galaxies. The same process operates in larger systems, but the shallower potential wells of dwarfs make escape far easier and more complete. Hubble spectra combined with infrared and radio observations let researchers tally both the oxygen still locked inside stars and gas and the oxygen that has already departed. The measurements show that only about fifteen percent of all oxygen ever created by stars remains inside these galaxies today. It combines a large set of new and archival observations of low-mass galaxies from both ground-based and space telescopes. Current computer simulations of galaxy formation under-predict this level of loss, which means the models still miss some detail of how massive stars couple their energy to the surrounding gas. The unanswered question is exactly which feedback channels—radiation, cosmic rays, or clustered supernovae—drive the most efficient ejection, and whether that efficiency changes with the galaxy’s star-formation history. We can measure the oxygen that remains and the oxygen that has left, but the precise balance of forces that decides how much gas escapes in any given dwarf galaxy remains an open puzzle.
