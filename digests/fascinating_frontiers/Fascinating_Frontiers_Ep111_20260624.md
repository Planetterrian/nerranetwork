### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Webb and Hubble Combine for Full M82 Starburst View — NASA**
   The James Webb Space Telescope’s infrared data added to Hubble archival images now show both the dust-obscured core and the extended plumes of ejected material in the Cigar Galaxy. Rapid star formation triggered by a past merger is driving gas and dust outward, which will eventually starve future generations of stars. The combined dataset reveals how feedback from young stars disrupts the disk on short timescales relative to the galaxy’s overall life. Follow-up observations will track how quickly the outflow clears channels through the remaining gas reservoir.

2. **Webb Counts 16.5 Million Stars in Edge-On Galaxy — NASA**
   Infrared observations pierce thick lanes of dust to map the thin disk and bulge structure of an unnamed edge-on spiral. The census provides a rare side-on census of stellar populations across the full radial extent. This view helps calibrate models of how dust and stars are distributed in galaxies viewed at other angles. Future papers will compare the star counts against simulations of disk formation.

3. **Curiosity Targets Smoother Terrain on Mount Sharp — NASA**
   The rover’s science team identified orbital texture differences and plans a short drive toward a smoother patch for closer inspection. The new site may expose different mineral assemblages than the rougher bedrock examined recently. Onboard instruments will measure chemistry and texture to test whether the change records a shift in ancient lake or wind conditions. The next planning cycle will decide whether to stop for contact science or continue toward higher strata.

4. **Apollo Astronauts Trained as Field Geologists in Alaska — NASA**
   Before their flights, crews practiced sampling and traverse planning in the Valley of Ten Thousand Smokes, a landscape shaped by volcanic ash flows. The exercises built skills for rapid identification of rock types and structures under time pressure. Lessons from those rehearsals shaped how astronauts documented samples returned from the Moon. Archival photos and audio from the training are now part of a new public exhibit.

5. **Hot Jupiter Faces Intense Stellar Irradiation — Universe Today**
   The planet’s orbit keeps one hemisphere under continuous high-energy bombardment from its host star. Models show the resulting temperature contrast drives strong winds that redistribute heat to the nightside. The extreme conditions test the limits of atmospheric circulation theories developed for cooler giants. Ongoing observations aim to measure how the upper atmosphere expands and escapes under this forcing.

6. **Chicxulub Crater Hosted Long-Lived Hydrothermal System — Universe Today**
   New dating of alteration minerals shows heated groundwater circulated for roughly eight million years after the impact. The system created a subsurface environment with liquid water, chemical gradients, and nutrients long after surface conditions stabilized. This duration exceeds earlier two-million-year estimates and widens the window for possible microbial colonization. Researchers will next search for biosignatures in core samples from the crater peak ring.

7. **NASA Watchdog Flags Kennedy Space Center Capacity Limits — SpaceNews**
   An inspector general report warns that aging infrastructure and growing launch demand are straining the Florida spaceport. Multiple heavy-lift vehicles now compete for pads, processing facilities, and range resources. The findings recommend near-term investments to avoid bottlenecks that could delay Artemis and commercial missions. NASA is reviewing the recommendations for the next budget cycle.

8. **Boeing Wins Up to $2 Billion Space Force Satellite Contract — SpaceNews**
   The award covers two next-generation military communications satellites after a competition against Lockheed Martin. The spacecraft will provide protected wideband services for U.S. and allied forces. Work will build on Boeing’s existing satellite production line in California. First launch is expected in the early 2030s.

9. **Hubble Spots Early Galaxy Clearing Its Surroundings — Phys.org**
   Ultraviolet light from clustered young stars in MXDFz4.4 is ionizing and dispersing neutral hydrogen both inside and immediately around the galaxy. The object existed when the universe was 1.4 billion years old. This is the earliest direct evidence that such compact starbursts helped end the cosmic dark ages. Additional spectroscopy will measure the exact fraction of ionizing photons escaping into intergalactic space.

10. **Sean Gallagher Named NASA Chief Information Officer — NASA**
    Gallagher, previously acting CIO, now leads the agency’s full portfolio of information technology systems and services. His appointment is effective immediately and follows several months in the acting role. Priorities include modernizing legacy systems and supporting growing data demands from science missions. The move centralizes oversight of cybersecurity and cloud infrastructure across centers.

11. **NASA and Boeing Maintain Starliner Return-to-Flight Commitment — Spaceflight Now**
    An uncrewed test flight remains in the testing and analysis phase before any crewed attempt. Internal schedules list a possible August window, yet officials caution that technical issues could push the flight into 2027. The companies continue parallel work on software and hardware fixes identified during prior attempts. A final readiness review date has not been set.

12. **SpaceX Demonstrates New Microgravity Research Vehicle on Starfall — Space.com**
    The capsule performed controlled flight after deployment from Falcon 9 and will splash down in the Pacific. The demo validates a design intended for routine, lower-cost access to microgravity for experiments and manufacturing. Payload capacity and reentry performance data will inform future operational missions. Additional flights are planned once the vehicle completes post-flight review.

13. **Gilmour Space Tests Hypersonic Rocket in North Queensland — Reddit**
    The Australian company completed its first hypersonic flight test as part of building defence-related launch capability. The vehicle reached speeds and altitudes relevant to future responsive launch concepts. Data from the flight will guide development of a larger orbital rocket. The test site offers proximity to existing range infrastructure.

    Student engineers advanced with a project focused on aerospace systems design. The team will compete against other universities in a final round later this year. Faculty advisors note the project builds directly on coursework in propulsion and avionics. Results will contribute to ongoing NASA education initiatives.

### Cosmic Spotlight
The eight-million-year duration of the Chicxulub hydrothermal system stands out because it triples earlier estimates and greatly extends the period when liquid water and chemical energy could have supported life beneath the crater floor. Researchers reached this conclusion by dating specific alteration minerals formed only while hot fluids were still circulating. The finding widens the window during which microbial life might have taken hold after the impact winter ended. One open question is whether any biosignatures survive in the peak-ring rocks that future drilling campaigns could recover.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Infrared Light Reveals Hidden Stars
If every visible star in the Milky Way were switched off tomorrow, the galaxy would still shine brightly in infrared from the dust-enshrouded stars we cannot see with ordinary telescopes. Dust grains absorb optical and ultraviolet photons and re-radiate the energy at longer wavelengths that pass straight through the same clouds. The James Webb Space Telescope’s detectors are tuned to these wavelengths, allowing it to count individual stars in regions where visible-light images show only darkness. In one recent observation the telescope recorded 16.5 million stars across a single edge-on disk whose visible appearance is little more than a thin dark line. That number comes from resolving point sources down to roughly the mass of the Sun at distances of several million light-years. Yet even with these data we still cannot say precisely how much additional mass remains locked in objects too faint or too deeply buried for current instruments to detect.
