1. **Total Solar Eclipse Due Across Northern Spain in August — Phys.org**
   The Moon will completely cover the Sun on August 12, briefly turning day into night along a path through northern Spain. Observers in the zone will see the solar corona and planets that are normally hidden by daylight. The event is the first total solar eclipse visible from mainland Spain in decades. Local interest has surged, with rural residents who once watched the night sky alone now part of widespread public excitement. Follow-up coverage will track viewing conditions and any last-minute weather forecasts closer to the date. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-total-solar-eclipse-due-august.html)

3. **Asteroid 18 Melpomene Reaches Opposition — Astronomy Magazine**

Opposition places it opposite the Sun in Earth’s sky, maximizing its brightness for the year. Observers can track its motion against background stars throughout the night. The asteroid is roughly 150 kilometres across and has been studied for decades. Amateur astronomers are encouraged to attempt imaging or visual observations while it remains well placed. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/the-sky-today-wednesday-july-8-2026/)

Further studies will examine how the collision continues to influence star formation. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-galaxy-mergers-obvious.html)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Galaxy Clusters Assemble Through Repeated Collisions
If the Milky Way sat at the center of one of these forming clusters, the nearest neighboring sub-cluster would lie more than a million light-years away—roughly ten times the distance to Andromeda. Two massive elliptical galaxies anchor each sub-cluster like gravitational anchors, pulling smaller galaxies into orbit while the whole system hurtles through space. When the sub-clusters slam together, their galaxies pass through one another with relatively few direct collisions because the spaces between stars are so vast; instead, the dark-matter halos and hot gas interact, slowing the motion and allowing eventual merger. The entire process can repeat several times before the final cluster settles. We still do not know exactly how much of the final cluster’s mass ends up locked in the central giant ellipticals versus remaining in the diffuse intracluster light.
