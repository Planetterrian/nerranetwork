5. **Mars “Ghost Snow” Falls as Dry Ice and Vanishes** — r/astronomy
   In the planet’s polar regions, carbon dioxide freezes and precipitates as fine crystals that sublimate before reaching the surface. NASA’s MARSIS radar has mapped layers of this dry ice several kilometers thick. Beneath the ice, the same instrument has detected what appears to be liquid water that has persisted for billions of years. The snowfall occurs only where temperatures drop below minus 125 °C. The radar reflections distinguish the ice layers from underlying rock and liquid. No surface deposit forms because the crystals disappear in the thin atmosphere. The process repeats each Martian winter.

6. **Bulgaria’s Second Cosmonaut Dies at 74** — collectSPACE.com
   Aleksandr Aleksandrov, who flew under the Interkosmos program in 1988, passed away on July 11. He became the second Bulgarian citizen to reach orbit after Georgi Ivanov. Aleksandrov’s mission on Soyuz TM-5 included Earth-observation and materials-science experiments. Colleagues remember him for his work training later crews in microgravity procedures. His flight lasted eight days and returned safely to Earth. The Interkosmos program gave allied nations access to Soviet spacecraft. Aleksandrov remained active in space education after retirement.

8. **University of Alberta Finds Two Record-Distant Quasars** — r/astronomy
   Astronomers have identified two quasars farther from Earth than any previously confirmed objects. The discovery relied on spectroscopic follow-up of candidates selected from wide-field surveys. Both objects are powered by supermassive black holes accreting at high rates. Their light provides new probes of conditions in the universe less than a billion years after the Big Bang. The spectra show strong emission lines shifted far into the infrared. Additional candidates from the same survey are under review. The findings extend the known reach of luminous early black holes.

9. **Permanent Magnets Proposed for Deep-Space Radiation Shield** — Phys.org Space
   Researchers from Italy and Germany have modeled a lightweight permanent-magnet array that could deflect solar energetic particles. The design avoids the power demands of superconducting coils and the mass penalty of water shielding. Computer simulations show the static field can reduce crew exposure during solar storms by a measurable fraction. The team’s preprint explores field geometry needed to cover a small habitat module. The magnets would be mounted externally on the spacecraft structure. No cooling systems or electrical current are required. The study compares several magnet configurations for coverage efficiency.

10. **Chandra Maps Uneven Expansion of Young Supernova Remnant** — Phys.org Space
    Dutch astronomers used Chandra X-ray Observatory data to track the shock front of G292.0+1.8. The remnant, rich in oxygen, shows faster expansion along some axes than others. The asymmetry suggests the original explosion was not perfectly spherical. Continued monitoring will test whether the pattern persists as the remnant ages. The X-ray images resolve the forward shock in multiple energy bands. Proper-motion measurements over several years reveal the velocity differences. The remnant lies several thousand light-years away in the southern sky.

12. **NASA Chandra Reveals Magnetic Structure of Distant Pulsar** — r/astronomy
    The Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer has mapped the magnetic field geometry around a pulsar nicknamed the “lighthouse.” Polarization measurements show the field lines twist in ways that match theoretical predictions for a rotating neutron star. The data cover multiple rotation phases and provide the first direct view of the emission region’s orientation. Follow-up observations are planned to test whether the pattern changes over months. The pulsar emits beams that sweep across the line of sight like a lighthouse. IXPE’s detectors record the angle of polarized X-rays from each phase. The results constrain models of the magnetosphere.

13. **Amateur Captures High-Frame-Rate Moon Animation** — r/astronomy
    A Nikon Z8 paired with a Takahashi TSA-120 telescope recorded 5,400 raw frames of the lunar surface. The sequence was aligned and stacked to produce a short time-lapse showing changing shadows across craters. The mount used was a ZWO AM5 in high-frame-rate mode at 20 frames per second. Processing combined PIPP, AutoStakkert, and Photoshop before final animation in After Effects. The best 10 percent of frames were selected for the final stack. The animation reveals subtle topographic relief under changing illumination. The observer used a 2.4× Barlow lens for additional magnification.

14. **Iraq Observer Images West Veil Nebula** — r/astronomy
    From Baghdad, a Seestar S50 telescope collected 12 hours of luminance data on NGC 6960 under light-pollution filters. The resulting 1.3× mosaic reveals fine filamentary structure in the supernova remnant. Stacking and processing were completed in PixInsight and Photoshop. The image demonstrates that useful deep-sky data can be gathered from an urban site. The Veil complex lies about 1,500 light-years away in Cygnus. The observer used an EQ mode mount to track the field. Total integration time reached the equivalent of multiple clear nights.

15. **Romanian Astrophotographer Records Fireworks Galaxy** — r/astronomy
    A 200 mm Newtonian telescope gathered 26 hours of luminance, 7 hours of RGB, and 2.5 hours of H-alpha on NGC 6946. Two cooled CMOS cameras operating at –15 °C captured the data from a Bortle 4 location. The final image shows star-forming regions and dust lanes across the spiral arms. Processing used PixInsight, Seti Astro tools, and Photoshop. The galaxy lies roughly 25 million light-years away in Cepheus. The long luminance exposure brings out faint outer structures. The H-alpha data highlight active star-forming complexes.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Magnetic Fields Channel Radiation Around Small Habitats
A neutron star squeezes the mass of the Sun into a sphere roughly 20 kilometres across; a teaspoon of its material would weigh as much as a mountain on Earth. Permanent magnets proposed for spacecraft create a comparable but far weaker static field that can nudge charged particles away from a crew module without any electrical power. Picture riding alongside a solar energetic particle as it approaches the habitat: instead of a straight path, the proton curves along the field lines and misses the thin shell by tens of metres. The geometry that works best is a simple dipole whose poles sit outside the living quarters, producing a protective bubble whose radius grows with the square root of the magnet strength. Even a modest field of a few tesla can reduce the dose from a typical solar storm by 30 to 40 percent according to the new simulations. The magnets would be arranged in a compact array mounted on the exterior of a small habitat, creating a localized deflection zone that follows the module wherever it travels. The remaining puzzle is whether the same arrangement leaves gaps at the “cusps” near the magnetic poles, where particles could still thread through and reach the crew.
