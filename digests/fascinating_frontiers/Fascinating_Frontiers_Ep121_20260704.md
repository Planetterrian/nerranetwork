### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Pegasus XL launches LINK spacecraft to reboost Swift** — SpaceNews
   The Pegasus XL lifted off from Kwajalein Atoll carrying Katalyst Space’s LINK robotic servicer on the SWIFT Reboost Mission. Stargazer released the rocket near 166°W 7.5°N at roughly 0836 UTC on July 3. LINK will rendezvous with NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory and perform a controlled boost to raise its orbit. Communications with the servicer have already been established after separation. The flight may mark the final use of the Pegasus rocket family. Teams will next test LINK’s proximity operations and docking hardware before the reboost maneuver. Source: [spacenews.com](https://spacenews.com/pegasus-launches-swift-reboost-mission/)

2. **Mars passes 0.1° south of Uranus in Taurus** — Astronomy Magazine
   The two planets reached closest approach at 1 a.m. EDT on July 4. By roughly 4:30 a.m. local daylight time both worlds stood about 16° above the eastern horizon. Mars appeared as a bright red point while Uranus required binoculars or a small telescope to resolve. The conjunction offers skywatchers a convenient reference for locating the ice giant. Observers in the Americas and Europe had the best viewing geometry. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/the-sky-today-saturday-july-4-2026/)

3. **TESS detects first exoplanet via gravitational microlensing** — Universe Today
   Archived data from NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite revealed a Jupiter-mass world whose gravity briefly magnified light from a background star. The event marks the spacecraft’s initial use of microlensing rather than its usual transit method. The planet, designated HD 126105 b in related catalog entries, orbits at 1.36 AU from its K-type host. The technique opens a new search channel for worlds that do not cross their stars from TESS’s line of sight. Follow-up observations from ground telescopes are planned to refine the mass and orbit. Source: [universetoday.com](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/bending-spacetime-reveals-new-planet-hidden-in-archived-tess-data)

4. **Hot Jupiters show magnetic fields through atmospheric winds** — Phys.org
   Extreme winds on several ultra-hot Jupiters have been mapped for the first time, revealing the presence of planetary magnetic fields. The measurements come from high-resolution spectroscopy of iron and other metals in the dayside atmospheres. Field strengths appear comparable to those inferred for Jupiter itself. The discovery supplies the first direct evidence that these close-in gas giants generate internal dynamos. Additional targets are now being observed to test how field strength varies with orbital distance and stellar irradiation. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-06-hot-jupiter-reveal-exoplanet-magnetic.html)

This marks the first clear detection of chloride-bearing clouds on any exoplanet. The finding helps refine models of cloud chemistry under intense stellar heating. Further spectra are expected to map how the cloud deck changes with longitude. The bar and spiral arms appear well developed despite the early epoch. The detection challenges models that predict slower formation of such ordered structures. Follow-up spectroscopy will measure the galaxy’s stellar mass and star-formation history. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-jwst-barred-spiral-galaxy.html)

### Cosmic Spotlight
The TESS microlensing detection demonstrates how a transit-focused mission can still uncover planets that never pass in front of their stars. By catching the brief brightening of a background star, the spacecraft effectively used the planet itself as a gravitational lens. The geometry requires near-perfect alignment, which is why such events are rare even in large datasets. Future reprocessing of the full TESS archive could reveal additional examples and expand the census of cold Jupiters. What fraction of the archive might still hide similar signals?

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Gravitational Microlensing
If the Sun were replaced by a bowling ball on a vast frozen lake, a passing star would bend the surrounding ice just enough to focus light from a distant object into a brief, bright flash visible from shore. That flash is gravitational microlensing: the foreground mass warps spacetime so that light rays from a background source converge toward the observer for a few hours or days. In the TESS case the lens was a Jupiter-mass planet roughly 1.4 AU from its host star, and the brightening lasted long enough for the satellite’s cameras to record it in archived frames. The probability of catching such an alignment is tiny—roughly one in a million for any given background star—yet the survey’s wide field and repeated visits made the first detection possible. The same physics that lets us weigh planets we cannot see also lets us measure the mass of free-floating worlds and even rogue black holes, provided the alignment is caught. The open question is whether the current archive already contains dozens more events that simply have not been searched with the right algorithms.
