### Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories
1. **Historical Eclipse Ended Ancient War — Astronomy Magazine**
   On May 28, 585 B.C.E., a total solar eclipse reportedly halted five years of fighting between the Lydians and the Medes. Greek historian Herodotus recorded that the sudden darkness convinced both sides to negotiate peace and establish a boundary. The event remains one of the earliest documented cases where an astronomical phenomenon directly influenced human conflict. Historians continue to cross-reference ancient records with modern eclipse calculations to confirm the date. Future studies of similar events may refine our understanding of how celestial surprises shaped early diplomacy.

The next mission will focus on demonstrating improved engine performance and booster recovery techniques. Regulators must complete their review before a new launch date is set. Teams at the Texas launch site are already stacking hardware for the follow-on test.

6. **Record-Energy Neutrino May Trace to Blazar — Space.com**
   The most energetic neutrino yet detected could have originated in a blazar powered by a supermassive black hole. Researchers suggest the particle was accelerated in relativistic jets before traveling across intergalactic space. Detection relied on observatories that monitor both neutrinos and high-energy gamma rays. Confirmation would strengthen the case that blazars act as efficient cosmic particle accelerators. Additional events will be needed to map the sources with greater precision.

7. **Mercury’s Polar Ice May Stem from One Asteroid — Space.com**
   A new study proposes that much of Mercury’s water ice arrived in a single slow asteroid impact within one Mercurian day. The impactor would have delivered ice that migrated to permanently shadowed craters near the poles. Modeling shows the timing and velocity allow ice to survive without immediate sublimation. Earlier theories invoked multiple smaller deliveries over longer periods. Follow-up observations by upcoming Mercury orbiters could test the single-impact scenario.

8. **Dust Rings Offer Way to Weigh Newborn Planets — phys.org**
   Astronomers at the University of Warwick, working with MIT and McMaster researchers, have developed a method to estimate masses of planets still embedded in their birth disks. The technique uses measurable properties of dust rings that form around young stars. Published in The Astrophysical Journal, the approach allows characterization of worlds invisible to direct imaging. Observations with current and future telescopes can apply the method across multiple systems. The team plans to refine the model with additional ALMA data.

9. **X-ray Pulsars Tested as Interstellar Navigation Beacons — Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas**
   A new study demonstrates that pulsars emitting in X-rays could serve as reliable reference points for deep-space navigation. The timing signals from these rapidly rotating neutron stars remain stable over vast distances. Researchers simulated spacecraft trajectories using real pulsar data to verify positioning accuracy. The approach could reduce reliance on Earth-based tracking for missions far from the inner solar system. Further hardware tests are planned for future probes.

### Cosmic Spotlight
The most energetic neutrino ever recorded may have been launched by a blazar’s relativistic jet. These jets form when material spirals into a supermassive black hole and is ejected at nearly the speed of light. Detectors on Earth captured the particle’s arrival after it crossed billions of light-years without interacting. Matching its direction with gamma-ray observations points to a specific class of active galaxies as the source. The open question is whether every such high-energy neutrino shares the same origin or whether multiple acceleration sites contribute.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Blazars Accelerate Particles to Extreme Energies
If the Sun were scaled to the size of a basketball, the supermassive black hole at a typical blazar’s center would be a marble several kilometers across yet containing the mass of millions of suns. Material falling inward forms a swirling disk that generates enormous magnetic fields; these fields twist and snap, flinging protons and electrons outward in narrow beams that travel for billions of light-years. One proton in such a jet can reach energies a hundred million times higher than anything produced in human accelerators, all within a region no larger than our solar system. We can measure the resulting neutrinos and gamma rays when they reach Earth, yet no one has fully explained why a fraction of particles gain these record energies while most do not. The precise balance between magnetic reconnection, shock waves, and plasma instabilities remains the missing piece.
