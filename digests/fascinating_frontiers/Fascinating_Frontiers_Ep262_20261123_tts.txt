Researchers modeled how laser-driven solar sails behave once they reach seventy-five percent of the speed of light according to ScienceDaily. At that velocity, relativistic aberration concentrates diffusely scattered light in the forward direction, producing a recoil force that acts as drag.

The study examined three momentum-transfer mechanisms: incident light, specular reflection, and diffuse scattering. Incident light remains the strongest contributor, yet all three lose effectiveness as the incoming laser beam redshifts from the sail's perspective.

The finding applies to concepts such as Breakthrough Starshot that aim to accelerate small probes to a substantial fraction of light speed.

A new study in The Astrophysical Journal tracked roughly two hundred jet-like plasma bursts called dynamic fibrils originating from sunspots according to Universe Today. Astronomers combined measurements from thirty-seven earlier studies into a single catalog to examine what drives one-sided mass distributions in spiral galaxies according to Phys dot org.

Stanford researchers showed that the size and movement of individual sand grains on dunes can serve as direct indicators of wind velocity on planets with atmospheres according to Universe Today.

On Earth we judge wind by watching flags or feeling it on our faces, yet on distant worlds the only record often comes from patterns left in sand. Dunes form wherever loose grains are moved by wind or other flows, and their overall shape and orientation have long told researchers which way the wind blows.

The new work shifts attention from the dunes themselves to the individual grains that build them. Grains of different sizes respond differently to the same wind; smaller particles lift more easily and travel farther, while larger ones require stronger gusts.

By measuring how far and how fast grains of known sizes move across a dune field, researchers can back-calculate the wind speeds that produced those movements. The Stanford analysis used laboratory experiments and numerical models to map grain trajectories under controlled winds, then showed that the same relationships appear in orbital images of dunes on Mars and Titan.

Because the method relies only on visible grain motion and dune morphology, it can be applied wherever high-resolution images exist.

What remains unknown is how well the calibration holds when atmospheric density, grain composition, or electrostatic forces differ sharply from the tested cases; direct wind measurements from future landers would provide the ground truth needed to refine the speed estimates across the solar system.
