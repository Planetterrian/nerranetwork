Webb’s mid-infrared maps, in a Phys.org report, show silica-rich dust in one reverse-shocked ejecta clump of Cassiopeia A and carbon-rich dust in another. Cassiopeia A is the leftover cloud from a star that exploded.

The Green Monster region holds complex magnesium silicates left from mass loss before the explosion. The data indicate oxygen-burning and carbon-burning shells in the progenitor combined only hours before the core collapsed.

Lead author Tea Temim of Columbia University said the merger “can cause the shells to merge shortly before the supernova explosion” and that it changes chemical abundances later found in the remnant. Co-author Dan Milisavljevic of Purdue University noted that resolving individual clumps at this scale now ties the explosion back to the progenitor’s makeup hundreds of years afterward.

Massive stars stack their chemistry like onion skins. Hydrogen fuses at the centre, then helium, carbon, oxygen and heavier elements burn in shells stacked outward. Gravity holds the layers apart until the star’s last hours.

When those oxygen-burning and carbon-burning shells combine, matter from different nuclear processes mixes and the interior is rewritten. 

How often such mergers happen among stars that collapse, and whether the abundance patterns show up in the spectra of distant supernovae, remains unknown. A larger set of young remnants seen at similar mid-infrared resolution would settle it.
