1. **World Space Week 2026 Focuses on Rocket Revolution** — The Federal
   Reusable rockets, private launch companies and advances in manufacturing are lowering barriers to orbit and expanding opportunities for space exploration. For decades, getting into space was an expensive and tightly controlled enterprise, largely limited to powerful nations and large defence organisations. That is changing rapidly. Reusable rockets, private launch companies, smaller launch vehicles and advances in manufacturing are bringing down the cost of reaching orbit and opening space to a wider range of countries, companies, universities and researchers. The theme for World Space Week is selected each year by the World Space Week Association, in collaboration with the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs. The annual observance marks two milestones in the history of space exploration: the launch of Sputnik-1 on October 4, 1957, and the coming into force of the Outer Space Treaty on October 10, 1967. Several technological shifts are driving this change, from reusable launch systems and heavy-lift rockets to dedicated small-satellite launchers and advances in propulsion and manufacturing. Reusability as the norm has been pioneered through commercial vertical takeoff and landing systems that transform rockets from single-use machines into reusable launch vehicles. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Lasers Could Clear Orbital Debris
Imagine a single piece of debris the size of a paint chip traveling at seventeen thousand miles per hour striking a satellite the way a bullet strikes glass. Low Earth orbit now holds millions of such fragments, each one a potential trigger for further collisions that multiply the hazard. A space-based laser station could fire short pulses at these objects, nudging them into slightly lower orbits where atmospheric drag finishes the job in weeks rather than centuries. One proposed system would need only a few hundred watts of power per pulse yet could handle dozens of targets per pass over a ground station. The real unknown is coordination: no single nation controls the traffic, and any miscalculation risks pushing debris into paths that endanger crewed stations or active constellations. Engineers continue to model the precise energy requirements and pointing accuracy needed for safe operations.
