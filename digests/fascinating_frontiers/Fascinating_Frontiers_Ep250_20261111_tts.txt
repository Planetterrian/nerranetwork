Two new studies, in Phys dot org coverage, detail how the LISA observatory will detect gravitational waves from double white dwarf binaries and identify planets orbiting within those systems, the first paper examining detection thresholds while the second explores confirmation strategies with infrared observations.

A planet’s gravitational tug imprints a Doppler modulation on the binary’s gravitational-wave signal, appearing as sidebands at higher and lower frequencies, and the method works best for planets with masses and orbital periods that produce those detectable sidebands.

NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope could confirm candidates by detecting excess infrared light from the planet out to at least five hundred light-years, and the Milky Way contains an estimated ten million double white dwarf systems with orbital periods under one hour.

Japanese astronomer Naoki Seto first proposed the concept in two thousand eight, and Casey McGrath, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County and NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center, led the detection-method paper and noted that an exoplanet’s presence becomes imprinted on the gravitational wave signal.

Dwarf galaxies are the universe’s most common residents, yet each one is only about one-tenth the width of the Milky Way.

If the Milky Way stretches one hundred thousand light-years across and holds hundreds of billions of stars, a typical dwarf galaxy spans roughly ten thousand light-years and contains just a few billion stars.
