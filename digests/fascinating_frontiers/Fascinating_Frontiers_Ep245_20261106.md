1. **Moon occults Jupiter over eastern North America — Astronomy Magazine**
   Katelyn Beecroft in London, Ontario captured the event on the morning of Oct. 6, 2026. The waning crescent Moon, about 20 percent lit, passed in front of Jupiter. She tracked by hand with an 8-inch Dobsonian reflector. Video came from a ZWO ASI224MC planetary camera. Sharpest frames were stacked into the final image. The occultation was visible from much of eastern North America that morning. Source: [astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/picture-of-the-day/photo/jupiter-occulted/)

5. **California Science Center opens major air and space expansion Nov. 13 — Daily Trojan**
   The Samuel Oschin Air and Space Center adds 200,000 square feet at Exposition Park. It was developed over four decades with NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Three galleries cover aviation principles, space exploration hardware, and the vertical display of space shuttle Endeavour with its external tank and boosters. The expansion nearly doubles exhibition space and adds 100 hands-on exhibits plus 100 artifacts. Curator Kenneth Phillips has worked at the museum nearly 40 years. Visitors can program a miniature Mars rover and explore a replica of the International Space Station. David Barnhart of USC noted the vertical display forces viewers to look up. The center will offer high school internships and connect students to aerospace opportunities across Los Angeles. The Korean Air Aviation Gallery includes part of a Boeing 747 with exposed internal structure. The Kent Kresa Space Gallery features rockets, robotic spacecraft, and a walk-through solid rocket booster section. The Samuel Oschin Shuttle Gallery places Endeavour vertically beside its tank and boosters to show the full launch configuration. Phillips said the exhibits aim to help visitors understand how flight and space exploration have changed over time and how that has changed us. Barnhart emphasized that hands-on exhibits create a visceral connection for students and adults to aerospace engineering. The location in South Central Los Angeles places the center within reach of several local communities and schools. 7. **Swift observatory ends 22-year mission with atmospheric reentry — NASA Breaking**
   NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory re-entered Earth’s atmosphere on November 4 at 7:39 EST over the North Sumatra region of Indonesia. The satellite had operated for nearly 22 years. Its Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope captured the first one million images used in a 2018 mosaic. Swift combined an X-ray telescope and a gamma-ray telescope to study the fastest and most energetic explosions in the universe. The most recent solar maximum accelerated the decay of the satellite’s orbit. An attempted rescue mission to boost its orbit was not successful. Swift burned on reentry like a shooting star. Its data will continue to support research for years. The observatory’s instruments worked together to examine the most violent cosmic events with unprecedented detail. Source: [science.nasa.gov](https://science.nasa.gov/image-article/apod-2026-november-6-a-farewell-to-swift/)

8. **Hubble image reveals history of globular cluster M30 — Phys.org Space**
   The NASA/ESA Hubble Space Telescope captured Messier 30, a globular cluster 27,500 light-years away in Capricornus. M30 contains tens of thousands to millions of stars and lies in the Milky Way halo. Astronomers estimate globular clusters like M30 formed between 11.5 and 13.5 billion years ago. Harlow Shapley used variable stars in such clusters to show the Sun is not at the galactic center. M30 orbits opposite the Milky Way’s spin direction, suggesting it may have been captured from elsewhere. Hubble observed M30 with the Advanced Camera for Surveys and Wide Field Camera 3 across multiple programs. Data revealed stars down to 20 percent of the Sun’s mass and helped study blue straggler stars. The combined image spans ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared wavelengths. Earlier programs surveyed dozens of Milky Way globular clusters in red and near-infrared light. A later program returned to M30 using three filters that captured bluer light. The most recent observations tracked how different populations of stars moved over fifteen years. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-hubble-galactic-history-stars.html)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Globular Clusters Record the Milky Way’s Early Assembly
A single globular cluster can hold more stars than the entire population of a mid-sized city yet fit inside a volume smaller than the distance from Earth to the nearest star beyond the Sun. M30 packs tens of thousands to millions of stars into a sphere whose outer edges lie only tens of light-years across, a density that would make night skies on any hypothetical planet inside it blaze with thousands of suns at once. These clusters formed when the universe itself was young, between 11.5 and 13.5 billion years ago, and their stars still carry the chemical fingerprints of that era because they have evolved in near isolation ever since. Imagine riding alongside a photon leaving one of M30’s ancient stars: after traveling 27,500 light-years you would cross the halo of the Milky Way and finally reach Earth, carrying information about elements forged before our own Sun existed. Hubble’s repeated observations of M30 combined data from the Advanced Camera for Surveys and Wide Field Camera 3 across programs spanning more than fifteen years, revealing stars down to just 20 percent of the Sun’s mass and tracking the motions of different stellar populations within the cluster. The same images that show blue straggler stars also map how the cluster’s overall structure has changed, because the telescope’s long baseline lets astronomers measure tiny shifts in star positions over time. Earlier programs surveyed dozens of Milky Way globular clusters in red and near-infrared light while a later program returned to M30 using three filters that captured bluer light. Those bluer observations proved critical for studying exotic objects and the multiple populations of stars that can inhabit a single globular cluster. The most recent observations tracked how different populations moved over the fifteen years since Hubble first imaged them. Yet M30 and dozens of other clusters orbit the Milky Way in the opposite direction from the galaxy’s spin, a fact that raises a direct question: were these clusters born inside the Milky Way or were they stolen from smaller galaxies the Milky Way swallowed long ago? The chemical patterns and orbital directions alone cannot yet distinguish between those two histories. Resolving the difference would require either precise measurements of the clusters’ internal velocities at scales Hubble cannot reach or new models that tie the observed star motions to the exact timing of galactic mergers. Globular clusters that formed within the Milky Way preserve the original chemistry of the young galaxy while captured ones may carry signatures from entirely different stellar environments. The combined ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared data from multiple programs already shows how these ancient systems continue to reveal details about the Milky Way’s assembly long after their own formation. We can see the clusters moving and measure their stars’ compositions, but nobody knows exactly when or how many such captures occurred during the galaxy’s early growth.
