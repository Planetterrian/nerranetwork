In brief, radio telescopes may soon help trace the origins of high-energy neutrinos that
pass through Earth unnoticed.
ESA leadership is exploring joint lunar projects with the United Arab Emirates.
A single neutrino can cross the entire diameter of Earth in a fraction of a second while interacting
with almost nothing, yet the same particle can carry information from the edge of the observable
universe that no other messenger survives to deliver.
However, when a high-energy neutrino strikes an ice molecule in Antarctica or a water molecule
in the deep ocean, it generates a cascade of secondary particles that emit a brief radio
pulse traveling outward at the speed of light.
Large antenna arrays listen for that pulse across thousands of cubic kilometers, turning
the planet itself into a detector whose effective volume far exceeds any optical telescope ever
built.
One measurement already in hand shows that the highest energy neutrinos arrive from directions
that do not line up with known gamma-ray sources, suggesting either undiscovered accelerators
or entirely new particle physics processes operating at extreme energies.
The remaining puzzle is why these arrival directions look almost uniform across the sky,
even though most candidate sources, blazars, gamma-ray bursts, or star-forming galaxies, should
cluster along the plane of the Milky Way, until larger radio arrays come online and deliver
statistically robust sky maps, astronomers cannot decide whether the neutrinos are produced
uniformly throughout cosmic time, or whether current instruments are simply tuned to the wrong
wavelengths. The radio technique also sidesteps the optical opacity that hides the universe's earliest
epochs, letting scientists peer back to times when the first stars and galaxies were still forming.
Each detected pulse carries directional information accurate to a few degrees,
energies, enough to begin cross-matching with catalogs from gamma ray and x-ray observatories.
The next generation of arrays will increase sensitivity by another order of magnitude, pushing
the detectable energy threshold downward and revealing whether the current isotropic pattern
persists at lower energies, or finally reveals clustering that matches known source populations.
Until then, every new radio detection adds one more data point to a map that remains mostly
blank.
