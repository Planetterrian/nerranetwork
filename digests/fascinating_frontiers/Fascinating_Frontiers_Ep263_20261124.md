1. **BepiColombo enters stable Mercury orbit after eight years** — European Space Agency (X)
   The joint ESA/JAXA spacecraft completed its orbit insertion despite a software issue that cut the manoeuvre short. It now sits in a stable orbit around the innermost planet. Separation of its two science orbiters is scheduled for 9 December. The mission will study Mercury’s surface, magnetic field and exoplanet-like environment in detail. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/esa/status/2124939363075100989)

2. **Webb maps distinct dust chemistries across Cas A remnant** — Phys.org Space
   Mid-infrared observations show silica-rich dust in one reverse-shocked ejecta clump and carbon-rich dust in another. The Green Monster region contains complex magnesium silicates from pre-explosion mass loss. The data indicate that the oxygen- and carbon-burning shells inside the progenitor merged only hours before core collapse. Lead author Tea Temim of Columbia University said the merger “can cause the shells to merge shortly before the supernova explosion” and change the chemical abundances later seen in the remnant. Co-author Dan Milisavljevic of Purdue University noted that the vast improvement in resolving individual clumps helps tie the explosion back to the progenitor’s makeup hundreds of years afterward. Source: [phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2026-11-webb-evidence-star-layers-merged.html)

### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Stellar Shell Mergers Shape Supernova Chemistry
Massive stars build their chemical structure in onion-like layers. Hydrogen fuses in the core, then helium, carbon, oxygen and heavier elements in successive shells. Gravity keeps these layers distinct until the star’s final hours. When the oxygen- and carbon-burning shells merge, material from different nuclear processes mixes and the star’s internal structure changes dramatically. The Webb observations of Cassiopeia A show exactly this mixing happened in the hours before core collapse. Mid-infrared spectra reveal silica-rich dust in one ejecta clump and carbon-rich dust in another, while the Green Monster structure contains magnesium silicates from earlier mass loss. These distinct chemistries record the moment the shells combined. The merger altered the chemical abundances later observed in the remnant, as noted by lead author Tea Temim. Co-author Dan Milisavljevic added that resolving individual clumps at this scale helps tie the explosion back to the progenitor’s makeup hundreds of years afterward. The data support a scenario in which the interior of the star underwent a dramatic change, possibly just hours before its demise. What is still unknown is how frequently such mergers occur across the population of core-collapse progenitors and whether the resulting abundance patterns can be identified in the spectra of distant supernovae. A larger sample of young remnants observed at similar mid-infrared resolution would settle the question.
