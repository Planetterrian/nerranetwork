Crew Dragon Grace docked at the Harmony module forward port seven hours and fifty-five minutes after liftoff from Cape Canaveral.

NASA confirmed the flight as the fastest launch-to-docking of any U.S. spacecraft in International Space Station history.

The previous U.S. record was held by an earlier Dragon cargo mission that took just over twelve hours.

The rapid transit resulted from favorable orbital phasing rather than new hardware.

The crew included NASA astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk, and Roscosmos cosmonaut Sergey Teteryatnikov.

The agreement builds on a 2024 MoU and four already-planned crewed missions to the International Space Station and Haven-1.

It creates a framework for potential European astronaut flights aboard Haven Station, ESA hardware contributions, jointly defined research campaigns, and dedicated science accommodations after the International Space Station ends operations.

As agencies plan for the post-ISS era, researchers are still finding brand-new worlds forming right now.

A team from Diego Portales University identified Elias 2-24b as the youngest confirmed exoplanet to date.

Researchers reanalyzed coronagraph observations from the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope and compared 2018 and 2020 data from the W.M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii.

The planet appears as a gravitationally bound point of light inside a gap in the disk around the star Elias 2-24.

The gap is thought to have been carved by the forming planet.

Traditional transit methods miss such young worlds because they are still embedded in dense disks of gas and dust.

While newborn planets are being spotted in distant disks, closer to home a joint ESA-CAS mission has captured something dramatic in our own atmosphere.

The ESA-CAS SMILE mission recorded a full auroral oval visible even during daylight hours.

The time-lapse video covers roughly one hour in late July and shows the oval’s turbulent variability in ultraviolet light.

Stars appear to drift across the frame because the spacecraft’s camera view shifts during its orbit.

One goal of SMILE is to study how the solar wind interacts with Earth’s magnetosphere to improve protection of astronauts, spacecraft, and power grids.

New analysis of Hubble observations suggests a planet candidate is accreting onto a white dwarf, implying a second-generation planetary system. The work revives an earlier cold case by showing material from a disrupted body falling onto the stellar remnant. Such systems would form after the star’s red-giant phase destroyed any original planets.

The study presents evidence that a planet formed after the host star became a white dwarf is now losing material to the star through spectroscopic signatures of ongoing accretion. This would be the first known example of a planet forming in the debris disk of a stellar remnant.

The report summarizes the Hubble and Nature findings on a planet candidate orbiting a white dwarf and notes that the system would represent the first detected case of planet formation after a star’s death.

From Mars surface geology we shift to the most extreme gravitational environment in the galaxy.

Researchers modeled how a neutron inside the ergosphere of Sagittarius A* could decay into a proton, electron, and neutrino while extracting rotational energy.

The ergosphere is the region where frame-dragging is so strong that nothing can remain stationary.

The process, first proposed by Roger Penrose in 1969, allows a particle to escape with more energy than it entered by leaving negative energy behind.

Magnetic-field interactions could make the mechanism far more efficient than the basic version.

The study appears on the arXiv preprint server.

We close today with a quick look at how orbital life affects the people living it.

RCSI University’s Dr Ronan Lordan reviewed how a ninety-minute orbital period creates sixteen sunrises and sunsets per day, breaking the usual light-dark cues for the circadian clock.

Two biological systems normally align sleep pressure and circadian timing, but spaceflight can separate them.

Astronauts receive eight and a half hours of scheduled sleep opportunity, yet the mismatch can leave them tired without being biologically ready for sleep.

Tailored lighting, exercise timing, and meal scheduling are used to mitigate the effects.
