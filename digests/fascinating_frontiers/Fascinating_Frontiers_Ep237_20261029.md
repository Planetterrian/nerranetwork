**Reid Wiseman Named JPL Deputy Director — NASA Breaking**
Former astronaut and Artemis II commander Reid Wiseman joins Caltech as associate vice president and NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory as deputy director on November 30. He will oversee strategic leadership, laboratory operations, and national security programs as the lab approaches its 90th anniversary. Wiseman flew 165 days on the International Space Station during Expedition 41 and later served as NASA’s chief astronaut. During Artemis II he traveled 695,081 miles on a lunar flyby farther than any humans before. Caltech president Ray Jayawardhana called the move a natural fit for institutions that send spacecraft to the far reaches of the solar system. Wiseman also commanded NASA’s 21st Extreme Environment Mission Operations expedition, living for eight days in an ocean-floor habitat in 2016. He earned a bachelor’s degree in computer and systems engineering from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute and a master’s in systems engineering from Johns Hopkins University. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman noted that Wiseman has spent his career at the edge of what is possible and brings technical judgment and operational experience to push that edge farther. Wiseman said the experience of seeing the Sun disappear behind the Moon from deep space was the jumping-off point to go do the impossible things that Caltech and JPL dream and do for NASA. Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/jpl/caltech-names-artemis-ii-commander-reid-wiseman-as-nasa-jpl-deputy/)

**Layered Mylar Proposed for Lunar Habitat Temperature Control — Universe Today**
Engineers are testing layered Mylar sheets to regulate temperatures inside future Moon habitats that must handle extreme heat swings and physical flexing. Researchers focus on balancing thermal protection with the mechanical demands of habitat assembly and daily operations. The approach aims to protect both trained astronauts and eventual civilian residents from the harsh lunar environment that contrasts sharply with Earth’s mild temperatures. **Supersonic Atmospheric Waves Proposed as Migration Driver for Hot Jupiters — Phys.org Space**

The research from The Pennsylvania State University team was published in The Astrophysical Journal and offers a new explanation for how these planets reach their stiflingly close orbits. ### Cosmic Deep Dive: How Venus Displays Phases Like the Moon
Venus swings between Earth and the Sun once every year and a half, placing it almost directly in line with our star from our viewpoint. When that alignment occurs, the planet’s night side faces us and only a thin sliver of its sunlit atmosphere remains visible, forming a glowing crescent whose tips extend beyond a simple half-circle. Those extended tips are called the horns, created when sunlight passes through the dense upper atmosphere and bends around the planet’s curved edge. The same geometry that produces our monthly new Moon therefore produces a new-Venus phase, except Venus’s thick air scatters and refracts light so the crescent appears brighter and wider than geometry alone would predict. Ancient Babylonian astronomers recorded these brightness changes thousands of years ago, and the Maya preserved similar observations in their oldest surviving book. The crescent can be seen with binoculars or a small telescope during twilight, and on rare occasions sharp-eyed observers have spotted it without optical aid. Because the alignment repeats on a predictable schedule, astronomers can forecast exactly when the horns will appear again. The featured image from October 29, 2026 shows Venus seen almost directly between the Sun and the Earth, with the backlit atmosphere refracting and scattering sunlight to create the glowing arc. This happens once every year and a half, and it is like the alignment that happens every month during a new Moon. But the new Moon is dark, while Venus’ backlit atmosphere refracts and scatters the sunlight, creating a glowing crescent arc that extends beyond 180 degrees. The tips of this arc are called the “horns.” Many other cultures have similar astronomical records, including the oldest surviving Maya book. The crescent can be seen with a small telescope or binoculars during twilight, but those with exceptionally acute eyesight may be able to see it with the unaided eye. What remains unexplained is precisely how much the atmospheric refraction varies with height and composition, and whether those variations change over Venus’s long-term climate cycles. We can watch the geometry unfold, yet the detailed light-bending physics inside the horns still holds unanswered questions that future spacecraft spectra may resolve. The process repeats on a cycle tied to the relative positions of Earth, Venus, and the Sun, giving observers repeated chances to study how the planet’s thick carbon-dioxide envelope bends incoming light. Future ultraviolet and infrared observations from orbit could map exactly where the refraction occurs and how temperature and pressure gradients shape the horns from one alignment to the next.
