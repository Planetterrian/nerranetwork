### Cosmic Spotlight
NASA’s Psyche spacecraft is using Mars as a gravitational slingshot this week, gaining speed without burning fuel while its cameras snap thousands of test images. The flyby demonstrates how missions can reach distant targets more efficiently by borrowing a planet’s orbital energy. Precise timing and trajectory are critical, because even small errors would send the probe off course for its 2029 metal-asteroid rendezvous. The same technique has powered many earlier missions, yet each new application still requires careful modelling of the planet’s gravity field. What remains unknown is how much additional data the Mars images will yield beyond camera calibration.

### Cosmic Deep Dive: Gravity’s Hidden Boost
A spacecraft can pick up thousands of kilometres per hour from a planet’s gravity without firing its engines once. Imagine riding alongside the probe as it dives toward Mars: the planet’s pull accelerates the craft, but because Mars itself is moving around the Sun the spacecraft leaves with a higher speed relative to the Sun than it arrived with. In the case of Psyche the boost adds roughly 2 kilometres per second, enough to shorten the journey to the asteroid belt by years. The geometry must be exact; arriving a few minutes early or late changes the exit velocity dramatically. Yet scientists still cannot predict with complete certainty how a planet’s uneven mass distribution will tug on the spacecraft during the closest approach. That lingering uncertainty keeps trajectory teams refining their models right up to the moment of the flyby.
