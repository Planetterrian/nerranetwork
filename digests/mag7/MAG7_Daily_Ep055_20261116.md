### Top News
**Amazon loses 40 Leo satellites in a ULA Vulcan launch failure: GeekWire**
United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan failed about ten minutes after lifting off from Cape Canaveral on Sunday and destroyed 40 satellites for Amazon’s Leo internet network, The Information and GeekWire reported. It was ULA’s first total mission failure since the Boeing–Lockheed Martin joint venture was formed in 2006, and the first of 38 Vulcan launches Amazon has purchased. ULA said it does not yet have specific data on what went wrong and will work with Amazon to return to flight as quickly as possible. Amazon had said a second Vulcan mission could fly within weeks; that flight now waits on the investigation. **Apple launches a study of how people use AI for health: TechCrunch**
Apple opened the Apple Health AI Usage Study in the United States with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform, and a study app that answers health questions with linked, peer-reviewed citations. TechCrunch noted that OpenAI said earlier this year users were asking over 300 million health-related queries each week. ### The Counterpoint
**Forty birds are not the constraint the FCC already wrote down: GeekWire**
Amazon Leo posted that risk comes with any launch, especially an inaugural mission, and that it believes ULA will learn and still deliver on long-term Vulcan plans. The Federal Communications Commission in June let Amazon keep deploying after it missed a July 30 deadline to have 1,616 satellites in service, but satellites that were not operational by then temporarily lose priority in key spectrum bands. Vulcan was the rocket slated to carry more than 1,500 of the 3,232 first-generation satellites, and the full fleet still faces a July 30, 2029 deadline. The number that decides the setback is how many birds are operational before that spectrum priority stays lost, not whether forty satellites are a large share of the paper constellation. ### The Thread: The ride above the satellite
Before this flight, Amazon had 396 Leo satellites up, flown on 14 missions since April 2025. The last success was in July, and it closed the Atlas 5 chapter: eight Leo missions on that older rocket, the last of them carrying 29 satellites. Vulcan was built to lift a heavier stack than that Atlas flight. Melissa Wuerl, Amazon Leo’s director of launch systems, had called it the program’s primary workhorse for the next several years. The network those satellites serve is based in Redmond and was formerly Project Kuiper. Amazon has said it still plans an initial commercial internet rollout this year.

The rocket that failed is a stack of other companies’ engines, which is why a ten-minute flight becomes a multi-firm investigation even when only one of the seven owns the payload. Sunday’s mission was Vulcan’s fifth, the vehicle’s first since a January 2024 debut to fly with six solid boosters, and the first Vulcan flight in nine months. Those Northrop Grumman boosters had thrown nozzle failures on the second flight, in October 2024, and again on a national-security mission in February. Both of those flights still reached orbit, but the Space Force paused Vulcan national-security launches after February. ULA flew this mission with an improved nozzle. The first stage is powered by two BE-4 engines from Blue Origin in Kent, Washington. ULA chief executive Mark Peller said before the flight that those engines had been retrofitted for the oxygen-valve problem behind a New Glenn ground-test explosion in May. The six boosters appeared to perform normally and burned out in less than two minutes. The anomaly came during the burn of the Centaur upper stage, which carries two RL10 engines made by L3Harris. GeekWire, citing Spaceflight Now, reported that the first stage appeared to burn longer than planned and that a telemetry animation showed the Centaur making unusual orientation adjustments early in its burn. Liftoff was at 12:40 p.m. Eastern after two days of delays and a countdown hold at 20 seconds for a technical issue; it is not known whether that issue played any role. Leadership on the rocket side already turned over once: longtime ULA chief Tory Bruno left last December for Blue Origin, and Peller, who led Vulcan’s development, was named chief executive in August.

What changes for Amazon is the order of the remaining book, not a new design of the satellite. New Glenn, the other Bezos-linked rocket, has 24 Leo launches reserved and has been grounded since that May explosion, so it is not a spare engine sitting on the pad. Ariane 6 and SpaceX’s Falcon 9 are also on the manifest. The next dated ride is an Ariane 6 targeted for November 23 at the earliest, carrying 36 satellites. Until ULA names a cause, the heavier Vulcan cadence Amazon bought as its primary workhorse stays in the hangar, and every other rocket in that book has to absorb a schedule written for a vehicle that just failed its first flight in nine months.
