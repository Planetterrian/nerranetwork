> **Thousands of UK publishers now face a CMA case over Meta's AI content terms.**

**What You Need to Know:** Thousands of UK publishers now face a CMA case over Meta's AI content terms. The watchdog is asking whether Meta can take website content for its own products, including AI, from businesses that use its services. The CMA board is due to consider its next strategic-market-status proposals in spring 2027.

### Top News
**CMA opens a Meta investigation over publisher content for AI: The Next Web**
The UK Competition and Markets Authority on Friday opened a Competition Act 1998 investigation into whether Meta holds a dominant position and is imposing terms that let it collect content from the websites of businesses and publishers that use its services, then use that content in its own products, including AI, without those businesses choosing whether or how it is used. Sarah Cardell, the CMA's chief executive, said digital platforms help UK businesses reach millions of customers, but a company with a dominant position must not use it to impose unfair terms. The watchdog said the terms could be an unlawful imposition of unfair trading conditions on thousands of UK businesses, and it acted on concerns from the News Media Association and the Movement for an Open Web. Meta had planned the terms for 30 October, pushed them to 20 November after talks with the CMA, and on 28 October paused the rollout until it has consulted industry groups. In Norway, a court earlier in the week temporarily banned Meta from enforcing the new terms after 10 media outlets argued the terms threatened their rights to their content. Separately, the CMA is asking Meta for information about Meta One, its business subscription, after the Federation of Small Businesses raised concerns about small firms posting external links on Facebook and Instagram without paying; the regulator has not decided on formal action, and that question sits outside the new investigation. Source: [thenextweb.com](https://thenextweb.com/news/cma-meta-investigation-publishers-ai-terms)

### The Counterpoint
**Meta says the publisher case has no grounds: The Next Web**
Meta said there were "no grounds" for an investigation. ### The Thread: Two backlogs, one cash test
Google Cloud's backlog climbed 256 percent to $561 billion. Amazon Web Services' backlog rose 189 percent to $578 billion. Together those stocks of contracted work are about $1.14 trillion, the figure Goldman Sachs strategists Mark Wilson and Tony Pasquariello are split on, TokenPost reported.

Google Cloud revenue increased 103 percent year over year. AWS revenue grew 39 percent. Wilson argues that the growth rates and the backlogs show AI demand remains strong and that returns are beginning to materialize at the large cloud providers. He also treats the figures as an answer to doubts about whether AI investment is paying off. Pasquariello argues the other side of the same print. Cloud growth, he says, may conceal weakness in other core businesses. Rising capital spending at technology companies has not, in his view, demonstrated comparable earnings-growth potential. He points to slower upward revisions to forward earnings-per-share estimates, and to signs that S&P 500 earnings growth may have peaked.

The cash number sitting next to the backlog is Alphabet's, not a blended cloud total. Alphabet recorded a $910 million free cash flow outflow for the quarter, compared with market expectations for a $3.5 billion inflow. Goldman Sachs strategist Rich Privorotsky framed the industry question as which companies in AI will capture the profits, and what financing costs they will face. In October, the S&P 500 excluding AI stocks outperformed technology shares. Both strategists described market volatility as unusual. They differed on whether the AI rally would continue.

The shared object is the backlog: work customers have committed to that has not yet been recognized as revenue. Both clouds are using that stock to show that AI capacity has buyers lined up, and both are still in the build. They are not the same shape of business. Alphabet's cloud revenue is the faster of the two, up 103 percent, and its backlog of $561 billion is the one that jumped 256 percent. Amazon's cloud grew 39 percent, and its backlog is the larger pile, $578 billion, up 189 percent. The $1.14 trillion sum is the joint claim. What it changes for Alphabet is already a cash result: an outflow of $910 million in a quarter when market expectations had been an inflow of $3.5 billion. What it changes for Amazon is that a $578 billion backlog now has to be read against Pasquariello's warning that cloud strength can hide slower earnings momentum elsewhere, while capital spending still runs ahead of the profit it is supposed to produce. Wilson reads the combined figure as returns starting to form. Pasquariello reads it as a mask. Privorotsky's financing-cost question hangs over both, because a backlog is a delivery promise, and delivery on this scale is being built with other people's capital as well as the cloud providers' own cash.
