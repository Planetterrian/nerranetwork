British Airways has written Meta’s AI glasses into its carriage rules.

PYOK reports the airline changed the General Conditions of Carriage so passengers cannot use those glasses to film staff or seatmates in secret.

Section 7A.10 lets British Airways refuse to carry someone who films, live-streams or photographs crew or other colleagues without consent.

Section 11A lets the airline put that passenger off the aircraft, refuse another flight including a booked return, and report them to the police.

Wearing the glasses is still allowed.

An LED is supposed to show that recording is on, and the report notes cases of people covering that light.

Cabin crew and other customer-facing staff at the airline are already barred from smart glasses themselves.

This sits months after a ban on photographing or filming those workers without express consent, and on live-streaming anyone without permission.

Amazon’s new open decision model was timed on NVIDIA and Apple silicon, not on an AWS instance the write-up describes.

Strands Decider 2B has two billion parameters and is meant to pick from a list the developer already wrote: routing, tool choice, classification, guardrails, context.

It returns a decision plus confidence scores, not a long answer.

The base is Qwen 3.5 at two billion parameters, with the language-model head swapped for a pointer head and a rank-sixteen LoRA adapter on top.

Weights, training data and scripts are public, through a package called strands-decider, on the command line or inside Strands agents.

Median decision time was about one hundred fifteen milliseconds on an NVIDIA RTX 3090.

Small tasks took one hundred fifty-three milliseconds on an Apple M3 MacBook.

On JevBench, checkpoint v nineteen scored seventy-two point three percent across two hundred thirty-one public tasks.

InfoQ sets it beside TypeSafe AI’s Jev, a managed API for the same kind of structured choice, and says the published bench does not show a direct win over Jev.
