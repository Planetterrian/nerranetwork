### Top News
**AlphaProof Nexus takes open problems into a Science study: The Decoder**
The Decoder reports that a new study in Science covers AlphaProof Nexus. The system is built around Gemini 3.1 Pro, which drafts and revises proofs in the formal language Lean; Lean checks each draft after every edit, and the error messages guide the next attempt. For harder sub-problems it can call AlphaProof, though DeepMind reports the Erdős problems were solved even without that specialized tool. It also proved 44 conjectures from the OEIS integer-sequence database and improved a known bound in min-max optimization, a technique used to train some AI models. The team shared those solutions online ahead of the paper. ### The Counterpoint
**Nine is a slice of 353, and the field is boycotting the other pile: The Decoder**
The Erdős score in the same Science study is nine out of 353 open problems, so the list the paper itself uses is still mostly untouched. OpenAI recently put hundreds of AI proofs on GitHub instead of submitting them to journals. The Association for Human Mathematics, led by Terence Tao, called for a boycott. That boycott is the live objection to treating machine proofs as settled when they skip journals; DeepMind's choice of Science does not, by itself, close the argument over what counts. Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/google-deepminds-alphaproof-nexus-solved-dozens-of-open-math-problems-and-actually-got-peer-reviewed/)

### The Thread: The bill, then the milliseconds
Amazon's capital spending to build AI data-center capacity is $220 billion this year alone, The Information writes, and the piece places Alphabet and Meta in that same spending class. The growth number it does publish belongs to Walmart: expected revenue growth this year of about 5.6 percent, described as about a third of Amazon's estimated growth rate. In that account, the outlay depends on business demand for AI services taking off. A different comparison, published by Tech Insider, sets three clouds against each other on game latency. The headline figure is 10-20 milliseconds for AWS Wavelength versus Azure edge zones and Google's edge. Those milliseconds are not a site count. The $220 billion is not a latency measurement. Together the two pieces still name four of the seven on one problem: Amazon, Alphabet and Meta on the spending line, and Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet on the edge-latency line. Microsoft appears as Azure, not as a capex peer in The Information's piece. Meta appears on the bill, not in the millisecond table. The five figures, with no closing price attached, are $220 billion, 5.6 percent, about one third, 10 milliseconds and 20 milliseconds. Neither article says how many of those dollars are aimed at shaving latency toward that 10-20 millisecond band rather than at full campuses. That missing split is the measurement both accounts leave open.
