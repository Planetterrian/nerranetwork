### Lead
**Commission selects 46 new Strategic Projects to bolster the EU’s supply of critical raw materials: The European Sting**
The European Commission said today it has selected 46 new Strategic Projects in 16 member states, chosen from 102 applications, to diversify supply and depend less on any single supplier of critical raw materials. Under the Critical Raw Materials Act, the 2030 benchmarks are that EU capacity meet at least 10 percent of annual consumption for extraction, 40 percent for processing and 25 percent for recycling. Eight projects are extraction, 11 processing and 19 recycling, plus eight integrated projects — three combining extraction and processing, five combining processing and recycling — covering 15 of 17 strategic raw materials in the Act. Battery chains are the largest cluster: seven lithium, 12 nickel, 10 cobalt, five manganese and four graphite; two magnesium and three tungsten projects are tied to defence and aerospace, and four rare-earth projects to permanent magnets. The Commission said these 46, added to 47 EU projects selected in March last year and 13 third-country projects selected in June last year, are expected to contribute to those derisking targets. Business Review reports that two of the new projects are in Romania. **Czech Local Elections Deliver Warning to Babis’s Governing Coalition: Balkan Insight**
Czech municipal elections on October 9–10, with votes counted by Sunday midday, did not overturn the political map less than a year after Prime Minister Andrej Babis’s coalition took office, on a turnout of 48 percent. Local associations and independent candidates again took the largest share, more than 50,000 seats out of 62,000, while 22 of 27 Senate mandates in this partial election go to a second round on October 16–17. Babis said close to 1,500 council seats showed ANO is the strongest political force at all levels except the Senate; ANO finished first in six of 13 regional capitals, against eight in 2022, including Karlovy Vary, Pilsen, Ostrava and Olomouc. Balkan Insight says most analysts found ANO down on both seats and the popular vote from four years ago, with its worst-ever Prague result and a setback in Usti nad Labem, a long-time stronghold. Junior partner Motorists for Themselves broke through only in Ostrava and did poorly in Prague and Brno — leader and Foreign Minister Petr Macinka said he was not happy and not pleased — while the Freedom and Direct Democracy party of House Speaker Tomio Okamura won fewer than 170 municipal seats, and vice-chairman Radim Fiala said the party must consider what to change before the next elections. **Arla to close packaging plant in Sweden: Dairy Business Middle East & Africa**
Arla Foods said it will close the Falbygdens Ost import and packaging site in Falköping, Sweden, affecting about 60 jobs, because of long-running losses and weaker demand for much of that range. The plant imports, stores, processes and packs speciality and dessert cheeses; Arla said it does not make cheese there and does not use milk from the co-op’s farmer-owners. Dessert cheeses sold as Falbygdens will be phased out, some popular lines moving to Castello, while aged Swedish cheese stays under Kvibille. Anna Schreil, head of logistics and production in Sweden and Finland, called the decision a heavy message for staff and Falköping, and said a farmer-owned co-op has to put money where it creates the most long-term value for owners, including more Swedish milk and food security. Arla said it is in talks with the unions and will support affected workers; it will keep Osthuset on Energigatan, with about ten roles, and said that since 2023 the board has approved more than 5 billion Swedish kronor, about 446 million dollars, of investment in Sweden, including a recent 28.3 million euro expansion of the cottage-cheese plant in Falkenberg. ### The Region and the World
**Can China and the EU avert a trade war?: ThinkChina**
Europe’s goods deficit with China reached 359.3 billion euros last year, more than double the 2019 gap, and Brussels says it cannot accept a deficit now running at 1.1 billion euros a day, which it attributes to unfair Chinese trade practices. Beijing says those figures do not hold up: nearly half of China-EU trade is intermediate goods that European firms buy, upgrade and sell on, so the surplus sits in China while Europe takes the profit, and the EU keeps a long-running services surplus with China. Trade Commissioner Maros Sefcovic held two days of talks in Beijing with Commerce Minister Wang Wentao from October 8, ahead of an EU summit in Brussels this week on the response to Chinese competition. Talks ended late Friday, October 9, with a 16-point consensus covering trade and investment, export controls and intellectual property; China agreed to curb hybrid-vehicle exports to the bloc, a step aimed at avoiding harsher restrictions. ThinkChina says that gesture is unlikely to reshape the wider relationship soon, and that it buys time for further talks in January and March. ### Both Sides: Retiring France’s central-bank-held debt
France’s argument is over a slice of public debt the state already holds on its own books, not over a bill with a named vote date. Emmanuel Moulin, governor of France’s central bank, wants large budget cuts. His stated reason is the risk of being gradually strangled by rising interest rates. Jean-Luc Mélenchon, leader of La France Insoumise and a presidential contender, has instead proposed dealing with about 600 billion euros — 673 billion dollars, or 18 percent of France’s aggregate public debt. Those are French government bonds the central bank owns and will hold to maturity. At maturity, the essay says, taxpayers would pay more tax, or borrow again, to repay their own central bank. Mélenchon’s case is that it is absurd for one arm of the French state to borrow 600 billion euros in order to pay another arm. His alternative is a swap between the Treasury and the central bank that, in practice, retires that debt and frees room for investment. The essay says the plan draws on debt-swap proposals made in 2015 by a then Greek finance minister. Moulin’s camp treats spending cuts as the way to keep interest costs from closing in. Mélenchon’s camp treats the internal debt as a loop that cuts would not break, and as space that could fund investment instead.
