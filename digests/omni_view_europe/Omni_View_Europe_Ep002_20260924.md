### Lead
**Poland says fire at Starlink station is sabotage as Denmark warns of rising Russian threat: BBC Europe**
Deputy Prime Minister and Digital Minister Krzysztof Gawkowski said a fire that broke out Wednesday night at a state-owned Starlink ground station in Wola Krobowska, in the Masovian Voivodeship, about 50 kilometres south of Warsaw, was deliberately designed to disable the station and disrupt internet access for institutions including the Ukrainian army. He told TVP Info that an investigation is ongoing but that many indications suggest the incident fits a new Russian doctrine of attack. The Kremlin has not commented. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said he disliked labelling the fire sabotage rather than an accident without 100 percent certainty, but that it looks bad. The station remains operational; the fire did not cause significant damage and there were no injuries, and Poland’s counterintelligence agency, the ABW, and the police are investigating. State-owned Exatel runs the site, which, with a station in Kaunas, Lithuania, links Starlink satellites to terrestrial networks serving Central Europe and Ukraine. Denmark’s Defence Intelligence Service said in a new assessment that it expects Russia to escalate hybrid warfare in the coming months, that an attack remains highly unlikely, and that there is a low but growing risk of a limited military attack on Nato countries. Moscow denies preparing attacks to test European support for Ukraine. Germany has blamed Russia for an attempted drone attack this month on Leipzig/Halle Airport, and France has ordered a plan to protect critical infrastructure and defence-industry sites. Source: [bbc.co.uk](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmp30r1klk37o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss)

### Across the Region
**Russia Bombards Ukraine as 51 Nations Call for Ceasefire: UNITED24 Media**
On 23 September, as 51 UN member states in New York called on Russia to halt attacks, Kyiv came under drone strikes for most of the day, with air-raid sirens sounding 10 times and drones hitting a market, a supermarket and a business centre. Ukraine’s Air Force said that from early 24 September it intercepted 227 aerial targets, listing three Iskander-M, S-400 or KN-23 ballistic missiles and 219 Shahed, Gerbera and other drones, with strikes recorded at 13 locations and debris at 10. Sirens warning of a missile threat began at 12:55 a.m., and the alert then ran from 3 a.m. until noon, more than 10 hours. The account says several people were killed and dozens injured, and that a maternity hospital, a clinic, homes, gas stations, internet providers and energy infrastructure were damaged. The 23 September joint statement, whose signatories include Ukraine, EU countries, Canada, Australia, Japan and South Korea, recalled that Ukraine agreed to a full and unconditional ceasefire in March 2025 and called on Russia to do the same. **Russia recruiting up to 25,000 North Koreans to assemble drones: What the data shows: Euronews**
A UN Security Council Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team report, as cited by Euronews, put 15,000 to 30,000 North Korean workers in Russia at the end of 2025, with up to 1,000 at defence plants, steelworks and shipyards. Ukrainska Pravda reported that up to 25,000 North Koreans are working on drone production in the Alabuga special economic zone in Tatarstan, where Russian versions of Iran’s Shahed drone are assembled. Euronews also reports that the UN says up to 25,000 North Koreans assemble drones in Russia and that most of their pay is seized by Kim Jong-un’s regime. Radio Liberty, cited in the same account, says workers are registered as students to bypass a UN ban on North Korean labour abroad; in 2025 Russia issued more than 36,400 visas to North Korean citizens, 35,800 of them student visas. The report describes fictitious study schemes, legal changes that let “students” be paid, and intermediaries, including from Belarus, moving workers from the Far East into Moscow, St Petersburg, Irkutsk and Tatarstan. ### Both Sides: The Parthenon sculptures
What is being decided is whether the Parthenon sculptures in the British Museum return permanently to Athens, and whether that step is for the museum or for Parliament.

Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis, writing in The Guardian, says the strongest reason to act is that the pieces are one monument and should be seen together in the Acropolis Museum, opened in 2009 with a gallery built for them. He says what is needed is the political will and courage to reunite the sculptures and turn a historic dispute into cultural friendship. He rejects any deal in which Greece would recognise the British Museum’s ownership, or borrow what he calls parts of its own monument. A Bayeux-style loan, he writes, would repeat the split: nobody would cut what survives of that tapestry in two and show half in London and half in Bayeux. He notes that Andy Burnham had previously called for the sculptures’ return with no strings attached.

The case against a permanent return, as put by the museum and by critics in the reporting, starts from the law., so a permanent transfer would need MPs. Director Nicholas Cullinan told the Financial Times last month that the Bayeux Tapestry loan had given hope that some tangible progress can happen. A spokesperson told AFP that the museum’s openness to a deal is unchanged, and did not confirm a Daily Telegraph report that the Greek side had walked away from years of talks over a lack of progress. The museum’s website says no loan request has been received. Burnham, who replaced Keir Starmer in July, told the Financial Times in August that the question is for the British Museum, the government’s longstanding position. Critics warn that moving the marbles could set a precedent for other objects held by UK museums.

Both accounts accept that the museum has held the friezes for more than two centuries and that the Bayeux loan has put cross-border cultural deals back in view. Greece says it.
