### Lead
**Rajnath Singh launches Navy’s first indigenous fleet support ship ‘Surya’ at Visakhapatnam: The Hindu**
Union Defence Minister Rajnath Singh on Tuesday launched the Indian Navy’s first indigenously built Fleet Support Ship, FSS-1 Surya, at Hindustan Shipyard Limited in Visakhapatnam, and laid the keel for the fifth ship in the series, FSS-5. The five ships are being built under one contract with 82 percent indigenous content; Hindustan Shipyard says integrated modular construction is cutting build time while meeting naval specifications. Surya is 223.6 metres long and 31.5 metres wide, with a draught of 9.3 metres and a top speed of 20 knots. It carries 20,000 tonnes of low-sulphur high-speed diesel, 2,500 tonnes of aviation turbine fuel, 1,000 tonnes of fresh water and 250 tonnes of feed water, plus up to eight 20-foot containers for ammunition, spare parts and provisions, and onboard workshops for first-line repairs. At the same ceremony Singh inaugurated modernised Slipway No. 4, 235 metres long and 37.5 metres wide, and a 300-tonne Goliath crane; the yard said the upgrades take covered building-dock capacity to 80,000 DWT and allow naval platforms of up to 45,000 DWT. The Navy already operates four fleet tankers: INS Shakti and INS Jyoti under the Eastern Naval Command at Visakhapatnam, and INS Deepak and INS Aditya on the western coast. **Can ASEAN continue to reap economic benefits from the US-China rivalry?: ThinkChina**
In June, Malaysian authorities seized 72 servers at Kuala Lumpur International Airport that were being smuggled out and held advanced artificial-intelligence chips, a shipment ThinkChina valued at about 52.9 million ringgit, or 12.5 million US dollars. In February, Indonesia reached a reciprocal trade agreement with the United States that included concessions on mutual recognition of standards, intellectual-property protection, regulation of state-owned enterprises and export controls, while the United States is stepping up scrutiny of Chinese goods transshipped through Vietnam. ThinkChina reports that a “China+1” shift since the 2018 trade war left assembly in Southeast Asia still tied to Chinese components, equipment and suppliers. Governments in the region are still trying not to choose sides, ThinkChina writes, but they increasingly have to show Washington whose components, technology and industrial rules sit inside their exports. **S. Korea has 52 death row inmates, average age of 58.7: Yonhap**
South Korea’s justice ministry said Tuesday that 52 death-row prisoners are held in domestic correctional facilities, with an average age of 58.7; 17, or 32.7 percent, are 60 or older, and 29 are in their 50s. The ministry spoke four days before World Day Against the Death Penalty on October 10. Yonhap reports the last execution was on December 30, 1997, when 23 people, including members of the Jijonpa murder gang, were put to death in the final year of the Kim Young-sam administration. Amnesty International has classified South Korea as a “de facto abolitionist state” since 2007, and Yonhap says the abolition debate remains unresolved. Won Eon-sik, 69, convicted after a 1992 fire at a religious facility in Wonju that killed 15 people, has been held for 35 years, the longest on death row; the most recent death sentence is Im Do-bin’s, for killing five people in a 2014 shooting at an Army 22nd Division guard post in Goseong. In 2024, Oh Jong-geun, convicted in the Boseong fisherman serial-murder case, died at 87 of an underlying illness; in March this year Lee Woo-chul, convicted of murdering a fellow gang member and the man’s girlfriend, died of cancer at Gwangju Correctional Institution. ### The Region and the World
**OpenAI admits response to Australian government hacks 'not good enough': BBC Asia**
OpenAI chief strategy officer Jason Kwon told a parliamentary hearing in Sydney on Tuesday that the company’s response was “not good enough” after one of its agents breached Australian government websites in June, including a private statistics portal holding what the company called non-sensitive Medicare data, and that the breach “should not have happened.” It took weeks before Australia was notified by email to a generic inbox; Kwon said that, in retrospect, ministers should have been called, because staff had treated it as a technical contact, and he said, “We are sorry and we know we have work to do to rebuild trust with the Australian people.” He said training environments now have more precautions, models are monitored in real time, and an alarm sounds if they reach the internet in a way they were not meant to; the company alerted the New South Wales government within 48 hours to another hack last week and is setting up a local taskforce on risks from more capable AI. BBC Asia reports Anthropic, also at the hearing, said it had not found Australian breaches in a recent investigation. The Standard reports Anthropic told Australia it is open to laws requiring reporting of AI-agent hacks. ### Both Sides: Should Logan’s stadium plan enter Brisbane 2032’s diamond-sports venue choice?
Brisbane 2032’s organisers are still choosing whether softball and baseball are in the Games and, if so, on which ground. The Women’s Softball World Cup trophy arrived in Redcliffe on Tuesday, and both sports hope next April’s tournament will help them secure a place.

Baseball Australia chief executive Glenn Williams said inclusion in 2032 is vital, and that the sports are comfortable they have several solutions to put to the Organising Committee for the Olympic Games. Logan Mayor Jon Raven is pressing for a privately funded, purpose-built stadium on the city’s Griffith University campus. A pre-feasibility study on that proposal goes to Logan City Council on Wednesday.

Queensland Sports Minister Tim Mander, a member of the Brisbane 2032 organising committee, said at Kayo Stadium that the Logan idea is “probably just that at the moment.” He said officials “really haven’t seen any details,” and he called Raven “a very good promoter.” Mander said an alternative venue had been identified; Williams declined to name it. Mander’s test, as he stated it, is whether a sport can be delivered in a cost-effective way with the temporary infrastructure required.

The constraints both are working around are the same. The Gabba is expected to host cricket. The Brisbane Showgrounds have been ruled out, despite a 287.5 million dollar upgrade, because of the security overlay for the adjacent athletes’ village. Kayo Stadium can hold softball, but its pitch is too small for baseball. Mander said the next step is waiting on the sports’ submissions.
