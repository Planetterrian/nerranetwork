**China's funding of development institutions is up sharply, but its power still lags, study shows: The Straits Times**
A Center for Global Development study released on Thursday found that China has raised funding of multilateral development institutions tenfold since 2010, while its voting power and presence still lag its economic weight. Funding for development banks reached 3 billion dollars in 2024, funding for UN development-related bodies rose 47 percent, and multilateral climate finance rose to 5.25 billion dollars in 2025, while support for Gavi was cut 32 percent. Co-author Ian Mitchell called it a big step up from 15 years ago but a tiny share of China’s economy, and said the choices were selective. Co-author Beata Cichocka said large World Health Organization contributions, made as the United States withdrew, do not fill every gap left by Washington and other Western cuts to development spending. China’s World Bank share of around 6 percent is less than half what the institution’s formula suggests, while the United States keeps a veto with about 16 percent; Western officials have held back share shifts, citing no consensus and a lack of Chinese transparency. ### Both Sides: Should the European Union answer China's export surge with tariffs?
What is being decided is whether the European Union should meet China’s export surge with tariffs and harder demands that Chinese markets open. The European Parliament voted on Wednesday, 454 to 86, for a resolution centred on economic reciprocity and a proportionate response if China does not open its markets. Commission trade envoy Maroš Šefčovič is in Beijing on Thursday for two days with Commerce Minister Wang Wentao, after he set an October deadline to narrow a deficit put at 360 billion euros, about 410 billion dollars, exceeding a billion dollars a day.

The case for pressure is industrial harm. Hilde Vautmans, the Belgian lawmaker who led the resolution, said Europe has economic power and that the time to use it has come. France’s High Commission for Strategy and Planning in February called for swift action, including 30 percent tariffs on many Chinese exports and a weaker euro against China’s currency. Associated Press reporting says politicians and economists across the bloc treat China’s subsidies, and exports diverted after the United States raised tariffs, as a major threat to steel and car plants.

The case for caution is that means and blowback are unsettled. The same report says it remains unclear what the EU can or will do. German auto sales are plummeting in China, while subsidised Chinese cars are positioned to undercut European makers on price, and Volkswagen has already had mass layoffs. France and Germany, in a letter the Associated Press said it had seen, called for a sweeping rethink of China policy.

Both sides accept the scale of the deficit, China’s place as the bloc’s second-largest trading partner, and the diversion of goods toward Europe after US tariffs. The next step named in the reporting is the two-day Beijing meeting, inside the October deadline Šefčovič set.
