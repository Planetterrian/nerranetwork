**What You Need to Know:** Thirteen Chinese military aircraft crossed the Taiwan Strait's median line overnight, and Taiwan's forces responded, as the carrier Liaoning sailed into the western Pacific. ### Lead
**Chinese naval carrier group enters western Pacific: Taipei Times**
A Chinese naval task group led by the aircraft carrier Liaoning sailed yesterday into the western Pacific for training, Chinese state-run Xinhua quoted the People's Liberation Army Navy as saying. The navy said the drill is part of the annual routine training plan and is not directed at any specific country or target. Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense said today the group was being monitored, and Deputy Chief of the General Staff for Intelligence Pan Chun-kuang told a news conference in Taipei that the move had caused heightened tensions and concern among countries in the region. Between 6pm yesterday and 6am today the ministry detected 15 Chinese military aircraft, 10 naval ships and seven official ships around Taiwan; 13 of the aircraft crossed the median line into Taiwan's northern, central, southwestern and eastern air defense identification zones. The armed forces monitored the situation and employed patrol aircraft, navy vessels and coastal missile systems, the ministry said. Pan declined to give further details of the carrier group's movements, said Beijing has held large-scale exercises encircling Taiwan every year since August 2022, and said the ministry would keep watching in real time; he also said Beijing has used propaganda to amplify what he called the cognitive-warfare impact of its drills, which he described as destabilizing. Separately, Taiwan Affairs Office spokesman Chen Binhua said yesterday that Taiwan must bear the consequences after its coast guard, on October 1, used a water cannon and expelled two Chinese fishing boats from prohibited waters near the Pratas Islands, more than 400 kilometres from Taiwan; a Taiwan official with direct knowledge called that normal, careful and proportionate law enforcement, while National Security Council Deputy Secretary-General Lii Wen said China is using militia boats and research vessels in incremental acts of aggression that could disrupt transport or damage infrastructure. ### Across the Region
**Japan PM Takaichi taps Arata Takebe as new farm minister: The Straits Times**
Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi on October 15 nominated Arata Takebe, a former vice-minister in the Agriculture Ministry who previously attended Chicago University, as agriculture minister after Kazuo Yana stood down on October 14. Yana had been linked to allegations of circumventing electoral rules and of threats to cut funding to localities that did not support him; Takaichi had resisted earlier calls for him to quit over reports he claimed to have cut roadworks funding to municipalities that did not back him in February's general election, until allegations that he arranged free transportation to an election kickoff rally, which some media suggested was tantamount to vote-buying. Takaichi told reporters she chose Takebe because he could start at once, including on parliamentary business, and because of his knowledge of agricultural administration, and she said she wanted him to help revive farming and fisheries, rural areas, food security and food technology. Top government spokesman Minoru Kihara said Yana resigned so his presence would not further disrupt Diet deliberations or keep him from focusing on agriculture. A bill to cut the sales tax on food was set to be the main business of the session, but the allegations have dominated debate at times. An NHK poll puts Takaichi's support at 51 percent, a fraction down from September and still above recent premiers; Takebe called Yana's exit very unfortunate and spoke of the need for transparency in agricultural policy. **Batam blackout may have been caused by lightning, bat interference: CNA**
A blackout that began around 6.30am local time on Monday cut power in Batam, Tanjung Pinang and Bintan for about 16 hours, Indonesian outlet Detik reported, and Coordinating Minister for Downstreaming and Energy Transition Bahlil Lahadalia said on Wednesday that lightning and a possible bat in the grid were among the suspected causes. The Batam branch of the Indonesian Economists Association estimated losses of about 233.5 billion rupiah, about 13 million US dollars; chairman Suyono Saputra put business losses in industry and trade services at about 233 billion rupiah, not counting households. Bahlil said several networks had trouble because of lightning, that an animal, possibly a bat, was also suspected, and that he had no further detail on how it may have affected the network; he said repairs and maintenance should be stepped up and that compensation would be discussed first with state power company PLN. The outage disrupted traffic lights, clean water, municipal apps and Hang Nadim International Airport, including air conditioning, baggage conveyors, internet and parking. PLN's commissioner said on Thursday that chief executive Darmawan Prasodjo had been replaced by former risk-management director Suroso Isnandar; it was unclear whether the change was tied to the Batam outage. The Jakarta Post has reported public scrutiny of PLN this year after other major outages, including a May blackout across much of Sumatra from the evening of May 22 until power was fully restored two days later. **Bird flu detected in seal, bird samples taken from remote southern island: ABC News**
Bird flu has been detected in samples from a southern elephant seal and a kelp gull on Macquarie Island, a World Heritage sanctuary about 1,500 kilometres south-east of Hobart with up to 4 million seabirds and 100,000 seals. The samples were brought back with island personnel and tested positive for H5 bird flu. Tasmania's Minister for Environment and Climate Change, Guy Barnett, said on Thursday it was the first recorded detection in a marine mammal in Tasmania. Federal Agriculture Minister Julie Collins told parliament that skuas, giant petrels and penguins had returned negative tests and that there had been no sign of a mass mortality event so far; she said the seal and gull samples would undergo further testing at a CSIRO laboratory. In August the Australian Antarctic Division planned to extract personnel because of the psychological risk of a possible outbreak, and expeditioners were brought back amid concern about witnessing a mass die-off. It is feared up to 90 percent of baby elephant seals could die, as on Heard Island over summer, where more than 13,000 pups are believed to have died from bird flu. Barnett asked the public to keep reporting sick or dead birds and to avoid contact with them. ### The Closer Look
**Taiwan president calls for Micron workers to negotiate 'sincerely' ahead of possible strike: The Straits Times**
President Lai Ching-te stood in Taichung on October 15, at the opening of a new Micron Technology advanced assembly and testing facility, and asked the US memory-chip maker and its workers to negotiate sincerely and in good faith ahead of a possible strike. He said he hoped the government and the private sector would promote stronger communication between labour and management. Both sides, he said, should keep the spirit that fish need water and water needs fish, and keep negotiating to build the broadest possible consensus. Only harmonious labour relations, he said, let employees work with peace of mind and let businesses move steadily and go further. He said he appreciated Micron's commitment to Taiwan, hoped the company would continue expanding investment on the island, and said Micron had secured government support for high-bandwidth memory research and development.

The dispute is about bonuses. Last week a Taiwanese union representing Micron workers said it had authorisation from members to strike over the bonus scheme, at the company's largest manufacturing hub. Micron employs about 15,000 people in Taiwan and has said it invested more than NT$1.6 trillion, about S$63.36 billion, on the island. Unions in Taoyuan and Taichung want a concrete profit-sharing plan. Mediation with the Taoyuan union ended without agreement in September. The Taichung union is still negotiating. The unions are encouraging members to take October 19 off to join a rally in Taipei, which they said would not be a strike.

The factory's customers and the workers are the people a stoppage would touch first. He said the company had already sold out all the capacity for 2027 that this factory will build, and that he expects high-bandwidth memory to grow faster than the rest of the DRAM industry through 2028. Taiwan is a crucial hub for Micron's DRAM and high-bandwidth memory chips, used in artificial-intelligence servers. The Taichung union told Reuters it looked forward to more substantive dialogue and negotiations. Neither Micron nor the Taoyuan union immediately responded to requests for comment on what Lai said. ### Both Sides: Should the president reject a Supreme Court recommendation?
Should President Lee Jae Myung appoint the Supreme Court justice Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae recommended, or send the name back?

Lee and Cho are in a standoff. Lee rejected a candidate Cho suggested and asked for a new recommendation, on the grounds that the name was proposed without sufficient prior consultation. The Constitution says Supreme Court justices are appointed by the president on the recommendation of the chief justice and with the consent of the National Assembly. Legal scholars remain divided on how to read that clause.

Government Legislation Minister Cho Won-cheol sided with Lee at a parliamentary audit on Thursday. His case is that the president is not bound by the recommendation and is not obligated to appoint the recommended candidate, and that the chief justice's power amounts to a form of suggestion. He said it makes no sense to ask parliament for consent when the president has no intention of appointing that person, and that the chief justice would be neglecting his constitutional duties if he refuses to submit a new recommendation.

Cho Hee-dae has refused to budge. His case is that Lee's demand lacks constitutional grounds and could be construed as undermining the judiciary's independence. On that reading, the recommendation is the constitutional route to the bench, not a suggestion the president may discard.

Lee is still asking for another candidate. Cho is still declining to offer one. Source: [en.yna.co.kr](https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20261015006600315)

### Progress Watch
**BIFF wraps up, draws more than 200,000 admissions: Yonhap**
The Busan International Film Festival is set to close later Thursday after more than 200,000 admissions in its 10-day run, the first time admissions have passed that mark since 2015, organizers said. Nearly 77,000 more people joined other cinema programs, and overall visitors rose by about 40,000 from the previous year. The 31st edition screened 315 films, including 94 world premieres, the biggest slate since 2019, and it was the first Busan edition with A-list accreditation from the International Federation of Film Producers Associations, alongside Cannes, Berlin and Venice. For audiences in Busan, that is a larger festival than the city has had in a decade; beginning this year, the top Busan Award winner is also eligible for submission to the Oscars in the Best International Feature Film category.
