The case on both sides of mandatory confiscation starts with a court urging, not a new statute. Justice B. Pugalendhi of the Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court told The Hindu that the Prevention of Corruption Act needs amendments so attachment and confiscation are mandatory for property believed to come from corrupt means, or from wealth out of line with known income.

His reason for the change is that the nineteen eighty-eight Act should do more than punish people found guilty. It should also stop them enjoying or selling what was acquired illegally.

He called attachment an important piece of that machinery, and the Court said corruption has to be treated more seriously than any other offence because it has reached every part of society.

Only mandatory, deterrent rules, the Court said, let the government actually attach property and put fear into officials who still enjoy what was obtained through corrupt means.

The reason to leave the Act alone, as the Court describes the law now, is that punishment of the guilty is already its job.

The Bench says that without a mandatory rule those attachment provisions would sit on paper, with no deterrent effect, and illegally acquired property could still be enjoyed or sold.

The judgment treats as shared ground that the Act already targets corrupt practices and that attachment is already part of the mechanism.

The Court hopes the government will move and bring in suitable amendments.
