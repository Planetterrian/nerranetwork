Starship propellant, from an engineering standpoint, only beats Falcon 9 economics after the vehicle reaches high flight rates.

A full stack holds roughly four thousand six hundred to five thousand one hundred tonnes of propellant.

One Falcon 9 tanks about four hundred ninety to five hundred fifteen tonnes, so ten of those flights burn a similar mass.

One half-loaded Starship on Flight fourteen already equaled the work of about ten Falcon 9 V2 Mini flights, carrying twenty-six V3 Starlink satellites rated at roughly one terabit per second apiece.

At full load the gap widens, because both stages are designed to fly again and the vehicle can put up to twenty times Falcon 9's orbital mass on a comparable propellant burn.

Reuse spreads the same tanks and fuel over many flights; until the rate is high, those tonnes do not yet mean cheaper satellites.

Oxygen and methane are liquefied from electricity and feedstock, so cost per tonne tracks power price and plant hours, not scarce ore.

A back-of-envelope figure for air separation is on the order of zero point four to one kilowatt-hour per kilogram of liquid oxygen, with methane via hydrogen adding more through electrolysis.

Kinetic energy to low Earth orbit is about thirty megajoules per kilogram; gravity and drag mean the chemical energy you burn is several times that, which is why the tanks dwarf the payload.

The rocket equation is unforgiving: most of the propellant exists to lift the rest of the propellant, so throwing stages away multiplies the metal you pay for.

Ten Falcon 9 flights spread that energy across ten vehicles and ten ground crews, while one Starship flight pours it into a single stack and a single pad.

Once the Super Heavy booster and the Ship both return and fly again within days, tower, pad, and factory stop dominating the per-flight bill.

On a generic rocket, dry structure might be on the order of a tenth of the fueled mass; discard it and you buy a new tenth every flight, but fly that metal a hundred times and a back-of-envelope split puts the structure near one percent per flight.

That is the Idiot Index in launch: the finished satellite costs many times the raw propellant and metal because expendable hardware ate the money — a rough estimate is tens of times those inputs.

Starship drives that ratio down by reusing both stages and flying often enough that overhead per flight becomes small.

When one stack flies as many times in a year as ten Falcon 9 boosters combined, the propellant math becomes a lower cost for every kilogram placed in orbit.
