### Engineering Deep Dive
Dragon’s return from the ISS demonstrates how a reusable capsule manages thermal loads and structural stresses during reentry without expending the vehicle. The capsule separates from the station, performs a deorbit burn, and then orients its heat shield forward to absorb the kinetic energy of orbital velocity. Peak heating occurs at roughly 8 km/s relative speed, where the PICA-X tiles on the shield radiate away the majority of the energy while the underlying structure stays below critical temperatures. Because the same heat-shield tiles flew on prior missions, the design margins must accommodate both the initial ablation and any micro-cracking from repeated cycles. After peak heating the vehicle deploys parachutes at subsonic speeds, shifting the problem from thermal protection to precise timing of drogue and main chute deployment so the splashdown loads remain within the capsule’s certified envelope. The science samples inside stay protected because the internal pressure vessel and avionics bays are isolated from the external heat pulse by multiple layers of insulation and active cooling loops that run until just before parachute deployment. Over successive flights the only hardware replaced at scale is the heat-shield coating itself, while the primary structure, avionics, and propulsion elements accumulate flight hours. This reuse pattern lowers the marginal cost of each cargo return to the price of inspection, refurbishment labor, and a new set of parachutes rather than a new capsule. The same margins explain why Dragon can be turned around for the next mission on a schedule measured in weeks once the samples are offloaded and the vehicle is inspected at the recovery site.
