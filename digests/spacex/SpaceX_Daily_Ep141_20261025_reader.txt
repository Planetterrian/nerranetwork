What converts a tanker plan from a spreadsheet into a flight manifest is the engineering reality of how much propellant actually arrives.

The in-space transfer test will measure boil-off, ullage, and residuals rather than leave them as paper allowances.

Those three are different physics, and they add. Boil-off is heat turning cryogenic liquid into gas you cannot use as dense fluid.

Liquid methane lives near one hundred eleven kelvin and liquid oxygen near ninety, so a tanker in orbit is a thermos sitting in sunlight.

The solar constant is about one thousand three hundred sixty watts per square meter.

Multi-layer insulation, stacked reflective films with vacuum between them, cuts the heat leak, but it never reaches zero.

Every watt that remains turns a trickle of liquid into vapor, which you vent as lost mass or accept as rising tank pressure.

A waiting tanker loops through roughly ninety-minute low-Earth orbits, sun and shadow, and if the campaign needs many ships, the early arrivals wait longer.

Wait time feeds boil-off, boil-off feeds extra tanker demand, and extra tankers feed wait time.

Ullage is free fall. On the ground, gravity parks liquid at the bottom of a tank and gas at the top.

In orbit, liquid and vapor mix, and pumps want liquid.

If the outlet swallows a bubble, the pump starves on vapor and the lander does not get dense fluid.

Settling thrusters give the stack a tiny acceleration so liquid collects at the aft end, and header tanks, small vessels kept full, give the pumps a guaranteed gulp of liquid.

Neither is free, because a settling burn spends some of the same methane and liquid oxygen, methalox for short, that you came to deliver.

Residuals are geometry. Pipes, valves, filters, and tank sumps hold a volume that does not shrink just because you wish it would.

Ordinary plumbing can leave on the order of one or two percent of a load behind, a larger share of a small transfer than of a large one.

Line volume is roughly pipe length times cross-section, so short, well-drained transfer lines beat long skinny ones that trap liquid in every bend.

Added velocity equals exhaust velocity times the natural log of mass ratio, the stack's mass at the start of a burn divided by its mass at the end.

Exhaust velocity is specific impulse times standard gravity, nine point eight one meters per second squared.

Specific impulse, in plain terms, is how many seconds an engine can hold a thrust equal to its own weight.

A typical methalox vacuum engine sits on the order of three hundred eighty seconds of specific impulse, about three thousand seven hundred meters per second of exhaust speed.

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky wrote that log relationship down in nineteen oh three.

A mass ratio near two point seven, the base of natural logs raised to one, buys only that one exhaust-velocity of added speed.

Fall short on the propellant that actually sits in the lander and you miss on the log, not on a straight line.

The repair is more mass launched from Earth, flown on extra tankers that each burn an ascent load of their own, a geometric series rather than a line of equal trucks.

A depot that stays nearly full beats a chain of leaky handoffs, because every extra coupling is another chance to fall off the mass ratio.

The magic wand number is methane and oxygen as industrial fluids, cheap molecules, against the mass that finally sits in the lander.

The idiot index, how many times the finished service costs over those raw molecules, stays high until wasted propellant stops ordering extra flights, because another ascent repeats engines, tanks, and range time.

Keep transfer waste small and the next tanker is mostly cheap fluid; let it run and you buy another whole flight to replace inventory that left as gas.

Later vehicles that loft more usable methalox per trip cut how often you pay that overhead, the same logic that made Falcon Nine reuse the point once first-stage recovery worked.
