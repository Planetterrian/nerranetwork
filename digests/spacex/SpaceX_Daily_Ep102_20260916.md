### Engineering Deep Dive
The decision to close Falcon 9 reservations reflects a mature reuse cycle in which each booster now flies multiple times before inspection or retirement. At the current cadence, the fleet's cumulative flight hours have consumed the available manifest slots that were once held open for new commercial buyers. This outcome follows directly from the physics of reuse: each flight consumes a fraction of the vehicle's certified life, and the rate of new booster production has not kept pace with demand growth. The aluminum-lithium tank structure and Merlin engine assemblies accumulate thermal and mechanical cycles that eventually require detailed inspection. Once those cycles reach the certified limit, the booster is removed from service even if the airframe remains structurally sound. 

The closed book forces a reallocation of existing capacity toward the highest-value contracts already on file, primarily Starlink and government missions. From a first-principles standpoint, the marginal cost of an additional Falcon 9 flight is now dominated by the opportunity cost of displacing a booked payload rather than by propellant or ground operations. That shift only occurs once the raw inputs—aluminum-lithium tanks, Merlin engines, and avionics—have been amortized across enough flights to make the vehicle scarce rather than abundant. The Idiot Index for a Falcon 9 booster therefore rises as reuse saturates the manifest, because the finished vehicle now carries an implicit scarcity premium that raw material costs alone cannot explain. 

The same constraint explains why Starship development remains the critical path for restoring launch elasticity. Until the next-generation vehicle can absorb the overflow demand, the Falcon fleet operates at its practical ceiling set by booster life limits and production rate, not by pad availability or regulatory approvals. Increasing the number of new boosters would lower the index by spreading flight hours across a larger pool, yet that approach collides with the time required to qualify additional airframes. Starship's stainless-steel architecture and Raptor engines are therefore positioned to reset the entire cost curve once orbital refueling and rapid reuse are demonstrated. In the interim, the reservation closure stands as a direct readout of how far reuse has already compressed the economics of the current fleet.
