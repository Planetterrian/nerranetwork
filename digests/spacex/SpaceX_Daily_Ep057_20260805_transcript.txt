From an engineering standpoint, the planned catch of the ship upper stage this month introduces a new set of first principles constraints compared with booster recovery.
The ship must survive reentry heating, then execute a precise flip and hover while the tower arms close around its forward attachment points.
Unlike the super heavy booster which returns with most of its propellant load and a relatively stable center of mass, the ship arrives nearly empty after an orbital mission shifting its mass distribution and aerodynamic behavior.
The tower must therefore accommodate a lighter, more slender vehicle whose control authority during the final seconds depends on cold gas thrusters and a handful of remaining Raptor engines.
If the catch succeeds, it collapses the recovery logistics that currently require either ocean splashdown or expendable loss of the upper stage.
The raw material cost of building a new ship is dominated by the stainless steel airframe, the tiled thermal protection system and the Raptor engines, each flight that returns the vehicle intact removes that entire bill from the next mission.
The idiot index for an expendable ship is therefore high, the finished article costs many times the sum of its input materials and energy because the vehicle is used only once.
A successful tower catch would drive that index sharply downward by amortizing the same hardware across multiple flights, the remaining engineering variable is thermal protection performance.
