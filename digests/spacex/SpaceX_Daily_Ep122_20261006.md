### Top News

1. **Dragon now holds all five fastest U.S. trips to the ISS**
   Crew Dragon Grace lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 and docked to the forward port of the Harmony module seven hours and fifty-five minutes later. That time beat the prior U.S. record by more than four hours. The uncrewed CRS-31 cargo flight had held the previous mark at a little over twelve hours. Crew-11 had been the fastest crewed trip at fourteen hours and forty-three minutes. Axiom’s Ax-2 mission sits fourth at fifteen hours and thirty-five minutes, and NASA’s Crew-4 is fifth at fifteen hours and forty-four minutes. All five flights used Dragon. SpaceX’s Julianna Scheiman said the station happened to be in an opportune spot in its orbit, allowing the approach to begin almost immediately after reaching orbit. The timing depends on launch date, so future flights will not reliably match the result. Source: [keeptrack.space](https://keeptrack.space/x-report/spacex-brief-2026-10-06)

2. **SpaceX seeks approval for a 32.4-mile natural gas pipeline in Florida**
   SpaceX subsidiary Coastal Connect Services asked the Florida Public Service Commission to approve a tariff for a new sixteen-inch transmission pipeline. The line would connect SpaceX facilities in Brevard County to the existing Florida Gas Transmission interstate network. Company filings state that truck deliveries and local pipelines can no longer meet growing fuel demand for Starship operations at Cape Canaveral. Natural gas would supply methane feedstock for Raptor engines that burn sub-cooled liquid methane and liquid oxygen. The project would replace tanker-truck deliveries with direct pipeline supply. SpaceX is already pursuing a similar eight-mile pipeline near Starbase in Texas. ### Engineering Deep Dive
From an engineering standpoint the record Dragon docking shows how orbital mechanics can be leveraged when the launch window aligns the vehicle with the station’s position. Dragon reached orbit and began closing the range almost immediately because the station was already near the right phasing angle. That geometry removed the need for multiple days of phasing burns that most missions still perform with the Draco thrusters. The approach trades schedule flexibility for speed: future flights will need the full series of burns unless the station happens to be in a similar spot. The same principle applies to any rendezvous where the chaser starts with a favorable relative position vector. Reducing the number of burns also lowers propellant consumption on the vehicle that must later return to Earth. Over many missions the cumulative savings in fuel and ground-support time become meaningful even if each individual flight cannot repeat the seven-hour-fifty-five-minute mark.
