The ex A I team is working with Doosan on a power plant meant to provide electricity just for their big A I training setups.

Reports indicate this is still in the early stages with no numbers on size or when it will start.

The idea is to secure steady power for their large computing facilities over time.

No details on where it would be built or when construction begins have come out yet.

The engineering reality is that cutting down on engine components brings direct benefits to weight, reliability, and how quickly units can be produced for the vehicle.

Removing brackets, seals, and extra flow paths trims potential leak sites and inspection steps during assembly.

Each saved kilogram on one engine scales across the full set of engines on a Starship vehicle, which improves the margin available for reaching orbit or lowers the propellant needed for a given velocity change.

Production moves faster because fewer castings and machined joints require checks before the engine reaches the test stand or the integration bay.

Qualification testing will show whether the simpler layout holds up without introducing new issues at the combustion chamber or turbopump.

A similar pruning of manifolds happened when the Merlin engine moved to its vacuum variant, raising reliability while shortening the build cycle and keeping specific impulse steady.

The Idiot Index tracks how many times the finished turbopump costs over the raw alloys, titanium, and machining hours that go into it.

Each removed bracket or seal narrows that gap by cutting both material scrap and labor hours.

The same cost-control idea shows up in the planned power plant, where dedicated generation avoids buying electricity at peak rates and reduces price swings for training runs.

Upfront capital buys steadier utilization across the computing clusters without risk of power shortfalls.

When the same logic moves to orbital data centers, every kilogram and watt saved on the ground matters even more because cooling in space carries its own mass penalties.

The Raptor changes therefore test the hardware discipline needed to support space-based computing on the timeline discussed for later this decade.
