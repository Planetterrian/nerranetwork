The power bottleneck those new cells are built to break is this episode's engineering
deep dive, because orbital compute is an electricity problem before it is a software problem.
Each satellite has to generate and store enough electricity to run inference or training accelerators
while surviving the thermal cycling and radiation of low-earth orbit.
And heterojunction stacks do that by layering materials with different band gaps, so the
cell captures a wider slice of the solar spectrum and throws off less waste heat that would
otherwise demand a heavier radiator.
Above the atmosphere, the solar constant is about 1,366 watts per square meter.
A typical terrestrial reference spectrum is on the order of 1,000 watts per square meter,
air, so space offers more raw light and no clouds than the orbit takes a cut back.
A low-earth orbit lasts roughly 90 minutes and the vehicle spends on the order of 30 minutes
of that in eclipse, which means the array must cover the daylight compute load and still
top up batteries before the next shadow.
Silicon's band gap sits near 1.1 electron volts, so a single junction silicon cell dumps
a large infrared tail as heat.
Ordinary silicon cells often sit near 20% conversion, as a back-of-envelope figure,
and a heterojunction stack that converts roughly 25% of the light still turns about three-quarters
of every incoming watt into heat that can leave a spacecraft only by radiation.
Raise efficiency a few points and you cut radiator area, cut structure, and raise specific power.
