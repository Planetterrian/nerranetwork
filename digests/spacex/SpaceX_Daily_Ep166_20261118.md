### Top News

1. **Dragon undocking from ISS delayed to November 19**
   Unfavorable weather at the California splashdown sites forced NASA and SpaceX to stand down from the planned Wednesday undocking of the CRS-35 Dragon. The next opportunity is set for 12:05 p.m. EST on Thursday, November 19. The spacecraft is returning more than 6,300 pounds of cargo, including 3D-bioprinted blood vessel constructs, frozen tumor organoids, and microbial samples. A lighting system tested for more than three years aboard the station will also return after evaluating its potential to support astronaut sleep and well-being on long-duration missions. The Dragon arrived at the station on October 15 after launching two days earlier on a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral. Mission teams will hold the next weather review at 12 p.m. EST on November 18 before confirming the Thursday window. Source: [nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/11/18/nasa-spacex-stand-down-from-dragons-undocking-on-wednesday/)

UK defence officials have not disclosed exact quantities or specific operational roles for the terminals. 4. **Recovered Starship upper stage could fly again after weeks of refurbishment**
   Elon Musk stated that Ship 42 could probably be reflown with a few weeks of refurbishment after he inspected the heat shield himself. The upper stage deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites in a single flight, capacity that would require roughly ten Falcon 9 launches with older satellites. SpaceX has targeted early 2027 for the first flight of a recovered Starship upper stage. ### Engineering Deep Dive
The Starship upper-stage recovery on Flight 15 demonstrated that a vehicle returning from orbit can be inspected on the ground and potentially prepared for another flight in weeks rather than months. The key variable is the heat-shield tile array: each tile must survive peak reentry heating while remaining attached under dynamic pressure and vibration. When the tiles stay intact, the refurbishment task reduces to targeted inspections, minor sealant work, and verification of the thermal-protection system rather than wholesale replacement. Ship 42 completed 14 orbits and spent 22.5 hours in space before the tower catch, giving engineers a full set of flight data on tile performance under real reentry conditions.

That outcome directly lowers the cost per flight because the dominant expense in any expendable upper stage is the one-time fabrication of the structure and propulsion system. With Ship 42 carrying 26 operational Starlink V3 satellites—equivalent to roughly ten Falcon 9 missions using older hardware—the marginal cost of each additional kilogram to orbit drops sharply once the same vehicle can be reused. The weeks-long turnaround also implies that ground-support equipment and procedures are already sized to handle rapid post-flight flows without new facilities. SpaceX has previously reflown two Super Heavy boosters after four months and seven months respectively, showing that booster reuse timelines are already measured in months rather than years.

If the same inspection and refurbishment cycle can be repeated on subsequent flights, the program moves closer to the point where upper-stage reuse becomes limited mainly by propellant loading time and payload integration rather than structural rebuilds. The next measurable step is whether the next recovered Ship can repeat the timeline without additional tile repairs or avionics swaps. Falcon vehicles cannot launch the newer V3 satellites, so achieving rapid Ship reuse is the only path to scaling that portion of the constellation at the required cadence. Early 2027 remains the internal target for the first operational flight of a recovered upper stage.
