### Engineering Deep Dive
The Cursor agent-swarm result rests on a simple economic split: one high-capability model generates the plan while multiple lower-cost models execute the steps. In raw terms the planner still consumes the expensive frontier tokens, yet the execution layer now runs on models whose per-token price is a small fraction of the planner’s. That division collapses the Idiot Index of the overall workflow because the bulk of the token volume no longer pays the frontier premium. 

The measured drop from $9,373 to $411 for the same SQLite task shows the gap is not theoretical. Most of the original cost came from routing every sub-task through the expensive model; once the planner hands off concrete instructions, cheaper models can finish the work without measurable quality loss in this benchmark. The remaining cost is dominated by the planner’s single pass plus the lightweight verification steps that still use higher-grade inference. 

What changes next is utilization. If the same pattern holds across larger codebases, inference clusters can keep a small number of frontier GPUs busy on planning while the majority of the fleet runs cheaper models at higher throughput. That rebalancing directly improves the economics of serving developer traffic at scale and reduces the power and interconnect demand that would otherwise grow linearly with every added user. The architecture also creates a clearer separation between the scarce resource (frontier planning capacity) and the abundant one (execution tokens), allowing operators to size clusters around actual token economics rather than uniform model pricing.
