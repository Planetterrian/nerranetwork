For the tissue payloads now on station, the engineering reality is that gravity itself is a process contaminant.

On Earth, everything in a flask feels nine point eight meters per second squared, and denser clumps sediment while warmer, lighter fluid rises.

Those two motions, sedimentation and buoyancy convection, never stop, and they smear the layers biologists are trying to build.

Take a cell about ten micrometers across, with a mass on the order of one nanogram.

A back-of-envelope Stokes estimate puts its settling speed on the ground on the order of one micrometer per second, so a one-millimeter stack can crush or scramble in roughly fifteen minutes.

On station, residual acceleration is on the order of one millionth of g, so that same settling time stretches by a factor of about a million, weeks instead of minutes, which is why photoreceptor precursors can sit in radial layers instead of a packed sludge.

Nutrient transport flips with it.

Protein-sized molecules diffuse through water at roughly ten to the minus six square centimeters per second, so crossing a millimeter of still media takes on the order of three hours.

On the ground, buoyancy-driven eddies mix that volume in seconds, but the mixing is patchy.

In orbit the convective term is gone, so the only even supply is a pump, which is why these runs need perfusion loops, forced media flow, sized to replace the missing convection rather than fight it.

Heart muscle cells, cardiomyocytes, gain for a different mechanical reason.

In one-g they sit under their own compressive load, which changes how gap junctions form and how electrical coupling spreads; unload that stress and alignment can run across larger volumes.

Glass melts see the same physics from the materials side.

Density-driven currents that normally sweep early crystals around are absent, so nucleation rates can be measured as a function of chemistry rather than stirring.

Thermal control has to hold incubator setpoints near thirty-seven degrees Celsius inside Dragon and the station's EXPRESS racks, because a few degrees of drift kills viability over weeks.

Power for those racks and pumps is the other constraint, and extra margin from the arriving arrays is what keeps sensitive payloads from competing with station housekeeping.

The magic-wand inputs are cells, sterile media, and a few tens of watts.

Data from this flight is meant to put a number on how much of that waste was just gravity.
