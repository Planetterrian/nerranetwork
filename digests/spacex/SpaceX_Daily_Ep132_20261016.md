### Engineering Deep Dive
Carbon-fibre composite tanks present a classic trade-off between specific strength and manufacturing repeatability. A kilogram of high-grade fibre delivers higher tensile strength than the same mass of aluminium or steel, yet the finished part’s performance hinges on fibre volume fraction—the ratio of actual fibre to resin matrix. Too high a fraction and the part becomes difficult to consolidate without voids; too low and excess resin adds weight while reducing stiffness. Automated fibre placement machines lay individual tows at precise angles to handle the multi-axis loads a rocket tank experiences during ascent, pressurization, and reuse cycles. The process must also manage fibre-grade selection, because higher-modulus grades that improve buckling resistance are both more expensive and more brittle during handling. Rocket Lab’s recent Neutron delay illustrates how these variables interact at scale: even after Electron proved the material in a smaller vehicle, scaling the same approach to a medium-lift first stage exposed new sensitivities in tank geometry and cure cycles. Closing that gap requires tighter control of layup tension, resin flow, and post-cure inspection—each step that directly determines whether the mass savings translate into usable payload or are consumed by added safety margins. Fibre orientation changes visible during placement reveal the directional strength properties that must be balanced against shear and pressure loads unique to reusable first stages. Manual hand-layup remains an option for complex geometries but introduces variability that automated systems are designed to reduce. The material science complexity extends to resin chemistry, where cure temperature and pressure profiles affect final interlaminar shear strength. Neutron’s propellant tank issues show that even proven small-vehicle designs encounter new constraints when tank diameter and length increase, forcing iterative adjustments to placement paths and inspection criteria.
