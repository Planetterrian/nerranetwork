For a Super Heavy tank set, the engineering reality of cryogenic proof is a controlled chill toward liquid-oxygen temperature while crews watch for leaks and deformation.

Stainless 304L shrinks about zero point three percent linearly from room temperature near two hundred ninety-three kelvin down to ninety kelvin, a drop of roughly two hundred kelvin.

Austenitic stainless moves at about seventeen millionths of strain per kelvin, and that coefficient times the drop is the contraction.

Liquid methane in service sits near one hundred eleven kelvin, so the oxygen-side chill is the harsher thermal step on the steel.

A cylinder that shortens and pinches inward loads hoop stress — tension around the belt, pressure times diameter over twice the wall thickness — and longitudinal stress along the length, and every weld and flange must stay below yield.

The proof pressurizes the tank while it is cold, so a micro-crack that only opens at flight temperature shows up on the stand.

Pass that gate, and the vehicle can take an ambient proof on the second tank, then a combined tanking that loads both propellants at once against the common bulkhead, the shared wall between oxygen and methane, and the feed-line interfaces.

Each clean cycle lowers the odds of a thermal leak on the first hot-fire, because the friction-stir welds, which join metal without melting it, and the flange seals have already seen flight-like cold.

Strain gauges from the test also retune the finite-element models, the computer stress maps, that predict fatigue across the targeted ten-plus flights per booster.

The magic-wand inputs are stainless plate, weld wire, and forming energy.

The Idiot Index is how many times the finished tank costs over those inputs, and a back-of-envelope figure for large welded aerospace tanks often lands on the order of ten to twenty times, mostly because a pad leak can take out the vehicle and the stand.

Proof testing collapses that ratio by catching defects before the pad, and because the same tanks are meant for ten-plus flights, each extra cycle spreads that inspection cost across the reuse program.

LNG land storage sees the same 304L contraction problem, but those tanks are not asked to fly, land, and chill again.

When the booster later returns, the same cold sequence can run at lower pressure to confirm fatigue has not opened new leak paths.

Any change in weld schedule or flange preload has to be re-checked at ninety kelvin, which is how the per-flight cost of the booster stays low as the flight count climbs.
