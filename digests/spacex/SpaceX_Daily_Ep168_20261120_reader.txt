Power and heat, from an engineering standpoint, become the payload constraint for those Project Suncatcher satellites the moment they leave the pad.

A typical ninety-minute low Earth orbit spends on the order of thirty-five minutes in Earth's shadow, so roughly forty percent of each pass needs stored energy — thirty-five divided by ninety is about thirty-nine percent — and that storage is batteries, mass you are no longer spending on processors.

A path that stays sunlit for most of a revolution lets the arrays keep feeding the chips, so battery mass can shrink.

Each satellite is meant to carry roughly one data-center rack of TPU silicon, so the mass budget has to hold both the processors and the radiators that reject heat under machine-learning load.

In vacuum every watt leaves as infrared; radiated power scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature, so doubling temperature in kelvin radiates sixteen times the power from the same area — two to the fourth is sixteen — but the chips still have to live inside their thermal limits, which forces area at a survivable temperature.

If those radiators match the TPU load at the expected orbit beta angle, the angle between the orbit plane and the sun, the design skips land, permits, and grid interconnection.

On the ground a finished cluster costs many times the raw silicon — a back-of-envelope Idiot Index of several times chip and metal — while in orbit the magic-wand inputs are processors, cell area, and radiator metal, and the multiplier is structure, pointing, and heat-rejection mass.

That mass trade decides whether several satellites stack inside New Glenn's payload envelope or each needs its own flight.

The open work is radiator coatings and thermal-control algorithms that keep the heat path stable without driving structure past the point where solar power stays net positive.
