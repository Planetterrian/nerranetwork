The heat shield, from an engineering standpoint, comes into play after a deorbit burn, a retrograde firing that drops the low point of the orbit into the atmosphere, and the capsule then faces that shield forward so one surface absorbs the kinetic energy of orbital velocity.

Peak heating hits at roughly eight kilometers per second relative speed, and at eight kilometers per second each kilogram of vehicle carries a back-of-envelope thirty-two million joules of kinetic energy — half times eight thousand meters per second squared.

Eight kilometers per second over a speed of sound near three hundred forty meters per second is on the order of Mach twenty-four, so the air ahead cannot move aside and compresses into a radiating plasma.

The PICA-X tiles, a carbon phenolic that chars and throws heat out as radiation, radiate away the majority of that energy while the structure underneath stays below critical temperatures.

Because those same tiles already flew on prior missions, the margins have to cover initial ablation — the sacrificial char wearing away — and any micro-cracking from repeated heat cycles.

After peak heating the vehicle deploys parachutes at subsonic speeds, shifting from plasma to timing: a drogue, the small first chute, then the mains, so splashdown loads stay inside the certified envelope.

What is inside stays protected because the pressure vessel, the sealed cabin, and the avionics bays, the flight computers and electronics, sit behind multiple layers of insulation, plus active cooling loops that keep running until just before the chutes deploy.

Over successive flights the one piece swapped at scale is the heat-shield coating, while the main structure, avionics, and propulsion keep adding flights, so the extra cost of each cargo return is inspection, refurbishment labor, and a new set of parachutes rather than a whole new capsule.

Wave a magic wand and you pay for coating, chutes, and technician time, not a new pressure vessel, so the Idiot Index — how many times the finished return costs over those raw inputs — shrinks toward labor, and those same margins let Dragon turn around in weeks once cargo is offloaded and the vehicle is inspected at the recovery site.
