### Engineering Deep Dive
Falcon 9 first-stage reuse at 36 flights rests on a simple energy accounting. Each ascent burns roughly 400 tonnes of propellant to reach staging velocity; the return flight then dissipates the same kinetic and potential energy through a boost-back burn, entry burn, and landing burn. The stage structure must survive peak dynamic pressure twice per mission plus the thermal pulse of reentry at Mach 8. Because the vehicle is sized for a single use plus margin, the extra propellant load for recovery is only about 30 percent of the ascent total. That margin is what allows the same hardware to fly again without a full redesign. Over 36 cycles the cumulative propellant cost per flight therefore falls by a factor of roughly 30 compared with building a new stage each time. The remaining cost is dominated by inspection, refurbishment, and the fixed overhead of the launch site itself. If those ground operations can be driven below 10 percent of the original build cost, the per-flight economics approach the raw propellant and avionics floor. Continued high flight counts on B1063 will test whether that ground-process target is realistic or whether cumulative fatigue eventually forces earlier retirement.

The same energy balance explains why orbital-class first stages remain harder to reuse than suborbital hoppers. A student-scale VTVL vehicle like Colibri only needs to manage low-speed vertical landing at roughly 1.2 kN thrust and 100 kg mass. It never encounters the hypersonic heating, multi-engine throttling after 100-plus-kilometer trajectories, or the requirement to perform a boost-back burn while still carrying residual upper-stage mass. Those additional loads scale with the square of velocity and the cube of linear dimensions, which is why even successful hoppers have not yet translated directly into operational orbital reuse. The Falcon 9 design absorbs those loads by carrying extra propellant margin and by flying the same structural tank set through repeated thermal and pressure cycles. Each additional flight therefore validates that the original factor-of-safety assumptions remain valid after repeated exposure to the full flight envelope.

Ground operations now set the next limit. Propellant itself is cheap; the metal, turbopumps, and avionics represent the real recurring cost. When inspection and refurbishment drop below the cost of building a new stage, the per-flight price approaches the raw-material floor. B1063’s 36th flight supplies another data point on whether that floor is reachable before fatigue or regulatory inspection requirements intervene. The same logic applies to future vehicles: if Starship’s Super Heavy booster can achieve comparable flight counts, the economics of rapid reuse become the dominant advantage over expendable architectures.
