### Engineering Deep Dive
The decision to place AI inference hardware in orbit begins with a simple power-and-thermal constraint: terrestrial data centers must reject heat into air or water that already carries the load of the electric grid. In orbit the same silicon can radiate directly to space at roughly 200–300 K, cutting the energy cost of cooling to near zero once the hardware is on station. Starship’s 100-plus-tonne payload bay and planned orbital refueling therefore collapse the “idiot index” of lifting the entire stack—servers, radiators, and power-conditioning—by replacing dozens of smaller launches with a handful of fully reusable vehicles whose propellant is the dominant consumable rather than the structure itself. The remaining variable is the refueling count per mission; each additional transfer reduces the effective cost per delivered kilowatt of compute by amortizing the booster and tanker flights across more hardware mass. Once that loop closes, the marginal cost of adding another orbital node approaches the cost of the silicon and solar arrays alone, exactly the first-principles trade that makes the architecture worth proving on the next Starship test flights.
