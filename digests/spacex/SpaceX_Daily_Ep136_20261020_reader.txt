Reuse physics is the engineering angle today, because a first stage only flies again if the energy budget closes.

Each ascent burns roughly four hundred tonnes of propellant to reach staging velocity.

The rocket equation says the velocity gained depends on how much of the vehicle is spent as exhaust.

Most of that ascent load exists to accelerate the rest of the ascent load, plus the upper stage.

The return dumps the gained energy in three burns: boost-back to reverse, entry to survive Mach 8, and landing over the droneship.

A back-of-envelope check on that Mach number: sea-level speed of sound is about three hundred forty meters per second, so it is on the order of two point seven kilometers per second.

Textbook orbital speed is about seven point eight kilometers per second, which is why the first stage is not the orbital vehicle — it never has to kill full orbital energy on the way home.

It still has to kill a hypersonic chunk of it.

Kinetic energy scales with the square of speed, so even a fraction of orbital velocity is not a gentle stop.

The entry burn exists to cut that speed before the airframe has to eat it as heat.

The structure sees peak dynamic pressure twice per mission, plus the thermal pulse of that hypersonic return.

Dynamic pressure is the punch of air at speed, density times velocity squared over two.

The stage gets one punch going up through the thick atmosphere, and another coming back in.

Thermal protection and the tank walls have to like both.

Because the vehicle was sized for a single use plus margin, extra propellant for recovery is only about thirty percent of the ascent total.

Recovery burns ride on leftover propellant after staging, so the vehicle is not lifting a second full stack.

The stage spends that margin while still carrying fluid, then reenters lighter.

That is why the recovery allocation can stay a minority of the ascent load instead of doubling the vehicle.

Over many cycles, cumulative propellant cost per flight falls by a factor of roughly thirty compared with building a new stage each time.

The magic wand number is the raw inputs: kerosene, liquid oxygen, and tank metal.

The Idiot Index, how many times the finished booster costs over those raw inputs, stays high because turbopumps and avionics dwarf the commodity bill.

A turbopump is not priced like aluminum.

Avionics and turbopumps do not get cheaper just because the stage flies again; they get amortized.

The floor is the sum of propellant, a small slice of those components’ life, and however many technician hours the pad still demands.

If inspection, refurbishment, and launch-site overhead drop below ten percent of original build cost, per-flight economics approach that raw-material and avionics floor.

If the hours come down, the Idiot Index of a flown stage collapses toward the magic wand number.

Leave the hours high and it is an expendable rocket that happens to land.

Ground operations now set the next limit, not the physics of the burns.

Orbital-class first stages stay harder to reuse than suborbital hoppers for the same energy reason.

A student-scale hopper like Colibri only needs a low-speed vertical landing at roughly one point two kilonewtons of thrust and one hundred kilograms of mass.

It never sees hypersonic heating, multi-engine throttling after a hundred-plus-kilometer trajectory, or a boost-back while still carrying residual upper-stage mass.

Those loads scale with the square of velocity and the cube of linear dimensions, so a clean hopper landing does not become operational orbital reuse.

A back-of-envelope figure on the cube law: stretch a vehicle three times longer in every dimension and the mass to keep intact is on the order of twenty-seven times, not three.

Velocity squared on the heat and aero side stacks on top of that, which is the gap between a hopper and an orbital booster.

A hopper at that mass never pays the heat tax, so its Idiot Index is mostly structure and avionics, not thermal protection and engine-out throttle profiles.

None of that is a reason reuse fails.

It is why the recovery propellant margin and a conservative tank set had to be in the original design, not bolted on later.

Falcon 9 absorbs the orbital loads by carrying extra propellant margin and flying the same structural tank set through repeated thermal and pressure cycles.

Each extra flight checks that the original factor-of-safety assumptions still hold after another full envelope exposure.

Super Heavy, Starship’s first stage, lives under that same accounting, and comparable flight counts would make rapid reuse the dominant advantage over expendable architectures.

The remaining question is process: whether inspection hours and fatigue allow that floor to be reached, or whether teardown rules get there first.
