Pull the engineering angle apart and drag is just density times speed squared times area, stealing kinetic energy the thrusters have to put back.

Orbital speed in this band is on the order of seven point six kilometers per second, so even ten-to-the-minus-eleven kilograms per cubic meter of residual air packs a punch because force scales with velocity squared.

Earth's mean radius is about six thousand three hundred seventy-one kilometers, which is why a semi-major axis near sixty-eight hundred kilometers still flies through the thermosphere rather than through vacuum.

Density there is not a constant; solar extreme ultraviolet and geomagnetic storms can swing it by a factor of ten or more, which is why a quiet week and a storm week do not cost the same propellant.

The thermospheric scale height is on the order of sixty kilometers, so a small drop in altitude raises drag sharply, and that is the ratchet an unpowered object cannot reverse.

Stations flying near four hundred kilometers replace lost energy with chemical burns measured in kilonewtons for seconds; a small satellite does the same job with millinewton-class electric thrust stretched over months.

A typical Hall-effect thruster, a solar-powered engine that accelerates ions from a noble-gas feed, delivers on the order of one thousand five hundred to two thousand seconds of specific impulse, which is how many seconds of thrust you get per unit of propellant, against nine point eight one meters per second squared of standard gravity.

Chemical monopropellant sits nearer three hundred seconds, so electric propulsion is roughly six times stingier with mass for the same delta-v, the change in velocity the orbit actually needs.

Back-of-envelope, that class of engine can buy more than a kilometer per second of total delta-v from a small consumable load, which is why short burns can cancel drag instead of fighting it continuously.

The thrusters therefore run at a very low duty cycle, the fraction of time they are actually firing, lighting only when a density spike walks the orbit out of tolerance.

Those same arrays have to make extra kilowatts for inter-satellite laser links, which cut ground-station dependence, and the solar constant at Earth is roughly one thousand three hundred sixty watts per square meter before cell efficiency, pointing cosine, and eclipse.

That is the trade: more area means more drag and more power, and the loop only closes if station-keeping stays a thin slice of dry mass so structure, radios, and lasers still fit.
