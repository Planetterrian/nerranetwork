The engineering deep dive into Falcon 9 reuse limits starts from the physics of cumulative cycles on aluminum-lithium tanks and Merlin engines, where each flight consumes a fraction of certified life at the current cadence of roughly fifteen flights per booster before inspection.

With new booster production lagging demand by about twelve units per year, the fleet's total flight hours have already filled every remaining slot through existing contracts rather than leaving margin for fresh commercial work.

From first principles the marginal cost of one more Falcon 9 flight now sits almost entirely in opportunity cost of displacing a booked payload, not in propellant or pad time, because the raw inputs of tanks, engines and avionics have been amortized across enough missions to make each airframe scarce.

That scarcity raises the Idiot Index for the finished booster well above its material cost of roughly thirty million dollars, since the vehicle now carries an implicit premium from manifest saturation that new production alone cannot erase without extending qualification timelines.

Starship's stainless architecture and Raptor engines reset the curve only after orbital refueling and rapid reuse are proven, because until then the Falcon ceiling remains fixed by booster life limits and production rate rather than pads or approvals.

Increasing new boosters would spread flight hours across a larger pool and lower the index, yet that path collides with the months required to qualify additional airframes under current inspection standards.
