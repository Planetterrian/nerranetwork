to the terminal SpaceX operates under contract. Reuse physics is the engineering angle today,
because a first stage only flies again if the energy budget closes. Each ascent burns roughly
400 tons of propellant to reach staging velocity. The rocket equation says the velocity gain depends
on how much of the vehicle is spent as exhaust. Most of that ascent load exists to accelerate
the rest of the ascent load, plus the upper stage. The return dumps the gained energy in three
burns, boost back to reverse, entry to survive Mach 8 and landing over the drone ship.
A back of envelope check on that Mach number. Sea level speed of sound is about 340 meters per second,
so it is on the order of 2.7 kilometers per second. Textbook orbital speed is about 7.8 kilometers per
second, which is why the first stage is not the orbital vehicle. It never has to kill
full orbital energy on the way home. It still has to kill a hypersonic chunk of it.
Kinetic energy scales with the square of speed, so even a fraction of orbital velocity
is not a gentle stop. The entry burn exists to cut that speed before the airframe has to
eat it as heat. The structure sees peak dynamic pressure twice per mission, plus the thermal
pulse of that hypersonic return. Dynamic pressure is the punch of air at speed, density times
velocity squared over 2. The stage gets one punch going up through the thick atmosphere,
and another coming back in. Thermal protection and the tank walls have to like both.
Because the vehicle was sized for a single use plus margin, extra propellant for recovery is
only about 30% of the ascent total. Recovery burns right on left over propellant after staging,
moving, so the vehicle is not lifting a second full stack.
The stage spends that margin while still carrying fluid, then re-enters lighter.
That is why the recovery allocation can stay a minority of the ascent load instead of
doubling the vehicle.
Over many cycles, cumulative propellant cost per flight falls by a factor of roughly 30 compared
with building a new stage each time.
The magic wand number is the raw inputs, kerosene, liquid oxygen, and tank metal.
The idiot index, how many times the finished booster costs over those raw inputs, stays
high because turbo pumps and avionics dwarf the commodity bill.
A turbo pump is not priced like aluminum.
Avionics and turbo pumps do not get cheaper just because the stage flies again.
They get amortized.
The floor is the sum of propellant, a small slice of those components life, and however many
technician hours the pad still demands.
