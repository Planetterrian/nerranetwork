### Engineering Deep Dive
Catching the Super Heavy booster with tower arms instead of landing legs removes the mass penalty of a set of legs sized for a vehicle that returns with residual propellant and structural loads after a full ascent. The design trades the need for heavy, single-use hardware for a precision hover that uses grid fins for steering during the three-minute engine-off coast and then three Raptors to hold position beside the tower. Because the booster is already at the launch mount when the arms close, turnaround time collapses from a multi-hour transport and reinstallation cycle to a direct lower-and-refuel sequence that targets under one hour. The same arms also eliminate the requirement to design and qualify separate landing pads or legs that would otherwise add dry mass and subtract payload on every flight. Early flight data showed the catch points below the fins can accept the loads once the vehicle is hovering, confirming the structural path chosen during booster development. The approach therefore converts a recovery problem into a manufacturing and operations advantage by collapsing both hardware count and ground-cycle duration.

The grid fins provide the primary steering authority during the coast phase after the booster separates and flips, allowing the vehicle to steer toward the tower without expending propellant on attitude control. Once the booster has fallen for roughly three minutes, thirteen of the thirty-three Raptors relight to begin the braking burn, with three engines kept running to maintain the hover beside the tower. This sequence removes the need for legs that would have to survive both the ascent loads and the return loads while adding hundreds of kilograms of dry mass that directly reduces payload capacity. The tower arms close under the catch points once the vehicle is stable, then lower the booster onto the launch mount for immediate inspection and refueling. The entire sequence is designed to enable a booster to be ready for another flight in under an hour, a target that would be impossible if the vehicle first had to be transported from a separate landing site. Structural testing on the flight article confirmed that the catch points and surrounding structure can handle the expected loads without requiring additional reinforcement that would increase mass.

By eliminating legs entirely, SpaceX avoids both the recurring cost of manufacturing and installing them on every booster and the one-time engineering effort of qualifying a new landing system for a vehicle that already carries grid fins for steering. The same arms that catch the booster can also be used to stack the Ship upper stage, further reducing ground infrastructure needs. This integrated recovery and stacking approach directly supports the goal of rapid reuse by keeping the vehicle at the launch site rather than requiring a separate recovery pad or transport system. The physics of the hover relies on the precise throttling of three Raptors to balance the vehicle’s mass against thrust while the grid fins provide fine attitude control, a capability demonstrated across multiple flight tests. The net result is a system that trades added control complexity during the final seconds of flight for a substantial reduction in both hardware mass and operational cycle time.
