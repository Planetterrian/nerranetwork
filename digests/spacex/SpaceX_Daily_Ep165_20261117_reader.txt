Starship's tanks and structure, from an engineering standpoint, are three-hundred-series stainless because carbon-fiber prototypes cost too much and would not scale.

The alloy is about three dollars per kilogram, while carbon fiber is nearly two hundred dollars per kilogram once scrap is included, so two hundred divided by three puts the composite on the order of sixty-seven times the steel, the Idiot Index on raw material, how many times the finished part costs over what you started with.

At liquid-oxygen and liquid-methane temperatures that alloy's yield strength, the stress where the metal starts to permanently bend, rises, so wall thickness can drop and still meet burst pressure, the load that would rupture the tank.

Less mass in those walls is more payload or more propellant for the same vehicle, the mass fraction that lunar and Mars missions have to win.

Stainless also tolerates brief temperature spikes well above the one hundred fifty degree Celsius steady-state limit of aluminum or composites, the kind of heat that would already trouble an aluminum airframe in steady flight, which simplifies thermal protection during reentry.

Carbon fiber demanded complex layup tooling and autoclave curing, a pressurized oven cycle; stainless uses conventional rolling, cutting, and orbital welding, a weld run around the tank circumference, already common in pressure-vessel shops.

When a tank section fails a proof test, a pressure check before flight, crews can cut out the damaged area and weld in a replacement patch the same day, which shortens the gap from a test anomaly to the next flight article by weeks.

Cheap sheet stock lowers the penalty for building several prototypes at once, so different structural variants can proceed in parallel without exhausting the materials budget, and those weld-and-repair loops in days rather than weeks are what a recovered stage needs if it is going to be inspected and flown again with little downtime.
