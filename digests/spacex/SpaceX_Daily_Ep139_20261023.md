### Engineering Deep Dive
Starship’s propellant load offers a direct comparison to Falcon 9 economics that reveals the core scaling advantage. A full Starship stack carries roughly 4600–5100 tonnes of propellant while a single Falcon 9 uses 490–515 tonnes, so ten Falcon 9 flights consume a similar total mass. The key difference lies in payload delivery: one half-loaded Starship Flight 14 already matched the throughput of about ten Falcon 9 V2 Mini missions when carrying 26 V3 Starlink satellites at roughly one terabit per second each. Full Starship capacity scales that advantage further because the vehicle reuses both stages and can deliver up to twenty times the orbital mass of a Falcon 9 for comparable propellant expenditure. Rapid reuse therefore collapses the per-kilogram cost by amortizing the same propellant and structural mass across many more flights.

The engineering implication is that Starship’s first-principles advantage appears only after the vehicle achieves high flight rates; until then the propellant numbers alone do not yet translate into lower delivered cost per satellite. Propellant production itself follows a simple energy balance: liquid oxygen and methane are synthesized from electricity and feedstock, so the marginal cost per tonne is dominated by power price and plant utilization rather than raw material scarcity. Ten Falcon 9 flights spread that energy cost across ten separate vehicles and ten separate ground operations, while a single Starship flight concentrates the same energy into one stack and one set of ground systems. Once the booster and ship both return and fly again within days, the fixed infrastructure cost per flight drops sharply.

This is the classic Idiot Index problem in launch economics: the finished delivered satellite costs many times the raw propellant and metal inputs because most expense sits in the non-reusable portions of the system. Starship’s design attacks that index by making both stages fully reusable and by flying the entire stack often enough that the per-flight share of tower, pad, and factory overhead becomes negligible. The data from Flight 14 already shows the payload side of the equation moving in the right direction; the remaining variable is flight cadence. When the vehicle reaches the point where a single stack flies as many times in a year as ten Falcon 9 boosters combined, the propellant math finally converts into measurable cost reduction for every kilogram placed in orbit.
