### Engineering Deep Dive
A student team just flew a 105-metre reusable rocket hop on a 1.2 kN bipropellant engine built for less than 250,000 Swiss francs. That hop demonstrates basic throttle control, guidance, and soft landing at roughly 100 kg scale. An orbital-class first stage must also survive hypersonic reentry heating, execute high-energy boostback and entry burns after traveling above 100 km, and manage multi-engine control across orbital staging margins. Energy and thermal loads therefore scale by orders of magnitude beyond the student demonstrator. The gap explains why most teams remain stuck at pure hops rather than operational reuse; the prediction is falsified only if a comparable low-budget effort achieves full orbital recovery or if reentry physics prove unexpectedly trivial.

The Swiss Colibri demonstrator used an in-house F-100 engine producing 1.2 kN of thrust and completed its entire flight in 60 seconds while translating 30 metres laterally before touchdown. At that scale the dominant challenges are control authority and propellant management during the short ascent and descent. An orbital booster like Falcon 9’s first stage or Starship’s Super Heavy must instead dissipate kinetic energy from velocities exceeding Mach 5, manage heat fluxes that can exceed 1 MW per square metre, and execute precise burns with engines that have already delivered thousands of kilonewtons for minutes. The raw-material cost of the student vehicle stayed under 250,000 Swiss francs because the structure, avionics, and engine were all built at laboratory scale with minimal qualification testing. Scaling those same functions to a vehicle that returns from orbit multiplies both the material and certification costs by factors that current reuse programs are still working to compress.

The Idiot Index for orbital reuse therefore sits far higher than for a 105-metre hop. The finished first-stage booster costs many times its raw propellant, metal, and electronics inputs because of the thermal-protection system, the structural margins required for reentry, and the repeated qualification of every engine and valve after each flight. Reusing that hardware twenty or thirty times collapses the per-flight cost only after the initial development and qualification investment has been amortized. The Swiss flight shows that the core closed-loop control problem is solvable at small scale with modest resources. The remaining engineering distance lies in surviving the energy environment of orbital return and in manufacturing the vehicle at a cadence that makes the reuse economics close.
