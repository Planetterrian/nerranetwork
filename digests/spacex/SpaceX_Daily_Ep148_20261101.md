The sequenced launches involve three different vehicles and providers. Blue Moon is expected to have the ability to loiter in orbit for roughly 30 days. That flight would be followed by the SLS/Orion crew launch and a Starship launch. Isaacman stated the three-launch, three-provider profile is unprecedented for a NASA crew mission. The approach reflects the multi-launch campaigns expected for future large-scale space infrastructure. He said the mission will exercise the three major flight elements together and gather operational data ahead of a later crewed lunar landing attempt. Under the current plan the profile relies on three separate launches from three different providers. Blue Moon is expected to loiter long enough for checkouts before the crewed vehicle arrives. The plan departs from the Apollo model and from earlier Artemis concepts that assumed a more tightly coupled architecture. ## The Counterpoint
A federal judge ruled that xAI’s gas turbines powering Colossus 2 cannot be treated as mobile sources under Mississippi’s Clean Air Act implementation plan, allowing the NAACP lawsuit to proceed on permit and standing grounds. The decision rejected both the company’s motion and a parallel Justice Department filing. Resolution now depends on whether the turbines receive the required preconstruction and operating permits or whether the case advances to findings on actual emissions. The facility is located in Southaven, Mississippi, while Colossus 2 is located in Memphis, Tennessee. According to the complaint, MZX Tech LLC is a subsidiary of xAI and owns and operates the facility that supplies electricity to the data center. The central issue was whether gas turbines could be considered mobile sources of pollution and therefore exempt from permitting requirements. xAI argued that turbines fell within the exemption, while the NAACP challenged that interpretation in its lawsuit. The judge ruled that the turbines do not fall into the category of mobile sources under the federal regulations incorporated into Mississippi’s implementation plan. Under this interpretation, the facility is not exempt from the relevant permitting requirements of the Clean Air Act. The NAACP alleges that xAI and MZX Tech installed and operate gas turbines without the required permits to build and operate them. The lawsuit also alleges that residents in the area have changed their daily habits because they are concerned about potential exposure to pollutants. These are allegations made by the plaintiffs, not findings confirmed by the court. The decision also examined whether the NAACP has standing to sue under the Clean Air Act. The judge accepted that organizations can sue on their own behalf when seeking to protect the interests of their members. The judgment concerns the ability to sue, not the ultimate liability of the companies. xAI also argued that citizen lawsuits to enforce environmental laws violate the Constitution and limit executive power. The judge rejected the argument, noting that the plaintiffs are suing in their own name and seeking redress for harms they claim they and their members have suffered. The court also considered a letter from the Mississippi Environmental Protection Agency, which had taken a different view on whether the units could be considered mobile. ### Engineering Deep Dive
Reusable first stages cut the marginal cost of each launch by recovering the largest single hardware element. Falcon 9 boosters have now flown as many as 37 missions because the vehicle was designed from the start with landing legs, grid fins, and throttleable engines that survive re-entry heating and landing loads. The same principle scales to Starship: the Super Heavy booster returns the 33 Raptors and the massive propellant tanks that would otherwise be expended on every flight. In-space 3D printing further reduces the logistics penalty by allowing replacement parts to be manufactured on orbit instead of launched from Earth. NASA’s first demonstration on the ISS in 2014 produced a functional printer component, proving that additive manufacturing works in microgravity without gravity-fed feed systems. When these two capabilities combine—rapid booster reuse plus on-demand spares—the effective cost per kilogram delivered to orbit drops because the same capital hardware flies repeatedly and the supply chain for consumables shrinks. The remaining constraint is how quickly the fleet can be inspected, refurbished, and turned around between flights. The manufacture of first-stage booster constitutes about 60 percent of the launch price of a single expended Falcon 9. After multiple attempts, some as early as 2010, at controlling the re-entry of the first stage after its separation from the second stage, the first successful controlled landing of a first stage occurred on December 22, 2015. Since then, Falcon 9 first-stage boosters have been landed and recovered 673 times out of 686 attempts. In total 56 recovered boosters have been refurbished and subsequently flown at least a second time, with a record of 37 launches and landings carried out by a single booster. SuperDraco is a hypergolic propellant rocket engine designed and built by SpaceX. It is part of the SpaceX Draco family of rocket engines. A redundant array of eight SuperDraco engines provides fault-tolerant propulsion for use as a launch escape system for the SpaceX Dragon 2. SuperDraco rocket engines utilize a storable hypergolic propellant which allows the engines to be fired many months after fueling and launch.
