They lifted off October first from Vandenberg carrying Cowboy Space's Reason-1, Google's Project Suncatcher, and Star Catcher's Protostar, Suara Garut reports.

Reason-1 is set to beam thirty to one hundred watts by laser to a station on the ground. That is about what a household bulb draws, and it is a first hop, not a power plant.

The follow-on, Reason-2, is planned for two thousand twenty-seven and would carry H200 graphics processors.

Project Suncatcher flies four Trillium tensor processing units, or TPUs. They draw roughly one kilowatt of solar power, about a portable heater, to run short Gemini queries.

Star Catcher's Protostar will demonstrate wireless power transfer between spacecraft. It deploys a cubesat, a small cube-shaped satellite, and sends measurable energy to solar panels to validate commercial in-space power delivery.

Cowboy Space began as Aetherflux before shifting toward orbital data centers and rockets. Joseph Yaffe, the company's chief operating officer and chief legal officer, described the broader goal as combining energy delivery, computing, and laser-based connections.

Cowboy Space's longer-term target is a one megawatt upper-stage data center slated for December two thousand twenty-eight. Starcloud and Aethero are among Star Catcher's publicized customers.

Worth keeping an eye on is that Google's testing found the TPUs survived a radiation dose equivalent to five years in orbit. Occasional silent errors occurred at about one per three million queries.

Current systems require shutdown periods every fifteen to twenty minutes for heat management. Resolving heat rejection and error mitigation at scale remains an open engineering step before larger clusters can operate continuously.

The physics behind today's engineering deep dive is simple and unforgiving: a satellite cluster must generate, condition, and route kilowatts while rejecting every watt of waste heat in vacuum, where only radiation works.

Convection needs air, and there is none, so the Stefan-Boltzmann law is the whole cooling system. Radiated power equals emissivity times area times temperature to the fourth.

At three hundred twenty kelvin, a near-black panel sheds about six hundred ten watts per square meter. Raise it to three hundred fifty kelvin and you get about eight hundred fifty watts per square meter, which is why hot radiators save mass.

A twenty percent rise in temperature nearly doubles the dump rate, because one point two to the fourth is about two. That is the entire lever short of adding more area, and area is metal you have to launch.

Future clusters described in Google's research paper target fifty to one hundred kilowatts per satellite group. At six hundred watts per square meter that is still roughly eighty to one hundred seventy square meters of radiator per group, before view-factor losses and the warm bus sitting behind the panel.

Double the compute and you roughly double that area unless the chips get more efficient or the radiators run hotter. A typical radiator might mass around three kilograms per square meter, so one hundred seventy square meters is already about five hundred kilograms of thermal hardware on a single group.

Two-sided panels help if both faces see cold sky, but in low Earth orbit one face often looks at the planet. Earth shines infrared back at you, on the order of two hundred watts per square meter, so some of the radiator's budget is spent fighting the ground instead of dumping chip heat.

Sunlight at Earth orbit delivers about one thousand three hundred sixty watts per square meter. With cells around twenty-five percent efficient you need about four square meters of array per delivered kilowatt, so a hundred-kilowatt group is already about four hundred square meters of collector plus structure.

The cells that do not convert sunlight also get hot, so the power system is a heat source too. Conditioning and conversion nibble another slice, often several percent, and that loss becomes heat on the spacecraft as well.

Continuous compute is a radiator-sizing problem, not a battery-sizing problem. The bus can soak heat for a while the way a frying pan soaks a burner, then you have to stop and let the panels glow it away, which is why cycling appears before the arrays and radiators are built to match average load.

Laser and wireless links try to move those kilowatts without copper between free-flying buses, because cabling is mass you cannot afford. Even a fifty percent end-to-end beam still dumps the other half as heat at the transmitter and the receiver, so beaming does not erase the thermal bill, it relocates it.

Radiation is the second tax. Flying commercial silicon instead of rad-hardened parts keeps mass and cost down, then retry logic and shielding have to carry the reliability burden if you want long inference runs rather than short queries.

The magic wand number is the raw silicon, sheet metal, and solar cells. The Idiot Index is finished satellite mass over those inputs, and for a typical bus that ratio still sits around ten to thirty, because structure, pointing, and thermal hardware dwarf the chips that do the work.

Closing that gap through tighter integration of compute, power, and thermal systems is the same first-principles path Falcon 9 took when it collapsed launch cost by reusing the entire booster rather than discarding it. Mass-efficient thermal design is what decides whether those fifty to one hundred kilowatt groups are viable.
