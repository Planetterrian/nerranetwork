For that Flight fifteen upper-stage catch, the engineering reality is converting hypersonic high-drag flight into a vertical capture only seconds before contact: the flaps steer the belly-flop, then the Raptor engines relight to flip the stage upright and brake.

Those flaps have to raise pitch and yaw while the Starship upper stage is still moving at hundreds of meters per second.

The flip and the landing burn have to be timed so the stage arrives inside the arm-geometry envelope.

Horizontal velocity and leftover rates have to die away before the arms close, and the landing burn does the last of that work.

The upper stage has no Super Heavy-style grid fins, and body-flap bite collapses below a few kilopascals of dynamic pressure.

That pressure is one half times air density times velocity squared.

Near the ground, density is about one point two kilograms per cubic meter.

At one hundred meters per second you get on the order of six kilopascals.

At fifty meters per second you get about one point five kilopascals.

Authority falls with the square of speed, so the flaps go mushy faster than the vehicle slows — right into the flip.

That square law is why the last second is the whole game.

Hypersonic means faster than Mach five, on the order of a kilometer and a half per second or more, before drag bleeds it down.

At vertical, residual horizontal speed and pitch and yaw rates have to be small enough for the arms and the skirt to take the load; SpaceX has not published the limits.

Twice the leftover speed is not twice the problem; energy and therefore load go with velocity squared, so it is four times the hit.

A leftover rotation turns a straight compressive load into a peel on the arm pins.

Structural steel yields on the order of a few hundred megapascals, so a sideslip at remaining descent speed is a spike, not a tap.

The flaps steer the descent, but the last seconds belong to the engines: the stage relights its Raptors to flip upright and brake, and the arms take what is left.

The controller trims that burn into the last half-second before capture.

At forty meters per second of descent, that half-second is on the order of twenty meters of travel.

The arms are a fixed geometry and cannot chase a miss, so the vehicle has to fly into a box measured in meters.

Ground tests cannot stack reentry heat soak, falling dynamic pressure, and flight mass properties in one article, so the first attempt is the first combined-margin data.

Expendable upper stages have always been the accepted waste in this business.

The Shuttle orbiter bought reuse with wings and a runway, and paid for it in dry mass.

Repeatable Starship upper-stage recovery would drop a second heat-shielded system and the propellant otherwise spent discarding the stage.

The idiot index of a thrown-away ship — the finished article over stainless sheet and methalox — stays high until you catch it.

Dual-use flaps must survive peak heating and still provide pitch in low dynamic pressure, so the design wants more area and faster actuators than reentry steering alone would ever need.
