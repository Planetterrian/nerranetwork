Falcon recovery and gas-turbine power, from an engineering standpoint, are the same machine wearing different clothes: you win by extracting more cycles before the next heavy intervention.

A droneship landing deletes the boostback burn that a return-to-launch-site trajectory has to fire. Extra change in velocity is not a rounding error. Kerolox engines — kerosene burned with liquid oxygen — throw exhaust at about three kilometers per second.

The rocket equation is exponential: the mass you must start with over the mass you finish with equals e raised to delta-v over exhaust velocity, where delta-v is the change in velocity. A three-hundred-meter-per-second add-on against three kilometers per second is a ratio of zero point one. Raise e to that power and you get about one point one — a ten percent propellant bump for that burn alone.

Skip the boostback, and that mass either becomes payload or unused margin. What you buy instead is a ship, a crane, and salt water. That is a logistics tax, not a physics tax.

Salt water is not free. Chloride ions attack welds and engine bells, so the inspection clock starts the moment the stage kisses the deck. Every extra hour of teardowns is a hidden tax on cadence, because a booster on stands is not a booster on the pad. Doing the catch at the tower for the Super Heavy booster and the Starship upper stage tries to delete that logistics tax too.

Splashdown still means a vessel, a port call, a road convoy, and an integration flow that takes days. Catch-and-restack aims to make the pad the factory: arms grab the vehicle, set it down, stack, load, fly. The magic-wand number is metal plus propellant — stainless sheet, engine alloys, and fuel at commodity prices.

Aerospace finished hardware often sits at an Idiot Index in the mid-teens or higher — how many times the finished article costs over those raw inputs — and that multiple is often fifteen to thirty, because abort-before-use design, one-off tooling, and inspection hours dominate the invoice.

Each extra flight that avoids a major teardown pays down that multiple, because the factory bill is already sunk.

Jet engines already proved the pattern. A commercial turbofan is built for tens of thousands of cycles. Early orbital stages were built for one. The physics did not change. The accounting did. Fly the same turbopumps through repeated thermal and pressure cycles — chamber walls swinging from cryogenic to several thousand kelvin and back — and the question becomes fatigue life, not peak thrust.

Metal wants to expand when it is hot and contract when it is cold. Do that in minutes, hundreds of times, and microscopic cracks grow. Each cycle adds a little length, and the inspection interval is how you stay ahead of it. Throat erosion and bearing wear usually set the intervention interval; the tanks are rarely the first thing to quit.

Keep inspections short enough that the vehicle stays in the rotation, and the amortized metal cost per flight collapses. Let inspections balloon, and you have an expendable rocket that happens to land.

A simple-cycle gas turbine is a Brayton engine: compress air, add heat, expand through a turbine. It converts fuel at about thirty-five percent thermal efficiency. A combined-cycle plant adds a steam bottoming cycle and can touch sixty percent, but you are now building a heat-recovery boiler and a steam turbine, which is slower to site.

Natural gas holds about fifty megajoules in each kilogram, so a back-of-envelope megawatt-hour needs roughly two hundred kilograms of gas once you account for conversion losses. Steel trades near two dollars a kilogram, so the commodity metal in a heavy frame is a small slice of installed cost. Permits, interconnection studies, and litigation live in the gap.

That is the Idiot Index on the power side. Install turbines instead of waiting for transmission upgrades and you get megawatts on a construction clock rather than a utility clock. Layer offsets later and you buy credits or add controls, and dollars per megawatt-hour rise with no extra joule at the busbar.

Close the gap with permitting certainty, or with a design that models clean enough to skip those add-ons. Rockets close it with flights between teardowns. Turbines close it with megawatt-hours between overhauls.
