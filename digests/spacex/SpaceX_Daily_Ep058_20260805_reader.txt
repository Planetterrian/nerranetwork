From an engineering standpoint, orbital data-center concepts face a fundamental heat-rejection limit where every watt of compute power eventually becomes waste heat that must leave the spacecraft through radiation.

Traditional fluid loops add mass, complexity, and single-point failure modes, so designers are exploring direct-conduction tiles that move heat from GPU dies to external radiator surfaces with minimal intermediate hardware.

The approach collapses the thermal path length, lowering the temperature gradient required to drive the same heat flux and thereby reducing the radiator area needed for a given compute load.

Early prototypes use high-conductivity ceramic-metal composites bonded directly to the GPU cold plate, eliminating pumps and manifolds that would otherwise dominate subsystem mass.

If the tiles achieve the targeted conductivity, the Idiot Index for the thermal subsystem drops because the finished assembly approaches the raw-material cost of high-conductivity ceramics and metal interfaces rather than the multiplied expense of pumps, manifolds, and redundant plumbing.

That reduction matters for orbital economics because every kilogram saved on the thermal system is a kilogram available for additional GPUs or propellant, directly improving the revenue per launch that an AI satellite can generate.

The same tiles also simplify radiation shielding because the conductive path itself can incorporate neutron-absorbing layers without adding separate enclosures.

The next engineering milestone is demonstrating sustained operation under orbital thermal cycling and radiation exposure without delamination or conductivity loss.

Success would validate a path to denser on-orbit clusters and, by extension, lower the cost floor for space-based inference workloads that Starlink backhaul could serve.

Ground testing will expose samples to vacuum outgassing, atomic-oxygen erosion, and total ionizing dose levels representative of a multi-year mission before the first Vera Rubin NVL72 rack flies.
