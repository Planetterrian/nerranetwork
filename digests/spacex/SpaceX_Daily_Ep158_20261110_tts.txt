Starship Flight 15 Preview: The First Ship Catch Attempt aims to catch an orbital Ship at the tower for the first time.

This is Space X Daily, episode one hundred fifty-eight.

This special is the briefing before launch day — after the plan on the pad, the minute-by-minute, why the Ship is the hard catch, and a scale for scoring whatever happens.

Space X plans Starship Flight fifteen as the first flight that tries to catch both stages at the tower: the Super Heavy booster on the way back, and then, more than twenty-two hours later, the Ship after it has reached orbit.

The attempt is targeted no earlier than Friday, November thirteenth, twenty twenty-six, from Starbase Pad two, and every date in this episode is a target that can move. You would see a change on Space X's official Flight fifteen mission page.

This preview is recorded before the vehicle leaves the pad. Nothing about Flight fifteen is a result. Your job on the day is to score what you see against a clear plan, not against a press release.

As of November tenth, that page lists a seventy-five-minute window opening at four forty-five p.m. Central Time, five forty-five p.m. Eastern, and closing at six p.m. Central — roughly twenty-two forty-five to midnight U T C. No fixed T-zero has been announced. The schedule is dynamic. A slip would be normal.

Space X Vice President of Launch Kiko Dontchev had already pointed at November thirteenth at the UP.Summit, and the live page still matches.

The launch is pending regulatory approval. That is the wording on the Space X mission page as of checks between November seventh and eleventh. A November tenth secondary article claimed the license was already in hand. That claim is unconfirmed, and it conflicts with Space X's own pending language.

The vehicles are Booster twenty-two and Ship forty-two, both Block three, also called V3, hardware. Booster twenty-two is the first V3 Super Heavy planned for a tower catch. Ship forty-two is the V3 ship assigned to this flight.

The payload is twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites. Three of them carry cameras to inspect the heat shield.

The planned profile, in order, is liftoff from Starbase Pad two; hot staging, which is the Ship lighting while the two stages are still attached; the Super Heavy booster returning for a catch at the Pad two Mechazilla tower — those are the chopsticks, the arms that squeeze and grab structural hardpoints; then the Ship continuing toward low Earth orbit and attempting a return catch at the same tower only after systems checks confirm it is ready for reentry and the landing burn.

Ocean off-ramps are already written into that plan.

Treat the times that follow as the planned timeline Space X has published, not as a promise. They come from the Flight fifteen mission page and its timeline table.

Liftoff is the Super Heavy booster lighting its engines under Ship forty-two, stacked on Pad two. The stack climbs. Hot staging is planned around T plus two minutes and twenty-one seconds: the Ship lights while it is still attached, the stages separate, and the Super Heavy booster begins the work of coming home.

That Super Heavy booster never intended to reach orbit. Its job on this flight is a high-energy hop, then a boostback — it turns around and burns toward the coast — then a landing burn. The landing burn is planned to start around T plus six minutes and ten seconds. The catch is planned around T plus six minutes and thirty-five seconds. This would be the first catch attempt for a V3 Super Heavy.

After staging, Ship forty-two is on its own. The plan is not a blind insertion. The mission page describes a passively safe suborbital trajectory at first — a path that falls back to Earth if nothing else is done — with orbit insertion only if the vehicle is cleared.

If it is cleared, the Ship targets about two hundred seventy-five kilometers, deploys the payload, and stays up for about fourteen orbits. Coverage is planned through the Ship catch, with possible extended Starlink views from orbit. While it is up there, the vehicle is not just a payload truck.

The cameras on those satellites are there so the company can look at the heat shield and decide whether the Ship is healthy enough to bring home.

That decision point is the hinge of the flight. Space X has said the Ship catch will be attempted only if ship systems for reentry and landing are confirmed healthy after those on-orbit checks. If they are not — vehicle health, tower, weather, or any other reason — the catch is called off and the Ship is aimed at a contingency ocean landing.

Treat a called-off catch as a planned off-ramp, not as an improvised failure. The company built those off-ramps in. The Super Heavy booster's water option is the Gulf of Mexico. The Ship's options are the Indian Ocean and the Pacific, with multiple pre-determined safe deorbit areas.

If the Ship is cleared, the deorbit burn comes late — the burn that drops it out of orbit. Reentry is on the timeline around twenty-two hours, ten minutes, and forty-six seconds. The landing burn, the flip, and the catch are on the timeline around twenty-two hours, thirty-two minutes, and thirty-four seconds, if cleared.

The Ship has to survive heating, keep control, relight, flip from a belly-first reentry attitude into a landing attitude, and arrive at the same Pad two arms that, if all has gone well, already caught the Super Heavy booster the evening before. Every recovery in that sequence names a stage: the Super Heavy booster on the short loop, the Ship on the long one.

From an engineering standpoint, those two tower catches use the same arms and are not the same problem.

The difference is energy, and energy is why the Ship's return is a different kind of engineering. The Super Heavy booster does not go to orbit. It lifts the stack, stages, turns around, and comes back from a suborbital trajectory.

It is fast, and catching it is not easy — Space X has already shown that a booster catch can work, and it has also shown that it can fail — but the booster never has to dump the energy of being in orbit. The Ship does.

Orbital velocity is a different regime. To stay in orbit the Ship has to be moving so fast that missing the Earth is the default. To come home, that speed has to go somewhere. In the atmosphere, somewhere is heat.

The heat shield's job is to take that energy so the vehicle structure does not. Tiles on the windward side — the face that hits the airflow — are the barrier between plasma and steel.

Flight fourteen already flew with reused heat-shield tiles from an earlier vehicle, which is one reason this attempt is even on the table: the company has at least some flight evidence that the shield can be flown, recovered in some form, and flown again. Space X wants to know how the tiles look before it commits the Ship to reentry and a catch. That is what the on-orbit inspection is for.

Control through reentry is the next problem. A booster returning from a hop is in thicker air sooner, with a different heating pulse and a different control task. A Ship coming from orbit spends a long time in a regime where the air is thin, the heating is intense, and the plasma around the vehicle can interfere with communications and with the clean picture a guidance computer wants.

The vehicle has to hold an attitude that keeps the heat shield into the flow. Then, low and late, it has to change its mind: relight engines, flip from that belly-first attitude into a tail-down landing attitude, and put itself in front of the tower arms with almost no extra propellant to waste on a go-around.

Landing legs would have given a last-resort pad or ocean landing with more forgiveness. This architecture does not carry that forgiveness on the vehicle. The arms are the landing gear.

That is also why Space X wants tower catches at all. Legs are dry mass — structure you fly that is not payload and not propellant. Dry mass on an upper stage is payload you did not carry, or propellant you cannot use to go farther. Catching on the tower means the Ship and the Super Heavy booster can be designed without landing legs, which cuts the mass that has to be flown on every mission.

It also means the vehicle comes back to the pad it left, in the hardware that can stack it again, which is the path to a fast re-stack rather than a recovery fleet and a road trip.

The arms catch by squeezing structural hardpoints — a method Space X has tested with suspended vehicles and with prior Super Heavy booster catches. Arriving at those hardpoints after an orbital reentry, a flip, and a landing burn is the part that has no flight heritage on a Ship. The Super Heavy booster catch on this flight deserves attention. It is not the hard part in kind. The Ship catch is.

The Ship is the stage that went all the way to orbital velocity, and orbital velocity is what the atmosphere has to erase.

The program did not jump to a dual catch. The Super Heavy booster catch was proven first, on earlier V1 flights, three times. Those catches are the reason Pad two's arms are not a theory. They are also not a copy-paste onto V3, which is why Booster twenty-two's catch, if it is attempted, is still a first for this block.

Flight fourteen is the immediate predecessor this preview has to carry. As this show has reported, that flight reached orbit and deployed the same satellite count Flight fifteen is assigned, despite an engine shutdown that Elon Musk described as a software failure on a single RVac unit — a Raptor Vacuum engine. What Flight fourteen did not do is catch the Ship. That is the open item.

The test campaign for these two vehicles is the last mile. Booster twenty-two's full-duration thirty-three-engine static fire on October twenty-fifth — engines lit at full set while the vehicle is held down — is the ground evidence that the booster can run the set.

Ship forty-two's six-engine static fire and single-engine deorbit simulation on October twenty-eighth are the ground evidence that the Ship can light the engines it will need to come home.

Final catch testing on the pad, including the squeeze test with Booster twenty-two as of about November tenth, is the tower side of the same argument: the arms have to be ready to grab a V3 Super Heavy, and later a Ship, by those hardpoints. Earlier reporting that the Pad two arms had already lifted Ship forty-two into catch position sits in the same campaign. None of that is flight.

It is why the company is willing to put a catch of both stages on the Flight fifteen plan. No verified slips, scrubs, or major new anomalies were announced between November seventh and tenth.

The spectacle is the catch. What rides on it is the reuse loop.

Space X frames full and rapid reuse of both stages as the path to cutting cost-to-orbit by ninety-nine percent or more. That is a company projection, not a measured result, and it should be heard as a projection.

The logic underneath it is the engineering of the arms: if neither stage is thrown away, and if neither stage carries landing legs, and if both come back to the tower that can stack them again, the cost of a flight starts to look like propellant, operations, and refurbishment rather than building a new Super Heavy booster and a new Ship every time. Flight fifteen is not that factory.

It is the attempt to show that the recovery half of that sentence can be done on one flight, for both stages, with the Ship coming home from orbit.

Starlink is the payload that makes the attempt commercially live, not a mass simulator. Earlier reporting from Keep Track put each V3 satellite at an added terabit of capacity and this set at roughly ten Falcon nine launches of V2 Mini satellites.

A terabit is a trillion bits a second — think of one of these satellites as a fat fiber line in the sky, and this load as about ten older Falcon nine Starlink missions stacked into a single Ship. A Ship that can deploy that and then be caught is a different production story from a Ship that can deploy that and then ditch in the ocean.

Even a successful deploy with a called-off catch still puts V3 hardware on orbit. The catch is what turns the upper stage from a costly expendable into a vehicle that can fly again.

Artemis and the company's own Moon-and-Mars plans sit behind that same reuse loop. This briefing does not have a new NASA milestone tied to this attempt, and it should not invent one. The honest version is the one Space X already uses: a rapidly reusable two-stage system is the architecture they say they need for the cadence those programs imply.

A first Ship catch would be a single data point on that architecture, not the architecture itself.

The business thread is secondary and stays that way. Ess Pee See Ex is the listed company. This research does not report a Flight fifteen-specific market move that belongs in this briefing. The engineering is the headline.

One thing worth watching is that this is a test flight with a catch on the plan, not a routine operational launch with a catch as a footnote. Score it that way.

The off-ramps Space X has named are the first ledger. A Super Heavy booster catch can be called off for vehicle health, tower, weather, or other reasons. A Ship catch is attempted only if reentry and landing systems are confirmed healthy after on-orbit checks. Orbit insertion itself is gated on that passively safe path first.

A called-off catch, on either stage, is a decision the company has already written down. It can still be a disappointment. It is not an unplanned mode.

The risks that follow from the physics are the ones this preview can name without inventing failure reports. The Super Heavy booster has to light, stage, boost back, and arrive at the arms as a V3 vehicle for the first time. The Ship has to insert, deploy, survive the long coast, pass the health checks, reenter on the heat shield, relight, flip, and arrive at the same arms with little margin.

Heat-shield damage, an engine that does not relight, a guidance error on the flip, a tower that is not ready, weather, and a hold before T-zero are all in the envelope. Elon Musk's own numbers treat the Ship catch as uncertain. Those are his predictions, not odds this show is adopting.

A clear scale of outcomes, from the top down. Full success is both catches plus the deploy: Super Heavy booster caught on the short loop, the satellites on orbit, Ship caught after reentry. Next is a Super Heavy booster catch, a successful deploy, and a healthy Ship that is waved off to the ocean. That is still a huge flight. It proves V3 booster recovery and V3 satellite delivery.

It leaves the Ship catch open. Next is a Super Heavy booster catch and a Ship that does not make a clean orbital mission — insertion not cleared, or a problem on orbit. The booster side of the dual-catch plan would still have been attempted. Next is an ocean landing or loss of the Super Heavy booster with a Ship that still completes the orbital piece.

The payload mission can succeed while the first V3 booster catch does not. An early loss — on ascent, at staging, or before the Super Heavy booster is back — teaches whatever the telemetry gives and delays everything that was stacked on this attempt. A test flight that fails informatively is still a test. That sentence is not an excuse.

It is the difference between a program that flies to learn and a press release that needed a perfect day. Whatever happens, you should be able to point at this scale and say which rung you saw.

Elon Musk's comments are the ones with the most weight because they are his, and because he put numbers on a first Ship catch. He previously estimated success probability at at least fifty or sixty percent, or more likely than not, if Flight fourteen succeeded, with backups available. On the third-quarter earnings call he used more likely than not for a successful Ship catch.

Recent X activity, including his reposts of mission videos, shows continued promotion of the flight. The research does not show an updated numerical estimate from him in this briefing. Treat every one of those figures as a prediction by Musk, not as a measurement.

Space X's own framing on the mission materials is that Flight fifteen is a potential first full recovery of an orbital launch system. That is a company claim about what the attempt would mean, not a result.

Gwynne Shotwell's earlier comment — that she expected the license to cover return-to-launch-site and a tower catch for both stages — is the most important regulatory note in this briefing, and it sits next to the live page still saying pending.

That is the argument worth having.

Noise about dates and licenses without a primary source is not.

Watch on the official Space X Flight fifteen mission page, and on X. The webcast is planned to start about thirty minutes before liftoff and to continue through the Ship catch. How-to-watch reporting from The Space Road matches that.

Four moments are worth watching for, in order. First, hot staging — the stack becoming two vehicles. Second, the Super Heavy booster's landing burn and catch, or a call to send that booster to the Gulf. Third, confirmation that Ship forty-two has inserted and begun deploying, and those on-orbit inspections of the shield.

Fourth, the late decision: whether the Ship is cleared for reentry, then reentry, then the flip, landing burn, and catch — or a wave-off to the ocean. If you can keep those four beats straight, you can watch the flight without getting lost in the hours between them.

What comes next depends on which rung you see. This preview does not invent a Flight sixteen date. The next concrete milestone is the window itself, and the license language on the mission page.

Carry three things onto launch day. First, Flight fifteen is the first planned dual tower catch, and the new thing in kind is the Ship: catching an upper stage after orbital velocity. The Super Heavy booster catch matters, and for V3 it is still a first, but it is not the same physics.

Second, the timeline is a set of gates, not a single moment — staging, Super Heavy booster catch or wave-off, on-orbit health, then a Ship catch that Space X has already said it will skip if the vehicle is not ready.

Third, Musk's own words put the Ship catch in more likely than not territory, not in the bag, and the company's cost-to-orbit claim remains a projection until reuse of both stages is actually routine.

The honest open question is the one the arms will answer only if the Ship is sent to them: can this heat shield, this flip, and this landing burn put an orbital upper stage onto the tower in one piece?
