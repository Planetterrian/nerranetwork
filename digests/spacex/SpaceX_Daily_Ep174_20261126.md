### Top News

1. **US regulators approve SpaceX Starmind constellation for up to one million orbital data-center satellites**
   The Federal Communications Commission granted the first authorization of its kind for a space-based AI compute network. Deployment must begin by November 25 2033 with 100 000 satellites in orbit by 2035, 500 000 by 2038 and the full million by 2040; the license does not require the entire constellation. Satellites will operate in low-Earth orbit between 500 km and 1 002 km, draw power from sunlight and radiate waste heat into space while routing processed data through Starlink optical links. SpaceX filed the application on January 30 2026 and plans an initial launch as early as the third quarter of 2027 using Starship; the company is manufacturing the spacecraft with Nvidia Vera Rubin processors. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the project supports US leadership in super-intelligence infrastructure. Amazon’s Kuiper, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society objected over orbital debris, congestion and impacts on optical and radio astronomy. Elon Musk replied on X that the constellation “will be a great asset to America and the world.” The order covers shells spanning 50 km at altitudes from 500 to 1 002 km in 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations. Each Starmind satellite has a reported wingspan of 75 meters and deployed height of 30 meters. The FCC also approved additional communications infrastructure for SpaceX’s growing optical network. SpaceX argued that orbital placement offers near-constant solar power and avoids terrestrial constraints on land, electricity and water. The authorization runs until 2040 according to the published FCC document. ## The Counterpoint
Astronomers and rival operators argue the Starmind constellation will increase orbital debris risk, radio-frequency interference and light pollution that degrades both optical and radio observations. The FCC order limits the license to backup Ka-band links and requires an accepted debris-mitigation plan before any satellite flies above 600 km. Amazon described the million-satellite scale as technically and economically unrealistic on the proposed timeline. The American Astronomical Society and Viasat raised concerns about congestion and harmful interference with existing systems. The order waives the surety bond but keeps the deployment milestones binding. ### Engineering Deep Dive
Orbital placement removes two terrestrial constraints that dominate ground data-center economics: continuous solar illumination and radiative heat rejection into space. At 500–1 002 km altitude a satellite sees sunlight for nearly the entire orbit except brief eclipse periods, eliminating the need for grid-scale battery or gas-turbine buffering that terrestrial facilities require during peak demand. Waste heat is rejected through large radiator surfaces rather than evaporative cooling towers or chillers that consume water and electricity; the 75 m wingspan and 30 m deployed height reported for Starmind spacecraft provide the surface area needed for that radiation balance. Processors still face total-ionizing-dose and single-event effects from trapped radiation belts, so the design must incorporate shielding mass or rad-hardened silicon whose raw-material cost is far lower than the finished packaged die. Day-to-day traffic moves over optical inter-satellite links while the FCC license covers only unprotected Ka-band backup telemetry, keeping the primary data path free of spectrum-allocation delays. The net effect is that the “idiot index” for compute—finished delivered FLOPS versus the silicon, metal and kilowatt-hours inside—can drop if launch cost per kilogram falls enough with Starship to offset the added mass of radiators and shielding. The same architecture also decouples site selection from terrestrial power-plant siting fights, which is why the FCC order explicitly frames the constellation as infrastructure for sustained US AI leadership.

The radiation environment at these altitudes forces a clear engineering trade-off between mass and reliability. Total ionizing dose accumulates steadily from trapped protons and electrons, requiring either additional aluminum or tantalum shielding or the use of silicon-on-insulator processes that raise per-die cost but reduce upset rates. Because the satellites communicate primarily through optical links to the existing Starlink fleet, the radio-frequency license is deliberately narrow and unprotected, meaning any detected interference immediately forces the backup channel offline. This design choice keeps spectrum filings simple while shifting the reliability burden onto the optical terminals already proven on Starlink. Heat rejection scales with the fourth power of surface temperature, so the large deployed radiators allow the processors to run at higher power density than ground systems limited by air or water cooling loops. The 30-meter height dimension directly supports the radiator area needed to maintain junction temperatures within commercial-grade limits without active refrigeration. If Starship achieves the targeted cost per kilogram, the added mass of shielding and radiators becomes a smaller fraction of total launch economics, collapsing the idiot index for orbital compute relative to terrestrial builds that must also pay for land, water rights and grid interconnection.

The deployment timeline itself reflects the same first-principles constraint on manufacturing and launch cadence. The FCC milestones require only 100 000 satellites by 2035, giving SpaceX roughly eight years after the first planned 2027 launch to reach that rate. That pace aligns with the company’s stated plan to begin slowly in 2027, ramp in 2028 and reach very large volume in 2029 once Starship flight rate and payload capacity stabilize. Because each satellite carries Nvidia Vera Rubin processors optimized for space, the production bottleneck shifts from silicon fabrication to satellite assembly and integration at the new Gigabay facilities. The 50 km orbital shells spaced across 500–1 002 km allow the constellation to be built in incremental planes rather than requiring simultaneous global coverage, further reducing the peak manufacturing load. This staged approach mirrors the Starlink rollout strategy that grew from hundreds to more than 11 000 spacecraft without ever halting service.
