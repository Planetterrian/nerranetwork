New Starship facilities in Louisiana, Advanced Television reports, are hitting trouble on permitting and on the calendars for building out the sites.

Those projects are launch-infrastructure works tied to Starship operations.

Local stakeholders have questioned the environmental reviews and whether supporting infrastructure will be ready, and that pairing could slide construction.

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum called, Mexico Business News reports, for two-country meetings of United States and Mexican airspace authorities on SpaceX launch permits after Starship-test debris washed onto beaches in Tamaulipas — the diplomatic follow-up to yesterday’s Playa Bagdad recovery.

Sheinbaum pressed for coordination on the rules for using that airspace.

She also put launch frequency on the table, and possible relocation of the launch pad if needed.

SpaceX said its partners found little debris in inspections, and that separate toxicity tests found Starship materials were not hazardous.

The move puts talks with regulators ahead of a lawsuit.

Cameron County, the Port Isabel-South Padre paper reports, rejected a SpaceX request for a tax break that had been aimed at incentives for facilities near Starbase.

Local officials objected that the relief SpaceX wanted looked large next to the community benefits they expect.

SpaceX’s stated goals, AvBrief notes, will take a launch volume no earlier system has flown, spanning Starship cadence, Starlink growth, and emerging orbital compute.

The requirement is thousands of launches, with Falcon 9’s reuse cadence as the yardstick.

The bottleneck is how fast factories and pads can turn around vehicles.

JetBrains is bringing GoLand’s code intelligence into VS Code and into Cursor, TechGig reports, the AI code editor tied to SpaceX through the Anysphere acquisition.

The integration puts advanced support for the Go programming language, plus refactoring tools and debugging, directly inside those editors so developers do not have to leave the environment they already use.

The same step widens JetBrains’ reach into AI-assisted development workflows.

Southeast Arizona is getting a named AI data-center project from Elon Musk, AZ Central reports, a terrestrial site that sits alongside other ground-based compute already built for AI workloads.

The project adds that ground capacity before any Starmind satellites might fly.

Chad Gibbs, a Starlink executive who helped scale commercial operations and the subscriber base, is leaving, Business Insider reports, while the constellation pushes into new markets and into direct-to-cell service that talks to ordinary phones.

Gibbs contributed to Starlink’s revenue growth, and no one is named yet to replace him.

Observers on X, in posts NASASpaceflight relayed that have not been confirmed, compared proposed Starmind scale with the roughly twenty-eight thousand eight hundred satellites ever launched.

Another of those posts said the Federal Communications Commission, or FCC, set a hard milestone of five hundred thousand satellites on station by two thousand thirty-eight.

Worth keeping an eye on, scientists and astronomers told PBS, is whether dumping heat as infrared from a spacecraft’s skin still hits a hard physics limit in vacuum, and whether collision risk climbs as orbital compute grows.

Heat dissipation and launch cadence, those experts argue, make the path to full deployment by two thousand forty unrealistic without major breakthroughs.

What would settle it is on-orbit cooling performance and collision-avoidance data from early prototypes.

Starmind’s heat problem is today’s engineering deep dive, because the satellites the FCC authorized only work if they can dump waste heat from thousands of GPUs — the chips that run AI workloads — with no water and no air.

Each vehicle is sized at roughly seventy by twenty meters once solar arrays and radiators deploy, far larger than a Starlink satellite, so it can take continuous sunlight in sun-synchronous orbit, a path that stays in daylight for most of every trip around Earth, and throw that heat overboard from giant panels.

A back-of-envelope figure for that radiator is about fourteen hundred square meters, which is how the design multiplies radiating area against a compact Starlink bus.

Even then, the Stefan-Boltzmann rule — hotter surfaces throw off much more heat — says rejection scales with the fourth power of surface temperature, so the panels still have to run hotter than water-cooled chips on the ground to shed the same load.

Six approved shells sit between five hundred and one thousand two kilometers, three of them at twenty-six to thirty-two degrees of inclination, meaning how tilted the path is to the equator, and three near-polar, a split that stretches sunlight and shortens eclipses, the stretches in Earth’s shadow that would otherwise force heavier batteries.

Sun-synchronous paths at ninety-seven to ninety-nine degrees are the near-polar piece of that split.

Laser links into the existing Starlink fleet handle the primary data return, with Ka-band, a radio backup, reserved only for telemetry, so the satellite does not spend mass and power on large radio antennas; that cross-link rides a network that already has more than eleven thousand one hundred satellites and global coverage.

At one million units the design treats each spacecraft as a replaceable node rather than a repairable one: a failed AI1 unit is deorbited and swapped by a later Starship flight, a write-off amortized across the constellation’s aggregate compute, because the FCC treats the satellite as a consumable whose economics hang on Starship’s projected launch cost per kilogram rather than on-orbit servicing.

The first operational satellite must fly by November two thousand thirty-three, with one hundred thousand on station by two thousand thirty-five, which forces manufacturing throughput and automated deployment sequences that have no direct precedent at this volume.
