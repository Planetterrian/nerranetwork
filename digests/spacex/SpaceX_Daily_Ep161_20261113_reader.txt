SpaceX plans a seventy-four billion dollar investment in supercomputing campuses to power its AI ambitions.

Starshield limits from Ukraine, Blue Origin walking away from a NASA station contract, and rockets rolling on both coasts come next, then the engineering angle.

SpaceX will split the outlay across two sites, GovCon Wire reports, putting more than twenty-four billion dollars into a Texas campus next to Starbase to run large-scale training and inference on the xAI and Grok stack.

The Arizona site in Cochise County carries a proposed fifty billion dollars, and opposition there has gathered more than two thousand four hundred petition signatures against a component projected to create six thousand five hundred jobs.

The company says the work will grow local tax bases and jobs while cleaning up debris that has sat since the nineteen sixties — scrap metal, discarded appliances, old tanks, fiberglass insulation, construction materials, and thousands of illegally dumped tires — and removal is underway as the Texas site is prepared.

SpaceX is calling that cleanup a chance to fix long-standing neglect, and it is positioning both campuses as shared infrastructure for its own operations and the broader Musk compute ecosystem, with coordinated planning from the Rio Grande Valley to Cochise County along the corridor from southern Texas to southern Arizona.

Ukrainian officials said speed restrictions on Starshield, the government version of Starlink, United24 Media reports, rose from ten percent of missions affected on the first day to fifty percent on the second and one hundred percent on the third.

The limits cap speed at one hundred sixty-one kilometers per hour, which they say blocks interceptor drones from matching incoming Russian Shahed threats and has complicated intercepts of jet-powered attack drones over the front line and occupied territory.

President Zelenskyy described the limits as systematic rather than random errors, and Ukrainian military personnel who investigated the disruptions concluded they were not technical errors.

SpaceX stated there have been no changes to the service and told Axios the account of new limitations is false.

Dave Limp told Blue Origin employees, in remarks circulating on X, that the company will not bid on NASA’s commercial low Earth orbit destination contract, or CLD, because its priorities remain New Glenn and Blue Moon.

Blue Origin has focused resources on finishing that heavy-lift vehicle and the lunar lander, and it views the orbital station award as lower priority, leaving the CLD competition to other bidders for whom it would be more consequential, a contest industry observers say carries greater weight for smaller commercial providers.

Brownsville is receiving two hundred twenty million dollars from SpaceX, pledged on X as the largest private contribution in the city’s history, aimed at long-term drinking-water reliability around Starbase.

The funds will support water and wastewater systems serving Brownsville and Cameron County, framed as part of broader Rio Grande Valley infrastructure work that expands supply capacity for generations of residents in one of the region’s most pressing challenges.

TO THE NEW is working with SpaceXSI, SMBTech reports, to put the Cursor coding platform inside larger companies that already run Grok.

The collaboration is built for organizations trying to scale agent-based development, so teams can hand tasks across multiple users and agents in shared workspaces, and it pushes Grok further into enterprise coding through the Cursor interface.

Cape Canaveral has a Vulcan on the pad, Florida Today reports, as United Launch Alliance rolls the VC6L configuration for Friday’s Amazon Leo LV-01 mission from SLC-41.

That stack is the most powerful Vulcan flown yet, lifting for Amazon’s Project Kuiper buildout on the same day Starship Flight fifteen is scheduled from Texas, November thirteenth on both manifests.

In a separate commitment, SpaceX is backing a child-protection initiative with seven hundred fifty free Starlink kits, Drive Tesla Canada reports, that will connect children’s homes across Africa.

NASASpaceflight posted updated imagery of Ship 42 being lifted and moved to the launch mount, the upper stage in the final stacking sequence at Starbase ahead of the planned Flight fifteen window.

StarshipGazer, in a post not yet confirmed by the company, had Ship 42 rolled out to Pad 2 and stacked atop Booster 22 with about twenty-four hours remaining, the clock at T-minus twenty-four and a half hours.

Worth keeping an eye on is the European Commission’s call, Kyiv Post reports, for SpaceX to restore full Starshield operations as soon as possible after President Zelenskyy said the speed cap has already allowed some Russian strikes to penetrate defenses.

The dispute raises a fair operational question when a foreign government is using the network for real-time intercepts: how fast a contested limit can be checked or lifted. A shared telemetry record, or a confirmed return to unrestricted speeds, is what would settle it.

For the vehicle now on the clock, the engineering angle is a double catch. The Ship upper stage has to live through a full orbital mission, fourteen revolutions, and a high-energy reentry before threading itself between the tower arms roughly twenty-two hours after launch, while the booster is already back in six and a half minutes.

The first stage already has three tower catches and never reaches orbital speed, so its heat pulse is a short slap next to the upper stage’s. Low Earth orbit moves at roughly eight kilometers per second, and a circuit takes on the order of ninety minutes.

A back-of-envelope figure puts kinetic energy near thirty-two megajoules in every kilogram of the Ship at that speed, almost all of it dumped as heat in the shock layer before the tower ever sees the vehicle.

Heating climbs on the order of velocity cubed, so the Ship sees far higher peak heating while still needing tiles intact for immediate reuse, which is why the first Block 3 ships flew with extra instrumentation and thicker tile bonds.

Catching both stages without ocean recovery is how the cost of the next flight falls toward the propellant bill. Each avoided splashdown drops weeks of logistics, salt-water exposure, and tile-replacement labor.

Treat methane, oxygen, and metal as the magic-wand inputs and the fluids for a vehicle this size sit, on a back-of-envelope figure, in the low millions of dollars at commodity prices; every recovery ship, barge, and depot-level tile swap is the Idiot Index, the factor by which the real operation exceeds those raw inputs.

An eighty-year goal on a one hundred twenty-four meter stack only works if the upper-stage heat shield and the legless catch can match the reliability the booster has already shown.

Flights twelve through fourteen showed progressive gains in ascent engine reliability and payload deployment, and they set the bar.

Flight twelve’s first Block 3 booster had boostback trouble — the burn that turns it around toward the tower — and was lost in the landing burn, while Ship thirty-nine lost a Raptor Vacuum, the vacuum-optimized upper-stage engine, on ascent yet still deployed its payload into an on-target Indian Ocean splashdown.

Flight thirteen’s Booster twenty flew a clean ascent and boostback before ice ingestion shortened the landing burn into a Gulf impact, and Ship forty flew cleanly, deployed twenty Starlink V3 satellites, and returned intact for tile study.

Flight fourteen, the first orbital Starship mission, saw Booster twenty-one lose two Raptors and still splash down soft, and Ship forty-one lose one Raptor Vacuum, deploy twenty-six Starlink V3 satellites, and fly a contingency reentry. Every one of those boosters still had at least one engine anomaly during boostback or landing.

Flight fifteen has to clear that on both stages to claim the first fully reusable orbital launch system.
