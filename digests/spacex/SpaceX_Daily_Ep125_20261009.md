1. **SpaceX acquires low-band spectrum to build nationwide mobile network**
   SpaceX will buy up to 14 megahertz of paired spectrum in the 800 MHz range, subject to final regulatory approval. The company plans to combine this with its existing 2 GHz holdings and a terrestrial network to reach devices inside buildings. Elon Musk called the move a "very big deal." The announcement follows FCC approval for 15,000 next-generation direct-to-device satellites that deliver more than 100 times the bandwidth of current Starlink Mobile units. Shares of AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile fell around 7 percent on the news. The new frequencies target improved indoor penetration and wide-area coverage without extra ground towers in every gap. SpaceX intends to integrate the 800 MHz allocation into the V2 satellite payload design. Final regulatory clearance for the transaction remains pending after the FCC placed some spectrum requests on hold. ### Engineering Deep Dive
Low-band spectrum at 800 MHz propagates farther and penetrates buildings more effectively than the 2 GHz frequencies SpaceX already holds. Pairing the new 14 MHz allocation with existing holdings lets the same satellite beam reach indoor handsets without requiring additional ground infrastructure in every coverage gap. The 100-times bandwidth increase planned for the next-generation V2 satellites then multiplies capacity once the signal reaches the device. This combination trades some spectrum efficiency for simpler user hardware, removing the need for specialized terminals that earlier direct-to-cell systems required. The approach also sets up a hybrid network where satellites handle wide-area coverage while a terrestrial layer fills urban capacity, a design that reuses the same orbital assets for both consumer broadband and mobile backhaul. Execution now hinges on integrating the new frequencies into the satellite payload without raising mass or power draw beyond current Starship lift margins.

The 171-foot-long Ship 40 returned to Starbase after its Flight 13 splashdown in the Indian Ocean. Recovery teams succeeded in towing the intact upper stage through the Port of Brownsville channel despite earlier concerns that the operation might fail. Engineers removed several heatshield tiles from the vehicle and flew them again on Flight 14, providing direct physical evidence of tile durability after reentry heating. The landing burn on Flight 13 was performed in a profile that matches the final descent needed for a future tower catch attempt. Because the stage remained intact rather than exploding on contact with the water, SpaceX obtained data on tile attachment and thermal protection that camera-equipped Starlink V3 satellites alone could not supply. This physical inspection shortens the iteration loop for the heatshield redesign required before repeated booster and ship reuse becomes routine.

Starship upper-stage recovery changes the economics of the entire stack. When a ship survives splashdown, the cost per flight drops from the raw material price of a new vehicle to the marginal cost of inspection, tile replacement, and refurbishment. The gap between those two numbers is the Idiot Index for the upper stage. Flight 13 demonstrated that the current tile attachment method can survive one reentry without total loss, lowering that index. The next engineering step is to close the remaining distance to zero refurbishment time so that the same ship can fly again within days rather than months. Achieving that target requires both the tower catch hardware now under construction at Starbase and the orbital refueling demonstration still ahead in the flight test campaign.
