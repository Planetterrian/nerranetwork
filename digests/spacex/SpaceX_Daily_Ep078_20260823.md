### Top News
1. **New Novaspace forecast: 6,500+ EO satellites to launch by 2035**
   Novaspace projects more than 6,500 Earth observation satellites will reach orbit by 2035, generating $155.9 billion in manufacturing revenue. Governments are driving much of the demand through expanded sovereign intelligence and surveillance programs. The forecast covers both government and commercial constellations that will require frequent, cost-effective rides to orbit. Reusable medium- and heavy-lift vehicles are positioned to handle the bulk of these deployments given the projected cadence. Paris-based analysts released the numbers this week after reviewing government procurement pipelines and commercial operator filings through the early 2030s. The $155.9 billion manufacturing figure reflects average satellite costs across optical, radar, and hyperspectral payloads. Source: [spacenews.com](https://spacenews.com/new-novaspace-forecast-6500-eo-satellites-to-launch-by-2035/)

2. **Elve qualifies millimeter-wave amplifiers for spaceflight**
   California startup Elve has completed spaceflight qualification of its 100-watt millimeter-wave Traveling Wave Tube Amplifier platform. The work was supported by U.S. Space Force Small Business Innovation Research funding. Qualification testing addressed the vacuum, thermal, and radiation environment of low Earth orbit. The amplifiers target high-bandwidth satellite communications links where millimeter-wave frequencies enable higher data rates than traditional bands. Engineers at Elve subjected multiple flight-representative units to thermal-vacuum cycling and total-ionizing-dose radiation exposure at levels expected in sun-synchronous orbits. The 100-watt output specification was verified across the full temperature range while maintaining spectral purity required for dense frequency reuse. Source: [spacenews.com](https://spacenews.com/elve-qualifies-millimeter-wave-amplifiers-for-spaceflight/)

3. **Europe cancels planned upgrades for Ariane 6 rocket**
   Arianespace has dropped previously announced performance upgrades for the Ariane 6 launch vehicle. No public cost figure for an Ariane 6 mission has been released. The decision leaves the rocket at its current payload capacity without the incremental improvements once planned. European operators now face a narrower set of options for medium-heavy payloads until a next-generation vehicle arrives. The baseline two-core configuration will therefore remain the sole operational variant for the foreseeable future. Industry sources indicate the canceled upgrades would have added roughly one tonne to geostationary transfer orbit performance. Source: [arstechnica.com](https://arstechnica.com/space/2026/08/europe-cancels-planned-upgrades-for-ariane-6-rocket/)

### Engineering Deep Dive
Millimeter-wave traveling-wave tube amplifiers solve a fundamental link-budget problem in satellite communications. At these frequencies the available spectrum is far wider than at S- or Ku-band, so the same aperture can carry many times more bits per second, yet free-space path loss rises sharply and atmospheric absorption becomes material. A 100-watt TWT must therefore deliver that power while surviving the vacuum, thermal cycling, and radiation environment without excessive mass or heat rejection. Qualification testing therefore focuses on cathode life, helix thermal management, and collector efficiency, because every watt not converted to RF output must be radiated away. Once qualified, the amplifier enables tighter beams and higher-order modulation on small satellites, directly lowering the cost per delivered bit and allowing denser constellations to share spectrum without mutual interference.

The same physics that makes millimeter-wave attractive also explains why optical links are being pursued in parallel: both approaches trade aperture size and pointing precision against raw bandwidth, but the TWT path avoids the cloud and daylight constraints that still limit laser terminals. In first-principles terms, the raw material cost of the helix, collector, and cathode assembly is only a few hundred dollars, yet the finished, space-qualified unit commands orders of magnitude more because of the precision machining, vacuum brazing, and multi-month test campaign required to guarantee twenty-year reliability. That gap is the classic Idiot Index for RF hardware; each successful qualification run collapses the index by proving the design can be reproduced at lower cost on the next batch. Elve’s SBIR-funded path therefore represents a deliberate attempt to move the index downward for the entire industry rather than for a single mission.

The net result is a practical middle ground that lets operators increase throughput without waiting for fully optical networks to mature. For constellations already sized around thousands of satellites, even a 20 percent improvement in bits per watt at the amplifier stage translates into measurable reductions in required solar-array area and battery mass. Those mass savings compound across every launch, directly affecting how many additional Earth-observation payloads can ride on the same reusable booster. Over the 2026–2035 window projected by Novaspace, the cumulative effect of such incremental hardware gains will determine whether the $155.9 billion manufacturing forecast can be realized within existing launch economics or whether new capacity constraints appear.
