Making Starship's return fuel on Mars, from an engineering standpoint, is a closed chemical loop that turns local ice and air into methane and oxygen.

The Sabatier reaction feeds carbon dioxide from the atmosphere with hydrogen cracked out of water ice, and it runs at three hundred to four hundred degrees Celsius over a nickel or ruthenium catalyst.

Methane and water come out; you then electrolyze that water to recover the hydrogen and to make the oxygen demanded by a three point six to one oxidizer-to-fuel mix.

A membrane sitting on the reactor pulls water vapor out as it forms, so the reaction keeps driving forward even when solar power blinks, and the same water never leaves the loop.

Once the ice and the air are in reach, the feedstock is effectively free, and cost collapses from an Earth-launch price of roughly one thousand dollars per kilogram down to the electricity for electrolysis and liquefaction.

A full Starship stack still wants roughly one thousand one hundred fifty tonnes of methane per flight, about twenty-two percent of the propellant load.

A back-of-envelope figure for the oxygen that rides with it is about four thousand one hundred tonnes, which puts the whole methalox load on the order of five thousand three hundred tonnes.

Lofting that methane from Earth at a thousand dollars a kilogram would be on the order of one point one five billion dollars in launched mass alone.

That is the magic-wand number: raw Martian air is about ninety-five percent carbon dioxide, the ice is already on the ground, and the idiot index versus Earth-launched methane sits in the ballpark of a thousand to one if you count only energy.

Sabatier needs four hydrogen molecules per methane molecule, then the water you get back returns half of that hydrogen when you split it, so you are not hauling the hydrogen in from Earth.

That loop only yields about two kilograms of oxygen per kilogram of methane, short of the mix the engines want, so extra ice still has to be mined and split — on the order of two thousand tonnes of additional water per flight.

Electrolyzing water takes on the order of fifty kilowatt-hours per kilogram of hydrogen, which means the plant is a power station that happens to make rocket fuel, and it has to live with sunlight that dies every night.

The membrane's job is to keep converting carbon dioxide through those power swings instead of stalling in a wet reactor that has hit equilibrium.

Tests with membrane integration have shown substantial gains in carbon dioxide conversion and long-term stability across a wide range of reaction conditions.

The same water-recycling chemistry also lets you make hydrogen when the solar array is up and coast when it is not, which is what you want for months of surface operations.

Net propellant therefore depends only on accessible local carbon dioxide and ice, not on tanks launched from Earth.
