Orion stays on SLS for the climb off Earth because the engineering reality is a crew stack built around one vehicle's loads, not a capsule you can move to another booster without starting the safety case over.

The crew module, the service module, and the abort tower were designed as a single unit whose structural loads, separation planes, and thermal-protection needs match the Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage adapter.

Swap the launch vehicle and you redesign the abort motors, the ogive fairing panels — those curved nose pieces that take the aerodynamic heat — and the diaphragm that keeps hydrogen gas off Orion, none of which have been qualified on another booster.

That diaphragm exists because SLS's upper stage burns liquid hydrogen, and hydrogen's molecular mass is two, the smallest molecule there is, so it leaks through seals that easily hold kerosene or methane and it can embrittle metal; a barrier is the practical answer.

The same Orion Stage Adapter can host up to seventeen CubeSats in mixed 6U and 12U sizes after the primary stack is clear of the atmosphere — a 6U is roughly ten by twenty by thirty centimeters, built from the textbook ten-centimeter 1U cell.

Starship's payload bay and fairing geometry are different, so an Orion adapter would need new load paths, meaning new routes for force through the structure, new avionics interfaces, and a new abort-trajectory analysis.

NASA's reference documents therefore keep SLS as the Earth-to-orbit stage for Orion and have Starship do the trans-lunar injection — the burn toward the Moon — after the two dock in orbit.

That burn from low Earth orbit takes on the order of three kilometers per second of extra speed, a textbook figure, which is why a high-energy stage or a refueled Starship sits in the architecture at all.

The split keeps the existing abort system and avoids requalifying the crew module for a different vibration spectrum and a different acceleration curve.

The Launch Abort System motors work in milliseconds to pull the crew module clear during ascent, and a jettison motor then throws the tower away once the vehicle is above most of the atmosphere, on the order of a hundred kilometers up.

Those motors and their mounting points were never sized for Starship's thrust curve or vibration spectrum, and the physics of why that matters is a race: the tower has to out-accelerate the booster.

Acceleration is force over mass; a different thrust-versus-time history changes the g-load on the crew and the distance the capsule travels in those same milliseconds.

A booster that spikes hard at ignition needs a tower that pulls harder and sooner; a booster that throttles deep on liquid engines has a smoother curve, and the old clearance analysis does not carry over.

Peak aerodynamic pressure is half the air density times speed squared, so a fatter, slower, or steeper booster hits a different max-q, and the ogive panels are sized for the old number, not the new one.

Vibration is the other trap — structural modes in the stack, the crew couches, and the avionics all sit at particular frequencies, and a new vehicle's spectrum moves those peaks, so you retest rather than assume.

Wave the magic wand and the abort motors are metal plus solid propellant, cheap on paper; the Idiot Index, how many times the finished qualified tower costs over those raw inputs, sits on the order of ten to a hundred because the money is the test campaign that proves those milliseconds, not the machining.

The architecture trades schedule risk for development cost: reuse the proven SLS-Orion stack for the first lunar missions and spend Starship effort on orbital refueling and landing instead of a second crewed-launch certification.

Once Starship shows reliable orbital refueling, the program can ask whether a fully Starship-based path is feasible; until then the near-term path is the two-vehicle solution in those same reference documents.

The service module still supplies power, propulsion, and consumables, and the crew module still houses the astronauts, each element staying optimized for the launch environment it already survived rather than forced through a single-vehicle redesign.
