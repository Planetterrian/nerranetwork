The engineering reality is that low-band spectrum at 800 MHz travels farther through obstacles than higher frequencies already in use.

Pairing the new allocation with existing holdings allows one satellite beam to serve indoor handsets while a ground layer handles dense urban spots.

The same orbital hardware can therefore support both consumer broadband and mobile backhaul without added mass or power beyond current Starship margins.

Ship 40 returned to Starbase after its Flight 13 splashdown in the Indian Ocean.

Recovery teams towed the intact upper stage through the Port of Brownsville channel despite earlier concerns the operation might fail.

Engineers removed several heatshield tiles from the vehicle and flew them again on Flight 14, providing direct physical evidence of tile durability after reentry heating.

Because the stage remained intact rather than exploding on contact with the water, SpaceX obtained data on tile attachment and thermal protection that camera-equipped satellites alone could not supply.

This physical inspection shortens the iteration loop for the heatshield redesign required before repeated booster and ship reuse becomes routine.

Starship upper-stage recovery changes the economics of the entire stack.

When a ship survives splashdown, the cost per flight drops from the raw material price of a new vehicle to the marginal cost of inspection, tile replacement, and refurbishment.

The gap between those two numbers is the Idiot Index for the upper stage.

Flight 13 demonstrated that the current tile attachment method can survive one reentry without total loss, lowering that index.

The next engineering step is to close the remaining distance to zero refurbishment time so that the same ship can fly again within days rather than months.

Achieving that target requires both the tower catch hardware now under construction at Starbase and the orbital refueling demonstration still ahead in the flight test campaign.
