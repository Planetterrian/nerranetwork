### Engineering Deep Dive
Producing methane and oxygen on Mars for Starship return propellant starts with the Sabatier reaction, which combines atmospheric carbon dioxide with hydrogen derived from water ice. The reaction runs at 300–400 °C over a nickel or ruthenium catalyst and yields methane plus water; the water is then electrolyzed to recover the hydrogen and generate the oxygen needed for the 3.6:1 oxidizer-to-fuel ratio. A membrane Sabatier system improves yield by continuously removing the water vapor in situ, allowing the reaction to proceed further toward completion even under intermittent solar power. On Mars the inputs are effectively free once the ice and atmosphere are accessible, collapsing the propellant cost from the Earth-launch price of roughly $1,000 per kilogram down to the energy cost of electrolysis and liquefaction. Scaling the system to supply a full Starship stack requires roughly 1,150 tonnes of methane per flight, which the same closed-loop water recovery can sustain without importing additional hydrogen. The architecture therefore turns local resources into the 22 % methane fraction of the propellant load and removes the need to launch that mass from Earth. Experiments with membrane integration have shown substantial gains in CO2 conversion and long-term stability across a wide range of reaction conditions. The same chemistry that recycles water also supports electrolytic hydrogen production when solar power fluctuates, making the overall loop robust for extended surface operations. Net propellant production therefore depends only on accessible local CO2 and ice rather than Earth-supplied feedstocks.
