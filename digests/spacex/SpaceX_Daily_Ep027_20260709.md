### Engineering Deep Dive
The 36th flight of booster B1067 offers a clear window into the physics of reuse limits. Each prior mission leaves residual thermal and mechanical stress in the tank walls, engine mounts, and interstage structure; the cumulative effect is governed by fatigue crack growth rates under repeated pressure cycles and vibration spectra. By flying the same hardware this many times, SpaceX is effectively measuring how far the original factor-of-safety margins can be consumed before inspection or refurbishment becomes mandatory. 

The practical payoff appears in propellant and labor cost per kilogram delivered. If the raw material and energy cost of building one Falcon 9 first stage is treated as the floor, each additional flight divides that fixed cost across more missions, driving the Idiot Index—the ratio of delivered launch price to raw-material cost—steadily downward. The current record demonstrates that the index has already dropped far enough to make high-cadence Starlink replenishment economically viable without new booster production at the same rate. 

Looking ahead, the open variable is whether the same margins hold once the vehicle begins carrying the heavier payloads or more aggressive trajectories that Starship operations will eventually demand. Continued flights of B1067 and its peers will reveal whether engine bay thermal protection or grid-fin actuator life becomes the binding constraint before the tanks themselves. Data from the post-flight inspection will therefore feed directly into decisions on whether to extend the reuse target beyond 40 flights or introduce targeted design changes for the next block of boosters.
