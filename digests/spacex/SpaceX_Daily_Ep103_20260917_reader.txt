From an engineering standpoint the 1.29-gigawatt target at the Memphis site shows how treating power delivery as an internal design variable rather than an external limit changes the entire cost structure of training clusters.

The facility integrates behind-the-meter generation, electrical conversion, and high-density GPU racks into one thermal-electrical loop so that interconnect distances shrink and signal propagation delays drop.

Raw inputs set a clear floor: natural gas or grid electricity at roughly three to five cents per kilowatt-hour plus the silicon and copper in the racks.

The Idiot Index is the multiplier between those raw inputs and the final cost per effective training token, and that multiplier is driven by interconnect latency, cooling overhead, and utilization rate.

At 1.29 gigawatts the cluster must reject heat at the same rate it consumes power or else sustained utilization falls.

Liquid cooling distribution and conversion efficiency therefore become the binding constraints once power exceeds one gigawatt.

The same thermal rejection limit appears in orbital compute planning, where placing hardware in space removes terrestrial transmission losses but still requires radiators sized to the electrical input.

Each additional reuse cycle on the Falcon 9 booster lowers amortized hardware cost per kilogram to orbit, which directly improves the economics of deploying heavier compute nodes once Starship reaches routine operations.
