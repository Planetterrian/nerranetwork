Full-flow staged combustion in Raptor is today's engineering deep dive, because dumping propellant overboard is the loss this cycle refuses to accept.

In a simpler gas-generator engine, several percent of total flow spins the turbines and then goes overboard, so that mass never makes thrust.

You already paid to tank it, pump it, and carry it, and then you throw it away as warm gas.

Full-flow routes every kilogram of methane and every kilogram of oxygen through the preburners — the small combustors that drive the turbopumps — and then into the main chamber.

Nothing is dumped. Every kilogram is available for thrust.

The closed loop raises specific impulse, which is simply how much push you get from each kilogram of propellant.

It still needs precise control of two separate preburner turbines, and it will damage hardware if mixture ratios drift.

You run two preburners because each propellant has to spin its own pump.

The methane side runs fuel-rich so the turbine gas stays cool enough not to melt the blades.

The oxygen side runs ox-rich, and hot oxygen attacks metal, which is why mixture-ratio control is not a nice-to-have.

Drift too far ox-rich and you start cutting the machine you are trying to fly.

SpaceX chose methalox, methane and liquid oxygen, over denser kerosene or toxic hypergolics because a clean burn lets a booster fly again with little inspection.

Kerosene cracks in hot cooling channels and leaves soot.

Methane is one carbon to four hydrogens, so there is far less carbon to deposit, and that is the first-principles reason a methalox full-flow engine can be inspected less between flights.

That choice cuts the maintenance hours that historically limited how often a first stage could return to flight.

Raptor 3 then pulled the plumbing inside.

External lines, flanges, and heat-shield patches disappeared as turbopump flows were internalized.

Engine mass dropped while sea-level thrust lifted from the earlier one hundred eighty-five tonnes-force class toward two hundred fifty.

Each external pipe you delete is brackets, welds, insulation, and leak checks you no longer carry, and leak checks you no longer perform after landing.

The mass reduction compounds across thirty-three engines on a Super Heavy booster, the first stage.

Less engine mass lowers the propellant load needed for the same delta-v, the change in velocity the vehicle has to produce, and improves the payload fraction delivered to orbit.

Losing a single chamber on that booster costs you on the order of three percent of sea-level thrust, which is the engine-out math that lets a shutdown be a continuation rather than a failure.

The same simplification shortens production time per engine, and that clock is the practical limit on how fast Starship flight rate can rise once tower catch and refueling are routine.

You cannot fly boosters faster than you can deliver engines, no matter how clean the catch looks.

The Bessemer process in eighteen fifty-six made the same move in steel by internalizing the flow and eliminating intermediate steps.

The Idiot Index, the ratio of finished-part cost to raw-material cost, can collapse by an order of magnitude when the redesign hits the dominant loss mechanisms.

For Raptor the index improves from higher thrust per kilogram and from fewer post-flight inspections, which translates into more flights per booster before refurbishment.

Raw metal and propellant are cheap compared with an engine you can only use once, or an engine you have to tear down after every hop.

The cycle also supports the airline-level reliability target SpaceX has set for point-to-point Earth transport, where engine-out tolerance and rapid turnaround are first-order requirements rather than afterthoughts.
