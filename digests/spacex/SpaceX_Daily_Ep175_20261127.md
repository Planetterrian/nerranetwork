## The Counterpoint
Scientists and astronomers remain skeptical that a million-satellite orbital data center network can overcome basic physics limits on radiative cooling in vacuum and the risk of increased orbital collisions. The FCC authorization requires at least one satellite by 2033 and full deployment by 2040, yet experts note that heat dissipation and launch cadence constraints make the timeline unrealistic without major breakthroughs. Resolution would require demonstrated on-orbit cooling performance and collision-avoidance data from early prototypes. ### Engineering Deep Dive
The Starmind satellites authorized by the FCC are sized at roughly 70 meters by 20 meters when solar arrays and radiators deploy, far larger than current Starlink vehicles. This scale is driven by the need to capture continuous solar power in sun-synchronous orbits while radiating waste heat from thousands of GPUs without water or atmosphere. Laser links to the existing Starlink constellation handle primary data return, with Ka-band reserved only for backup telemetry, reducing the mass and power budget otherwise spent on large RF antennas. The six approved orbital shells sit between 500 and 1,002 km, with three at 26–32° inclination and three near-polar to maximize sunlight exposure and minimize eclipse periods that would otherwise force larger batteries. At one million units the constellation would exceed the total number of satellites launched in history by nearly two orders of magnitude, so the design treats individual spacecraft as replaceable nodes rather than repairable assets; a failed unit is simply deorbited and swapped by a later Starship flight. The first operational satellite must fly by November 2033, with 100,000 on station by 2035, forcing an early focus on manufacturing throughput and automated deployment sequences that have no direct precedent at this volume.

The radiative cooling approach removes the need for water loops that dominate ground data-center thermal design, yet it introduces a hard limit set by the Stefan-Boltzmann law: heat rejection scales with the fourth power of surface temperature and the effective radiating area. Deploying 70-by-20-meter radiator surfaces per satellite multiplies that area dramatically compared with compact Starlink buses, but the vacuum environment still requires the panels to run hotter than equivalent water-cooled chips to reject the same load. Sun-synchronous orbits at 97–99° inclination keep the arrays illuminated for longer fractions of each revolution, cutting battery mass that would otherwise be needed for eclipse periods in lower-inclination shells. The laser cross-links to the operational Starlink fleet avoid the mass penalty of high-power Ka-band transmitters while leveraging an existing network whose 11,100-plus satellites already provide global coverage. Because the FCC order treats each satellite as a consumable, the economics hinge on Starship’s projected launch cost per kilogram rather than on-orbit servicing; a single failed AI1 unit represents a write-off whose replacement cost is amortized across the constellation’s aggregate compute output. Early prototypes will therefore need to prove both thermal rejection margins and autonomous fault isolation before the 2033 milestone forces volume production.
