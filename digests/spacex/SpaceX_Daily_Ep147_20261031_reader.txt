Sizing the AI1 orbital compute satellite from an engineering standpoint starts with heat rejection, not with the chips.

Each AI1 bird carries one hundred ten square meters of deployable liquid radiators that dump heat from both faces while flying knife-edge to the Sun, so the panel area facing the Sun is only the thickness of the sheet and absorbed sunlight stays near zero.

An ammonia loop with redundant pumps rejects about fourteen hundred watts across each square meter of panel.

Ammonia is the working fluid because water would freeze in a shadowed tube — it freezes at zero Celsius — while ammonia stays liquid down to about minus seventy-eight.

That radiator set supports a sustained one hundred twenty kilowatt compute load and one hundred fifty kilowatt peaks with no duty-cycle throttling.

The payload matches a single Nvidia GB300 rack of seventy-two GPUs, flying near six hundred kilometers at about seventy kilowatts per ton.

The layout builds on Starlink V3 thermal design but grows radiator area because Starship can lift the extra mass at low marginal cost.

In orbit the sink is deep space at about three kelvin, and a radiator follows the Stefan-Boltzmann law: heat scales with area, emissivity, and temperature to the fourth power.

Dual-sided panels at that flux imply a back-of-envelope skin near seventy degrees Celsius if emissivity is around zero point nine — hot to the touch, but inside a normal ammonia-loop range.

Panel area times that flux is about one hundred fifty-four kilowatts of thermal headroom, which is why the peaks do not force a duty cycle.

On the ground the same load wants air-cooled chillers or evaporative towers whose power and water grow with every extra rack.

A typical facility power-usage effectiveness, total site power over compute power, near one point three means about thirty percent of the electricity is cooling that simply vanishes when you radiate to space.

Treat the satellite as a rack that dumps waste heat at the radiation limit instead of fighting it with compressors, and the cooling plant disappears.

The same mass budget deploys a seventy-meter wingspan for solar arrays collecting about two hundred fifty watts from each square meter of cells, plus the radiator panels, still inside a single Starship payload envelope.

Magic-wand the raw inputs — aluminum sheet, ammonia, silicon — and they are cheap.

The idiot index, how many times the finished article costs over those inputs, sits on the order of twenty to fifty because you are buying pointing, redundant pumps, and a leak-tight space loop, not metal.

Chip suppliers can change while thermal and power stay fixed, which cuts redesign when GPU generations move.

That continuous load at the stated density is a back-of-envelope mass near one point seven tons for the compute core, so the limits shift from cooling plant to launch cadence and orbital slots.
