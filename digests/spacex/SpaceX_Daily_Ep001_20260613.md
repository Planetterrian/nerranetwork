### The Engineering Angle
The IPO prospectus reveals that Starlink already covers two-thirds of revenue and is profitable while the launch business subsidizes Starship development at a loss. This split shows how reuse economics on Falcon 9—now past 650 flights—generate the cash that funds the next architecture, rather than any single breakthrough in Raptor thrust or propellant transfer. Public markets will therefore price the company on quarterly subscriber adds and booster turnaround times, metrics that directly measure whether the cost-per-kilogram curve continues to fall.  

Reaching one thousand Starships per year at Gigabay requires not only factory floor space but also a closed-loop supply chain for engines, tanks, and avionics that has never been executed at that rate. The prospectus lists AI infrastructure and Starship as explicit uses of proceeds, implying that orbital compute satellites and lunar cargo missions are now competing for the same production slots. Investors can watch pad turnaround data and engine test counts as leading indicators of whether that factory output will actually translate into flight rate.  

Orbital refueling remains the gating item for any revenue beyond low Earth orbit, yet the same dual-class structure that gives Musk control also means the timeline for that demonstration is set internally rather than by quarterly earnings pressure. If the first public earnings miss on launch margins, the market will have a concrete signal on whether the loss-making side of the business is narrowing or widening. That single number will tell shareholders more about Starship progress than any static-fire headline.
