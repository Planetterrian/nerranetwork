Carbon-fibre tanks sit at the engineering angle this week, because a kilogram saved in the wall is a kilogram you can sell as payload, a trade NASASpaceflight’s reporting on Neutron keeps forcing back onto the table.

A rocket tank is a pressure vessel, and the hoop stress in the wall is pressure times radius divided by thickness.

Longitudinal stress is half that, so the cylinder wants fibres mostly around the hoop, while the domes want paths that run toward the polar bosses.

Double the diameter and, for the same pressure, you need roughly twice the wall thickness, so dry mass climbs unless strength-to-weight climbs with it.

High-grade fibre beats aluminium and steel on tensile strength per kilogram, which is the entire reason a composite tank looks good on a mass budget.

Aluminium sits around two point seven grams per cubic centimeter; a cured carbon-fibre laminate is on the order of one point six.

Fibre tensile strength typically lands in the three-to-seven gigapascal range, versus a few hundred megapascals for common aluminium tank alloys.

Those raw inputs are the magic-wand number: fibre plus resin plus heat and pressure in a cure cycle.

The finished tank costs many times that, an Idiot Index that in aerospace composites often lands on the order of ten to fifty, because autoclaves, scrap, inspection, and rejected plies eat the savings.

What actually decides whether mass savings reach orbit is fibre volume fraction, the share of the laminate that is fibre rather than resin.

Too high, and the part will not consolidate without voids; too low, and extra resin adds weight and cuts stiffness.

A typical shop target sits around sixty percent fibre by volume, a back-of-envelope figure chased with layup tension and resin flow.

A one percent void fraction is not a cosmetic flaw; it is a stress raiser, and under cyclic pressure it becomes a crack starter.

Automated placement machines set tows on programmed paths so the wall can take ascent loads, internal pressure, and the buckling and shear of a reusable first stage.

Reuse is the extra problem metal tanks already understand: landing and boost-back put the skin in compression, so buckling stiffness matters as much as burst strength.

Higher-modulus grades help that buckling resistance, yet they cost more and snap more easily in handling, so a dropped tow can scrap a whole panel.

Rocket Lab’s Neutron delay shows how the same material that worked on Electron misbehaves when the tank gets wider and longer on a medium-lift first stage.

Geodesic paths that sat nicely on a small dome start bridging and wrinkling on a large one; tension that was fine at Electron scale can starve or flood the resin at Neutron scale.

Cure temperature and pressure, often in the one hundred twenty to one hundred eighty degree Celsius band for epoxies, set interlaminar shear strength.

Scale the geometry and those profiles stop being interchangeable, because the thermal mass of a big tool soaks heat at a different rate than a small one.

Cryogenic propellant adds another cut: resin and fibre shrink at different rates, and repeated cold soaks can microcrack the matrix even when room-temperature coupons looked clean.

Metal polar bosses still have to meet the composite, and that joint is where leak paths hide if the ply drop-offs are sloppy.

Post-cure inspection, ultrasound or thermography, is the step that decides whether a tank is flight hardware or scrap, and it is also where the Idiot Index quietly grows.

Hand layup still handles awkward shapes, but it injects variability that automation is built to kill.

Fibre orientation you can see during placement is the directional strength map, and it has to balance hoop tension against shear unique to a booster that flies more than once.

Close the process window on tension, flow, cure, and inspection, and the mass savings show up as payload; miss it, and the extra safety margin eats every kilogram you thought you had bought.
