The Super Heavy booster catch, from an engineering standpoint, is a decision to stop flying landing legs that would have to take residual propellant and the structural loads of a full ascent and a full return.

Those legs would add hundreds of kilograms of dry mass, the vehicle's unfueled weight, taken straight out of payload on every flight, and they would have to be built, fitted, and qualified even though grid fins — folding steering surfaces — already handle descent control. Separate landing pads would have to be designed and qualified too.

After the Super Heavy booster separates and flips, grid fins steer during a three-minute engine-off coast and point the vehicle at the tower without spending propellant on attitude. Then thirteen of the thirty-three Raptors, the booster's engines, relight for the braking burn, and three stay running to hold position beside the tower.

Throttle those three until thrust matches weight, and the grid fins handle fine attitude; that pairing has shown up across multiple flight tests.

Early flight data showed the catch points below the fins can take the load once the vehicle is hovering, and structural testing on the flight article confirmed those points and the surrounding structure handle expected loads without extra reinforcement that would put mass back on.

The tower arms close under the catch points once the booster is stable, then lower it onto the launch mount for inspection and refueling. Because the booster is already at the mount, the cycle drops from a multi-hour haul and restack off a separate pad to a direct lower-and-refuel sequence that targets under one hour, which does not work if the vehicle has to come from another landing site.

The same arms can stack the Ship upper stage, so the pad does not need a second stacking system.

The sequence trades extra control work in the last seconds of flight for fewer parts, less dry mass, and a ground cycle that never leaves the mount.
