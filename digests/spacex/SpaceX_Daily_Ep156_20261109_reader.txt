If you want the engineering angle on why Super Heavy carries thirty-three Raptors, start with the mass it has to lift.

The booster is a roughly five-thousand-ton vehicle that has to leave the pad with engine-out margin, the ability to lose an engine and still fly, then shut down a subset of chambers for boostback, the burn that turns it toward the tower, and for the landing burn, without losing control authority.

Each Raptor three makes between two hundred thirty and two hundred fifty metric tons of thrust at sea level, so the full set supplies the eighteen million pounds required at liftoff.

A back-of-envelope figure: thirty-three times two hundred forty metric tons is about seven thousand nine hundred metric tons of thrust on a five-thousand-ton stack, a liftoff thrust-to-weight on the order of one point six.

Many smaller chambers avoid the combustion instability and fabrication limits that constrained single-chamber designs such as Saturn V's F-1, which made on the order of one point five million pounds each, five of them on that first stage.

High-volume production of smaller engines is how per-unit cost falls while turbomachinery and chamber materials iterate quickly.

Super Heavy is nine meters wide and seventy-two point three meters tall in Block three, with liquid oxygen and liquid methane tanks split by a common bulkhead, one shared wall, the same approach Saturn V used on the S-II and S-IVB stages, keeping the center of mass manageable on ascent and return.

That common wall saves the mass of a second dome, at the price of more plumbing and a harder tank-to-tank inspection path, which is the trade.

Raptor is the first full-flow staged combustion engine to reach flight, both turbopumps driven by preburner gas, burning methane and oxygen at six hundred fifty kilograms per second.

Thirty-three at full throttle is on the order of twenty-one thousand kilograms of propellant every second.

Vacuum versions reach two hundred fifty-eight to two hundred seventy-five metric tons.

A landing burn cannot use the full ring, because on a light booster that thrust would be many times the remaining weight, so the design shuts down to a subset and keeps control authority.

The layout trades added plumbing for the ability to lose multiple engines and still complete the profile, which is why thirty-three became the baseline rather than a few larger units.
