Falcon 9 first-stage recovery is the engineering angle on a single Merlin performing what engineers call a hoverslam.

The engine loses the ability to modulate if it goes below roughly forty percent throttle, so the booster has to light the landing burn already at forty to seventy percent while it is still going fast, and whatever impulse is left has to bring speed to zero at the exact instant of touchdown.

That single-engine floor is why the profile has to be continuous: there is no lower setting to drop to in the middle of the burn.

If the minimum throttle still pushes harder than the booster weighs, you cannot hover; you can only slam, matching leftover energy against leftover distance so both hit zero together.

Super Heavy does not try to do that with one engine; it lights thirteen Raptors to cancel most of the speed, then cuts to the three center engines for the final approach.

Each of those three still modulates between roughly forty percent and full thrust, which gives the Super Heavy booster a near-hover the tower arms need for capture.

Going from thirteen engines down to three is on the order of a fourfold cut in thrust, the coarse brake, and then the remaining trio handles the fine control.

Too many engines at the end would keep even a forty percent floor too strong for a hover; three engines bring that floor into the band the arms can work with.

Three engines can also be throttled a little unequally, so the Super Heavy booster can correct a small lean that a single Merlin, thrusting only along its own axis, cannot.

Falcon 9 therefore keeps landing legs, because a one-engine hoverslam leaves little margin for being in the wrong place, while Super Heavy deletes the legs and saves the mass that would otherwise fly to orbit every time.

The catch hardware now has to do the last bit of positioning the legs used to do.
