The power bottleneck those new cells are built to break is this episode's engineering deep dive, because orbital compute is an electricity problem before it is a software problem.

Each satellite has to generate and store enough electricity to run inference or training accelerators while surviving the thermal cycling and radiation of low Earth orbit, and heterojunction stacks do that by layering materials with different band gaps so the cell captures a wider slice of the solar spectrum and throws off less waste heat that would otherwise demand a heavier radiator.

Above the atmosphere the solar constant is about thirteen hundred sixty-six watts per square meter; a typical terrestrial reference spectrum is on the order of one thousand watts per square meter, so space offers more raw light and no clouds, then the orbit takes a cut back.

A low Earth orbit lasts roughly ninety minutes, and the vehicle spends on the order of thirty minutes of that in eclipse, which means the array must cover the daylight compute load and still top up batteries before the next shadow.

Silicon's band gap sits near one point one electron volts, so a single-junction silicon cell dumps a large infrared tail as heat; ordinary silicon cells often sit near twenty percent conversion, as a back-of-envelope figure, and a heterojunction stack that converts roughly twenty-five percent of the light still turns about three-quarters of every incoming watt into heat that can leave a spacecraft only by radiation.

Raise efficiency a few points and you cut radiator area, cut structure, and raise specific power — watts per kilogram — which is the figure that decides whether extra accelerators are worth the array that feeds them.

Fewer defects from an integrated process raise yield, and yield is what shrinks the Idiot Index, how many times the finished, space-qualified cell costs over the magic-wand inputs of a silicon wafer, aluminum, and silver paste.

For space solar that index has historically sat on the order of ten to a hundred, because inspection, radiation qualification, and broken wafers are paid for by the cells that survive; drive the gap down and the marginal cost of each additional watt in orbit falls toward those raw materials, which is what makes a sustained orbital compute cluster look like a manufactured commodity instead of a one-off demonstration.
