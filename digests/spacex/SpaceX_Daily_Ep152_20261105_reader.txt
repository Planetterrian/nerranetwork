Hidden inside that vendor pick is the engineering angle: how many bits you can push through a tower before the utility drop says stop. A Massive MIMO panel is a phased array, so each extra transmit path lets you add the same radio wave in phase toward one user and cancel it toward another, and one frequency channel can be reused across people standing in different directions.

Earlier macros often ran eight transmit and eight receive paths, then thirty-two and thirty-two; denser arrays form narrower, higher-gain beams without asking the regulator for more megahertz.

For a mid-band carrier near two gigahertz, the wavelength is about fifteen centimeters and half-wavelength spacing sits around seven and a half centimeters, so a few-dozen-element panel still fits on a face a couple of meters on a side.

A back-of-envelope array gain is ten times the base-ten log of the element count, about eighteen decibels at this scale versus about nine on an eight-path radio — roughly eight times the power density in the wanted beam.

Integrating radio-frequency processing and latency-sensitive physical-layer functions into one compact set of boxes cuts the discrete enclosures that each need power and cooling, which is the kilowatt-hour lever across a national footprint.

If that saves even five hundred watts at a site — a rough estimate, not a vendor spec — it is often the difference between the existing utility drop and a new grid interconnection.

The Idiot Index on a weatherproof radio can sit around ten, meaning the finished unit costs on the order of ten times the silicon, copper, and aluminum inside it, because filters, calibration, and certification dominate.

The magic wand number is kilowatt-hours at the tower: even if electricity were free, heat and the local drop would still cap the radios you can hang. Low-band wavelengths stretch to a meter or more, punch through walls, and let you build fewer macros while mid-band carries capacity. Satellite backhaul can reach towers that would otherwise wait on fiber.

Light-time from low Earth orbit is on the order of a couple of milliseconds, so a consistent orbital-to-ground handoff is a protocol problem more than a physics problem.

A radar engineer would recognize the same trick: you do not make the transmitter stronger, you make the beam narrower, so the watt that used to heat the horizon now lands on one handset. Spatial degrees of freedom got cheaper than buying new spectrum.

That is also why backhaul at the site matters: a tower that cannot get fiber can still take a radio if the power drop and the link both exist, and the power drop is the one that permitting actually counts.
