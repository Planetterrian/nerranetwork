The engineering reality behind the final minute scrub centers on the Falcon 9's automated
start-up sequence, which begins roughly 60 seconds before liftoff and hands primary
control to the flight computer.
At that point, the vehicle checks propellant pressures, valve positions, and engine health
in a rapid cascade that must all report nominal before the hold-down clamps release.
A single out of tolerance reading, whether from a sensor glitch, a valve response time,
or a ground system handoff, triggers an immediate abort to protect the booster and the 21
Northrop Grumman satellites on board.
The sequence is deliberately conservative because the same first stage booster, B1103, is already
on its sixth flight and will attempt its 37th landing at LZ4 if the mission flies.
That reuse-driven reliability requirement forces tighter margins on every ground check than an expendable vehicle would tolerate.
The net effect is that a scrubbed attempt today simply shifts the same hardware stack to the next available window without consuming a flight article,
preserving the cadence that has already delivered three missions in 13 hours earlier this week.
The Falcon 9 first stage uses 9 Merlin 1D engines that must reach stable chamber pressure
within a narrow time window before the vehicle commits to flight.
Each engine carries its own set of turbo pump speed sensors and propellant valve position
encoders that feed the flight computer in parallel.
When any parameter deviates beyond preset limits, the computer issues an immediate hold and
the ground systems vent residual pressure.
This layered check prevents a partial ignition or valve misalignment from propagating into
a more serious anomaly once the vehicle is committed.
The approach trades a small amount of schedule margin for the ability to reuse the same booster
dozens of times without cumulative damage from marginal starts.
