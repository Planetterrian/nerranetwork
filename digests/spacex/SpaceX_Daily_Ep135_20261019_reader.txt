The Gruyère Space Program's Colibri hop is, from an engineering standpoint, a closed-loop landing solved at laboratory scale.

Swiss students flew a one hundred five metre reusable hop on a one point two kilonewton bipropellant engine they built for under two hundred fifty thousand Swiss francs.

Their in-house F-100 engine took a vehicle of roughly one hundred kilograms through a sixty-second flight that climbed, translated thirty metres, and settled back on the pad.

On a back-of-envelope figure, that thrust is only about twenty percent above the vehicle's weight, so throttle, guidance, and propellant management dominate.

A Super Heavy booster or a Falcon 9 first stage still has to survive hypersonic reentry, fire boostback and entry burns after flying above one hundred kilometres, and hold multi-engine control through staging.

A pad hop at tens of metres per second sits far from return speeds above Mach five, with heating that can run past one megawatt on every square metre, on engines that already pushed thousands of kilonewtons over minutes of ascent.

The student stack stayed cheap because structure, avionics, and engine were laboratory scale with almost no qualification testing.

Scaling those jobs to a booster returning from orbit multiplies material and certification cost by factors reuse programs are still compressing.

The Idiot Index is how many times the finished booster costs over its raw metal, propellant, and electronics.

For this hop a rough figure sits on the order of two or three. For orbital reuse it runs tens of times those inputs, thanks to thermal protection, reentry structure, and requalifying every engine and valve after each flight.

Reuse twenty or thirty times is what amortizes that bill.

Colibri shows the control loop is solvable at small scale; the remaining distance is orbital-return energy and a manufacturing cadence that makes the economics close.

That reading is wrong only if a similarly cheap effort recovers a vehicle from orbit, or if reentry physics proves trivial.
