### Engineering Deep Dive
Full-flow staged combustion in the Raptor engine routes all propellant through the preburners before the main chamber, eliminating the gas-generator exhaust loss that wastes several percent of total flow in simpler cycles. This closed-loop approach raises specific impulse by keeping every kilogram of methane and oxygen available for thrust, but it demands precise control of two separate preburner turbines and raises the risk of thermal damage if mixture ratios drift. SpaceX chose methalox over denser kerosene or toxic hypergolics because the clean burn allows rapid reuse with minimal inspection, directly cutting the maintenance hours that historically limited booster flight rates. The Raptor 3 redesign removed most external plumbing and heat-shield elements by internalizing turbopump flows, dropping engine mass while lifting sea-level thrust from the earlier 185 tf class toward 250 tf. That mass reduction compounds across 33 engines on a Super Heavy booster, lowering the propellant load needed for the same delta-v and improving the payload fraction delivered to orbit. The same simplification also shortens production time per engine, which is the practical limit on how quickly Starship flight rate can rise once tower catch and refueling are routine. Historical parallels to the Bessemer process show how internalizing flows and eliminating intermediate steps can collapse the Idiot Index—the ratio of finished-part cost to raw-material cost—by an order of magnitude when the redesign targets the dominant loss mechanisms. In Raptor’s case the index improvement comes from both higher thrust per kilogram and fewer post-flight inspections, directly translating into more flights per booster before refurbishment. The cycle choice also supports the airline-level reliability target SpaceX has set for point-to-point Earth transport, where engine-out tolerance and rapid turnaround become first-order requirements rather than afterthoughts.
