SpaceX said on X that a proposed Texas data-center abatement would have given the county predictable revenue through payments in lieu of taxes — a fixed substitute for the usual property bill — while still backing further investment. Without the agreement, property owners now face greater year-to-year tax-rate swings as computing equipment depreciates.

Under the state’s no-new-revenue tax framework, rising property values generally drive tax rates down, but rapid depreciation of expensive hardware creates significant annual fluctuations.

Worth keeping an eye on: Texas commissioners rejected a public hearing on a one hundred percent, ten-year tax abatement for SpaceX’s planned twenty-four billion dollar Starbase data center. The proposal had covered turbines and switchgear, and alternative structures that balance investment incentives with stable county income are what remaining local talks would have to solve.

Data-center power systems are this episode’s engineering deep dive, because silicon and turbines do not live on the same clock. Computing gear is written off in three to five years, while the gas turbines and grid tie-ins around it are built to run twenty to thirty.

Take five years of accelerators against a twenty-year plant: five divided by twenty is one-quarter, and each new generation of graphics processing units, or GPUs, replaces the last before the buildings have lived even that quarter.

If the tax code treats assessed value as steady or climbing, a fast drop in the servers’ book value still jerks the rate around even when output does not change, and as clusters grow the gap widens.

Spreading the county’s expected take across the project’s life avoids letting each year’s appraisal reset the rate. Skip that conversion and the tax base shrinks every year as servers and GPUs get swapped, forcing rate moves that can exceed ten percent year over year.

It also blocked the opposite swing: if values climbed faster than planned, the county’s windfall would have been capped while the promised amounts still arrived.

Lift that same megawatt into orbit and cooling stops being an air problem. Compute above the atmosphere sheds fans and water, then pays in solar-array area and radiator mass.

One megawatt from a ground turbine sits on roughly zero point two square meters of pad; putting that same megawatt on orbit takes tens of square meters of array and radiator, on the order of a hundred times the ground footprint if you divide those tens by zero point two.

The trade only closes when the cost to loft a kilogram drops below land, water, and a grid hookup on the ground, the job Starship’s catch-and-reuse loop is built to do. Each successful tower catch of a Super Heavy booster or a Ship lowers the cost of lifting the next megawatt of orbital compute, cutting at the extra area that still keeps these plants on Earth.
