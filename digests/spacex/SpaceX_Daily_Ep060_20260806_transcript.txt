From an engineering standpoint catching the ship upper stage on the tower introduces a fundamentally different control problem than recovering the super heavy booster.
The ship lacks the booster's eight sea level raptors and their high thrust vectoring authority.
So residual propellant margins after deorbit must be allocated with tighter precision to keep touchdown velocities inside the tower arms capture envelope.
Autogenous pressurization during any final propellant settling burn adds another variable.
Ullage gas temperatures directly affect the density of the remaining liquid shifting the vehicle's center of mass by centimeters that the catch arms must accommodate.
Because the ship's dry mass is roughly one fifth that of the booster its structural stiffness under lateral loads from the tower arms is lower.
Designers must therefore trade between arm compliance and the risk of inducing bending modes that could crack tiles already stressed by reentry.
The first principles limit here is the available impulse from cold gas or remaining RCS thrusters once the main engines are shut down.
Any excess propellant carried for margin directly reduces payload to orbit so the catch sequence must be validated with the smallest possible propellant overhead.
