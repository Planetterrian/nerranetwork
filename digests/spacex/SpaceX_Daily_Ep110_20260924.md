### Top News

1. **SpaceX gains access to Pentagon orbital tracking data**
   SpaceX has reached an agreement with the U.S. Space Force that allows the company to use the Pentagon’s most detailed classified data on objects in space. The arrangement also gives the military access to tracking information gathered through SpaceX’s Starlink network. Under the deal, SpaceX is providing data from Stargaze, a system that relies on about 30,000 cameras mounted on Starlink satellites to monitor activity in low Earth orbit. In exchange, the company can use classified U.S. government tracking data to refine its assessment of possible orbital collisions involving its Starlink and Starshield spacecraft. SpaceX operates roughly 11,000 Starlink and Starshield satellites. The company says its fleet carries out around 20,000 orbital adjustments each day, with about 1,000 of those maneuvers related to collision avoidance. The agreement limits SpaceX’s use of the Pentagon information to internal purposes and bars the company from applying it for commercial benefit. Jon Herman, senior director for Starlink at SpaceX, told Breaking Defense that Starlink satellites have limitations in detecting smaller objects, making radar information a valuable addition to Stargaze. ### Engineering Deep Dive
From an engineering standpoint the Stargaze camera array on Starlink satellites represents a distributed sensor network that trades individual aperture size for massive spatial coverage. Each of the roughly 30,000 cameras is small and low-power yet collectively they generate a near-real-time map of low Earth orbit objects down to sizes radar often misses. The raw input cost is dominated by the silicon sensors, optics, and the incremental power draw on satellites already carrying solar arrays sized for communications. Once the images are downlinked or processed locally the system feeds directly into the daily collision-avoidance pipeline that already executes about 1,000 avoidance maneuvers. Adding classified radar tracks from the Pentagon closes the gap on smaller debris that optical cameras struggle to detect at range, lowering the false-negative rate without requiring larger individual apertures or heavier satellites. The architecture therefore collapses the classic sensor trade-off between coverage and resolution by turning the constellation itself into the instrument, an approach whose marginal cost per additional data point falls as more satellites reach orbit. The marginal cost per additional data point continues to drop as constellation density rises because each new satellite adds both communications capacity and sensing coverage without separate dedicated hardware.
