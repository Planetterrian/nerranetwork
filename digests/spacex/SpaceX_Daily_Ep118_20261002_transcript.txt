Engineering deep dive on why placing tensor processing units on small dedicated satellites changes
the cost and performance math for orbital compute. In low-earth orbit, a satellite receives
near continuous sunlight, delivering up to 8 times the solar energy per square meter,
compared with most terrestrial sites.
The Google prototype therefore carries oversized radiators, sized for vacuum heat rejection.
A constraint that does not exist when air or liquid cooling is available on the ground.
If the raw material cost of the solar arrays and radiators is treated as the magic wand baseline,
line, the finished satellite still carries an idiot index several times higher because
of radiation-hardening, attitude control, and laser cross-links required for data return.
That multiplier shrinks only when launch costs fall enough that an entire constellation
can be refreshed every few years instead of designed for decade-long life.
The same physics explains why SpaceX's own Star-Mind concept pairs large solar arrays
with radiators on each AI-1 satellite, targeting 120 kilowatts average compute power.
Continuous solar input removes the terrestrial grid bottleneck,
but vacuum heat rejection remains the binding limit on compute density per kilogram.
Early data from the current test will therefore directly inform
whether 120 kilowatt-class nodes can be scaled to thousands of units,
without exceeding acceptable mass or power budgets
for the planned 1 million satellite constellation. The 1.2 gigawatt ground plant under construction
