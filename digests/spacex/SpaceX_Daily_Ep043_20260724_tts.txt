From an engineering standpoint, launch commit criteria for orbital-class vehicles rest on a narrow set of atmospheric variables that directly affect structural loads and control authority.

Wind shear, lightning probability, and upper-level winds each impose hard limits because the vehicle must maintain a stable trajectory while its engines deliver maximum thrust through the dense lower atmosphere.

Tropical Storm Bertha's approach forces a multi-day stand-down because even modest increases in cross-range wind can exceed the gimbal authority margins designed into the booster's flight control system.

The decision to scrub therefore protects both the test article and the ground infrastructure from an unrecoverable trajectory deviation.

Starship's current weather tolerance is set by the same first-principles trade that shaped its tank geometry and engine count.

Larger propellant loads give the vehicle enough impulse to reach the required velocity even after a late-day scrub recovery, yet they also increase the mass that must be steered through gusts.

The design therefore accepts a narrower launch envelope in exchange for simpler tank manufacturing and higher payload fraction once the weather window opens.

This choice keeps the Idiot Index—the ratio of finished vehicle cost to raw propellant and metal—lower than a more weather-hardened but heavier architecture would allow.

The raw inputs of stainless steel sheet, liquid oxygen, and methane set a floor cost that reuse and rapid iteration aim to approach, but the added mass margin for weather resilience would raise that floor by increasing both material volume and the energy needed to lift it.
