### Top News

1. **Elon Musk detailed Starship V4 dimensions and performance targets**
   The next version will stretch to roughly 70 meters, a 40 percent length increase that adds more than 60 percent propellant capacity. Engine count rises to nine with three additional Raptor-Vacuum units and higher-thrust V4 engines. SpaceX expects V3 to reach about 100 metric tons to orbit while future versions, potentially V4, target 200 metric tons. Musk stated a V4 tanker could deliver more than 200 tons of propellant per flight, reducing the number of lunar refueling launches from around 22 to five or six. A NASA safety panel member had estimated around 22 tanker flights would be needed for a lunar mission, while Musk has countered that the number would be closer to six to eight with current vehicles. The larger V4 tanker capacity narrows that gap further by allowing each flight to deliver substantially more propellant. SpaceX already advertises cargo flights to the lunar surface starting no earlier than 2028 at $100 million per metric ton, with a Starship lander able to put up to 100 tons on the surface. Musk first said in August 2025 that V4 would add three engines to a significantly longer ship and fly in 2027. Sunday’s post supplies the most specific dimensions released so far. ### Engineering Deep Dive
The F-1 engine remains the most powerful single-chamber liquid rocket motor ever flown, producing 6.77 MN of sea-level thrust. Combustion instability during its development forced extensive redesigns and testing that ultimately limited further scaling of single-chamber designs. Modern engines such as Raptor avoid that instability by using smaller chambers that are easier to stabilize, throttle, and replace after a flight. Clustering 33 of these engines on Super Heavy already exceeds 80 MN total thrust while preserving engine-out capability and rapid reusability. The approach trades peak single-chamber performance for manufacturing simplicity, higher specific impulse, and the ability to iterate hardware between flights rather than between programs. Apollo demonstrated one-shot lunar capability with five F-1 engines; today’s clustered, reusable architecture supports sustained orbital refueling and higher flight rates. The raw-material cost of the propellant and metal in a single Raptor is a small fraction of the finished engine price, and reuse is the primary lever collapsing that Idiot Index. RD-170 family engines generate more total thrust than the F-1 but require four chambers precisely to manage the same combustion instability that plagued early F-1 testing. Raptor sea-level variants produce between 230 and 250 tonnes of thrust while vacuum-optimized versions reach 258 to 275 tonnes. This modular sizing lets SpaceX build, test, and fly individual engines at rates impossible with a single massive chamber. The resulting cluster on Super Heavy delivers more than double the thrust of Saturn V while enabling engine-out tolerance and post-flight inspection. Reusability further reduces the effective cost per flight because the same engines return and fly again with minimal refurbishment. The design choice directly supports the propellant-cost target below $500,000 per Starship flight once payload capacity exceeds 200 tons.
