### Top News
1. **FAA Authorizes A Wider Swath For Starship Ops**
   The Federal Aviation Administration approved an expanded airspace corridor for Starship test flights originating from Starbase. This change directly supports higher-energy trajectories needed for orbital refueling demonstrations and future lunar missions. The authorization reduces the need for repeated temporary flight restrictions over the Gulf of Mexico. Engineers at SpaceX can now plan trajectories that keep the Ship upper stage on a path compatible with tanker rendezvous profiles. Next steps include integrating the new corridor into the Flight 14 mission timeline. The wider ground track gives mission designers more flexibility when selecting ascent profiles that preserve propellant margins for later rendezvous operations. Source: [aviationweek.com](https://aviationweek.com/space/operations-safety/faa-authorizes-wider-swath-starship-ops)

2. **Historic All-Female Spacewalk: Earth from SpaceX Dragon**
   Two astronauts conducted the first all-female spacewalk while docked to the International Space Station aboard a SpaceX Dragon spacecraft. The crew performed external maintenance tasks on the station's exterior while the Dragon capsule remained attached as the primary return vehicle. Live video feeds showed Earth passing below during the extravehicular activity. Dragon's environmental control systems maintained cabin pressure and power throughout the event. The spacecraft served as the stable base for the spacewalk, supplying resources and a reliable hatch interface for the crew. Source: [cw39.com](https://cw39.com/video/historic-all-female-spacewalk-earth-from-spacex-dragon/12129845/)

### Engineering Deep Dive
The FAA's expanded Starship corridor changes the energy profile available for upper-stage trajectories. By widening the allowed ground track over the Gulf, mission planners gain additional delta-v margin for the Ship upper stage without violating range-safety constraints. This margin directly supports the propellant-transfer sequences required for orbital refueling, where the tanker must match velocity and position with the target vehicle within a narrow time window. The corridor also reduces the need for last-minute trajectory adjustments that previously consumed onboard propellant reserves. From a first-principles standpoint, the raw input cost of that extra propellant is simply the mass of liquid oxygen and methane loaded at the pad.

Every kilogram saved through better trajectory freedom lowers the Idiot Index of the overall flight by shrinking the gap between delivered payload and total propellant mass. The corridor approval therefore functions as a systems-level optimization that trades regulatory volume for reduced propellant overhead. In practice this means the Ship upper stage can carry a larger fraction of its propellant load specifically for the rendezvous and transfer burns rather than for corrective maneuvers. The physics constraint remains the same: the tanker and target must close the relative velocity vector while both vehicles are in the same orbital plane and within communication range. Any extra propellant margin translates directly into longer allowable rendezvous search times or higher probability of successful docking.

The next visible test will be whether Flight 14 can exploit the new envelope to demonstrate a full rendezvous profile without exceeding thermal or structural limits on the Ship. Engineers will watch the actual propellant residuals after the initial orbit insertion burn to confirm the corridor delivered the expected savings. If those residuals meet projections, the program gains a clearer path toward repeated tanker flights that keep the overall stack mass budget inside the limits of a single Raptor 3 cluster. The corridor change therefore moves the refueling demonstration from a theoretical trajectory study into an executable mission profile with measurable margins.
