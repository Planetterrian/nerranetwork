Engineering deep dive on why placing tensor processing units on small dedicated satellites changes the cost and performance math for orbital compute.

In low Earth orbit a satellite receives near-continuous sunlight delivering up to eight times the solar energy per square meter compared with most terrestrial sites.

The Google prototype therefore carries oversized radiators sized for vacuum heat rejection a constraint that does not exist when air or liquid cooling is available on the ground.

If the raw material cost of the solar arrays and radiators is treated as the magic-wand baseline the finished satellite still carries an Idiot Index several times higher because of radiation hardening attitude control and laser crosslinks required for data return.

That multiplier shrinks only when launch costs fall enough that an entire constellation can be refreshed every few years instead of designed for decade-long life.

The same physics explains why Space X's own Starmind concept pairs large solar arrays with radiators on each AI1 satellite targeting one hundred twenty kilowatts average compute power.

Continuous solar input removes the terrestrial grid bottleneck but vacuum heat rejection remains the binding limit on compute density per kilogram.

Early data from the current test will therefore directly inform whether one hundred twenty kilowatt class nodes can be scaled to thousands of units without exceeding acceptable mass or power budgets for the planned one million satellite constellation.

The one point two gigawatt ground plant under construction for Colossus in Memphis follows the identical constraint on Earth where power delivery and cooling already limit the November ramp of four hundred twenty thousand additional G P U's toward the one point four four million total target.
