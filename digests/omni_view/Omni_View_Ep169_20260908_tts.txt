To really understand a trade-surplus headline, you have to follow the paperwork under it, not the adjective on the screen.

A common mistake is to treat one month's export total as a clean snapshot of economic strength, when the published number is the end of a long chain.

Customs offices first gather detailed invoices from each shipment.

Statisticians then convert those invoices at the going exchange rate and strip out re-exports — goods that merely passed through — before they add the country total.

Weeks or months later, late paperwork arrives and valuation mistakes get fixed, so a headline that looked firm can read differently in the next quarter's tables.

Seasonal adjustments then smooth holiday and harvest swings that would otherwise make one month look wildly unlike the next.

A sharp year-on-year jump can therefore owe something to when cargo cleared the dock, not only to a lasting change in orders.

When a minister or a commentator waves the latest surplus as proof of resilience, or of imbalance, it helps to ask whether the print is still preliminary and whether earlier months were revised up or down.

Cross-check the figure against the original customs release rather than a second-hand roundup, and, when you can, set the same month's count from the exporting country beside the count from the importing country; the footnotes on method often explain why those two numbers refuse to match.
