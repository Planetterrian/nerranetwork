Serbia's Interior Ministry confirmed nine serving officers and one former officer were detained after accusations they used excessive force against students during two thousand twenty-five protests, coverage Al Jazeera ties to a broader push to reform state institutions under the new government after incidents in a government-compound garage in Belgrade in August two thousand twenty-five.

The ministry said the suspects belonged to an elite unit and are accused of using force and threats to inflict severe pain and suffering on several injured people in order to obtain information, intimidate them, and unlawfully punish them, and it released only the officers' initials.

Among the detainees, state broadcaster Radio Television of Serbia said, was Igor Zmiric, commander of the Special Antiterrorism Unit.

The detentions follow last month's parliamentary elections in which a student-led movement defeated former president Aleksandar Vučić after years of anti-corruption demonstrations that began when the canopy at a railway station in Novi Sad collapsed in November two thousand twenty-four, killing sixteen people, a collapse seen as symptomatic of alleged widespread corruption under Vučić, who served as president for twelve years before conceding defeat last month.

The winning student-led protest movement has demanded reforms within state institutions, including the police and intelligence services, and Amnesty International's two thousand twenty-five report documented repeated cases of excessive force and surveillance against protesters and journalists, with the rights group saying protesters were subjected to excessive force in multiple cases across the country.

Protesters have also called on top officials to resign, accusing them of colluding with Vučić while he was in power, so on the terms set by that movement, elite-unit detentions leave the demand over senior collusion unmet.

Malawi, in AllAfrica's account of a new Afrobarometer poll, is a country where six in ten people believe things are heading in the wrong direction.

Respondents cited food shortages, lack of income, and rising living costs as their primary concerns — not enough to eat, no wage coming in, and prices that keep climbing — and the survey captures household-level strain in a country where many families already face chronic food insecurity, meaning hunger is a regular condition rather than a one-season shock.

Afrobarometer is a long-running continental survey project that tracks attitudes across dozens of African states, and the published findings avoid pinning the discontent on a single policy, stressing instead how widely it runs.

The poll reflects ongoing economic pressures including cash shortages that affect daily life for ordinary households, so even routine purchases become hard when notes and coin are simply not there to use.

PanStar Acro, a South Korean container ship, finished a round-trip from Busan to Rotterdam via the Northern Sea Route in fifty days, a voyage Deutsche Welle records as saving at least ten days compared with the fastest travel times along the Suez Canal route.

The Northern Sea Route is around seven thousand kilometres shorter and avoids the geopolitical flashpoint of the Middle East, and the vessel left the South Korean port of Busan on August twenty-second, navigated around Russia to the southern British port of Felixstowe, continued to Rotterdam in the Netherlands and Gdansk in Poland, then headed back along the same Arctic passage with ice-breaker support, consistent with current Arctic conditions.

Western European governments worry that closer commercial ties with Russia could undermine sanctions pressure, while Seoul hopes a working economic relationship might give it leverage to discourage Russian arms supplies to North Korea, including hoped-for influence over Vladimir Putin, alongside the route's economic potential.

According to the Arctic Council, last year saw a record one thousand eight hundred twelve vessels operating in the Arctic, an increase of forty percent compared to two thousand thirteen.

Yemen's internationally recognised government, one month into Operation Dawn of Yemen, has seen fighting settle into a pattern of incremental territorial gains and losses, and Al Jazeera reports that Sanaa remains largely calm aside from occasional air strikes.

When news broke on October fifth that the government was launching a major new offensive to take control of territories held by Houthi fighters, residents began to prepare for the worst, and Houthi forces have since scored territorial gains in other provinces, including Taiz and Lahij in southern Yemen, where fighting has not ceased.

Houthi forces have adapted by shifting supply lines and leveraging terrain, while government and Saudi-backed forces have disrupted some maritime routes near the Bab al-Mandeb strait, the narrow mouth of the Red Sea, and local residents interviewed describe reduced immediate fear of urban combat.

Turki al-Goblan, head of the Saudi Daimumah Center for Studies, argues the operation has weakened three pillars of Houthi logistics, while also saying the Houthis have survived in previous years by adapting to strikes and leveraging complex geography, clandestine supply and concealment networks, and the use of civilians as human shields.

The first pillar, according to him, is the fuel and supply system, which keeps Houthi combat vehicles moving, transports ammunition and delivers reinforcements, and the second is the maritime areas around the Bab al-Mandeb strait and the western coast, outlets through which alternative external supply routes pass.

Chinese scientists used a robotic arm on the Tiangong space station to make millimetre-scale cuts on large-scale equipment via remote laser, a test the South China Morning Post presents as a world-first demonstration of in-orbit maintenance technology.

Precision laser cutting involves focusing a beam of light to slice or melt material with extreme accuracy, and the test marks the first time precision laser cutting has been carried out in orbit.

Police arrests in a European Union candidate country are easy to read as ordinary criminal news, and to really understand how accession talks shape domestic police reform you have to follow the negotiation chapters on justice and home affairs, not the charging documents alone.

Membership talks are divided into chapters, and the justice and home affairs chapter only opens and closes when a candidate shows verifiable progress on independent oversight of the police, training standards for officers, and actual prosecution of misconduct, not promises on paper.

Those talks can sit frozen for years on rule-of-law benchmarks, which means Brussels, the political centre of the European Union, or EU, will not move to the next stage until the court cases and the oversight rules line up.

When a new government signals that it is willing to meet those benchmarks, the EU can unlock funding and political support that previous administrations forfeited, turning a domestic discipline case into a diplomatic test.

If prosecutors then pursue misconduct cases through transparent procedures, the EU can cite measurable improvement when it decides whether to resume formal talks, which is why the same arrest can be both a message at home and a data point in an enlargement file.

Primary documents such as court filings or EU progress reports often contain the precise conditions attached to any policy shift, so ministry statements about accountability are best read beside independent monitoring groups rather than in isolation.

Open the European Commission's annual enlargement report and look for which justice and home affairs benchmarks are still listed as needs further progress — that list is the scoreboard sitting behind any day's charging announcement.
