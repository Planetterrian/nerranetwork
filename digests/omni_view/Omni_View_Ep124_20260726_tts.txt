Now, to really understand this story, there is something most coverage leaves out.

Most reporting treats El Nino damage projections as straightforward predictions, yet the process involves several layers of modeling that many readers do not see.

Climate agencies first track sea-surface temperatures in the central Pacific.

When anomalies exceed a threshold for several months, they classify the event’s strength.

Those temperature readings then feed into regional rainfall and temperature models that African Development Bank economists combine with data on crop yields, infrastructure exposure, and population density.

The resulting dollar figures represent an average across multiple possible weather outcomes rather than a single guaranteed total.

Knowing this, when you hear a round number such as twenty billion dollars, you can check whether the source has published the underlying temperature threshold or the specific sectors included in the calculation.

Next time coverage cites an El Nino cost estimate, look for the date of the latest Pacific temperature update and whether the figure assumes a moderate or extreme scenario.
