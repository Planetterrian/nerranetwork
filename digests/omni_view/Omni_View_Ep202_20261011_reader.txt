The government issued the declaration on Saturday, after Friday's shaking, which the United States Geological Survey measured at seven point seven and six point six.

Officials reported twenty-seven injuries and no deaths.

More than one hundred aftershocks were recorded, some reaching magnitude five point eight.

The first quake struck at a depth of ten kilometres, about ten kilometres south-west of the remote community of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province.

The second followed shortly afterward.

The shaking toppled buildings near the epicentre and prompted evacuations, with damage also reported in neighbouring Colombia.

The Guardian describes damage concentrated in central provinces including Veraguas, Herrera, Cocle and Los Santos, where landslides cut off some communities and left residents waiting outdoors for official guidance.

Local resident Tony Jiménez, whose home partially collapsed in La Villa de Los Santos, told the paper the shaking was the most intense he had experienced in ninety years.

The National Civil Protection System reported that four hundred twenty homes and an equal number of public schools had been damaged, along with seven hotels, at least thirteen churches and nine bridges.

Two Indigenous territories were affected as well.

Officials will continue damage assessments and issue guidance to residents still waiting in streets or temporary shelters.

To really understand a disaster-time state of emergency, you have to look at the sequence of paperwork that has to exist before extra legal powers can be switched on.

A lot of coverage treats the declaration as an automatic step once the ground stops moving.

In practice, governments first have to compile rapid damage assessments from several ministries at once.

Those assessments have to show that ordinary local resources are already overwhelmed.

Only then can the cabinet invoke the legal powers that come with the emergency label.

That order is the mechanism, not a formality.

It is how a listener can judge whether a declaration matches the documented scope of destruction, instead of assuming every quake produces one.

Later public counts of damaged buildings and injuries become the concrete test of whether those powers were justified.

The process also requires verification that aftershocks are still a live risk, not only that the first shock was large.

Primary documents such as seismological readings and civil-protection tallies are the clearest baseline for that test.

Official statements still need to be read beside independent reporting from more than one region before anyone settles on the scale of the event.

The useful check is the next civil-protection update: whether the damage tallies are revised, and whether the government asks for international assistance.
