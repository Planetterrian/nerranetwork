Yemen’s recognised government has opened a wide round of air strikes on Houthi-held ground. Al Jazeera reported that military spokesman Colonel Majed al-Nuzaili said government forces carried out seven hundred twenty-six attacks in the twenty-four hours before Friday, and claimed one hundred twenty-one fighters and commanders were killed or wounded.

From Sanaa, Al Jazeera’s Yousef Mawry said the Saudi-led coalition that backs the government hit more than six governorates overnight. A gas station in the Tahrir district injured thirteen people, three of them critically. The Houthis said a later strike killed a man and his wife in their home.

Mawry also described a fuel station in Bani Matar, a raid on Taiz, and five raids in Mocha that hit a power station. Houthi-affiliated media said a Saudi strike in al-Turbah, in Taiz, killed eleven civilians, most of them children. Al Jazeera could not confirm that toll, and the coalition did not comment.

The government-run Saba news agency said Houthi attacks on government locations in Taiz on Saturday killed three civilians, including a ten-year-old boy, and wounded three others, including an infant. Coalition officials said a Houthi missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted. None of those casualty claims was independently verified.

The World Food Programme and the Food and Agriculture Organization, the United Nations agencies for food aid and farming, warned on Friday that hunger is likely to worsen over the next six months. More than three hundred thousand people have been newly displaced since the escalation began, mostly in Taiz, Lahij and Aden.

The agencies said the Houthis encircled Taiz in early October, that their areas are harder to reach, and that they are holding seventy-three United Nations staff. Aid groups say cuts by donors, including the United States and other Western countries, have deepened the crisis as food and fuel prices soar. The Houthis, backed by Iran, seized Sanaa in twenty fourteen.

Saudi Arabia led a coalition from March twenty fifteen for the recognised government. Fighting resumed in July after a twenty twenty-two ceasefire collapsed, in the tension around the United States-Israel war on Iran. Al-Nuzaili said fighting was still going in Ma’rib, al-Jawf, Taiz, Lahij, Shabwah, Midi and al-Dhale.

Both armies are fighting again, and the hunger and displacement numbers come from those two United Nations agencies, not from either spokesman. Al-Nuzaili’s account is a military campaign: hundreds of attacks in a day against fighters and commanders, answering Houthi fire that includes a missile toward Riyadh and attacks Saba said killed civilians in Taiz.

The Houthi account is that a Saudi strike killed eleven civilians, most of them children, so the bombs are hitting families. Each claim stops at the same wall. The civilian toll was not confirmed, the coalition was silent, and no outside count has verified either side’s dead.

Spain’s voters go to a snap election on Sunday that could put the far-right Vox party in national government for the first time in five decades, half a century after Franco’s dictatorship ended. Al Jazeera reported that the campaign, closed on Friday, turned on housing and immigration. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez, fifty-four, of the Socialist Workers’ Party, has governed since twenty eighteen.

Polls give the conservative People’s Party the most seats, but it needs Vox, projected third, to reach one hundred seventy-six seats in the three-hundred-fifty-seat Congress of Deputies. Most surveys put the two together at that line, which would let People’s Party leader Alberto Nunez Feijoo form a coalition or a minority government with Vox behind it.

Sanchez called the vote in October after parliament rejected two measures meant to protect renters, following protests over housing costs and evictions. The European Commission forecasts growth of two point six percent for Spain this year, against one point two percent for the eurozone.

France twenty-four reported that Vox built its campaign on immigration after tens of thousands of people crossed in July from Morocco into Ceuta, a Spanish city in North Africa. The South China Morning Post noted that Europe is opening a year of elections that could shift the balance inside the European Union.

Alvaro Sanchez-Garcia, a political scientist at Spain’s Centre for Political and Constitutional Studies, told Al Jazeera the vote has been cast as a yes or no on Sanchez, and that people who switch parties mostly stay inside the same left or right bloc. In twenty twenty-three his party finished second and he stayed in office with parties to his left and with Catalan and Basque groupings.

He has clashed with United States President Donald Trump, refused a request from other members of NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, to lift defence spending to five percent, and called Israel’s war in Gaza a genocide.

On Friday he told a radio interviewer he could not conceive of losing, and told a Madrid rally the election was about one thing only: whether Spain moves forward or goes backwards. His case is that the country has never been in better shape, growing faster than a stagnant eurozone, with a progressive record that made him a reference for the European left.

The limit is the October defeat on renters that forced this vote. Feijoo’s case, put to him in a televised debate last week, is that you cannot eat gross domestic product, and that alleged corruption and opportunism require a new government.

Isabel Labayen, sixty-four, a longtime People’s Party voter, asked how Spain can be praised abroad when people cannot afford a house or food, or see a doctor in days rather than months. Vox’s case, as France twenty-four described the campaign, is that the July arrivals in Ceuta should decide who governs. Polls are not ballots, and Feijoo still needs Vox for a majority.

Watch whether those two parties together reach one hundred seventy-six seats when Sunday’s count is finished.

Iraq’s prime minister has American backing for a crackdown on oil smuggling.

Al Jazeera reported that President Donald Trump wrote on Friday, on Truth Social, that Washington supports Ali al-Zaidi’s effort to assert state authority throughout Iraq, stop the smuggling of Iraqi oil, end corruption, and bring all weapons under the exclusive control of the state, and to build an Iraq that makes its own political decisions.

It was Washington’s first comment on the strain between Baghdad and Erbil, seat of the semi-autonomous Kurdish region. On Tuesday al-Zaidi moved to put the region’s border crossings and several oilfields under federal control. On Wednesday Iraq’s top court ruled that Baghdad may deploy federal forces along every international border, including the Kurdish region’s.

On Thursday Iraq closed two crossings there, one with Syria and one with Iran. Talks between Baghdad and Erbil are due on December first, sponsored by the head of the Supreme Judicial Council. The campaign began in Jurf al-Sakhar, also called Jurf al-Nasr, in Babil province south of Baghdad.

Kataib Hezbollah, an Iran-backed group the United States designates a terrorist organisation, has held the town since twenty fourteen, after it and allied groups took it from the Islamic State and most Sunni residents were displaced.

Senior Iraqi security and oil officials told Reuters the group has run a smuggling network there, siphoning state crude worth hundreds of millions of dollars since twenty twenty-one. Nine senior security and oil officials have been dismissed and sent to the courts. Kataib Hezbollah denies the allegations. Al-Zaidi gave the group until November twentieth to leave.

A day before that deadline, after reported intervention by Esmail Qaani, head of Iran’s Quds Force, government forces entered and set up a joint operations centre. Salim Zakhour, a constitutional-law researcher, told Al Jazeera the American post tells Kurdish leaders that Baghdad’s measures have strong American backing.

Piura, in northern Peru, is under waist-high water after twelve hours of rain that began on Thursday, part of downpours Al Jazeera linked to an exceptionally strong El Nino, a warming of the sea surface in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific that shifts wind and rain worldwide.

Luis Vasquez, chief of the National Institute of Civil Defence, told RPP radio that preliminary reports put affected families in Piura at about two thousand. The Jose Cayetano Heredia Hospital moved patients from its respiratory-infection ward to the auditorium, and more serious patients to intensive care. Uganda has opened Africa’s first clinical trial of an mRNA vaccine against the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, the strain behind the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Al Jazeera reported that the state-run Uganda Virus Research Institute said on Friday the trial is with Moderna, the United States company that makes the shot. It is the second trial.

The first began in Canada in August and enrolled adults from eighteen to sixty-five. This one is the first to test safety and immune response in children aged one and above, and it aims to enrol two hundred twenty-four volunteers.

Richard Hatchett, chief executive of the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations, which is helping fund the vaccine, said children are often hit hardest when Ebola strikes and are too often last to receive new vaccines. A second candidate, ChAdOx1, from India’s Serum Institute and the University of Oxford, is due to start testing in the Congo on Monday.

Gavi, the alliance that helps lower-income countries buy vaccines, signed a purchase commitment last week for one point five million doses and plans to have hundreds of thousands ready if the trials succeed and the World Health Organization and the Congolese government approve them.

Emanuele Capobianco, Gavi’s director of global health security, said he hoped those trials would finish in early twenty twenty-seven and that the deal could bring deployment forward by months. On Thursday, Congolese data showed at least twelve thousand twenty-one confirmed infections and five thousand nine hundred four deaths since mid-May.

Uganda declared its own outbreak over on July twenty-eighth, after twenty infections and two deaths. The trial asks whether the shot is safe and whether it prompts an immune response, including in children. It does not replace the response already under way in the Congo.

To really understand a result like Spain’s, you have to separate the party that finishes first from the party that gets to govern. A common misreading of a European election night is that the largest pile of seats is the government. It is not. What follows is my own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from a separate study.

A prime minister who loses a vote treated as essential can dissolve the chamber and pick an early date. That is a snap election. The calendar moves. The rule for forming a government does not. In a multiparty chamber, governing takes a majority of seats, or enough partners to supply one, not merely first place.

A leader who is short of that line must bargain with a rival the campaign just opposed, or stay out while someone else gathers a wider set of partners. Regional and nationalist parties can matter as much as the national runner-up, because each of their seats counts the same when a government is proposed. Campaigns that sort voters into two blocs make the arithmetic starker.

Few people cross, so the night tests which bloc held its own voters and can then find allies. A leader can be admired abroad for stands that never appear on the domestic ballot, where rent, food and a doctor’s appointment decide the vote. Next time a headline names a winner in a parliament like this, check whether any named combination can actually seat a government, not only who finished first.
