## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Panama declares state of emergency after major earthquakes
**What happened (neutral):** Panama’s government declared a state of emergency on Saturday after two powerful earthquakes struck on Friday. The quakes, measured at 7.7 and 6.6 magnitude by the US Geological Survey, damaged homes, schools, health facilities, roads and hotels in all 10 provinces and two Indigenous territories. Officials reported 27 injuries but no deaths, with more than 100 aftershocks recorded, some reaching magnitude 5.8. The first quake struck at a depth of 10 km about 10 km south-west of the remote community of Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, and the second followed shortly afterward. The earthquakes toppled buildings near the epicentre and prompted evacuations across the Central American country, with damage also reported in neighbouring Colombia.

**Context & perspectives:** The Guardian describes widespread damage concentrated in central provinces including Veraguas, Herrera, Cocle and Los Santos, where landslides cut off some communities and left residents waiting outdoors for official guidance. Local resident Tony Jiménez, whose home partially collapsed in La Villa de Los Santos, told the paper the shaking was the most intense he had experienced in 90 years. The US Geological Survey pinpointed the first quake’s epicentre about 10 km south-west of the remote community of Pitaloza Arriba at a depth of 10 km. Presidential minister Juan Carlos Orillac announced the emergency during a news conference in Panama City, citing harm to homes, schools, healthcare facilities, roads and hotels. The National Civil Protection System reported that 420 homes and an equal number of public schools had been damaged, along with seven hotels, at least 13 churches and nine bridges.

**What happens next:** Officials will continue damage assessments and issue guidance to residents still waiting in streets or temporary shelters.

## Understanding the Issue: How states of emergency are activated after natural disasters
Most coverage treats a state of emergency declaration as an automatic step once shaking stops. In practice, governments must first compile rapid damage assessments from multiple ministries showing that local resources are overwhelmed before the cabinet can invoke the legal powers. In Panama’s case, the presidential minister cited harm to homes, schools, hospitals, roads and hotels across every province plus two Indigenous territories. The National Civil Protection System later supplied concrete counts of 420 damaged homes, 420 damaged schools, seven hotels, 13 churches and nine bridges. Knowing this sequence helps readers judge whether the declaration matches the documented scope of destruction rather than assuming every quake triggers one. When officials later report specific counts such as 420 damaged homes or 27 injuries, those figures become the concrete test of whether the emergency powers were justified. Readers can check the next official update from the National Civil Protection System for revised damage tallies and any request for international assistance. The process also requires verification that aftershocks continue to pose risks, as seen with more than 100 recorded events reaching magnitude 5.8.

## Media-literacy note
Cross-reference official government statements with independent reporting from multiple regions before drawing conclusions about the scale of any event. Primary documents such as seismological data and civil-protection tallies provide the clearest baseline.
