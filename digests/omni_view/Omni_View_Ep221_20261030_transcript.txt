To really understand how cryptocurrency exchanges manage large-scale thefts, start with
the chain of custody decisions made long before any breach.
The common misunderstanding is that a major hack is simply a sudden technical failure.
Exchanges typically hold the majority of customer assets in offline cold wallets that require
multiple human approvals to move funds.
They keep only a small fraction in online hot wallets, which stay connected so daily trading
can settle.
When coins are stolen, they often move through mixers or cross-chain bridges that obscure
ownership.
Investigators then follow wallet addresses on the public ledger rather than real-world
names.
That work is done by blockchain analysis firms, which cluster addresses that appear to
be controlled together.
Theft of this kind has also been used as a revenue stream that bypasses traditional
financial sanctions. Because the ledger is public, the coins can still be mapped after the fact
even when they cannot be seized at once. That is why I claim that an exchange loss funds
is not the same as the coins vanishing. They can remain visible even when recovery is slow.
If a report names a particular unit, look at whether the attribution rests on those wallet
clustering patterns or on a broader intelligence assessment. Next time a theft is reported,
Look for whether the exchange has published an on-chain address for any recovered portion
of the stolen assets.
