Here's the thing that changes how you see this whole debate.
Most coverage of new tariffs assumes the exporting country pays the bill, yet the mechanism works differently.
When goods arrive at a US port, the American importer pays the tariff directly to customs and border protection before the shipment clears.
That added cost then enters the importer's pricing calculation and is typically passed along in whole or in part to wholesalers, retailers, and finally households.
Historical episodes such as the 2018-2019 tariffs on steel and washing machines showed measurable price increases for those specific products inside the United States even though the tariffs were formally levied on foreign shipments.
Knowing this flow clarifies why broad tariff lists tend to appear in domestic inflation data rather than in the budgets of foreign governments.
When you next hear claims that the other country will pay, check whether the reporting follows the cost through to US import records or consumer price indexes.
Importers often absorb part of the tariff in the short term to protect market share, but sustained pressure usually leads to higher shelf prices within months.
The exact pass-through rate varies by product category and competition level, which is why some sectors see sharper jumps than others.
Reviewing monthly import price indexes published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics offers one concrete way to track whether the added costs are staying with importers or moving downstream.
