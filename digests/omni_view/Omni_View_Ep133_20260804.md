## Understanding the Issue: How the Big Mac index measures currency values
Most people assume exchange rates simply reflect trade balances or central-bank policy, yet the Big Mac index reveals a different layer: how much a standardized basket of goods actually costs in local currency. The index works by comparing the price of one McDonald’s Big Mac across countries and then checking whether that price matches the official exchange rate; if a burger costs far more in one place after conversion, the local currency is considered overvalued on a purchasing-power basis. This approach draws on the economic idea of purchasing-power parity, which holds that identical goods should cost the same everywhere once exchange rates adjust. Over four decades the index has repeatedly shown large and persistent gaps, especially in emerging markets where non-traded costs such as rent and labor differ sharply. One concrete illustration appears in the 1990s Brazilian data, where the local-currency price of the same burger jumped from 3,800 cruzeiros to 77,000 cruzeiros in a single year before the currency was retired. Knowing this mechanism helps readers see why a headline about a “cheap” or “expensive” currency often rests on more than export data alone. When coverage cites the index, check whether the story also mentions adjustments for taxes, local wages, or non-traded services that the simple burger price cannot capture. A reader can verify the latest figures directly on the Economist’s interactive tool and compare any two currencies side by side.
