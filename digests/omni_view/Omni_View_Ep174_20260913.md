## Understanding the Issue: How international tribunals handle long-standing water treaties
Most coverage of treaty disputes treats suspension as a simple on-off switch, yet the Indus Waters Treaty contains a permanent commission and a multi-step dispute process that continues even when political relations break down. The commission has met regularly since 1960, exchanging data on river flows and resolving technical questions without requiring the approval of either capital. When one side suspends cooperation, the treaty’s arbitration clause still allows the other party to bring the matter before an independent tribunal whose ruling is binding on the technical provisions. The 1960 text created this commission precisely to keep technical cooperation separate from wider political disputes. The recent Hague decision addressed only the legality of suspension and left the underlying allocation of river water unchanged. Historical records show the commission continued functioning through earlier wars and diplomatic breaks. When you next hear claims that a treaty has been “ended,” check whether the commission or arbitration process is still operating and what the actual text says about unilateral withdrawal. The treaty’s design illustrates how some agreements build in insulation against political shocks through permanent technical bodies.
