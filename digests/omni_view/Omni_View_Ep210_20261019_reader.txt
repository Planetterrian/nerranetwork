To really understand how governments produce a count of young people outside work or study, you have to watch how two separate data streams are stitched together.

A lot of coverage treats that share as a simple headcount.

The figure is actually built by joining labour-force surveys to enrolment records from schools.

Those two sources are gathered on different calendars.

They also use definitions of training that do not match.

What you get is a snapshot, and a snapshot can move with the calendar of students leaving school.

It can also move when the rules for recording an apprenticeship change.

Statistical offices merge what people tell household surveyors about their current activity with administrative files on who is enrolled in school or college.

Household surveys ask what a person is doing now.

Enrolment files record whether that person is on the books at a school or college.

Seasonal effects appear because many young people finish courses in the summer.

They may then take several months to enter a job or further training.

When a proposal is said to cut the total quickly, the test is whether it would change survey methods or change actual job-creation incentives.

Training itself is not a single thing.

The label can cover formal apprenticeships, short courses, or unstructured learning on the job.

Different countries set different thresholds for what counts.
