To really understand a Brazilian presidential runoff, you have to see the absolute-majority rule as a coalition machine, not a simple rematch.

The constitution written in nineteen eighty-eight, after military rule, requires an absolute majority in the first round.

Absolute majority means more than half of the valid vote, not merely finishing first.

If nobody clears that bar, the system orders a second contest between only the top two finishers.

The rule was introduced to push candidates toward broader coalitions.

In most recent cycles, no single candidate has won outright on the first ballot, so the second round is the normal path, not an exception.

That is where smaller parties become decisive.

They can withhold or grant support in exchange for cabinet posts or written policy concessions.

Coverage that treats a runoff as just another vote often skips this bargaining step.

When a campaign claims it has momentum, the mechanism to test is whether eliminated rivals have already pledged that second-round support.

Momentum without those pledges is still a first-round leftover.

Momentum with them is a governing coalition being assembled in public.

Historical patterns show that second-round deals often set the cabinet’s composition and the legislative priorities of the incoming administration.

Those bargains keep recurring because first-round knockouts have been rare under this design.

The concrete thing to check before ballots are cast is whether either camp publishes formal agreements with parties that finished third or fourth.

Until those papers appear, the open trail is public statements from the eliminated candidates themselves.

Electoral court filings sit underneath the commentary, and they are the baseline that secondary summaries can drift away from.
