To really understand a federal voting rule, you have to follow the split of labor in the Constitution: Washington may set limited conditions, but each state still builds the machinery that puts a ballot in someone’s hand.

The common misunderstanding is that the federal government can simply take over election administration.

It cannot, in the ordinary sense of running the poll books, designing the ballot, choosing polling places, or counting the votes — those jobs belong to the states, and they always have.

That arrangement dates to the founding.

Each state sets its own voter registration rules, ballot design, polling locations, and counting procedures, subject only to federal laws that protect voting rights and prevent discrimination.

When Congress passes a voting law, states must still enact and administer the change through their own systems.

A federal proposal can add conditions that states have to implement without actually transferring the running of elections to federal officials.

The practical test is in the text: does the language direct states to update their registration databases, and which state officials would have to carry that out.

Earlier federal voting laws followed the same path — Congress wrote requirements, and state officers had to rewrite forms, retrain clerks, and adjust local procedures before anything changed at a precinct.

Knowing that history calibrates the present: the last several times Washington set a new voting condition, the visible work landed on state election offices, not on a national elections bureau, because no such bureau runs American polling places.

When the next federal voting proposal appears, read the clause that tells states how to update their registration databases, and note which state officials would have to carry it out.
