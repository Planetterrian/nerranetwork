To really understand how meetings between adversaries get arranged, look at the mandate, not the handshake.

A common misunderstanding treats a single envoy visit as either a breakthrough or a betrayal, when the machinery underneath is more ordinary than either headline.

Envoys typically carry limited instructions set in advance by their governments, often after coordination with allies and with military commanders, so the conversation in the room is bounded before anyone sits down.

Because those instructions are already fixed, the meeting itself rarely changes battlefield orders that are already in motion.

Historical parallels run the same way: even when talks occur, each side continues operations until a written agreement is reached and then verified on the ground, not until a photo is released.

That pattern is a calibration, not a forecast — it is how this channel has worked when it has worked at all.

When you hear that one session opened the door to peace, or that it signaled surrender, the firmer test is whether any written proposals were exchanged and whether forces on both sides received new instructions.

Joint communiqués and verified casualty reports sit on firmer ground than the first wire summary, and reading outlets based in different regions is how you see which facts are shared and which are still disputed.

The concrete thing to check is whether the next round still appears on official calendars after the most recent strikes.
