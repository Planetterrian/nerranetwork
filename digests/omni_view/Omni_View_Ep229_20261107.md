## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Paraguay removes president after major bank collapse
**What happened (neutral):** Regulators seized a large bank, cutting off access to savings accounts and pension payments for millions of customers. The Senate then removed President Santiago Peña from office and placed him under house arrest. Peña had offered his resignation over the crisis.

**Context & perspectives:** The Hindu traced how the seizure quickly escalated into a national political crisis that removed the sitting leader. France24 reported the Senate's unanimous impeachment vote and the immediate house arrest that followed. Both accounts present the financial failure as the direct trigger for the leadership change in a country where such rapid institutional response to a banking event is uncommon. The sequence began when regulators took control of the bank, immediately locking nearly half the population out of their accounts and halting public pension disbursements. This created widespread hardship that reached public services and ordinary households alike. The Hindu's step-by-step account shows the timeline moved from regulator action to unanimous impeachment vote without requiring new legislation. France24 added that lawmakers pressed ahead with impeachment proceedings even after Peña offered to resign, stripping him of political immunity in the process. The coverage from both outlets underscores how a single bank's failure can expose vulnerabilities in state-linked financial systems and activate constitutional removal powers within days.

## Understanding the Issue: How a bank collapse can trigger rapid political change
Most coverage treats a banking failure as a purely financial event that stays inside regulatory agencies. In Paraguay the seizure of one major bank immediately blocked pension payments and everyday withdrawals for millions of account holders, creating visible hardship that reached beyond depositors to public services. The constitution allows the legislature to remove presidential immunity when evidence of serious misconduct tied to the crisis emerges, and the Senate used that mechanism within days. The article details how regulators' seizure locked nearly half of Paraguayans out of their savings, freezing both private accounts and public pension and health-care funds in one step. This direct impact on state-linked payments created immediate public pressure that lawmakers could not ignore. The Hindu notes that Peña offered his resignation, yet the Senate proceeded with impeachment proceedings that stripped him of immunity and led to house arrest. Knowing this sequence, when reports describe a “banking scandal” in any country, readers can check whether the failure directly affected state payments or public funds and whether the legislature has explicit removal powers that can be activated quickly. The next time coverage mentions a financial crisis and political fallout in the same sentence, ask whether the banking event froze government-linked accounts and whether the constitution sets a low threshold for legislative removal of the head of state.
