Bahrain’s military told CNBC its air defences stopped several Iranian aerial attacks short of their targets, while France 24 carried the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — a branch of Iran’s armed forces — saying the weapons were aimed at a United States radar at the Al-Riffa helicopter base. No American or Bahraini authority immediately confirmed that the radar itself was hit.

Sirens were the first since September. People reported blasts near four in the morning and again near six-thirty. The interior ministry told residents to stay calm and move to the nearest safe place. Bahrain hosts the Fifth Fleet, the US Navy command for the Gulf. The Guardian quoted Hayat Ibrahim, sixty-five, telling Agence France-Presse this was the return she had hoped was over.

Two days earlier the same Guards said they had struck an early-warning radar there. US Central Command, which runs American operations in the region, said it shot down two Iranian drones that time, with no casualties and no damage.

CNBC also reported that President Donald Trump has said he might hold off further attacks on Iran if Tehran and the Iran-backed Houthis in Yemen offer no new provocations, while keeping a naval blockade. On that other front, the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted two Houthi ballistic missiles fired toward Riyadh and three drones in the oil-rich Eastern Province.

France 24 quoted the Guards adding that the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow oil route out of the Gulf, is closed, and that further American force would draw a harsher answer. Bahrain’s foreign ministry said the kingdom will not be an arena for settling scores and holds Iran fully responsible. The Guards’ reason is retaliation for what they call hostile US actions.

Bahrain’s reason is that the shots were stopped, the pattern is hostile, and the firing state owns the result. Whether Central Command later confirms damage at that base, or only interceptions, is the check.

Cape Verde opened the polls with a woman on a presidential ballot for the first time, Al Jazeera reported. More than three hundred thousand people could vote, from eight in the morning until six in the evening, among six candidates. President Jose Maria Neves wants a second term. Joana Rosa, a former justice minister, is the first woman to run since independence in nineteen seventy-five.

A winner needs more than half the vote. If nobody gets that, the top two meet again on November twenty-ninth. A count is expected in two to three days. Neves belongs to the African Party for the Independence of Cape Verde, the oldest party.

He was prime minister for fifteen years, became president in twenty twenty-one, and after his party took thirty-seven of seventy-two parliamentary seats in May he named Francisco Carvalho, then mayor of the capital Praia, as prime minister. Rosa’s Movement for Democracy had governed for ten years before that election. In this hybrid system the president can veto and mediate.

The president does not run the cabinet day to day. About five hundred thousand people live on the islands, and more citizens live abroad than at home, mostly in Portugal and the United States. Nearly seventy-seven thousand of those abroad are registered to vote at embassies. Jobs are scarce, so leaving has long been the pressure on the ballot.

Roselma Evora, who teaches political science and gender studies at the University of Cabo Verde, told the Associated Press that many voters feel the political class does not grasp daily life. Rosa argues for a government that lets young people build a life on the islands instead of leaving. Neves argues for a second term that would organise a youth summit aimed at the same exodus.

Watch the first count: one candidate over fifty percent, or a runoff three Sundays from now.

Manila opened the annual summit of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, an eleven-country bloc that wants one market but cannot order its members to comply. Al Jazeera put the meetings at November fifteenth through the seventeenth, and the membership at nearly six hundred ninety-four million people, about eight point four percent of the world.

Protesters met the arrivals over the price of basic goods and over Pax Silica, a US-led plan for the artificial-intelligence supply chain. Oil and gas shortfalls from the war on Iran are what import-dependent members carried into the hall. The Philippines, Vietnam, and Malaysia are among the most exposed. Manila declared a national energy emergency in March.

Millions of the bloc’s citizens live and work in the Middle East. Governments also fear the fighting could reach the Strait of Malacca, the tight sea lane between Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. Tariff rises of ten to twelve point five percent since President Trump’s second term have pushed members to trade elsewhere.

He is expected at a special sitting with the bloc on November seventeenth, then at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meeting in China on the eighteenth and nineteenth. Al Jazeera also named India’s Narendra Modi, Canada’s Mark Carney, Brazil’s Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva — the first sitting Brazilian president to visit the Philippines — and Russia’s foreign minister Sergey Lavrov.

At sea, China and four members, Brunei, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Vietnam, plus Taiwan, have disputed waters thought to hold oil and gas under the seabed. Talks on a code of conduct began in two thousand five. A twenty twenty-three pledge was to finish a draft this year. At least ten rounds ran this year, the last in October, with Malaysia in the chair.

Portugal’s cities filled with marches against surging rents. The Associated Press called the crowds tens of thousands. Al Jazeera called them thousands. Authorities have started changes meant to bring prices down. Marchers told Al Jazeera those changes are not enough. Neither account names a new statute, a rent figure, or a date for the next official step.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has pressed for a looser version of rules on advanced artificial-intelligence chips sold to data centers outside China that still serve Chinese firms, The New York Times reported.

A site called A-two-zero-one, in southern Malaysia, is due to open next year with hundreds of millions of dollars in Nvidia’s most advanced servers, and to sell that computing power to a Chinese tech giant. The Times said the arrangement is legal, and that it steps around a ban on selling those chips straight to Chinese companies.

The operator is a client of Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm Lutnick used to lead and then passed to his sons. He now writes the rules that would bear on that business. Washington already bars cutting-edge chip sales to China, and has argued for much of two years over how far to go in Southeast Asia.

China hawks in that argument treat a center sitting outside China, but working for Chinese firms, as a way around the ban. Lutnick’s argument is that tighter oversight of sales to international data centers would cut what American firms, Nvidia among them, can sell in Asia, and that the restriction should not go that far.

The Times said lawmakers and others inside the administration have criticised that softer touch. No date for a finished rule appears in the piece.

South Korea’s top scorer on the national exam for would-be tax accountants confessed to cheating with generative artificial intelligence, The Guardian reported. Across two sittings, in April and July, he missed one question out of one hundred sixty. The confession came on Sunday, after months of doubt about marks that looked unreal.

The same report said organisers of the national college entrance exam this Thursday now carry a sharper risk. It did not describe a penalty already handed down.

SpaceX, in news that counts as progress worth knowing, caught Starship with the arms of the launch tower after the vehicle came back from orbit, a first, the Associated Press reported. France 24 said the catch followed a miss the day before. Earlier, smaller rockets have landed again, but they carry heavy legs and are not fully reusable. The articles stop at the catch.

They do not name a next flight date.

To really understand a fight over chip rules, separate the person who buys the processor from the person who rents the work it does. What follows is this show’s own explanation of that mechanism, not a finding copied from today’s reporting. Rules of this sort usually stick to a sale: who may purchase the physical chip, and which border that box may cross.

A warehouse of computers in a country allowed to buy them can still sell hours of calculation to a customer in a country barred from buying the hardware. The machine never moves. The questions and the answers do. A tight reading says the paying customer is the real user, so a serious control has to read the service contract, not only the shipping label.

A narrow reading says a legal sale, in a legal country, should not turn every later cloud customer into a sanctions case. Next time a draft rule appears, look for one line: does it bind the buyer of the hardware, or also the customer who pays for the computing time?
