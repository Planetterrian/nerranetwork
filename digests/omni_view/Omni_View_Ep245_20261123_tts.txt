Seeing your own side break a rule outright nearly doubled the chance that people would break it too, in an online test led by Lucas Molleman at the University of Amsterdam, Deutsche Welle reported. The team recruited seven hundred fifty adults in the United States, about half Democrats and half Republicans, a week before the twenty twenty-four presidential election.

A shooting star crossed one hundred squares. The brightest square was always near the middle, but squares farther right paid more, so a cheat was worth money. Alone, people picked that farthest square about twenty-three percent of the time. After they saw someone from their own party do it, the share was almost forty percent. A small bend in the rule had only a small effect.

Just over four percent then went further than the person they had watched. A clean example rarely changed the next choice. There was no punishment, and nothing outside the screen was at stake. Some people obeyed every time. Some cheated every time, no matter what they were shown. In simulations of groups of one hundred, the few who escalate could pull the group into heavy rule-breaking.

Molleman said actors like that can take the guardrails off.

The split is whether that pattern is contagion or something that only travels inside a group. Molleman's numbers say a blatant break by your own side did the damage, and a good example did not repair it.

Stephen Reicher, at the University of St Andrews, told the Guardian that influence which depends on who the other person is does not spread like a disease, and that people who see a riot often come back to clean up. To really understand a climate cover text, forget the idea of a majority vote — what follows is my own explanation of that rule, not a line taken from one article today. The big political statement at these summits is adopted by consensus. That means it passes only if no government in the room blocks it.

A large group can want a paragraph and still watch the whole text die, because one holdout is enough. Side pledges are a different instrument. Countries that agree with each other can announce a target together without waiting for the single text everyone must sign. So a meeting can fail on its cover statement and still produce a coalition promise, and those are not the same piece of paper.

Next time a headline says a climate meeting collapsed or broke through, check which document it means: the text that needed consent from all, or a pledge a smaller group made on its own.
