One hundred twenty-eight countries at a United Nations conference in Geneva have agreed to elaborate a possible accord on lethal autonomous weapons over the next two years, Al Jazeera reports. These are machines that can kill without a human making the final decision to fire.

The Saturday decision came under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, a treaty on weapons considered especially harmful. Nearly one hundred nations had pushed for swift, legally binding rules. The United States, Russia, and Israel opposed that push. The convention decides only if every member agrees.

Diplomats said Russia’s objections on procedure led to some formal sessions being called off, and talks on the final wording ran through the night. Swiss chair Julien Thoeni said some countries rejected the word negotiate, so the text says elaboration instead. Reuters headlines the same decision as talks aimed at regulating these weapons by twenty twenty-eight.

The states that want a ban and the states that blocked one are talking about the same machines, under a treaty that cannot move unless every member says yes.

Nicole van Rooijen, executive director of Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of three hundred organizations, told Agence France-Presse that autonomous weapons are already in combat, including systems that use artificial intelligence to choose targets, and that delaying a binding instrument puts civilians at risk. The coalition called the mandate watered down, with no clear route to binding rules.

Verity Coyle, deputy director for arms at Human Rights Watch, told the agency the outcome was disappointing and did not match the urgency, because the technology is racing ahead.

Thoeni’s argument for the wording that passed is that elaborate was the verb objectors would sign, and that more than ten years of talks still produced a step: the process can sort which weapons international humanitarian law, the rules for protecting civilians in war, can ban, and which it can permit but regulate.

Will the text, when it is written, name any weapon that must be banned, or only a process for talking further before twenty twenty-eight?

Levent Alpoge, a mathematician on the technical staff of Anthropic, an artificial-intelligence company, says he used the company’s Claude tool to produce a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, Deutsche Welle reports. He posted the claim on his website on Friday. He believes he has, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. Bernhard Riemann stated it in eighteen fifty-nine.

It is about how prime numbers, numbers divisible only by themselves and one, are spaced as they get larger. It is one of seven Millennium Prize Problems chosen by the Clay Mathematics Institute, a U S institute that offers one million dollars for a solution to each.

Alpoge said the work generated more than two hundred fifty thousand pages, and that he had not yet mastered all the mathematics involved. He wrote that Claude far outpaced him near the finish line. Other mathematicians still have to check the proof.

Martin Bridson, president of the Clay institute, called the announcement one of historic significance in a statement quoted by the Associated Press, and said it holds the promise of a dramatic change in what people think is possible in mathematics. He also said the journey from the announcement to a place of real understanding will be long and difficult.

In September, Open A I said its technology had helped with a problem on the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe how fluids such as water and air move, another of the seven prize problems. Until other mathematicians sign off, it remains a claim, not a settled theorem.

To really understand a demand that one capital confine a visitor’s papers to its own borders, you have to see that a European short-stay visa is a shared door, not a private key. What follows is this host’s own explanation of that mechanism, not a finding from today’s articles.

Most European Union countries, plus a few neighbors, dropped routine checks on the borders between them and kept checks on the outside edge. A short-stay visa is usually issued by one state but written for the whole area, because the point of abolishing internal controls was that a visitor cleared once should not be stopped at every next frontier.

The issuing country does the paperwork and is supposed to be the main destination, yet the document is not that country’s private pass. Any state on the external border can still refuse entry if the traveler fails the common conditions, purpose, funds, a return ticket, security flags, even with a visa in the passport. That is a refusal at the door, not a rewrite of the visa’s map.

Changing the map, so that a visa issued in one capital works only there, is a change to a shared instrument. It needs the states that issue the common visa to agree, because a government that cannot print a Europe-wide visa by itself also cannot, alone, un-print the Europe-wide visas other capitals have already issued.

Next time a government says it will confine that visa to one country, check whether the text is an instruction to its own consulates or a proposal the other issuing states still have to adopt.
