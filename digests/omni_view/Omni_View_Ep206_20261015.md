## Understanding the Issue: How the EU negotiates its seven-year budget
Most coverage treats the EU’s multi-year budget as a simple spending plan, yet the process rests on a constitutional requirement for unanimous approval among all member states. The budget, formally called the Multiannual Financial Framework, sets spending ceilings for seven years and must be agreed before the period begins; failure to reach consensus by the deadline can trigger automatic extensions of the prior framework at reduced real terms. Negotiations begin with a European Commission proposal that balances net contributors’ demands for restraint against net recipients’ claims for cohesion and agricultural funds. Because every country holds a veto, deals typically emerge only after side payments, revised spending headings, and last-minute summit bargaining that can stretch across multiple European Council meetings. The current talks cover the 2028-2034 period and come less than a week after the Commission reached an agreement with Beijing to curb imports of hybrid cars from China. Knowing this structure shows why reports of “breakthroughs” at a single summit are often preliminary and why delays until a later special meeting are routine rather than surprising. When you next read that leaders “failed to agree,” check whether the article specifies which spending category remains blocked and which governments hold the decisive veto rather than assuming general deadlock. The same dynamic explains why German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and French President Emmanuel Macron have already called for new measures to counter market distortion while net recipients emphasise the need for continued cohesion funds.

## Media-literacy note
Cross-reference official European Council conclusions with national government statements to see which compromises each capital claims as its own. Primary documents such as the Commission’s budget proposal and the final regulation text provide the clearest record of what actually changed.
