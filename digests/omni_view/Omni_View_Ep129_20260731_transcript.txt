Now, to really understand this story, there's something most coverage leaves out.
Most coverage of factory slowdowns treats the monthly purchasing managers index as a simple up-or-down signal.
Yet, the index is built from a survey of purchasing managers who answer questions about new orders, output, employment, and inventories on a scale that produces a single diffusion number.
When the headline figure drops below 50, it means more firms reported contraction than expansion in the previous month.
But the underlying components can move in different directions, revealing whether weakness is concentrated in exports, domestic demand, or hiring plans.
The survey is conducted by a private firm in many countries and by official statistical agencies and others, so timing, sample size, and seasonal adjustments differ and can produce conflicting readings even for the same economy.
In China, the official version draws from a larger state-linked sample while a separate private gauge often shows sharper swings.
Knowing this, when you hear a single PMI number cited as proof of recession or recovery, you can check whether the sub indices for new orders and employment moved in the same direction, and whether the survey method matches the one used in prior months.
The concrete thing to verify next time is whether the reporting agency publishes the full component breakdown and the response rate for that month's survey so readers can judge how representative the headline number actually is.
