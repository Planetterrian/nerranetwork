## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Poll finds most Americans view Iran conflict as not worth the cost
**What happened (neutral):** A new AP-NORC poll shows that most Americans believe the war in Iran has not been worth fighting. The results also indicate that public goals for the conflict differ from those stated by President Trump. The survey captures views across party lines at a moment when U.S. involvement continues. Respondents were asked directly about the value of the military effort and about the specific objectives they consider most important. The poll was conducted by the Associated Press in partnership with NORC at the University of Chicago.

**Context & perspectives:** The poll comes from a joint project of the Associated Press and NORC at the University of Chicago, which has tracked opinion on foreign engagements for years. Administration supporters argue that the poll reflects short-term fatigue rather than a full assessment of strategic aims such as preventing Iranian nuclear advances. Critics of continued involvement, including some members of Congress from both parties, point to the same numbers as evidence that the costs in lives and resources have already exceeded any achievable gains. The factual baseline is undisputed: the survey sampled U.S. adults and found majority opposition to ongoing operations. The split centers on whether that opposition should alter operational decisions or simply register public sentiment. White House statements have continued to emphasize the importance of the mission even as the poll numbers circulated.

**STEEL-MAN THE DISAGREEMENT:** One position holds that presidents retain constitutional authority to direct military operations even when polls show public doubt, because elected leaders must weigh long-term security interests that surveys cannot fully capture; supporters of this view note that past presidents continued engagements after similar readings. The competing position maintains that sustained public opposition eventually constrains funding and troop commitments through congressional action, and that ignoring clear polling risks repeating earlier episodes in which domestic support eroded mid-conflict. Each side concedes that polls measure sentiment at one moment and do not dictate every tactical choice.

## Understanding the Issue: How U.S. public opinion on foreign conflicts reaches policymakers
Most coverage treats poll numbers on wars as simple popularity ratings, yet the path from survey to decision runs through congressional funding votes, military planning cycles, and White House calculations about re-election coalitions. When a poll such as the AP-NORC survey records majority opposition, it first registers with members of Congress who control annual defense appropriations; those members then weigh whether to attach conditions or limits in the next spending bill. Historical cases show that sustained polling weakness has sometimes prompted presidents to accelerate negotiations or reduce troop levels even while publicly defending the mission. Knowing this sequence lets readers distinguish between a poll that merely records sentiment and one that is already shaping budget hearings or diplomatic timelines. The concrete check is to watch whether the next defense appropriations markup includes new reporting requirements or spending caps tied to the conflict in question.
