The Economist’s Big Mac index has now been used as a currency yardstick for forty years.

It began as a jokey comparison: take the local price of a McDonald’s burger, convert it into one currency, and see whether a nation’s money looks expensive or cheap against the dollar.

Over four decades that burger math has been cited in academic papers and in central-bank work on purchasing power.

The newspaper marked the anniversary by looking back at how the same simple measure has followed exchange-rate swings in dozens of countries.

What started as an informal thought experiment now sits inside serious discussion of what money can actually buy, and of how far official rates drift from street prices.

Economists in more than one region have copied the idea, using a single everyday product to track inflation and trade distortions without waiting on figures that later get revised.

The index is refreshed each year and has spread into more markets as McDonald’s itself has spread.

Its staying power is the method: the same basket, year after year, so a comparison still means something when government statistics are restated.

To really understand how a middle power resets its alliances when relations with Washington turn rough, set aside the idea that a leader’s trip is only a courtesy call.

The usual mistake in the coverage is to treat outreach as a single day’s diplomacy, when the machinery underneath is older and more technical than a podium.

Countries that are not superpowers have long kept several trade and security relationships at once, precisely so no single partner can dictate the terms.

When United States policy shifts and uncertainty rises, governments of that size have often widened their work with the EU or with Asia-Pacific groups.

They do it through formal deals on tariffs, through lining up regulations so goods can cross borders without a second test, and through joint investment funds.

The same logic showed up in earlier tariff fights, when Ottawa sped up talks with Brussels on auto and farm standards rather than waiting for North American weather to clear.

The sequence is rarely a surprise once you know it: technical working groups go first, mapping which rules already overlap, and only then do political leaders talk about wider cooperation.

Later, those quiet maps can harden into memoranda that actually cut tariffs, or that accept another country’s safety or product standards on a defined list of goods.

So when a speech is billed as high-stakes, the useful test is whether energy or digital-trade memoranda, or any other binding text, appear beside the adjectives.

In the days after an event, the concrete check is not the tone of the first announcement; it is whether joint working groups or funding pledges are named, and whether the wording of a communiqué matches the text of any new agreement.
