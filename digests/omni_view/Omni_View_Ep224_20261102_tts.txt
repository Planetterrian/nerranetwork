To really understand how an emergency alert can still reach a phone when ordinary networks fail, you have to follow the path the message takes.

A lot of daily coverage treats a breached alert as simple vandalism.

These systems are built as one of the few reliable channels left when phone networks and broadcast towers are damaged or jammed.

Governments preload the apps on devices sold in the country.

They push messages through dedicated servers that bypass normal cellular congestion.

That is why the same app can still function when voice calls fail.

This model was developed after earlier conflicts showed that traditional sirens and television alerts often reach too few people too late.

This design creates a single point of failure.

Once an attacker gains server access, false messages travel at the same speed and with the same authority as real ones.

Users have only minutes to decide whether to act.

When later stories assign blame, the technical detail that sorts a claim is origin — official server logs, or a cloned site that mimicked the app's appearance.

A concrete check in the next round of reporting is whether authorities publish those server access logs, or only issue verbal denials.
