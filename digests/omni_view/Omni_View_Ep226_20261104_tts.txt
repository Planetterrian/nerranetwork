Midterm coverage often treats a congressional win as one event, yet each chamber has its own rulebook for how fast a party can rewrite the law, and to really understand the mechanism you have to watch the House and the Senate as different machines.

The House of Representatives has four hundred thirty-five voting seats, each representing a district of roughly equal population, and every one of those seats is contested every two years, so the full membership can be on the ballot in a single cycle.

The Senate has one hundred seats, with one-third of members elected every six years, giving that chamber greater continuity than the House and spreading change across several elections instead of one night.

Because most seats are safely held by one party, control usually hinges on a small number of competitive districts and states, and the Guardian's interactive results page explains that experts classify only a handful of races as genuine toss-ups each cycle, which is why a shift of just a few seats can flip an entire chamber.

Before the election, the Cook Report classified six Senate races as genuine toss-ups, while Democrats were favored in enough House seats to take the chamber without winning any of the eighteen seats the Cook Political Report classed as toss-ups, and thirty-three of the one hundred Senate seats were up for election along with two special elections.

Republicans were defending a majority in the House, where Democrats held two hundred twelve seats and needed two hundred eighteen for control, gubernatorial elections were taking place in thirty-six states, and a large popular-vote margin does not automatically translate into proportional legislative power, so when a claim lands that one party now controls everything, check whether the account distinguishes the short-term House majority from the longer-term Senate calendar.
