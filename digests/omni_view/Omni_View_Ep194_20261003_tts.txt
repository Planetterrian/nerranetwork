To really understand how proportional elections shape coalitions in small states, begin with the seat allocation rule itself.

Seats are distributed roughly in line with each party's share of the total vote across one hundred positions rather than awarding all seats to the largest vote getter.

This means even a party that finishes first usually falls short of the fifty one seats needed for a majority on its own.

Coalition formation then requires negotiations that trade policy concessions and cabinet posts among several groups.

The same pattern has appeared in other small European parliaments where no single bloc dominates, producing governments that reflect compromises rather than outright victories.

Knowing this, when reports emphasize one party's poll lead, readers can assess whether that margin is large enough to avoid lengthy bargaining or whether smaller parties will hold decisive influence.
