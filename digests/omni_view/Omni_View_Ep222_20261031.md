## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Trump administration seeks citizenship proof requirement for US midterms
**What happened (neutral):** The Trump administration has pressed states to adopt new federal-level checks, including the SAVE Act that would require documentary proof of US citizenship to register or vote. An AP-NORC poll found 55 percent of Democrats confident the final count will be accurate, against 38 percent of Republicans. US elections are run by states under the Constitution, and citizenship is already required with ID checks in place. The administration has repeatedly claimed large numbers of noncitizens are voting, even though records show such cases are exceedingly rare and subject to fines, prison or deportation. The Heritage Foundation database lists 100 cases of noncitizen voting from 1982 to 2025, representing roughly 0.000008 percent of more than 1.3 billion votes cast in presidential elections during that period. The United States does not issue a national identity card, so driver’s licenses and other state documents would not meet the proposed standard unless they record place of birth.

**Context & perspectives:** France24 describes repeated administration attempts to shape nearly every stage of the process ahead of the November 3 vote, noting Trump's past challenges to results and claims of noncitizen voting that supporting think tanks have not substantiated at scale. The outlet points to the absence of a national ID card and the limited evidence of fraud in existing records. Jonathan Diaz of the Campaign Legal Center observed that the administration has tried to insert the federal government into almost every aspect of the election process, creating an atmosphere of fear and uncertainty even when changes have not succeeded. The piece also records that think tanks aligned with the administration’s goals have themselves found no evidence of widespread fraud.

**STEEL-MAN THE DISAGREEMENT:**
Advocates for the SAVE Act maintain that explicit documentary proof closes even small vulnerabilities in a system where public trust is already divided along party lines, thereby strengthening the perceived legitimacy of outcomes without altering who is eligible to vote. They note that penalties for noncitizen attempts already exist and argue the requirement aligns with basic verification standards used in other official processes. They further contend that the current patchwork of state rules leaves room for doubt that only a uniform federal standard can resolve.

Opponents argue that the rule adds a barrier where none is needed, given that noncitizen voting remains exceedingly rare and states already enforce citizenship through existing ID systems, risking reduced participation among eligible citizens who lack ready access to the specific documents required. They point out that the absence of a national ID means many lawful voters would need to obtain new paperwork, and they note that existing penalties and ID requirements already deter violations at rates below one in ten million votes.

## Understanding the Issue: How US states run federal elections
Most coverage of federal election disputes skips over a basic constitutional fact: the US Constitution assigns primary responsibility for running elections to the states, not the federal government. Each state sets its own voter registration rules, ballot design, polling locations, and counting procedures, subject only to federal laws that protect voting rights and prevent discrimination. This structure dates to the founding and means that even when Congress passes a law such as the SAVE Act, states must still enact and administer the change through their own systems. The absence of a national ID card further complicates uniform proof-of-citizenship requirements, because states issue driver’s licenses and other documents that may or may not record place of birth. The SAVE Act would require documentary proof of citizenship, yet no national document exists that automatically satisfies the standard for every eligible voter. Knowing this, when you hear claims that the federal government can simply “take over” election administration, you can check whether the proposed change actually alters state procedures or merely adds conditions that states must implement. The next time a federal proposal appears, look at the specific language directing states to update their registration databases and see which state officials would have to carry it out. You can also examine how states have responded to earlier federal voting laws to gauge the practical steps required for compliance.
