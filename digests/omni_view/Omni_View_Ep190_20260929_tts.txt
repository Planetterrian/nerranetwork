American forces are scheduled to leave their last remaining bases in Iraq by Wednesday.

The pullout follows an agreement reached in 2024 under President Joe Biden and carried out by the Trump administration.

Iraqi security experts say the withdrawal removes a military presence that has acted as a counterweight to Iranian influence for years.

The move occurs even as Washington continues military action against Tehran.

Iran and its allies inside Iraq view the departure as a strategic victory that could allow greater influence over security and political decisions.

Jasim al-Bahadli, an Iraqi security analyst specialising in armed Shiite groups, stated that pro-Iranian militia groups will portray the exit as a victory and seek to translate that momentum into greater control.

Other observers note that the timeline was set years earlier through bilateral talks, with the 2024 framework fixing the sequence of base closures and equipment transfers.

Iraqi officials have described the process as the end of a foreign military footprint that began after 2003.

Those same officials warn that the vacuum could also allow groups such as Islamic State to stage a comeback.

The South China Morning Post frames the exit as the conclusion of long-planned bilateral steps rather than a sudden shift.

Al Jazeera emphasises how the withdrawal opens space for Iranian-aligned actors to expand their role in Iraqi affairs.

Even those who see strategic gains for Iran acknowledge that the original 2024 pact still permits some limited training and intelligence cooperation after combat units depart.

What happens next: the first base closures begin this week, and Iraqi security forces will assume full control of the remaining sites.

To really understand how troop withdrawal timelines are set and executed, begin with the bilateral agreements that lock in both the schedule and the handover of facilities years before the final date.

In the Iraq case the 2024 framework set the sequence of base closures and equipment transfers, giving each side predictable steps rather than an abrupt departure.

Those agreements also include clauses on residual training missions or intelligence sharing that can continue after combat units leave.

The same structure appears in earlier U S drawdowns from other countries, where the calendar was adjusted only after new security guarantees were negotiated.

One concrete check is to look for the text of the original status-of-forces agreement and any later amendments that list the exact bases and dates involved.

Another is to compare the original timeline with any public updates issued by the governments involved.

Readers can also examine whether equipment transfers or training programmes are mentioned separately from the combat-unit exit.

This approach reveals whether the withdrawal is truly complete or whether narrower forms of cooperation persist under the same legal umbrella.
