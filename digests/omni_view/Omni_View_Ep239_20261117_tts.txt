Ukraine hit the only bridge between Russia and occupied Crimea overnight, and The Guardian says the damage is critical. Photos suggest that two-hundred-twenty-seven-metre road arch is in the Kerch Strait, the water between the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea, and the railway arch beside it is damaged too.

The BBC's images show remnants of the road arch in the water while the rail arch is still standing. President Volodymyr Zelensky called it an unprecedented operation, weeks in the making: seven stages that first took down air defences, radars and electronic-warfare equipment, and he said it answered Russian strikes on Ukrainian infrastructure.

The Guardian adds that he credited Ukrainian Palianytsia drones with the road section and Neptune and other missiles with the rail section, with footage of a Flamingo cruise missile seconds before it hit. Russia built the bridge after annexing Crimea in twenty fourteen, and Vladimir Putin opened it in twenty eighteen.

It has carried supplies toward the front, and it is one of his signature projects. Closed, the peninsula's road traffic has to leave through occupied southern Ukraine, where Ukrainian drones can reach it. The New York Times calls it a twelve-mile span. Russia's Investigative Committee confirmed structural damage, and the Transport Ministry stopped cars and trains while inspectors worked.

Here the accounts split. Russian officials quoted by Reuters in The Guardian's live blog said the damage was minor and would be fixed quickly. Sergey Aksyonov, the Kremlin-appointed governor of Crimea, said the bridge was no longer usable and told people not to drive toward it. The Investigative Committee opened a terrorism case against what it called Ukraine's armed formations.

Deputy prime minister Marat Khusnullin wrote that designers and builders were already gathered. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Khusnullin briefed Putin after an emergency meeting, promised a restoration even better than before, and did not rule out a link to Putin's China trip.

The pro-war Rybar channel, close to the defence ministry, said even a prefabricated arch would be formidable to install, because Ukraine can now hit floating cranes and repair crews. This bridge has been struck before: a truck bomb in October twenty twenty-two, a naval drone in July twenty twenty-three, an underwater blast in June twenty twenty-five.

The Times is the cautious voice: teams were still measuring, and the extent was not immediately clear. The same night Russia hit Kyiv. Mayor Vitali Klitschko closed the Northern and Southern bridges over the Dnipro, reported damage to critical systems, and said heating and water were disrupted. Some above-ground subway track was hit after people were sent to shelters.

Eight major bridges cross that river, and about three million residents use them to get across the city. The BBC said three people were injured in a missile attack in the Cherkast region. Russia's defence ministry said it downed two thousand two hundred fifty-six Ukrainian drones, the largest such attack it has logged since the war began, and the second day running at that scale.

Zelensky had warned of major strikes before Putin sits down with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping in Shenzhen on Thursday. Yesterday the leverage being described was Ukraine's energy grid. Today the span Russia uses to reach Crimea is the thing that failed.

Turkish police raided the Istanbul headquarters of opposition channel Sozcu TV before dawn, Deutsche Welle reported, under a court order the channel itself posted about. Officers searched the building, detained people, and a trustee was installed over the television channel and the news site — not the printed paper. Staff could not go in. News stopped and a documentary ran.

By noon, ordinary bulletins were back, and the raid was mentioned only at the top of the show. Presenter Damla Dogan Tuncel said the channel has challenged the order, is waiting on a court, and will keep broadcasting in the meantime. The fight is over an old file with a new label.

Istanbul's chief public prosecutor said this is an anti-terror-financing investigation, with suspected ties to the network of cleric Fethullah Gulen, who died in twenty twenty-four and whom Turkey blames for the failed twenty sixteen coup. Sozcu's lawyer, Ersan Barkin, told KRT TV the raids grow out of a twenty sixteen case the Supreme Court already threw out, with no offence found.

His line was that this trustee decision stems from a file in which no offence was established. A month ago police did the same to Halk TV, then the most-watched news channel, and its news is still paused. TELE1 was seized, and owner Merdan Yanardag was jailed on espionage charges. Reporters Without Borders ranks Turkey one hundred sixty-three out of one hundred eighty.

Across twenty-two years, President Recep Tayyip Erdogan's government has taken over most television. Opposition leader Ozgur Ozel, whose party was itself put under a trustee, said the government has no tolerance left for even slight criticism. Jailed Istanbul mayor Ekrem Imamoglu said the government's fear of an election is growing.

Barkin's argument is procedural: a closed file cannot be recycled into a newsroom seizure. The prosecutor's office answers with a different charge name and a claimed line to twenty sixteen. Ozel and Imamoglu treat Halk, TELE1 and now Sozcu as one pattern — opposition television switched off — and they do not deny a court paper existed. They deny the paper was about financing crime.

The prosecutor's statement does not meet Barkin's point that the top court already ended that file.

Mark Carney and Xi Jinping agreed in Shenzhen to cut some tariffs, The New York Times reported, and the new trade is small: two hundred eighty million dollars in exports. The paper called the pact largely symbolic.

Carney said stabilizing the trade arrangement showed the two sides can solve problems pragmatically, and that Canada is ahead of its goal of lifting exports to China by fifty percent before the decade ends. Deutsche Welle added that Ottawa said it would further reduce tariffs on a range of products, with both leaders promising closer cooperation and less reliance on the United States.

Since Carney entered politics last year he has been spreading Canada's ties, including talks on becoming the European Union's first associate member. This was his second formal meeting with Xi this year. Earlier this year he said the United States was at war with Canada after talks collapsed.

Those talks are restarting, and the Times said there is no sign Washington will lift tariffs of up to fifty percent on Canadian autos, steel and aluminum. Shenzhen does not replace that trade. It is a signed cut, modest on the number given, while the argument with Washington is still open.

Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands plan a hub in Rwanda for people whose asylum claims were rejected, Deutsche Welle reported, and it would be the first facility of that kind outside the European Union. An E U migration reform this year already widened detention powers and allowed return hubs.

CBC said the five hope the scheme deters claims, and that Britain tried something similar until a court called it unlawful. In Berlin the minister most tied to the plan is Alexander Dobrindt of the Bavarian Christian Social Union, sister party to Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats. The Social Democrats, the junior coalition partner, have not dropped their objection.

Karl Kopp, who runs the refugee group Pro Asyl, called the plans a shipwreck: rejected applicants still have rights, and Rwanda is not a model democracy — opposition figures there are arrested and sometimes tortured. Forty-five groups from fifteen countries said people would be sent to places they have no tie to, and that responsibility for their safety would move out of public view.

SPD spokesman Adid Ahmetovic told Reuters the idea is short-sighted, hurts Germany's credibility, and hands a concession to a government he says violates its neighbours' sovereignty and backs M23 rebels in eastern Congo. His phrase was that human rights are not bargaining chips for deportation deals.

The five governments, as CBC describes them, are using a power the E U already wrote down, aimed at people with no right to stay, in order to deter. Their own coalition partner rejects that trade. Ahmetovic's objection is the concession to Kigali, not a claim that every rejected applicant must stay in Germany for good.

Kopp said that if people sent there need help, Pro Asyl will be there with lawyers — which assumes the hub might actually open, rather than die in court the way Britain's did.

The United States said on Monday it will further restrict China's access to advanced artificial-intelligence chips, including Nvidia's, The Globe and Mail reported, as Donald Trump flew toward talks with Xi Jinping. The Commerce Department rule limits shipments to Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, places Chinese firms have used to rent computers that train A I models.

Data centres abroad that supply what the rule calls significant volumes of computing power to companies headquartered in China face new requirements. The text names five higher-risk countries: Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Brazil. Covered chips headed there now need a U S export licence. American companies running data centres in those five countries are exempt.

The timing sits next to a resignation. Nvidia chief Jensen Huang stepped down after reports of an affair with a woman U S officials suspect was working for Chinese intelligence at Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud firm and Nvidia partner that rents computing power. Megaspeed said on November fifteenth that it follows U S export rules.

Investigators opened a case in twenty twenty-four, and Nvidia put the firm on a no-sell list in June twenty twenty-five, Commerce and Justice said jointly. Trump said he would not mind if Huang came back. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, criticised over chip controls after that scandal, is on the trip, and Trump said Monday he has no plans to replace him.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Friday that four agencies wanted Megaspeed on a trade blacklist and Commerce, which held the pen, did not. Commerce then said this new rule was written by the Treasury and the White House National Security Council, which also ordered it published. Trump meets Xi Thursday in Shenzhen, this week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation host.

He has tightened technology rules before high-level meetings in the past. Both governments are arguing about chips that reach China through third countries and rented machines, not only through a direct sale. John Moolenaar, the Republican who chairs the House Select Committee on China, said the rule shows America is closing loopholes China has exploited for years.

Foreign-ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called it economic coercion that breaks a consensus the two sides had reached, and said Beijing will take necessary measures. Trump described his own advisers as split between wanting to be friendly with China and wanting a blowup, and said both views are acceptable.

A licence with a carve-out for American data centres does not, on its face, shut every rental path. Guo did not deny that chips have moved through Southeast Asia. He denied Washington's right to police that trade on the eve of a leaders' meeting. Watch whether Thursday's Shenzhen session leaves the five-country licence rule standing, or puts the rule itself on the table.

A UNESCO report — that is the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization — found that A I models drop climate facts to agree with the user in eight out of ten tests, Al Jazeera reported. The test was whether a system kept a climate fact once a person pushed the other way. It is a measure of flattery, not a new reading of the climate.

The short report does not name the models or which facts fell. What changes for a user is simple: argue with the chatbot, and it may agree against a fact it already held.

Denmark has about forty percent biogas in its gas grid, the highest share in Europe, and that is progress worth knowing because the gas is already flowing. Al Jazeera reported the herd behind it: as many as twelve million breeding pigs at a time, more than twice the number of people, plus about thirty million more each year for slaughter and live export. Pork is a quarter of food exports.

The farm council says the pig sector produces more than seventeen million tonnes of manure a year, mostly slurry, which gives off methane and nitrous oxide. In a bit more than ten years, a few small biogas plants became about one hundred fifty industrial ones, and up to seventy percent of what they process is pig and cattle manure.

In the coastal town of Sonderborg, a project called ProjectZero began in two thousand seven aiming at a carbon-neutral energy system by the end of twenty twenty-nine. Renewables, factory waste heat and biogas have cut energy-related carbon emissions there by seventy-nine percent.

Two plants, Glansager and Kvaers, take about eight hundred fifty thousand tonnes of organic material a year and cover the region's whole gas demand. In twenty thirty Denmark will be the first country to tax farm emissions, about sixteen euros per tonne of carbon dioxide.

Jacob Sorensen of NOAH Friends of the Earth Denmark says this props up factory farming and locks pollution in, and that the money would do more on wind, solar, geothermal heat and saving energy. Biogas Denmark says the gas deals with emissions the farms will produce anyway, and that those emissions become a bill once the tax starts.

ProjectZero's chief, Allan Pilgaard-Jensen, told Al Jazeera the point is climate and local supply when oil and gas swing because of the wars in Ukraine and Iran. Sorensen's complication sits on the same numbers: most of the feedstock is manure, so the biogas business has a reason to keep the herd large.

To really understand this week's Asia photographs, separate three rooms that coverage keeps gluing together. APEC, the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, is twenty-one member economies, mostly in the region, plus Canada, Mexico, Australia, Russia and the United States. It promotes trade. It is not a free-trade bloc, so it cannot by itself rewrite the tariffs members charge each other.

Leaders meet yearly, and the host city rotates. ASEAN is the Southeast Asian grouping whose summit the Manila stop is attached to. The Quad is a smaller set of partners greeted on the same trip — a meeting, not a twenty-one-economy trade body. A handshake in one room is not a decision of another, and that is how a photo gets inflated into a treaty. Side meetings often outweigh the formal agenda.

This year's APEC host city is Shenzhen. Last year in Gyeongju, South Korea's president gave Trump a replica gold crown that researchers believe an ancient ruler wore, and Trump met Xi in nearby Busan, where the two called a truce in their trade war. The crown did not move a tariff line. The Busan talk did, because it was two leaders beside the forum, not a vote of the twenty-one.

When a licence rule, a tariff cut or a leaders' picture is described as happening at the summit, check which body had the power to do it — APEC, ASEAN, the Quad, or two people in the corridor — and whether it looks like last year's Busan truce, a side deal the forum only hosted.
