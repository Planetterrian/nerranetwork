After that result, Trump is on the ground in Shenzhen with a split among Ukraine’s allies, Brussels has put a loan on the table for Kyiv, and crowds in Port-au-Prince turned out for foreign troops.

Mary Peltola, a Democrat, has taken Alaska’s Senate seat from two-term Republican Dan Sullivan. Al Jazeera, citing The Associated Press, puts her at fifty point one percent of first-choice votes to his forty-five point eight, with about ninety-nine percent counted. The BBC says that confirmation caps the Democrats’ rout in the midterm Senate races.

It is the party’s first Senate win in Alaska in more than a decade. Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, which means a majority of first-choice votes elects someone outright and no later rounds are counted. Peltola is Yup’ik.

She became the first Alaska Native in Congress by winning a special election for the state’s only House seat in twenty twenty-two, served until January twenty twenty-five, and lost that seat in twenty twenty-four.

A second man, retired teacher Daniel J. Sullivan, stood with no party label beside the incumbent, listed as Dan S Sullivan. In June the state elections division took the teacher off the ballot, saying the name was chosen to confuse voters and noting a consultant who had worked for Peltola. A superior court judge put him back, and the Alaska Supreme Court agreed on June twenty-ninth.

The Trump administration alleged that Peltola’s campaign recruited that second Sullivan on purpose, a tactic called ballot stacking, to split Republican votes. Al Jazeera reports that even if every namesake vote had gone to the senator, he still would have lost.

Scott Lucas, of University College Dublin’s Clinton Institute, told Al Jazeera she campaigned as a pragmatist on energy jobs, the border and guns, and played down the Democratic label, while Sullivan carried the cost of Trump’s ticket.

The administration’s charge is that a lookalike name was a decoy, and that the old consultant link shows it was not a random independent bid. The record the courts and the count produced is different: judges said the teacher could stay on the ballot, and the tally Al Jazeera cites from the Associated Press shows those extra votes were not enough to flip the seat.

President Donald Trump is now in Shenzhen, after Manila, for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, CNBC reports, with talks set for Thursday with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin. NPR says Xi met him on the tarmac, matching a greeting Trump gave Xi in Washington in September.

CNBC says the three are expected to talk about the Iran war, Russia’s war in Ukraine and strategic stability, and that a concrete deal is not widely expected. China’s foreign ministry said Monday the discussions should go deep on peace and strategic stability.

Opening the summit, Xi urged free trade and cooperation on artificial intelligence, and said, “We should actively build partnerships for scientific and technological innovation, deepen cooperation in the digital economy and artificial intelligence and promote a green and low-carbon transition.” Putin told the hall that sanctions on Russia have backfired.

The Guardian’s live coverage has him calling Europe’s condition a humiliating decline.

Xi had already sat with Putin on Wednesday, before Trump landed. Chinese state media said Xi wanted steadier business ties and a multipolar world, and Putin talked trade, energy and education; those accounts skipped Ukraine and Iran. Xi also met South Korea’s Lee Jae Myung. Seoul said Korean Peninsula peace came up. Beijing’s write-up left that out.

A separate Trump-Xi meeting is meant to chase a trade truce past January twenty twenty-seven, while Washington still wants tighter controls on artificial intelligence chip exports. The New York Times reports that U.S. intelligence expects Putin to keep fighting in Ukraine anyway, and that Trump has paused some sanctions on Russia and said the United States will buy Russian diesel.

That same runway into Thursday is where Washington and two European allies are arguing about how Ukraine fights. CNBC reports the clash. At NATO headquarters on Tuesday, Undersecretary of Defense Elbridge Colby said Ukraine’s deep strikes inside Russia look like a spectacle: they have not ended the war and they have not slowed Russia at the front.

He said hits he treats as civilian — he named the retailer Wildberries — have probably stiffened Russian opinion and the Kremlin’s will to escalate. He also said Ukraine’s mid-range drones have been very effective, and that in October Ukraine took more ground than Russia for a second month running. Kyiv says the warehouses it struck were feeding Russian forces drone parts and navigation gear.

Wildberries and the Kremlin say the company is not part of the war. German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius said attacking criticism at strikes on strategic military and military-use targets is quite a stupid thing, and that the strikes worth condemning are Russia’s nightly hits on Ukraine’s energy, water and heat, which he called terrorism.

Britain’s Wes Streeting said it is not for outsiders to tell Ukraine how to fight, and that the campaign has worn down Putin’s war machine, buying time and saving lives.

Colby’s point is that blows far from the front line do not stop advances and can make escalation easier, while the fighting that changes the map is the closer drone war — and he grants that closer war is working.

Pistorius and Streeting answer that these are military-use targets, that Kyiv should choose its own tactics, and that the strikes cost Moscow; they do not, in these remarks, say deep strikes by themselves will end the war. By the end of Thursday, the check is whether Trump, Xi and Putin put out any shared line on those strikes, or only separate remarks.

Ursula von der Leyen, president of the European Commission, the European Union’s executive, on Wednesday proposed a loan of forty-five billion euros to Ukraine for twenty twenty-seven, France24 reports. She said it would cover about two-thirds of Kyiv’s financing needs, and that Russia would repay it later through war reparations.

France24 does not report that member governments have signed off, and it names no date for that vote. No opposing capital is quoted.

Thousands of people marched Tuesday in Port-au-Prince for Salvadoran soldiers President Nayib Bukele sent against the gangs, Al Jazeera reports. Signs said thank you, Bukele, and long live Bukele. He has sent more than two thousand troops since late October, after four Salvadoran soldiers went missing in an operation by the United Nations-backed Gang Suppression Force.

This newer deployment sits outside that force, and Haitian authorities have welcomed it. On November tenth Bukele said he had destroyed a main gang’s headquarters and put out video of a helicopter strike. Salvadoran troops and Haiti’s national police have since taken a football stadium in the capital.

His crackdown at home has wide public support and has drawn human rights accusations the report does not pin to a named group. The same day as the march, the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights told every security force in Haiti to protect civilians and to use lethal force only as a last resort.

In the first ten months of this year the United Nations counted four thousand five hundred ninety-five killings and two thousand fifty injuries tied to armed violence there.

Britain’s main inflation gauge hit a twelve-month high in October, the South China Morning Post reports from Wednesday’s release by the Office for National Statistics, the official numbers agency. The Consumer Prices Index was three point six percent, up from three point five in September, on petrol, diesel and tobacco duty. Economists had predicted that reading.

It was the fourth monthly rise in a row, and it moved inflation further from the Bank of England’s target. The report does not give the target number, and it quotes no one on the next rate decision.

In northern Thailand, authorities are hauling clean water to more than twenty thousand households in Chiang Rai after arsenic tied to mining in Myanmar showed up in the taps, Al Jazeera reports. This month they shut supply to thousands of homes in fourteen villages in Mae Sai, on the Myanmar border, after the local waterworks tested at thirty times the safe limit.

Untreated water in the Sai River, which feeds the Mekong, was twenty-five times the drinking limit. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul late Tuesday told agencies to find other sources and watch the taps, deputy spokeswoman Lalida Persvivatana said. Two trucks are carrying drinking water, and other rivers are under consideration.

Researchers at Naresuan University said last week that chemistry and satellite modelling traced much of the poisoned sediment to gold mines across the border. Rare earth digging in rebel-held Myanmar, feeding Chinese processing plants, is being blamed for heavy metals in Thai rivers, including the Mekong.

The civil war there lets that digging go unregulated; the metals go into phones, turbines and electric vehicles. Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow, in Myanmar last week, called the jump in Sai River pollution from mine wastewater dramatic and urgent. Bangkok says it has also taken the issue to Beijing. Researchers say many of those mines are Chinese-run.

Officials said arsenic showed up in the urine of seventeen of one hundred seventy-seven people tested in October in Nong Khai, beside Laos.

A payment that counts as progress worth knowing: Rio Tinto will pay two hundred fifty million dollars to address environmental damage from its long-closed mine in Papua New Guinea, The New York Times reports, and the paper calls the deal a landmark. The same money is meant to remedy human rights harms from that mine.

The limit is in the timeline — the pit is already shut, so this is money for damage done, not a fix at a working site. The report as given does not set the sum against an earlier offer or a full damage estimate.

To really understand a month when fuel prices shove inflation around, separate the bill at the pump from whether an economy can grow while burning less. People often treat growth and fossil fuels as locked together, so a dearer litre looks like proof that climate policy and output cannot share a year.

Deutsche Welle reports Earth has already crossed seven of nine planetary boundaries treated as critical for life, among them climate change, ocean acidification and the integrity of living systems.

Helmut Haberl, a social ecologist at BOKU University in Vienna, describes green growth as business as usual with more sustainable tools: wind and solar instead of coal and gas, heat pumps instead of gas furnaces, and more reuse of materials. With colleagues he published a twenty twenty report that weighed more than eight hundred studies.

Scenarios from the United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change show output can keep rising while emissions from coal, oil and gas fall hard, and Deutsche Welle says that split has already run for years in the United Kingdom, the United States, Japan and Australia, even counting offshore production.

In the European Union the economy has grown more than seventy percent since nineteen ninety while carbon emissions have fallen about forty percent, a full decoupling Haberl’s group still calls rare. In India and in several countries in Africa and Southeast Asia, each unit of output is cleaner, but emissions are still rising, which does not match the path for avoiding the worst climate damage.

Steel and chemicals are tougher because carbon sits in the chemistry, not only in the furnace, and stand-ins such as green hydrogen take heavy investment. Cheaper clean production can also rebound: firms and households use more, and part of the saving disappears.

A fuel-price headline, read that way, is a monthly cost story, not a verdict that growth and emissions must rise together, and one country’s split is not the world’s. Next time a government claims green growth, ask whether the emissions figure includes offshore production, and whether total stuff used actually fell or only the fuel per product.
