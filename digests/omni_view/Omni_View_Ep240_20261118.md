## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Mary Peltola wins Alaska’s Senate seat — CONTESTED
**What happened (neutral):** Democrat Mary Peltola has won Alaska’s United States Senate seat, unseating two-term Republican Dan Sullivan, Al Jazeera reports, with The Associated Press count at 50.1 percent of first-choice votes to Sullivan’s 45.8 percent and an estimated 99 percent of votes counted. The British Broadcasting Corporation, or BBC, reports that confirmation of her victory caps the Democrats’ rout of Republicans in the midterm Senate election. Al Jazeera reports her win gives Democrats 56 seats to the Republicans’ 44, and that it is the Democrats’ first Senate win in Alaska in more than a decade. Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, so a candidate with a majority of first-choice votes is elected outright, with no further rounds. Peltola, a Yup’ik Alaskan, was the first Alaska Native elected to Congress when she won a special election for the state’s only House seat in 2022; she served until January 2025 and lost re-election in 2024.

**Context & perspectives:** A second candidate, retired teacher Daniel J. Sullivan, appeared on the ballot with no party affiliation beside incumbent “Dan S Sullivan.” The state elections division removed the retired teacher in June, saying he had chosen the name to cause confusion and pointing to a consultant who had previously worked for Peltola; a superior court judge ruled him eligible, and the Alaska Supreme Court upheld that on June 29. The Trump administration alleged that Peltola’s campaign had deliberately recruited the second Sullivan to divide Republican votes, a practice called ballot stacking. Al Jazeera reports that even if the incumbent had received every vote cast for his namesake, he still would not have beaten Peltola. Scott Lucas, a professor at University College Dublin’s Clinton Institute, told Al Jazeera that Peltola is viewed as a pragmatist who played down the Democratic label and stressed energy jobs, border security and gun rights, and that Sullivan was hurt by being tied to President Donald Trump’s ticket.

The Trump administration’s case is that a near-identical name was put on the ballot to split Republican votes, and that a consultant linked to Peltola’s earlier campaign is evidence the second Sullivan was not an ordinary independent. The count and the courts cut the other way: judges kept Daniel J. Sullivan on the ballot as eligible, and the Associated Press tally, as reported by Al Jazeera, shows the namesake votes were not large enough to change the winner.

**What happened (neutral):** After a stop in Manila, President Donald Trump has landed in Shenzhen for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation, or APEC, summit, CNBC reports, ahead of talks on Thursday with Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russia’s Vladimir Putin. **Context & perspectives:** Xi met Putin earlier Wednesday, before Trump arrived; Chinese state media said Xi called for a more stable business environment and a “multipolar world,” while Putin spoke of expanding trade, energy and education, and those reports did not address Ukraine or Iran. Xi also met South Korea’s Lee Jae Myung; Seoul’s readout said they discussed peace on the Korean Peninsula, which Beijing’s record omitted. Trump is also due to meet Xi bilaterally, with hopes of a trade truce beyond January 2027, as the United States seeks to curb exports of artificial intelligence chips. The New York Times reports that U.S. intelligence says Putin will continue the Ukraine war despite the planned meeting, and that Trump has halted some sanctions on Russia and announced that the United States will buy Russian diesel.

Ahead of those talks, Germany and the United Kingdom rejected U.S. criticism of Ukraine’s deep strikes inside Russia, CNBC reports. At the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, or NATO, headquarters on Tuesday, U.S. Undersecretary of Defense Elbridge Colby said those deep strikes have not driven an end to the war or blunted Russia’s front-line advances, and that strikes he called hits on civilian infrastructure — citing retailer Wildberries — have probably hardened Russian public resolve. He also said Ukraine’s mid-range drone campaign has been “very effective,” and that Ukraine’s forces gained more territory than Russia in October for a second straight month. Kyiv says warehouses it hit supplied Russian forces with drone parts and navigation gear; Wildberries and the Kremlin deny the firm is involved in the war. German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius said criticizing strikes meant to destroy strategic military and military-use targets “is quite a stupid thing, I think,” and that criticism should instead fall on what he called nightly “terroristic” Russian strikes on Ukraine’s energy, water and heating. British Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said it is “not for us to tell Ukraine how to fight their war,” and that the strikes have degraded Putin’s war machine, “buying time and saving lives.”

Colby’s argument is that spectacle far behind the front does not stop Russian advances and can make the Kremlin more willing to escalate, while the campaign that actually matters is the one denying ground at the front — a limit he himself marks by calling that mid-range effort effective. Pistorius and Streeting answer that Ukraine is hitting military-use targets, that outsiders should not dictate Kyiv’s tactics, and that the strikes raise Moscow’s costs; they do not claim, in these remarks, that deep strikes alone will end the war.

**Questions to consider:** By the close of Thursday’s Shenzhen talks, is there any joint line from Trump, Xi and Putin on Ukraine’s strikes inside Russia, or only separate statements?

### 2) Arsenic from Myanmar mining shuts taps in northern Thailand
**What happened (neutral):** Thai authorities have rushed clean water to more than 20,000 households in Chiang Rai after unsafe arsenic, linked to mining in Myanmar, was found in the supply, Al Jazeera reports. Tap water to thousands of homes in 14 villages in Mae Sai district, near the Myanmar border, was suspended this month after arsenic in the local waterworks tested at 30 times the safe limit. Untreated water from the Sai River, a Mekong tributary, was at 25 times the drinking-water limit. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul late on Tuesday ordered agencies to find other sources and to watch tap quality, deputy government spokeswoman Lalida Persvivatana said. Two trucks are supplying drinking water, and the government is considering other rivers.

**Context & perspectives:** Researchers at Naresuan University said last week that chemical analysis and satellite modelling traced much of the arsenic-laden sediment in the Sai River to gold mining across the border in Myanmar. A surge in rare earth mining in rebel-held parts of Myanmar that supply Chinese processing plants is being blamed for heavy metals in Thai waterways, including the Mekong; Myanmar’s civil war enables unregulated digging of elements used in phones, wind turbines and electric vehicles. Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow, visiting Myanmar last week, called a “dramatic” rise in Sai River pollution from mining wastewater urgent. The Thai government says it has also raised the issue with Beijing; researchers say many of the Myanmar mines are Chinese-run. Officials said arsenic was found in the urine of 17 of 177 people tested in October in Nong Khai, on the Laos border.

## Understanding the Issue: How an economy can grow while burning less fuel
A common misreading is that a growing economy must burn more coal, oil and gas, so any rise in fuel prices is treated as proof that climate policy and growth cannot share a year. Deutsche Welle reports that Earth has already crossed seven of the nine planetary boundaries scientists treat as critical for life, including climate change, ocean acidification and biosphere integrity. Green growth, as social ecologist Helmut Haberl of BOKU University in Vienna describes it, means “business as usual, but with more sustainable technologies”: wind and solar instead of coal and gas, heat pumps instead of gas furnaces, and more reuse of materials. Haberl and colleagues published a 2020 report weighing more than 800 studies. Scenarios from the United Nations’ Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change show economic output can keep rising while emissions from coal, oil and gas fall sharply, and Deutsche Welle reports that this has already happened for years in the United Kingdom, the United States, Japan and Australia, even when offshore production is counted. In the European Union, the economy has grown by more than 70 percent since 1990 while carbon emissions have dropped by around 40 percent — a full split Haberl’s group still calls relatively rare. In India and in several countries in Africa and Southeast Asia, production is getting cleaner per unit, but emissions are still rising, which does not match the path needed to avoid the worst climate damage. Steel and chemicals are harder still, because carbon is part of the process, not only the fuel, and substitutes such as green hydrogen need heavy investment. A further catch, the rebound effect, is that cheaper green production can lead firms and households to use more, eating part of the savings. Reading a fuel-price story with that map means separating a month’s petrol bill from the longer emissions trend, and not treating one country’s decoupling as a global result. The next time a government boasts of green growth, check whether the emissions number includes offshore production, and whether total resource use fell or only the fuel burned per product. ## Media-literacy note
When two outlets cover the same result, read the one that shows the count beside the one that only confirms the winner. A proposal is not a law, and a minister’s call for payment is not a signed transfer. Primary documents — a court ruling, a statistics release, a treaty text — still beat a live blog’s mood.
