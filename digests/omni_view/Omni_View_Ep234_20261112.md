### 1) US envoys meet Putin amid new Starshield restrictions in Ukraine
**What happened (neutral):** Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stated that military units recorded significant disruptions to Starshield operations across the entire front line and in occupied territories for several days. The restrictions set an effective speed limit of 161 kilometers per hour on terminals, disconnecting any moving faster, and these were described as systematic rather than random errors. US special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner arrived in Moscow to meet Russian President Vladimir Putin after trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi produced no major breakthrough on a ceasefire or territorial settlement; the envoys were later scheduled to meet Zelenskyy on the sidelines of a summit in Ireland.

**Context & perspectives:** CNBC and France24 present the speed caps as a concrete operational problem for Ukrainian interception of jet-powered drones, noting that similar limits appeared last month and that strikes have already succeeded because systems could not reach required speeds. BBC and Al Jazeera place the technical changes alongside the arrival of the US envoys in Moscow and the earlier Abu Dhabi meetings, underscoring Zelenskyy's request to clarify the circumstances directly with the American side. The reports share the undisputed technical detail that terminals above 161 kilometers per hour lose connection and that this affects drone defense; they diverge on whether the pattern reflects deliberate policy choices during active diplomacy or adjustments by the commercial operator.

The strongest case for viewing the restrictions as systematic policy holds that uniform speed thresholds applied consistently along the front line, combined with prior disputes over military use of the network, indicate coordinated limits rather than isolated faults, as Ukrainian documentation showed repeated disconnections at the same threshold. The strongest case for treating them as technical or negotiable holds that Starshield remains a system whose operators retain authority to set performance parameters during ongoing talks, with the possibility of full restoration once diplomatic progress occurs.

## Understanding the Issue: How satellite speed limits affect frontline military operations
Most coverage of satellite service restrictions treats them as simple technical glitches, yet the Starshield case shows how performance parameters can directly shape battlefield outcomes. The network, built on Starlink technology for government and national-security use, allows operators to set speed thresholds that disconnect units exceeding those limits. In Ukraine's situation, the 161-kilometer-per-hour cap prevents systems on faster-moving platforms from maintaining connections, which in turn reduces the ability of interception units to match the speed of incoming jet-powered drones. This mechanism arises because commercial satellite constellations prioritize network stability and regulatory compliance over unrestricted military speeds. Knowing this, when reports mention "disruptions," readers can distinguish between random outages and deliberate parameter changes by checking whether the limits are uniform across regions and consistent over time. The same logic applies to any future claim about restored access: verify whether the speed threshold itself has been lifted rather than assuming connectivity equals full capability. One concrete check is to ask whether official statements specify the exact speed or altitude parameters still in force after any announced fix. Another useful step is to compare the technical description in operator updates against statements from military users on the ground to see whether the same numerical limit appears in both. This approach helps separate operational constraints from diplomatic signaling. Source: [cnbc.com](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/starlink-starshield-ukraine-restrictions-spacex.html)
