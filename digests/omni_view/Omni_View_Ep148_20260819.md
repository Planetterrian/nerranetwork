## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Former Ukrainian defense minister urges wartime presidential election
**What happened (neutral):** Mykhailo Fedorov, who served as defense minister until recently, publicly called for presidential elections to resume despite the ongoing conflict with Russia. Martial law has been in force since February 2022, which under Ukrainian law suspends elections for the duration. The statement comes as the country continues to face Russian attacks and manages its wartime governance. Fedorov argued that Kyiv must restore its democratic process even while the war continues. The call directly challenges the current suspension that has kept President Zelenskyy in office beyond the original term.

**Context & perspectives:** Ukrainian authorities have maintained that holding national votes while large parts of the country remain under threat would be logistically impossible and potentially unsafe for voters and candidates. Opposition voices, including the former minister, argue that prolonged suspension of elections risks concentrating power without sufficient checks, even during conflict. International observers have noted that many democracies have adjusted electoral rules during wars, yet Ukraine’s specific constitutional provisions tie the suspension directly to the declaration of martial law. The debate centers on balancing security needs against the requirement for periodic renewal of political legitimacy. BBC reporting highlights the legal barrier created by martial law, while CNBC coverage focuses on the challenge this poses to Zelenskyy’s wartime authority. This development fits into the ongoing questions about how the Russia-Ukraine war affects governance structures.

The strongest argument for resuming elections rests on the principle that democratic legitimacy must be renewed through regular votes, even in wartime, because indefinite suspension of the electoral calendar can erode public trust and allow unchecked executive power to accumulate. Advocates of this view, including former defense minister Fedorov, point to the constitutional requirement that elections resume once the legal trigger of martial law is addressed. The strongest argument against immediate elections rests on the practical reality that large areas of territory remain under active threat or occupation, making safe and fair voting impossible without endangering citizens or producing results that lack credibility across divided regions. Officials aligned with the current government emphasize that the constitution itself provides for this suspension precisely to avoid such risks during declared states of emergency.

**What happens next:** Watch whether parliament or the constitutional court issues any formal response to the call for elections before the end of the year.

## Understanding the Issue: How martial law suspends elections in Ukraine
Most coverage assumes elections are simply postponed by practical difficulties, yet Ukraine’s constitution contains an explicit clause that ties the suspension of national votes directly to the legal state of martial law. Under that framework, the president and parliament remain in office until the law is lifted, creating a built-in mechanism that prevents scheduled elections without needing separate legislation each time. The same rules have been applied in earlier periods of conflict, showing that the current arrangement follows an established constitutional path rather than an ad-hoc decision. Because the clause is automatic, any move to restore elections requires either ending martial law or amending the constitution—both of which carry their own procedural thresholds. Knowing this distinction helps readers separate logistical arguments from the legal trigger that actually keeps the electoral calendar frozen. When officials or critics discuss “holding elections during war,” the key test is whether they are addressing the constitutional bar or only the security and organizational obstacles. Readers can check the text of Ukraine’s martial-law statute and the relevant constitutional articles to see exactly which provisions are active and what would be required to lift them. The mechanism also means that lifting martial law would automatically reopen the electoral timeline, shifting the focus from security logistics alone to the broader political decision of ending the emergency declaration. Historical application during prior conflicts demonstrates that the suspension has operated as a standard constitutional response rather than a unique wartime exception.
