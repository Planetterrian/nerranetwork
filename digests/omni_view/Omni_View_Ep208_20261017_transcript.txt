To really understand a Brazilian presidential runoff, you have to see the absolute majority
rule as a coalition machine, not a simple rematch. The Constitution written in 1988 after
military rule requires an absolute majority in the first round. Absolute majority means
more than half of the valid vote, not merely finishing first. If nobody clears that bar,
the system orders a second contest between only the top two finishers. The rule was introduced
to push candidates toward broader coalitions. In most recent cycles, no single candidate has
one outright on the first ballot, so the second round is the normal path, not an exception.
That is where smaller parties become decisive.
They can withhold or grant support in exchange for cabinet posts or written policy concessions.
Coverage that treats a runoff as just another vote often skips this bargaining step.
When a campaign claims it has momentum, the mechanism to test is whether eliminated rivals
have already pledged that second round support.
Momentum without those pledges is still a first round left over.
momentum with them is a governing coalition being assembled in public.
Historical patterns show that second-round deals often set the cabinet's composition
and the legislative priorities of the incoming administration.
