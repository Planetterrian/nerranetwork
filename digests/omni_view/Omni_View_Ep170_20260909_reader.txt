To really understand why measles keeps finding a way back, you have to follow a chain of delivery, trust, and follow-up — not a simple shortage of vials in a warehouse.

A lot of coverage treats a shortfall as if the only problem were supply.

The disease needs roughly ninety-five percent coverage to stop sustained transmission, because it spreads so efficiently through airborne droplets.

When routine childhood shots fall below that level in any cluster of communities, an imported case can start chains of infection that last months.

Historical records from other countries show that even brief dips during conflict, migration, or strain on health systems can leave whole age groups unprotected.

Those cohorts can then keep an outbreak alive for years.

The same dynamic has appeared where mobile populations or remote areas received fewer follow-up visits from health workers.

First-dose numbers can look acceptable in a national summary while second-dose completion lags in particular districts, which is how infection chains persist.

When a report cites an overall national rate, check whether district-level second-dose figures are also moving up, rather than assuming the average captures localized risk.

A useful check is whether the next official update states the share of affected districts that finished targeted extra vaccination drives within the last thirty days.

Another is whether independent surveillance groups publish separate tallies you can set beside government figures.

Primary records such as district immunization registers remain the clearest picture of where coverage actually stands, and they help separate counted cases from modeled estimates.
