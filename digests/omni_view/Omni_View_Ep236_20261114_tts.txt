Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky told reporters on Saturday, the BBC reports, that American negotiators put a new question to Kyiv: pull the forces still holding ground in the eastern Donbas, and join the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, or NATO. He said he would send any package to a referendum only if it carried security guarantees, no cap on Ukraine's army, and money to rebuild.

Envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff saw him in Dublin on Thursday after sitting with Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow the day before. The Moscow stop was already on this show. What is new is the trade itself, which he said had never been raised, and which made him think alliance membership was back on the agenda after U S officials had taken it off the table.

He still called the talk with Putin extremely difficult. Moscow's published terms have included a smaller Ukrainian military, land handed over, and guaranteed neutrality. Kyiv has rejected those as leaving it unable to stop another invasion, and Kremlin comments suggest that list has not moved.

France 24, using remarks his office released, puts the same American question after talks in Abu Dhabi rather than after Dublin, and quotes him warning that a Kremlin rewrite of Europe's security setup leads to wars or escalations, and that real security in Europe means the European Union and NATO.

The BBC adds that giving up the sliver still held would be small on a map and large as a symbol, because it would accept the Donbas as Russian, and that First Lady Melania Trump last month helped open a short ceasefire in occupied Oleshky so people and aid could move.

His case is that a question is not a deal: without guarantees, an unconstrained army, and rebuild funds, there is nothing he is willing to ask voters to weigh, because membership is the deterrent against the next attack.

Putin's stated case is that another alliance member on Russia's border is unacceptable, and that neutrality plus a smaller force is what removes the threat Moscow says it is fighting. Zelensky marks the limit on his own reading himself. Putin's signals make the conversation extremely hard. The reports do not show Moscow giving any term back.

If a written package with those three pieces appears, watch whether he actually puts it to a referendum.

Civil air travel across the United States stopped on Friday when links between air-traffic facilities failed. The Federal Aviation Administration, the agency that runs that airspace, saw aging radar and telecom begin to falter at three thirty-six in the afternoon, The New York Times reports.

France 24 reports that more than eight thousand two hundred flights were canceled, over thirty-one percent of the U S schedule, in a stop that lasted nearly five hours — the longest in the country since September eleventh, two thousand one. Officials blamed old equipment and routers overloaded with data. The order is lifted and aircraft are moving again.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said it would still take time to get back to capacity, and that he expected a normal schedule by the Thanksgiving rush. The Times says this was only the third nationwide ground stop the agency has issued this century, and the second-longest after the two thousand one attacks. A shorter one followed a pilot-alert outage on January eleventh, twenty twenty-three.

More than one thousand aircraft were already in the air and headed for U S airports while the stop was on. A typical day puts more than two million people through security checkpoints; on Thursday the Transportation Security Administration screened nearly two and a half million.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was never an indication of a cyberattack and that lives were never in danger, and he also said investigators were still looking and would not rule an attack out. France 24 reports officials said there was no evidence of one.

Congress approved twelve and a half billion dollars last year to upgrade facilities and technology, a sum Duffy has called a down payment. On Friday night he asked for another fifty billion dollars in emergency money before the end of the year, up from a thirty billion dollar request, because he said control of Congress and the budget then switches from Republicans to Democrats.

His argument is that the same old links will fail again unless that cheque arrives while his party still holds the budget. Some Democratic lawmakers have already asked whether the agency has spent what it already received efficiently. The articles do not quote them saying the current kit is fine. Watch whether that emergency sum is approved before the budget changes hands at the end of the year.

Sudan's emergency stocks of shelter and basic household goods are running out, the International Organization for Migration and the U N refugee agency say, Deutsche Welle reports. The United Nations plan needs two point nine billion dollars to reach twenty point four million people, and forty-seven percent of that money is in. Thirty-three point seven million people in the country depend on aid.

Fifteen point four million need shelter and household items, and agencies have been able to help only ten percent of them.

More than three and a half years after war began between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, about eight point six million people have fled inside the country, more than four point six million have crossed into neighbouring states, and another five point two million have gone back to towns that are already destroyed.

Death estimates run from forty thousand to two hundred fifty thousand. Fresher counts are blocked by fighting, curbed satellite internet, and the departure of many observers.

Marina Peter, who chairs the Sudan and South Sudan Forum in Germany, told Deutsche Welle that some people have lived on roots, that rations in refugee camps have been cut sharply, and that child mortality is above average while the armed parties still obstruct access.

Ivo Körner of Caritas International told the same outlet that the United States, Germany, and the European Union are giving far less than they did four or five years ago. Caritas still provides cash, drinking water, basic medical care, and seeds, but its twenty twenty-six projects in Sudan have been paid for only by donations.

On October twenty-third the United Kingdom and thirty-one other partners, Germany among them, said nineteen point five million people — about forty-one percent of the population — face acute food insecurity. Körner hopes for money from the German Foreign Office and expects the door to be narrower than in earlier years.

Paris is due to see a fifth straight Saturday of marches, France 24 reports, with campaign groups, students, and Yellow Vest protesters joining demands on wages, education, and civil liberties. The Hindu reports that thousands of arrests, police beatings, and limits on marches have added to a swell that shows no sign of easing before next year's presidential election.

The New York Times sets those marches, and recent youth protests in Belgium, Germany, Greece, Italy, and Portugal, against a budget fact: as Europeans live longer, pensions are routinely the largest lines in national budgets. Union leaders warn that fatigue is setting in, France 24 says, while officers want better pay for the risk of policing the streets.

Many teachers back students who have blockaded high schools for nearly two months, and headteachers gathered on Friday saying threats and violence have become unbearable and asking the state to protect them.

Sébastien Ducros, a thirty-six-year-old data analyst who supports the students, told the Times that dilapidated schools lose because a huge portion of the money goes to retirees, and that people like his parents, about to retire, could surrender some of their pension.

Laurent Grinwald, seventy-two, who retired after decades as a postal worker, told the Times he is not eager to give any of it back for other services, especially with rents and living costs up, after a working life already paid in. Jorge Galindo, director of the Spanish think tank EsadeEcPol, called the split a generational clash and noted that Spain has some of Europe's most generous pensions.

Neither man is quoted accepting the other's arithmetic.

A regional health body has taken a status away from the United States that the country had held since two thousand. The Pan American Health Organization revoked measles-elimination status after twenty-six years, Deutsche Welle reports, citing a Wall Street Journal account of people familiar with the decision.

A source close to the process told CNN that an independent commission reporting to the organization's director found the country no longer qualified. The test was whether one strain had circulated without a break for twelve months or more. A source familiar with the U S Centers for Disease Control and Prevention findings told Reuters the agency found twelve months of continuous spread.

As of Thursday the agency had recorded five thousand one hundred eighty-two confirmed cases, across forty-five states plus New York City and Washington, D.C. — more than double twenty twenty-five's two thousand two hundred eighty-nine. About seven thousand five hundred people have been infected and thirteen have died since twenty twenty-five.

Nearly all of this year's cases are in people who were unvaccinated or whose status is unknown, and roughly one in nine patients were hospitalized, CNN reported, citing the C D C.

Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina professor who chairs an independent measles panel and who presented that C D C report on Tuesday, told Reuters it is a fully preventable disease and unconscionable to have this amount of measles. Johns Hopkins epidemiologist William Moss told Reuters the loss damages America's reputation as a public-health leader and means large outbreaks will continue.

The stretch has come with more vaccine hesitancy under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who for years has doubted measles-vaccine safety without scientific proof. C D C Director Erica Schwartz, before the decision, said the agency would keep working with states to encourage vaccination.

A country gets the status back only after transmission ends and it stays outbreak-free for at least twelve months.

Space X sent Starship up on a fifteenth test flight, France 24 reports, aiming to reach orbit and release fifteen satellites for its Starlink network. Deutsche Welle reports the plan was, for the first time, to catch and later reuse both stages of what the company calls the world's largest spaceship. The spacecraft continued into orbit. The booster, meant to return to the pad, did not.

France 24 says it splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico minutes after liftoff. Deutsche Welle says it exploded over that same water. Neither report includes wreckage images or a company statement that would settle which account the hardware matches. Both say the upper stage flew on and the booster was not brought home.

Dakar is a piece of progress worth knowing: the Summer Youth Olympics there ended Friday after two weeks, Deutsche Welle reports, the first Olympic event an African country has hosted. Nearly two thousand seven hundred young athletes contested one hundred fifty-three medal events in twenty-five sports.

African teams took a record medal haul, and Senegal won an Olympic gold for the first time, with one hundred thirty-six Senegalese athletes on the start lists. China led with eighteen golds, Japan took eleven, Russia eight, Egypt six, and Ethiopia and Senegal five each.

International Olympic Committee President Kirsty Coventry said expectations had been exceeded and praised what happens when a whole country comes together for its young people. The hard edge is political. Russian athletes competed under their own flag for the first time at an Olympic event since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February twenty twenty-two.

Chile, Paraguay, and Thailand have confirmed bids to host the next Summer Youth Olympics, in twenty thirty.

To really understand the pension anger behind those European marches, look at the headcount, not the stereotype that the fight is only about whether older people have been too comfortable. Germany's public pension insurance had three point seven three contributors for every retiree in nineteen fifty-seven. By twenty twenty-five the ratio was two point one.

By twenty forty-five it is projected at one point seven three — thirty-five point three five million contributors financing sixteen point eight two million retirees, Deutsche Welle reports in a data check on the generations.

Holding contribution rates steady already costs about one hundred eighteen billion euros a year in government subsidies, roughly a quarter of the federal budget, so a rising share of everyone else's taxes is committed before schools or training are argued.

Retirees on federal basic income support rose from two hundred fifty-seven thousand in two thousand three to seven hundred thirty-eight thousand in twenty twenty-four. Low-income households spent about twenty-four percent of income on rent in nineteen ninety and nearly thirty-six percent in twenty twenty-one.

In twenty twenty-five, eleven percent of the population spent more than forty percent of disposable income on housing, and among people at risk of poverty that share was thirty-five percent.

Public education spending on people under thirty has doubled since twenty eleven, from about four thousand euros to about eight thousand euros a person each year, and in twenty twenty-two it was still four point six percent of gross domestic product against an OECD average of four point seven percent.

Youth researcher Simon Schnetzer told Deutsche Welle that it is fair to ask whether earlier generations had it hard, and that young people are asking whether they have been given the conditions to live, work, and keep the system funded.

A crowd report reads differently once you know the ratio and the subsidy share, because the argument is about what is already promised before this year's march starts. Next time, check whether the piece states that contributor-to-retiree ratio and the budget share, or only describes the street.
