### 1) France braces for second weekend of protests after last week's unrest
**What happened (neutral):** French authorities plan to deploy 70,000 police officers and soldiers nationwide on Saturday as hundreds of thousands of students, trade union members and other groups prepare demonstrations in Paris and other cities. The interior ministry has imposed restrictions limiting authorised marches to those organised by recognised unions and has banned gatherings in several cities including Lyon, Nice and Montpellier. President Emmanuel Macron met high-school student representatives on Friday and his advisers said the government would announce commitments on school staffing and conditions starting Monday. The protests began with demands from high school students to improve school conditions and fix staff shortages before violence occurred last weekend. Hundreds of people have already been jailed for vandalism, arson and looting after fast-track court hearings. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez stated the ministry will be uncompromising with vandals, looters and those committing acts of violence, citing calls for violence on social media from the far left and radical extreme left. Disillusionment remains high among the French public over rising cost-of-living pressures and warnings of future deep spending cuts by any incoming government.

## Understanding the Issue: How French governments authorise and restrict demonstrations
Most coverage of protest days in France focuses on turnout numbers and clashes, yet the underlying rules that determine which marches are permitted receive less attention. French law requires organisers to declare demonstrations in advance to local authorities, who can impose route changes or conditions; only marches backed by recognised trade unions receive automatic protection under recent decrees. When the interior ministry or city officials issue blanket bans, as happened this week in Lyon and Montpellier, groups without union sponsorship must either cancel or risk dispersal. This structure gives the state significant discretion while still allowing large-scale events when unions participate. The interior ministry has said it will deploy 70,000 police officers, ramping up the security presence in French cities, after the government was accused by opposition parties of failing to foresee the scale of the unrest on 17 October. Knowing this distinction helps readers see why Macron’s Friday meeting with student representatives and the union requirement both matter for Saturday’s outcome. The same rules also explain why opposition parties criticised the government for not anticipating last weekend’s scale of unrest. Macron decreed earlier this week that only demonstrations organised by a recognised trade unions would be permitted to take place on Saturday. When you next read about French protest authorisations, check whether the reported restrictions apply to all gatherings or only to those without recognised sponsors.
