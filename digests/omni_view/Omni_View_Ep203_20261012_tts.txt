To really understand why a cheaper model can skip a fact that is already in its input, you have to look at how memory compression works inside a large language model — the kind of system that writes text and code from a huge training set.

A lot of talk about efficient models stops at lower training bills and cheaper running costs.

It often skips the machinery that produces those savings.

These systems cut memory by squeezing how they store and fetch information across a long stretch of text or code.

That squeeze can shorten the effective context window in some positions — the span of material the model can actually keep in play.

When that happens, the model is more likely to overlook a detail in those spots, or to give it the wrong weight.

The pattern is not the property of a single laboratory.

It shows up whenever designers trade storage capacity for speed or for cost.

So when a cheaper model is said to match frontier performance, the useful check is whether the tests included long-context work, or only short prompts.

Ask whether the model was tried on inputs longer than a few thousand tokens, the small chunks of text it reads, and whether accuracy stayed level when the important detail was moved around inside those inputs.
