Coverage of a French demonstration day is easy to misread, and to really understand the outcome you have to follow the authorisation rules, not only the crowd.

The common mix-up is to treat every march as a single legal object — either allowed or forbidden — when the system actually sorts organisers before anyone steps into the street.

French law requires organisers to declare a demonstration in advance to local authorities.

Those authorities can impose route changes or conditions rather than a simple yes or no.

Under recent decrees, only marches backed by recognised trade unions receive automatic protection.

When the interior ministry or city officials issue a blanket ban, groups without union sponsorship must cancel or risk dispersal.

That design gives the state wide discretion, and it still lets very large events proceed when unions are on the paperwork.

Opposition parties accused the government of failing to foresee the scale of unrest on October seventeenth, and the same rulebook is why a union stamp and a student meeting can matter more than a slogan.

Prefecture orders and union calls to action are the primary papers; ministry statements are the plan, not yet the street.

Comparing one city with another shows whether a ban is uniform or local.

When you next read that a French march was restricted, check whether the limit applies to every gathering or only to groups without a recognised sponsor.
