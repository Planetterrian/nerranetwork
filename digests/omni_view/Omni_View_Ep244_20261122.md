## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) A new COP31 draft still omits fossil fuels as overtime runs on
**What happened (neutral):** Diplomats from nearly 200 countries are still in Antalya, Turkey, on a second day of overtime at COP31, the United Nations climate summit, after a Friday close slipped, Deutsche Welle reports. The latest draft still does not mention fossil fuels, the main driver of human-caused warming. Al Jazeera reports that the new draft would, for the first time, acknowledge that global warming is on course to pass the 1.5C threshold. German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider called the draft “not acceptable” and “a step backward from previous decisions on climate protection.” Former Greenpeace chief Jennifer Morgan said it would be better to end with no deal than with a text that “sets us back,” and that “Saudi Arabia’s fingerprints are all over” the draft. She also said this year’s Turkish presidency was “not well prepared” and had left four days of shuttle diplomacy with no text.

**Context & perspectives:** CONTESTED. The dispute turns on whether a thin text is better than none. The Turkish COP presidency has suggested it is better to accept the current draft than to leave with no resolution at all — a finished line, even a weak one, keeps nearly 200 governments inside one decision rather than scattering them. Schneider’s case is that a conference whose only job is climate protection cannot adopt a text that moves backward from earlier decisions; Germany, he said, cannot support that. Morgan’s case, aligned with the German minister, is that a decision which vulnerable islands and people reject would set the process back, and that oil-producing states shaped the omission. She praised Germany for refusing the text and contrasted this presidency with last year’s Brazilian one, which she said had spent the year pulling countries together. Deutsche Welle also notes fossil fuels are the flashpoint while energy prices are high and the US-Israeli war with Iran continues. The summit stays open until there is consensus, or until governments choose to leave without one.

**Questions to consider:** Will Antalya close with an adopted text or with no decision at all?

### 2) Somalia expels the UAE ambassador after years of security cooperation
**What happened (neutral):** On November 19, Somalia ordered the United Arab Emirates’ ambassador to leave and recalled its own envoy from Abu Dhabi, Al Jazeera reports — the sharpest break yet with a partner President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud once called “one of the greatest pillars” of the war against al-Shabab, the al-Qaeda-linked armed group. The UAE said it had already withdrawn its diplomatic staff from Mogadishu in January. The expulsion came 10 months after Somalia annulled commercial, defence, and security agreements, accusing Abu Dhabi of undermining its sovereignty.

**Context & perspectives:** CONTESTED. Mohamud’s own framing, two days before the order, was familial and absolute: “A brother does not burn your house. A brother does not buy your property without your knowledge. A brother does not divide your family.” National security adviser Awes Hagi Yusuf Ahmed wrote that, after years of engagement, the federal government judges UAE policy a threat to national security — financing rival regional administrations and armed actors outside federal control. ### 2) Kenya weighs mandatory internet meters
**What happened (neutral):** A bill before Kenya’s National Assembly would make consumption-based internet billing mandatory, Al Jazeera reports. The Kenya Information and Communications (Amendment) Bill, 2025, sponsored by Aldai MP Marianne Jebet Kitany, would require each customer to have a unique meter number, with providers monitoring use, turning it into readable detail, and billing by consumption. Providers would also send billing-system information, including meter numbers, to the Communications Authority of Kenya at least once a financial year. The stated aim is to stop unfair billing so people pay for the data they use. The bill does not expressly require recording message content or websites visited.

**Context & perspectives:** The Communications Authority has urged lawmakers not to make metered billing compulsory. Telkom Kenya has warned of consequences for privacy, affordability, and investment. Demas Kiprono of the International Commission of Jurists Kenya argues the regulator already can handle billing complaints, and questions whether identifiable usage records are necessary and proportionate. Critics want the text to say who may see the records, how long they are kept, and when they may be shared. Kenya’s Data Protection Act, 2019, already governs personal information; opponents want Parliament to say how it would apply here. The assembly’s Departmental Committee on Communication, Information and Innovation is considering the bill.

## Understanding the Issue: How sending an envoy home differs from tearing up a treaty
People often hear “the ambassador was expelled” and picture a treaty torn in half. That picture mixes three different acts. What follows is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a claim drawn from today’s articles. Under the Vienna rules that most capitals still use, a receiving state may declare a diplomat persona non grata — no longer welcome — and the sending state is expected to withdraw that person; the embassy as an institution can remain, sometimes with a lower-ranking chargé d’affaires. Recalling your own ambassador is the mirror move: you pull your representative without necessarily closing the building or ending trade. A third act, annulling a named defence or commercial agreement, is a legal step of its own and has to say which contract dies. Staff can also be gone for months before an expulsion order is issued, which means the public rupture and the practical absence of diplomats need not fall on the same day. Reading today’s coverage, the useful split is between a political signal — who is allowed to stay in the capital — and a legal inventory of which contracts still bind. Next time an expulsion leads the news, look for a list of cancelled agreements separate from the sentence that sends the envoy home; if that list is missing, the legal relationship has not been described yet.

## Media-literacy note
When two institutions count the same crowd, write down both numbers and who produced each before you pick one. A draft climate text is a primary document: the presence or absence of a single phrase is the fact, and a minister’s rejection is a separate fact. Diplomatic quotes travel well as slogans; the checkable piece is which agreements were annulled, and on what date.
