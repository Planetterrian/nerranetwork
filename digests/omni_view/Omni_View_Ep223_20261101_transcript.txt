how a strike on one tanker can lift energy costs for utilities and factories far from the water,
start with the straight of hormones as a choke in the pipe, not a local accident. Roughly one fifth
of global oil and a growing share of liquefied natural gas has to pass that narrow stretch.
Tankers cannot easily reroute because alternative paths add weeks and require different vessel types or port infrastructure that many buyers lack.
Buyers without the right ports or ship classes cannot simply switch routes the way a truck can switch highways.
When one carrier is struck, insurers raise premiums across the route.
Those higher premiums directly lift delivered prices for utilities and manufacturers far from the incident.
The price signal does not wait for a full shut down.
Slower transits and fatter insurance bills are enough to move what households and plants
pay.
Historical parallels show that even temporary closures or speed reductions in the straight
have produced measurable spikes in European and Asian energy costs within days.
Coverage often treats each tanker incident as a one-off when the cost travels through insurance
and routing markets whether or not a single haul is badly damaged.
When the next tanker headline lands, check the daily vessel counts that week from the
International Maritime Organization or UK Maritime Trade Operations, rather than
