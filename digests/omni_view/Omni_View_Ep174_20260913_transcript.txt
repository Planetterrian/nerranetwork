To really understand how a longstanding water treaty is supposed to work when politics collapse, you have to look at the technical machinery built into the 1960 text, not at the on-off language of a press statement.
A common misunderstanding treats suspension as a simple switch that ends the whole bargain.
The Indus Waters Treaty instead created a permanent commission and a multi-step dispute process that was designed to keep running even when political relations break down.
That commission has met regularly since 1960, exchanging data on river flows and resolving technical questions without needing a green light from either capital.
The 1960 text created the commission precisely to keep technical cooperation separate from wider political disputes.
When one side stops cooperating, the treaty's arbitration clause still lets the other party take the matter to an independent tribunal, whose ruling is binding on the technical provisions.
The recent Hague decision addressed only the legality of suspension and left the underlying allocation of river water unchanged.
Historical records show the commission continued functioning through earlier wars and diplomatic breaks.
The design is a form of insulation against political shocks, using a permanent technical body rather than hoping goodwill survives a crisis.
When you next hear that a treaty has been ended, check whether the commission or the arbitration process is still operating, and read what the actual text says about unilateral withdrawal rather than relying on a summary of a speech.
