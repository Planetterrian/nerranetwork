Judge Patricia Nyaundi of Kenya's High Court said on Thursday the government broke the constitution. Al Jazeera reports she found that Nairobi signed a deal with the United States for an Ebola quarantine site and never showed the public the terms or asked people what they thought. Further work stays stopped until that disclosure and that consultation happen.

The beds are inside Laikipia airbase, near the town of Nanyuki. The plan was to hold people who might have been exposed, including American citizens. In July, seven American aid workers who had been helping in the Democratic Republic of the Congo quarantined there. Opposition has not stayed in the courtroom. In June, police opened fire on protesters and at least one person was killed.

The week before this ruling, hundreds marched in Nanyuki; at least two people were killed and one was wounded. This is not the first court stop. In late May the same court suspended construction, then found Health Minister Aden Duale had not obeyed. He later told the judges he had ordered an immediate halt.

The medical backdrop is the Bundibugyo strain, a form of Ebola with no approved vaccine and no approved treatment. The World Health Organization, the United Nations health agency, called the outbreak an international public health emergency on May seventeenth. Kenya's first imported case came in early October, a traveller from Congo by way of Uganda.

By mid-November the count was two deaths and seven other cases, all of those seven recovered, and no new case since October nineteenth. The government has not commented on Thursday's ruling. Both sides of the political fight accept that a site was built and that an outbreak is real. They split on whether it could go up in secret.

Residents of Nanyuki and Laikipia say the United States is parking Ebola risk on Kenyan ground, and their reason is simple: the beds are in their county, and they were not shown the paper.

Kenyan and American authorities had already restated, in public, that they were sticking with the plan even after the May suspension, because exposed people, including Americans back from Congo, needed a place to wait out a virus that still has no shot. Nyaundi did not pick a medical winner.

She said residents were left standing outside a sealed wooden window, able to tell that decisions were happening and given no way in. The court record also shows the earlier pause was not kept until the minister said he had finally ordered work to stop.

Germany's federal prosecutor on Thursday ordered two Iraqi citizens held, Deutsche Welle reports, identified as Mostafa Al-R. and Ameer J., one taken in Heidelberg and one in Cologne. Prosecutors call them strongly suspected members of a domestic terrorist organization.

More than two hundred fifty officers were in the operation, and searches at seventeen sites across three states were still underway. The case they describe runs through a man in the United States, Mohammad Al-Saadi, accused of building a group known as HAYI, Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiyya.

Prosecutors say that group carried out attacks on American, Israeli, and Jewish institutions in Europe from March, after Donald Trump's war with Iran began. They say Mostafa and Al-Saadi then set up a separate organization at the end of twenty twenty-five to prepare more attacks in Germany and Europe, raised money, and recruited people for bombings.

On two nights late in March, prosecutors wrote, unknown assailants went, presumably carrying explosives, toward synagogues in Cologne and in one other European city. The plan was not carried out. Why it stopped is still unknown. The tip, they said, came from the BfV, Germany's domestic intelligence agency.

The two men were due before judges later Thursday so the court could decide investigative detention, which is pretrial jail. A third person is still wanted. This report carries the prosecution's account only. The accused have no quoted answer in it, and Deutsche Welle marks the story as still developing. Watch whether those judges ordered the two men kept in investigative detention.

Israeli attacks killed at least eight Palestinians across Gaza on Thursday, Al Jazeera reports, and the dead include Khaled Abu Nada, undersecretary of Gaza's education ministry, and his wife. Medics at Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital in western Deir el-Balah said an air strike killed the couple and wounded six others, several of them critically.

Correspondent Ashraf Abu Amra said Apache helicopters fired two missiles, one after the other, at a residential apartment during dawn prayers, in a district packed with tents of displaced families. In the north, al-Shifa Hospital said a drone hit a group in Shati refugee camp, killing three and wounding four. A separate strike on the Tuffah neighbourhood killed three.

Another drone attack, on Nassr, wounded several people. A ceasefire has been in effect since last October. The argument is about the job title, and about what a strike in a ceasefire is doing.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defence Minister Israel Katz said Abu Nada was a Hamas figure in charge of the group's economy and education portfolios, so the strike, on their account, hit Hamas administration rather than a civilian school office. Gaza's interior ministry says Israel is trying to spread chaos by killing security and police commanders.

That charge follows two other killings this week: Mohammed Abu al-Ouf, internal security chief for northern Gaza, died early Wednesday with his wife and two daughters, and Naim Abu Nahl, the internal security director, was killed the day before.

Health ministry spokesperson Zaher al-Wahidi told Al Jazeera that Israeli attacks since the ceasefire have killed more than sixteen hundred Palestinians, one hundred thirty of them this month, and wounded more than six thousand. He called it a systematic hit on every pillar of life in the strip.

The ministry's count since October twenty twenty-three is seventy-four thousand seven hundred forty-six killed. Neither side grants the other's description of Abu Nada.

An explosion and fire at Bangchak Corporation's Phra Khanong oil facility in eastern Bangkok injured at least seven people on Thursday, four of them seriously, Al Jazeera reports, citing the city's Erawan emergency centre. The company said it shut every production unit, contained the blaze, and got the situation under control.

The Bangna district office told anyone nearby to leave immediately and said investigators do not yet know the cause. A citywide phone alert repeated that the fire was under control and told people to stay away. Bangchak's own description of the plant puts maximum capacity at one hundred twenty thousand barrels a day.

It feeds not only Bangkok but a large share of the oil imports for central Thailand, and it sits near the main port. Correspondent Tony Cheng said the blast was heard around three in the afternoon local time, and that the highway and most major routes in the area stayed open.

Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said he was watching, that the fire appeared under control, and that it did not seem to be in the main production area. He gave no further detail. The same refinery blew up in July twenty twelve, when it was still called Bangchak Petroleum. That fire was huge and, by the reports then, hurt no one.

People asked at the time why a plant that large stood in what is now a residential district. Cheng noted other industrial accidents in the area over the past couple of years. How much of the plant Thursday's fire actually ruined is not yet public.

A new paper from the USTR, the Office of the United States Trade Representative, puts American tariffs on India back where they were when the trade talks started. That is The Hindu's report, and the experts it cites say the move is not a substantial threat, so India should not hurry into a deal.

Deutsche Welle adds that New Delhi has denied a U S claim that Indian policy creates too much factory capacity, and has told Washington to settle the tariff fight inside the negotiations. Reuters describes President Donald Trump's new tariffs as a tool to pull global trade out of China's grip. Start with India's own framing.

The government, and the experts The Hindu summarizes, treat this text as a return to the opening bid, not a gun to the head, and they reject the idea that Indian policy is flooding the world with surplus goods. Washington's framing, as Reuters states the aim, is not really about that India paper alone.

Tariffs are there to loosen China's hold on trade, which puts India's terms inside a wider contest. Nothing in these reports shows either capital conceding a point.

Researchers are strapping seabirds with G P S units, satellite trackers, and with microphones, The New York Times reports, so the birds can lead them to fishing boats that sail with their tracking signals off. Those signals are how satellites usually see a vessel. Switch the box off and the boat disappears from that map. A bird that follows it can still log the track and the sound.

The piece is about the method. It does not name a fleet, a fine, or a government that has acted on what the birds found.

Attacks on electricity lines and a drought are hitting Sudan's harvests at the same time, Al Jazeera reports, and millions of people are left without water. Farmers are the ones whose season depends on both the grid and the rain. The report puts the war damage and the dry weather on the same fields.

It does not name a repair crew, a relief shipment, or a ceasefire clause that would turn the power back on.

To really understand a blast on a Russian ship far from Ukraine, separate a random spillover from a ship that geography already trapped. It is easy to hear a hit in the Baltic or the Mediterranean as the land war simply leaking into NATO seas. Deutsche Welle's account of those operations is more precise than that. Russia's Baltic shoreline is a thin strip between Finland and Estonia.

Through it, Moscow supplies Kaliningrad, a piece of Russia cut off from the mainland, and ships oil out on a shadow fleet of tankers. Ukraine had already struck oil terminals on that same coast, at Ust-Luga and Primorsk, near St. Petersburg.

A corvette that belonged to the Black Sea Fleet could not go home, because Turkey has closed the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, the two straits between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, so the warship was escorting cargo ships in the Mediterranean instead. Elisabeth Braw of the Atlantic Council told Deutsche Welle this is not a sweep of every tanker selling oil above a price cap.

The cargo ship Sparta II, struck off Finland, was suspected of hauling military equipment. Moscow denies that. What Kyiv is demonstrating, she said, is that it can find those ships in whatever water they occupy. The stated military aim is to snap supply lines. Experts and politicians also hear a political message to Western allies ahead of any peace talks.

This is not the first far strike: Ukraine has hit an oil refinery in Omsk, in Siberia, and, before December twenty twenty-four, Russian military sites in Syria. Next time a report names a ship a long way from the front, check which label it actually earns — warship, alleged arms cargo, or shadow-fleet tanker — and whether Moscow denies what was said to be in the hold.

A court order, a charge sheet, and a minister's sentence are not the same kind of fact. One you can read as a ruling, one is still an allegation, and one is an official summary of a file you have not seen. When two governments name the same dead man in opposite ways, the document each will publish matters more than a blend of both.
