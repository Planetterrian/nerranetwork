One year after the United Nations Security Council endorsed a twenty-point plan to end the fighting in Gaza, Al Jazeera reported that five aid groups put a number on how that plan is working for people still living there.

The Danish Refugee Council, the Norwegian Refugee Council, Oxfam, Refugees International and Save the Children scored humanitarian access at zero and civilian protection at one point. The plan itself forbids holding territory and tells Israeli forces to pull back. The Yellow Line, the boundary Palestinians are not allowed to cross, has moved west instead.

The UN office that tracks humanitarian conditions, the Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, put Israeli control at fifty-eight percent when the ceasefire began thirteen months ago and at sixty-five percent now. Israel has said the share is seventy percent, and a UN official told the Security Council that figure back in June.

Last month that UN office said about two point one million Palestinians were squeezed into one hundred twenty-eight square kilometres, roughly sixteen thousand people in each one. A later map reading by NPR put the space at one hundred nine square kilometres, close to nineteen thousand three hundred people per square kilometre.

Agencies count at least one thousand six hundred fifty Palestinians killed and five thousand nine hundred seventy-five wounded since the ceasefire announced in October last year. This week tanks moved west of the line in central Gaza, with new orders telling civilians to leave.

Shaun Hughes, the World Food Programme’s country director for Palestine, said it does not feel like a ceasefire on the ground: nearly seventy percent under military control means almost nowhere to grow food, fishing is banned and dangerous, and unemployment is over eighty percent. Lilu Thapa of the Danish Refugee Council called the expanding line displacement by design.

Ramy Abdu, who founded the Euro-Mediterranean Human Rights Monitor, said territory is being taken by force, and that the US-chaired Board of Peace has no real Palestinian voice. Mohsen Abu Ramadan of the Palestinian NGOs Network said that board has given political cover for atrocities. The five groups answered with the scorecard itself.

Denmark is the country in a dispute over whether a Greenland security deal is already law. CNBC reported that Denmark has ratified an agreement with the Trump administration, after months in which President Donald Trump said the United States should take control of Greenland for national security.

The Globe and Mail, on the same talks, said lawmakers were still expected to ratify, and that the text would enlarge the American military presence, including permission for a Golden Dome missile-defence system. One desk states a finished vote. The other still has it ahead, and is the one that names the missile-defence permission. Neither text here quotes a Danish minister explaining the choice.

What both describe is a security bargain set against an earlier demand for control.

Myanmar is now under a finding of genocidal intent from the International Court of Justice, the UN court for cases between countries. Al Jazeera reported that the court tied that intent to specific cases in twenty seventeen operations, and that court president Yuji Iwasawa said the evidence shows forces killed some ten thousand Rohingya.

He said security forces killed thousands of civilians, committed widespread sexual violence, used extreme violence against children, and attacked people fleeing in large numbers into Bangladesh. More than seven hundred thirty thousand, mostly Muslim, left in that crackdown.

The Hindu, covering the same sitting, still described the ruling as ongoing while reporting killings, rape, violence against children and destroyed villages.

The Gambia brought the case in twenty nineteen under the nineteen forty-eight Genocide Convention, the treaty that defines genocide and obliges states to prevent and punish it, and asked for illegal acts to stop and for those responsible to be punished. Myanmar has said the operations answered attacks on police posts by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army.

The New York Times noted that Aung San Suu Kyi addressed the judges in twenty nineteen and did not condemn the pogroms. The UN counts more than one point three million Rohingya still displaced across South and Southeast Asia, including one point two million in the camps at Cox’s Bazar, with aid shrinking and sea crossings still dangerous.

Venezuela’s interim government and the US defence secretary have a new military agreement, and the public text is missing. Al Jazeera reported that Pete Hegseth announced it on an unannounced visit to Caracas on Wednesday, after meeting interim president Delcy Rodriguez and defence minister Gustavo Gonzalez Lopez.

The deal covers joint training, shared intelligence, military equipment and border security. Hegseth said the forces would go after drug trafficking and armed groups together. Rodriguez called the visit extraordinary and said her forces would get better training, newer equipment and new technology without giving up sovereignty. How joint operations would actually run has not been published.

The administration’s reason is practical: help Caracas retake ground held by armed groups and trafficking networks. Since US forces seized then-president Nicolas Maduro and flew him to New York, Washington has in effect taken charge of Venezuela’s oil industry, and the country holds the world’s largest reserves.

On November tenth the House voted two hundred eleven to two hundred four against impeaching Hegseth for now. Republican Thomas Massie had offered eight articles, including the US-Israel war on Iran, strikes on suspected drug boats, and the January operation that removed Maduro. Massie said that operation was an act of war without Congress, aimed at the oil, not only a trafficking arrest.

The split sits on the unpublished pages. Hegseth and Rodriguez describe a security partnership that modernises Venezuela’s armed forces and leaves sovereignty intact.

Analyst Carlos Pina reads the timing instead: Democrats won the November third midterms and have promised harder scrutiny of the twenty twenty-six deals with Caracas, and the impeachment vote already failed, so the trip gets ahead of the next Congress. Massie’s charge is about that earlier operation, and his own resolution lost the vote.

Until the clauses are public, neither the sovereignty promise nor the oil motive can be checked against the document.

Oil demand a decade from now is the subject of a new International Energy Agency scenario. Reuters reported that an energy-security response to the war in Iran could cut oil demand by four percent by twenty thirty-five. The New York Times called the same paper the agency’s fullest look at the war’s fallout, and said the oil shock could speed a shift to other kinds of power.

That four percent is a path for global use, not a count of barrels this month, and neither dispatch lists which countries’ policies produce it.

Iran’s own economy is the place those pressures are already showing up in official numbers. Al Jazeera reported that a US naval blockade of Iranian ports, plus disrupted overland trade and sanctions, is expected to shrink the economy further, with the cost landing on tens of millions of people.

Official figures show output fell ten point one percent year on year from March twenty-first to June twentieth, the first quarter of the Persian calendar.

Economist Peyman Molavi said that if this holds, growth in the money supply could move from nearly fifty-one percent to between fifty-five and sixty percent by late March, and annual inflation could reach about eighty-five percent, one hundred percent measured point to point, with energy, food, metals and industrial goods hit hardest.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the embargo brings a twenty-five percent contraction by the year ending March twenty twenty-seven. Central bank governor Abdolnaser Hemmati said the bank has locked in thirty billion dollars in foreign currency for the months ahead, essentials first.

Economy minister Ali Madanizadeh said every land route is still open except one into Iraq, shut for security. Al Jazeera also said Washington has moved a third aircraft carrier in, to back a possible escalation. AP said the US military is back to two carriers in the Middle East. Those counts do not match, and neither report here explains the gap.

The war that frames the demands began with strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei on February twenty-eighth. Washington wants the Strait of Hormuz reopened and the nuclear programme curbed. Tehran says it can last, and has demands of its own.

Governments there have long filled budget holes by printing money, which feeds inflation, and blocked oil exports have tightened the currency further. Both sides are staring at a smaller economy. Bessent’s argument is that a twenty-five percent drop by March twenty twenty-seven is large enough to force a political outcome.

Hemmati’s is that winter is too soon to call, because thirty billion dollars is already set aside for essential imports. Madanizadeh’s mechanism is the open land borders. Hemmati does not say the pressure is light. He says reserves and time mean collapse, or a forced concession, is not a result already booked.

A bank bargain in Frankfurt is progress worth knowing, because Italy’s UniCredit and the German government have signed terms that clear a path to buy Commerzbank. The Wall Street Journal reported that UniCredit will keep the headquarters in Frankfurt, the bank stays listed there, and Berlin will stop trying to name board members directly.

The hitch is the trade itself: Germany gives up that direct board hand so a foreign buyer can go ahead, while the home city and the listing stay put. No price and no closing date are in the report. It is a finished political deal on place and seats, not a finished merger.

To really understand a blockade, separate a customer who wants the oil from a partner who will guarantee the seller’s politics.

Deutsche Welle reports that in twenty twenty-five Chinese buyers took more than eighty percent of Iran’s seaborne crude, much of it through independent refiners and at a steep discount caused by sanctions, while Iran leaned harder on Chinese machinery and industrial parts.

Mohsen Solhdoost, who teaches international relations at Xi’an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, told Deutsche Welle that cutting export earnings and slowing a recovery does not require Beijing’s help, and that stopping trade is easier to prove than changing another government’s choices.

In November Washington stood up Task Force Iron Gate to disrupt sanctioned goods, military equipment and revenue on Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea, even as goods still cross through Iraq, Pakistan and Turkey under tighter watch. His physical point is that a willing buyer cannot keep the money flowing if freshly loaded tankers never reach a market.

A senior official at Iran’s Plan and Budget Organization said planners are writing the twenty twenty-seven to twenty twenty-eight budget under several scenarios, including oil income at zero — a plan for the lifeline to fail, not a decision to adopt that budget. Trucks and trains can still move spare parts and keep some factories open. They cannot replace ships at the same scale or cost.

And a Chinese economic tie, he says, is not an endorsement of every policy in Tehran, because both Beijing and Moscow weigh Iran against their own talks with Washington. Next time a blockade story quotes a buyer’s appetite, ask what share of the crude actually sailed, and whether the budget still counts on oil money.
