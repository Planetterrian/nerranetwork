## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Taiwan sets record defence budget to signal readiness
**What happened (neutral):** President Lai Ching-te announced a record NT$1.1 trillion ($34.5 billion) defence allocation for 2027, equal to more than 3 percent of GDP. The move comes as China continues military and grey-zone pressure across the Taiwan Strait. Lai stated the spending aims to deter war rather than provoke it.

**Context & perspectives:** Taiwan’s government frames the increase as a necessary investment in its own capabilities so that peace does not rest on wishes alone. Outlets including The Hindu report the budget as part of successive administrations’ efforts to improve fighting ability and demonstrate seriousness to the United States, Taiwan’s main security backer. Al Jazeera notes that Lai welcomed U.S.-China talks while insisting on respect for Taiwan’s right to exist. The two framings share the undisputed fact of rising military activity by China; they differ on whether the spending primarily reassures Washington or simply raises the cost of any future confrontation. Successive administrations have ramped up military spending as Taiwan improves its fighting abilities to avoid a potential Chinese invasion. Mr. Lai’s government has earmarked the record sum while stating that peace cannot rely solely on wishes and tolerance cannot become a pretext for aggressors. The accounts agree that China insists the island is part of its territory and has threatened force to annex it.

## Understanding the Issue: How Taiwan uses defence budgets to reassure partners
Most coverage treats higher military spending as simple preparation for conflict. In Taiwan’s case the budget also functions as a signalling device within an informal security relationship. Successive governments have increased outlays to demonstrate that Taipei is serious about its own defence, which in turn makes U.S. support politically easier to sustain in Washington. The mechanism works because the United States has no formal treaty obligation; visible self-help by Taiwan reduces the domestic political cost for any administration that chooses to sell arms or maintain forward presence. Knowing this, when you hear claims that the spending is either purely provocative or purely defensive, you can check whether the stated rationale includes explicit references to reassuring the United States. One concrete thing to check is whether future budget documents list specific U.S. interoperability goals alongside the headline totals. The United States switched official diplomatic relations from Taipei to Beijing nearly 50 years ago, but Washington remains Taipei’s most important security backer. Taiwan’s 23 million people live under the constant threat of an attack by China.
