Now, to really understand this story, there's something most coverage leaves out.

Most coverage of an ICC prosecutor’s removal treats it as a simple personnel decision.

Yet the court’s structure gives the Assembly of States Parties, the body of member countries, the formal power to remove the prosecutor through a two-thirds vote after an independent review.

The process begins with an internal investigation by the court’s disciplinary mechanisms.

It then moves to the assembly for a final decision.

This creates multiple layers that aim to balance independence with oversight.

This setup was written into the Rome Statute to prevent any single government from easily sidelining a prosecutor.

It also means removals can take months and require broad diplomatic agreement.

The Rome Statute established these steps in nineteen ninety-eight when the court was created.

It drew on lessons from earlier tribunals where political pressure had interfered with prosecutions.

Knowing this, when reports describe a removal as sudden or politically driven, readers can check whether the assembly vote and the underlying investigative report have been made public.

The assembly’s role ensures that no one country can act alone.

This protects the court’s independence while still allowing accountability when evidence of misconduct emerges.

The concrete detail to verify next time is whether the assembly has published the timeline and vote tally for the removal.

Checking the official record also reveals how many states participated in the decision and whether any formal objections were recorded during the process.
