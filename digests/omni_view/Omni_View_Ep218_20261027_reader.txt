To really understand how a party-list vote becomes a government, you have to look past the night's projection and into the bargaining that starts once the count is certified.

The common misunderstanding is that the seat tally itself decides who won.

The system allocates seats strictly by party-list vote share.

You vote for a party slate, not a local candidate, and seats follow that share.

A low threshold means even small parties can enter the Knesset and hold leverage in coalition talks.

The structure rewards parties that can deliver narrow but reliable blocs of seats to larger alliances.

Historical patterns show that prime ministers have repeatedly traded policy concessions on settlements, religious issues, or budgets to secure those seats.

The same low-threshold rule has produced multi-party coalitions in every recent Knesset.

That makes the post-election negotiation phase the true test of any reported lead, not the first projection on television.

When a bloc is declared the winner on exit polls, those numbers may not guarantee a stable majority.

They may only open extended bargaining.

Primary documents from the Central Elections Committee provide the raw seat totals that determine who actually controls the majority threshold.
