Now, to really understand this story, there is something most coverage leaves out.

Most coverage treats the IEA’s deficit numbers as simple counts of missing barrels.

Yet the agency actually combines reported production data, tanker tracking, and refinery throughput estimates that can shift when conflict disrupts reporting from one side.

During past supply shocks, such as the 2019 attacks on Saudi facilities, the IEA revised its initial deficit figures upward once satellite and shipping data filled gaps left by official statistics.

The same process is now underway with the Iran-related disruption, where some export routes face restrictions while others reroute.

The agency was founded after the 1973 oil crisis to coordinate responses among industrialised importers and has since expanded its monitoring tools to include commercial data providers.

When conflict limits access to ground reports, analysts rely more heavily on vessel tracking and satellite imagery of storage sites.

Knowing this, when you hear a headline claiming the deficit has doubled, you can check whether the increase stems from new physical losses or from improved data collection that simply reveals earlier under-counting.
