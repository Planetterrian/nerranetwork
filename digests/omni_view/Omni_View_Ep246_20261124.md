## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Malaysia sets dates to send Rohingya back to Myanmar
**What happened (neutral):** Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim told parliament that Malaysia will send more than 6,500 Rohingya refugees back to Myanmar, Al Jazeera reported. The announcement came five days after the International Court of Justice — the United Nations’ top court — ruled that Myanmar’s security forces committed genocide against the Rohingya, a mostly Muslim minority. Anwar said 1,500 Myanmar nationals will leave on three navy vessels from December 6 to 7, and that 5,303 more will be flown back in December at the Myanmar government’s expense. As of August, the UNHCR — the UN refugee agency — had 225,740 refugees and asylum seekers registered in Malaysia, about 90 percent of them from Myanmar. The United Nations says the Rohingya make up two-thirds of the 190,000 Myanmar refugees and asylum seekers there.

**Context & perspectives:** CONTESTED. In late September, about 1,500 migrants were sent back in a scheme rights groups called opaque. The court has said Myanmar must allow and facilitate the safe, voluntary and dignified return of people displaced inside the country or abroad. Anwar said many asylum seekers were victims of atrocities by Myanmar’s regime, that hosting them had become a financial burden and a security risk, and that waiting for other countries to resettle them had not worked. He said talks with Myanmar’s former junta chief, now President Min Aung Hlaing — hosted in Malaysia last month despite rights-group criticism — were meant to protect Malaysia’s interests and fit the court’s call for a safe and dignified return. Rights groups have warned that legitimising that government can endanger people sent back; Malaysia said late last month it would seek clarification of reports that hundreds of recent returnees were in military detention, a request Anwar did not mention in this speech. The South China Morning Post adds a separate limit on outside help: Anwar confirmed Turkey will not take 10,000 Rohingya from Malaysia, which hosts more than 133,000 members of the community and is not a party to the UN Refugee Convention, the treaty that sets international rules for protecting refugees.

The shared legal text is the court’s return order. Anwar’s case is that Malaysia cannot keep carrying the cost and the risk, that third-country resettlement has failed, and that a negotiated return is how a host state gives effect to “safe and dignified” language. The rights-group case is that an opaque earlier transfer, reports of military detention, and official contact with the former junta make “voluntary” and “safe” hard to credit unless the people themselves are choosing to go and someone independent can see what happens on arrival. Anwar has already said many of those in Malaysia were victims of atrocities; the articles do not record a matching concession from the rights groups.

**Questions to consider:** Will the three navy vessels leave on December 6 and 7 with the 1,500 people Anwar named?

**What happened (neutral):** South Korea scrambled fighter jets and briefly told residents of Ulleung Island to take shelter after Russian military planes approached on Tuesday, Al Jazeera reported. In 2019, South Korean jets fired warning shots after a Russian aircraft entered territorial airspace near Dokdo during a joint patrol with Chinese bombers; Moscow denied a violation then. Moscow has not commented on Tuesday’s flight. The last panic on Ulleung was in 2022, when North Korean missiles triggered sirens. The two Koreas remain technically at war under a 1953 armistice, not a peace treaty — which is why a North Korean label on the alert mattered, and why the correction mattered.

### 3) US Army says Iowa lead-explosive blasts will go ahead
**What happened (neutral):** The United States Army will blow up a deteriorating stockpile of toxic lead explosives at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, about 240 kilometres southeast of Des Moines, Al Jazeera reported. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle said late Monday that going ahead was a “consensus decision” among the federal agencies involved, the courts, and state officials. The plan is two controlled detonations of 68 drums holding almost 4,500 kilograms (10,000 pounds) of degraded lead azide, a chemical used in munitions. A three-judge panel of the 8th US Circuit Court of Appeals lifted an earlier stay on Monday, after President Donald Trump had told the Army to wait until the court ruled in its favour.

**Context & perspectives:** CONTESTED. Everything turns on which danger is larger: a planned blast, or leaving volatile material in place. The Environmental Protection Agency — the US pollution regulator — says a managed detonation is far less risky than a spontaneous explosion, a risk it says grows as Iowa gets colder, and that chemically neutralising the drums or moving them could itself set off an uncontrolled blast. Environmental groups sued to block a special permit, arguing the blasts could send a plume of toxic lead dust over homes, crops, and livestock well beyond the site. Telle said every expert agency agrees and that Iowa’s current and incoming governors say the call belongs to the experts. Before the stay was lifted, Army lawyers had told the court the service intended to detonate with or without approval, citing an emergency exemption from federal environmental law. The groups’ suit treats that permit, and the reach of any dust, as the point the experts have not settled. The articles do not quote the groups conceding the cold-weather risk, or the EPA conceding how far a plume might travel.

## Understanding the Issue: A return order is not a ban on sending people back
People often hear a genocide judgment and assume every country must keep the survivors and must refuse every flight home. That mixes three different jobs. What follows is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from a single document in today’s list.

A court can decide that a state’s forces committed genocide and can order that state to allow people to come back in safety, of their own choice, and with dignity. That order binds the state that was sued. It does not, by itself, give every other capital a new duty to house those people forever.

A second rule sits in the refugee treaty: a country that has joined it generally may not push someone back to a place where they face serious harm. A country that never joined does not carry that treaty duty in the same way, even if its officials still talk about safety and choice.

“Voluntary” is the hinge. A voluntary return is a person’s decision, not a bus timetable set by the host and the country they fled. “Safe” and “dignified” are conditions on the receiving state — papers, no punishment for having left, somewhere to live — not a slogan either government can stamp on a passenger list.

Coverage that says a return “fits” a court ruling is using the court’s adjectives. Coverage that says people are being removed after a genocide finding is using the direction of travel. Both can be literally true and still hide whether anyone asked the passengers.

When you next read a return story, look for two checkable things the adjectives do not replace: a record that the people agreed, and the name of someone independent who will see them after they land.

## Media-literacy note
When two outlets cover the same decision, read the paragraph that carries a number before you read the paragraph that carries a motive. A court order, a parliamentary sentence, and a rights-group warning are three kinds of claim; each can be accurate and still answer a different question. If a figure matters to you, open the document or the speech it came from, not only the headline that borrowed it.
