Now, to really understand this story, there is something most coverage leaves out.

Most people assume exchange rates simply reflect trade balances or central-bank policy, yet the Big Mac index reveals a different layer: how much a standardized basket of goods actually costs in local currency.

The index works by comparing the price of one McDonald’s Big Mac across countries and then checking whether that price matches the official exchange rate.

If a burger costs far more in one place after conversion, the local currency is considered overvalued on a purchasing-power basis.

This approach draws on the economic idea of purchasing-power parity, which holds that identical goods should cost the same everywhere once exchange rates adjust.

Over four decades the index has repeatedly shown large and persistent gaps, especially in emerging markets where non-traded costs such as rent and labor differ sharply.

One concrete illustration appears in the nineteen-nineties Brazilian data, where the local-currency price of the same burger jumped from thirty-eight hundred cruzeiros to seventy-seven thousand cruzeiros in a single year before the currency was retired.

Knowing this mechanism helps readers see why a headline about a cheap or expensive currency often rests on more than export data alone.

When coverage cites the index, check whether the story also mentions adjustments for taxes, local wages, or non-traded services that the simple burger price cannot capture.
