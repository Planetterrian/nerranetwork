### 1) Google loses final appeal over record EU antitrust fine
**What happened (neutral):** The European Court of Justice rejected Alphabet's challenge to a 2018 European Commission penalty of roughly 4.7 billion dollars. The original ruling found Google had abused its Android dominance by requiring device makers to pre-install its search and browser apps. The decision ends years of litigation and requires payment of the fine.

**Perspectives:** European regulators view the outcome as validation of their authority to curb practices that lock users into one company's ecosystem. Technology companies and some US officials argue such fines risk fragmenting global markets and slowing innovation by punishing successful scale. Both sides accept that Android holds a large share of mobile devices in Europe; the disagreement centers on whether pre-installation deals harmed competition or simply reflected consumer preference for integrated services.

**Questions to consider:**

## Understanding the Issue: How EU courts review competition fines
Most coverage of large tech fines assumes the appeal process simply re-examines whether a company broke the rules. In practice the European Court of Justice reviews whether the Commission followed its own procedures, applied the correct legal tests, and set a proportionate penalty; it rarely re-tries the underlying facts. This structure means companies can succeed on narrow procedural grounds even when the core finding of dominance stands. Knowing this distinction helps readers separate headlines about "Google wins" from the narrower legal victories that sometimes occur. Next time you see a report on a fine being upheld or reduced, ask: which part of the Commission's reasoning was actually challenged, and what precedent does the ruling set for future cases involving similar market power?
