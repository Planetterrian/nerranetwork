## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Iran rations power as winter gas runs short
**What happened (neutral):** Iran has begun rolling electricity cuts of about two hours a day, Al Jazeera reports, across a grid that supplies 92 million people. Mohammad Allahdad, head of the national electricity company Tavanir, told state television that distribution limits would start Sunday because of the fuel supply. President Masoud Pezeshkian has asked people to use less power; in a televised interview with state broadcaster IRIB, the Islamic Republic of Iran Broadcasting, the office lights were off and daylight was let in instead. The country holds part of the world’s largest natural gas field and the world’s third-largest proven oil reserves, yet a United States naval blockade of Iranian ports, sanctions, and strikes on energy sites have left the system short. Government spokeswoman Fatemeh Mohajerani warned that the gas gap could reach 430 million cubic metres a day this winter, against about 300 million in earlier years. Saeed Tavakoli, managing director of the National Iranian Gas Company, said four processing plants damaged early in the fighting had cut capacity by 230 million cubic metres a day, of which about 100 million had been restored. The company warned that 11 of Iran’s 31 provinces could struggle, especially Mazandaran, Golestan, and North Khorasan. Pezeshkian told the ISNA news agency that attacks on gas infrastructure had made a hard winter more likely, and told investors on November 21 that Iran faced a “silent economic war” tougher than a war with missiles, the state agency IRNA reported. An Al Jazeera reading of the grid puts about 86 percent of electricity on natural gas, about 7 percent on oil-fired plants, about 5 percent on hydropower, and about 2 percent on nuclear power, mainly the Bushehr reactor. Energy Minister Abbas Aliabadi said in August that war damage had taken 4,200 megawatts offline and that repairs were under way. Shortages and cuts were already happening before the United States and Israel attacked Iran on February 28.

**Context & perspectives:** Tehran has refused Washington’s terms for ending the war, which Al Jazeera describes as stuck. Some gas plants can switch to diesel or fuel oil when gas is tight, which is why a gas shortfall shows up as timed power cuts rather than only as cold homes.

## Understanding the Issue: How a winter gas shortage becomes a timed power cut
A common misreading is that a country with huge oil and gas reserves goes dark only if the fuel itself has vanished, or that a short daily cut means the whole grid has collapsed. The host’s own explanation of the mechanism is this. In a gas-heavy system, the same fuel often heats homes and turns the turbines that make electricity. In winter, heating demand rises just as the network is least able to spare gas for power stations. Operators then have two scarce things to protect: pressure in the gas pipes, and the steady frequency of the electric grid. If they let every customer draw at once, either the gas pressure falls until more plants trip offline, or the grid frequency slips and larger areas go dark without warning. A rolling cut is a queue. Neighbourhoods, or whole districts, lose power for a set stretch so that hospitals, water plants, and some industry can stay on, and so that the same homes are not dark for the entire day. That is a ration, chosen in advance, not the same event as a storm tearing down lines. It also explains why fixing a damaged turbine does not, by itself, end the cuts: if the missing piece is fuel to burn, a repaired plant still sits idle. When you next read a story like this, separate three lines that are often folded into one: how much generating equipment is broken, how much fuel is actually reaching the plants, and whether the darkness is on a published schedule or arriving without warning.
## Media-literacy note
When two official voices describe one explosion, write down who claims a fire and who claims an interception before you decide which picture you believe. A wage figure, a treaty clause, and a death count are all checkable against the office that issued them, not against the loudest headline. If a story matters to you, open the second link and see which sentence that outlet actually adds.
