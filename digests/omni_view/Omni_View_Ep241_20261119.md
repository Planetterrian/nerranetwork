## Today's lead story (1)

### 1) Aid groups score the Gaza peace plan four of twenty-six
**What happened (neutral):** One year after the United Nations Security Council endorsed the Trump administration’s 20-point Comprehensive Plan to end the Gaza Conflict, five aid organisations graded that plan at four of 26 possible points, Al Jazeera reported. The Danish Refugee Council, the Norwegian Refugee Council, Oxfam, Refugees International and Save the Children gave it zero on humanitarian access and one point on civilian protection. The plan bars territorial occupation and requires Israeli forces to withdraw. The Israeli military “Yellow Line” — the boundary Palestinians are not allowed to cross — has instead moved west. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, the UN office that tracks humanitarian conditions, known as OCHA, put Israeli control at 58 percent when the ceasefire began 13 months ago and at 65 percent now. Israel has said it controls 70 percent; a UN official told the Security Council that in June. OCHA said last month that about 2.1 million Palestinians were confined to 128 square kilometres, about 16,000 people per square kilometre. A more recent map analysis by NPR put the area at 109 square kilometres, nearly 19,300 people per square kilometre. Agencies said at least 1,650 Palestinians have been killed and 5,975 wounded since the ceasefire declared in October last year. This week Israeli forces began a new ground offensive in central Gaza, moving tanks west of the line and issuing new forced evacuation orders.

**Context & perspectives:** Shaun Hughes, World Food Programme country director for Palestine, said that in Gaza it does not feel like a ceasefire: military control of nearly 70 percent leaves almost no space to grow food, fishing is prohibited and life-threatening, and unemployment is over 80 percent, so people have little means of survival without aid. Lilu Thapa of the Danish Refugee Council called the expanding line “displacement by design” and the ceasefire a ceasefire in name only. Ramy Abdu, founder of the Euro-Mediterranean Human Rights Monitor, said Israel is acquiring territory by force, and that the US-chaired Board of Peace — the body set up under the plan, with an International Stabilization Force — has no meaningful Palestinian representation and has become political cover for occupation. Mohsen Abu Ramadan of the Palestinian NGOs Network said the Board failed the international community and continues to provide political cover for atrocities. The five organisations’ scorecard is the public accounting they released as the offensive resumed.

### 3) World Court finds genocidal intent against the Rohingya
**What happened (neutral):** The International Court of Justice — the UN court that hears cases between countries — found that Myanmar acted with “genocidal intent” in specific cases during 2017 operations that, the court said, killed some 10,000 Rohingya, Al Jazeera reported. Court president Yuji Iwasawa said Myanmar’s security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, committed widespread sexual violence, used extreme violence against children, and attacked people fleeing in large numbers to Bangladesh. More than 730,000 mainly Muslim Rohingya fled in that crackdown. The Hindu’s headline on the same sitting described the ruling as ongoing even as it reported that the court found soldiers had killed and raped Rohingya, harmed children, and destroyed villages.

**Context & perspectives:** The Gambia filed the case in 2019, accusing Myanmar of trying to destroy the Rohingya and of breaching the 1948 Genocide Convention, the treaty that defines genocide and requires states to prevent and punish it. It asked the court to order an end to illegal acts against the Rohingya and punishment for those responsible. Myanmar has said the crackdown answered attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army on police posts. The New York Times noted that Aung San Suu Kyi addressed the court’s judges in 2019 and did not condemn the pogroms. The UN says more than 1.3 million Rohingya remain displaced across South and Southeast Asia, including 1.2 million in camps in Cox’s Bazar, Bangladesh, with shrinking aid and dangerous sea crossings.

### 4) A new US–Venezuela military agreement
**CONTESTED**

**What happened (neutral):** US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a cooperation agreement with Venezuela’s interim government during an unannounced visit to Caracas on Wednesday, Al Jazeera reported. He met interim President Delcy Rodriguez and Defence Minister Gustavo Gonzalez Lopez. The two governments agreed to strengthen cooperation on joint training, intelligence sharing, military equipment and border security. Hegseth said the armed forces would work together against drug trafficking and armed groups. Rodriguez called the visit an extraordinary step and said cooperation would improve training, modernise equipment and open access to new technologies, and that it would not compromise sovereignty. Her government said the Bolivarian National Armed Forces — Venezuela’s armed forces — would benefit directly. The full terms, including how joint operations would work, have not been published.

**Context & perspectives:** The Trump administration says closer military ties will help Venezuela regain control of areas where armed groups and trafficking networks operate. The United States has, in effect, taken control of Venezuela’s oil industry since US forces abducted then-President Nicolas Maduro and took him to New York; Venezuela has the world’s largest oil reserves. On November 10 the House voted 211–204 against impeaching Hegseth for now. A resolution from Republican Thomas Massie had eight articles, covering the US–Israel war on Iran, strikes on suspected drug boats, and the January operation that removed Maduro. Massie said Hegseth ordered an act of war without congressional authorisation, and that the operation was meant to give Washington control of the oil, not only to arrest Maduro on trafficking charges. Democrats have said they want more scrutiny of the 2026 agreements with Caracas after they hold a House majority.

**The disagreement:** Everything turns on a text the public has not seen. Hegseth and Rodriguez present the agreement as a practical security tool: shared training, intelligence, equipment and border work against traffickers and armed groups, with Rodriguez adding that Venezuela’s forces get modern kit without giving up sovereignty. Political analyst Carlos Pina locates the visit in US politics after the November 3 midterms, when Democrats said they would scrutinise Venezuela policy, and after the failed impeachment push — a trip, in his reading, to get ahead of the next Congress. Massie’s articles supply the sharper charge about the earlier operation: that it was war for oil reserves, not a drug arrest. His own text concedes the narrow vote already failed, 211–204, and the new agreement’s operational clauses are still unpublished, so neither the sovereignty promise nor the oil motive can be checked against the document.

### 6) Iran’s blockade, and two readings of whether it works

**What happened (neutral):** Iran’s economy is expected to shrink further under a US naval blockade of its ports, overland trade disruptions and sanctions, Al Jazeera reported, with the pain falling on tens of millions of people. Official figures show gross domestic product shrank 10.1 percent year on year between March 21 and June 20, the first quarter of the Persian calendar. Tehran-based economist Peyman Molavi said that if conditions hold, money-supply growth could rise from nearly 51 percent to 55–60 percent by late March, and that annual inflation could reach about 85 percent, with point-to-point inflation at 100 percent, hitting energy, food, metals and industrial goods hardest. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said the embargo will produce a 25 percent contraction by the year ending March 2027. Central bank governor Abdolnaser Hemmati said the bank has secured 30 billion dollars in foreign currency for the coming months, with essential goods first. Economy Minister Ali Madanizadeh said all overland routes remain open except one with Iraq, closed for security reasons. Al Jazeera also reported that the US has brought a third aircraft carrier into the region to support a possible escalation. AP reported separately that the US military is back to two aircraft carriers in the Middle East. Those two counts are not the same, and neither dispatch here reconciles them.

**Context & perspectives:** After a deadlock in the US–Israel war on Iran, which began with strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei on February 28, Washington has demanded that Tehran reopen the Strait of Hormuz and curb its nuclear programme. Iranian authorities say they can withstand the pressure and have set out their own demands. For decades governments in Tehran have covered budget deficits by printing money, which has fed chronic inflation; the currency crunch from disrupted oil exports has made that worse.

**The disagreement:** The shared ground is a shrinking economy: a 10.1 percent drop in that first quarter, and a blockade both governments treat as real. Bessent’s case is that the embargo is doing the work he wants — a 25 percent contraction by March 2027, large enough to force a political result. Hemmati’s reply is that it is too early to count the winter, and that 30 billion dollars is already set aside so essential imports continue. Madanizadeh adds a mechanism: neighbours’ land routes, except the Iraq crossing, are still open, so isolation is not total. Hemmati does not claim the pressure is painless; his argument is that reserves and time keep a collapse, or a forced concession, from being a result already in hand.

## Understanding the Issue: Why an oil customer is not a political guarantor
A common misreading is that a willing buyer in China makes an economic blockade of Iran fail, or succeed, as a unit. Deutsche Welle reports that in 2025 Chinese buyers took more than 80 percent of Iran’s seaborne crude, much of it through independent refiners and at a steep discount because of sanctions, while Iran grew more dependent on Chinese machinery, electronics and industrial inputs. Mohsen Solhdoost, an assistant professor of international relations at Xi’an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, told Deutsche Welle that if the aim is to cut export earnings, restrict equipment and slow economic and military recovery, the United States can get substantial results without Chinese cooperation — and that interrupting trade is easier to show than determining another government’s political choices. In November, Washington announced Task Force Iron Gate, aimed at disrupting sanctioned goods, military equipment and revenue across Iran’s land borders and the Caspian Sea; goods still move through countries including Iraq, Pakistan and Turkey, under greater scrutiny. Solhdoost’s point is physical: a Chinese buyer cannot keep export revenue flowing by staying willing to buy if newly loaded tankers cannot reach the market. A senior official at Iran’s Plan and Budget Organization said the government was preparing its 2027–2028 budget under several scenarios, including oil revenue at zero — not a decision to adopt that budget, but a sign Tehran is planning for the lifeline to fail. Rail and road can still move spare parts and keep selected factories running; they cannot replace ships at the same scale or cost. Beijing’s economic tie, Solhdoost says, does not mean support for every Iranian policy, and both China and Russia weigh Tehran against their own dealings with Washington. Reading a blockade story, the check is simple: separate what a buyer was willing to purchase from what actually sailed, and see whether the budget on the table still assumes oil income. ## Media-literacy note
When two wire services give different counts for the same deployment, or one desk says a vote is finished and another says it is still expected, the honest move is to name both and wait for the document. A court reading, a scorecard and a budget scenario are all checkable against the institution that issued them. Pick one story from today and open the primary text — a judgment, a treaty note, or the agency release — beside a second newsroom that does not share the first one’s habits.
