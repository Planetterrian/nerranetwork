## Understanding the Issue: How central banks weigh market signals against domestic inflation targets
Most coverage treats central-bank rate decisions as simple reactions to the latest inflation print. In practice, each bank maintains an explicit inflation target and a separate mandate for employment or growth; market pricing is only one input among several. When traders push yields higher, the bank must decide whether those prices reflect genuine shifts in inflation expectations or simply positioning ahead of the meeting. The Bank of Japan has operated for years with an explicit 2 percent target and a commitment to overshoot if needed, while the Federal Reserve balances its dual mandate. Knowing this distinction lets readers separate routine market volatility from actual changes in the bank’s reaction function. The next time a headline claims “markets force the central bank to act,” check whether the bank’s own published projections still show inflation returning to target on its existing path. Historical episodes show that both the Bank of Japan and the Federal Reserve have at times held rates steady despite market pricing that suggested otherwise, when their internal forecasts indicated the target remained achievable. This pattern appears in records from the past decade when forward guidance diverged from futures prices for several quarters. Readers can therefore compare the central bank’s quarterly outlook documents with contemporaneous market-implied rate paths to judge whether policy is truly being driven by external pressure or by the institution’s own assessment.
