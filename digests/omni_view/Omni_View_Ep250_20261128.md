## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Yemen strikes widen as hunger hits emergency levels
**What happened (neutral):** Yemen’s internationally recognised government has launched air strikes across Houthi-held territory, and Colonel Majed al-Nuzaili, a military spokesman, said government forces carried out 726 attacks in the 24 hours before Friday and claimed 121 fighters and commanders were killed or wounded, Al Jazeera reported. Al Jazeera’s Yousef Mawry, reporting from Sanaa, said the Saudi-led coalition that backs the government hit more than six governorates overnight, including a gas station in Sanaa’s Tahrir district that injured 13 people, three of them critically, and a later strike that the Houthis said killed a man and his wife in their home. He also described raids on a fuel station in Bani Matar, on Taiz, and five raids in Mocha that targeted a power station. Houthi-affiliated media reported that a Saudi air strike killed 11 civilians, most of them children, in al-Turbah in Taiz; Al Jazeera could not confirm that toll, and coalition forces did not comment. The government-run Saba news agency reported that Houthi attacks on government locations in Taiz on Saturday killed three civilians, including a 10-year-old boy, and wounded three others, including an infant. Coalition officials said a Houthi missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted. The claims and casualties could not be independently verified. The World Food Programme and the Food and Agriculture Organization — the United Nations agencies for food aid and farming — warned on Friday that hunger in Yemen is likely to worsen over the next six months. About 1.8 million people faced emergency levels of hunger in government-held areas. More than 300,000 people have been newly displaced since the escalation began, mostly in Taiz, Lahij and Aden. The agencies said the Houthis encircled Taiz in early October, that Houthi-held areas were becoming harder to reach, and that the group was holding 73 United Nations staff. Aid groups say donor cuts, including by the United States and other Western countries, have deepened the crisis, with food and fuel prices soaring.

**Context & perspectives:** The Iran-backed Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014, and Saudi Arabia led a coalition from March 2015 in support of the recognised government. Fighting resumed in July after a 2022 ceasefire collapsed, amid tensions around the United States-Israel war on Iran. Al-Nuzaili said fighting was ongoing in Ma’rib, al-Jawf, Taiz, Lahij, Shabwah, Midi and al-Dhale.

This is CONTESTED. The shared ground is that strikes and ground fighting have resumed and that the two United Nations food agencies, not either army, produced the hunger and displacement figures. Colonel al-Nuzaili’s case is that 726 attacks in a day are a military campaign against Houthi fighters and commanders, with 121 of them killed or wounded, and that Houthi fire — including a missile toward Riyadh and attacks that Saba said killed civilians in Taiz — is what government forces are answering. The Houthi account, carried by Houthi-affiliated media, is that a Saudi strike in al-Turbah killed 11 civilians, most of them children, so the offensive is landing on families, not only on fighters. Al Jazeera could not confirm that civilian toll, the coalition did not comment, and neither side’s casualty claims were independently verified — a limit each account has to live with.

Vox’s case, as France 24 described the campaign, is that the July arrivals in Ceuta made immigration the issue that should decide the government. Polls are not ballots, and Feijoo still needs Vox for 176 seats.

## Understanding the Issue: Why the party in first place can still miss the government
A common misreading of a European election night is that the party with the most seats has won the government. That is not how this kind of parliament works. The following is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from a separate study. A prime minister who loses a vote he or she treated as essential can dissolve the chamber and pick an early date, which is what a snap election is: the calendar moves, the rules for forming a government do not. In a multiparty chamber, governing requires a majority of seats, or enough partners to supply one, not a plurality. A first-place party that is short of that line must bargain with a rival whose voters it spent the campaign opposing, or stay in opposition while a second-place leader assembles a wider set of partners. Regional and nationalist parties can matter as much as the national runner-up, because their seats count the same on the night a government is proposed. Campaigns that sort voters into two blocs make that arithmetic starker: few people cross the aisle, so the result is less a swing of individuals than a test of which bloc can hold its own voters and then find allies. Abroad, a leader can be admired for foreign-policy stands that never appear on the domestic ballot, where rent, food and a doctor’s appointment decide the vote. When you next read a “winner” headline from a parliament like Spain’s, check whether any combination of named parties can actually seat a government — not only who finished first.

## Media-literacy note
Casualty claims in a war, and the first hours after a blast, often arrive from one side’s spokesman or one hospital, and they move. When two outlets give two tolls, keep both and note who counted. A poll is a survey of voters before they vote, not the seat total. For the stories that will still matter next week, open a second outlet from another country and look for the document underneath: a court ruling, a United Nations food analysis, a company or ministry statement.
