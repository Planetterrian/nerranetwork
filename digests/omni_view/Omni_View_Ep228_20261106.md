## Today's lead story (1)
### 1) Israeli president to name candidate for new government on Sunday
**What happened (neutral):** Israeli President Isaac Herzog will announce on Sunday, November 8, which party leader he will ask to try to form a government. Herzog reached this decision after holding post-election talks with leaders of every party represented in parliament. Forty-six members of the 120-seat Knesset formally recommended Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for the mandate. Sixty-seven members recommended opposition figure Gadi Eisenkot instead. Seven lawmakers abstained from making any recommendation.

**Context & perspectives:** France24 frames the recommendation tally as the direct result of Herzog’s systematic consultations with all party heads, presenting the 67-to-46 split as the decisive numerical outcome that will guide the president’s Sunday decision. Al Jazeera places the same count in the longer context of Netanyahu’s extended period in office, highlighting that the larger number of recommendations for Eisenkot opens the possibility of a leadership change even though Netanyahu’s party remains the single largest bloc. Both outlets treat the raw recommendation numbers and the fixed Sunday announcement date as undisputed facts; they differ only in the degree of emphasis each places on whether the outcome represents routine coalition arithmetic or a potential break from the current prime minister.

## Understanding the Issue: How coalition governments are formed in Israel
Most coverage of Israeli elections focuses on the final vote totals, yet the actual formation of a government depends on a separate, post-election recommendation process that can produce outcomes different from the raw seat count. After elections, the president meets every party leader and tallies formal recommendations for who should receive the mandate to form a coalition; a simple majority of recommendations is sufficient even if no single party holds a majority of seats. This mechanism, rooted in Israel’s proportional-representation system, allows smaller parties and shifting alliances to determine the prime minister rather than the largest single bloc alone. In the current case the numerical split of 67 to 46 recommendations has produced a clear signal that the president will follow when making the Sunday announcement. The same process previously allowed parties with fewer seats to assemble coalitions when they secured enough external support. Knowing this process shows why a party that finished first in seats can still lose the chance to govern if it cannot secure enough external recommendations. When you next hear claims that one leader “won” an Israeli election, check whether the story distinguishes between seat totals and the separate recommendation count that actually decides who receives the mandate. The 67-to-46 split reported by both France24 and Al Jazeera illustrates how the recommendation tally, rather than seat numbers alone, determines the next step.
