To really understand how a government is assembled in Israel, you have to separate the election-night seat count from a later recommendation process that can send the first mandate to a leader whose party did not finish first.

The usual mix-up is to hear who won and assume that person automatically becomes prime minister, but proportional representation hands out seats according to each party's share of the national vote, so many parties enter parliament and no single one may hold a majority on its own.

After the votes are counted, the president meets every party leader, asks whom they formally recommend to receive the mandate — the official first chance to form a coalition — and tallies those recommendations.

A simple majority of the recommendations is enough even if no single party holds a majority of seats, which is why smaller parties and shifting alliances can determine the prime minister rather than the largest bloc alone, and why a coalition — an alliance of parties that can survive together in parliament — often matters more than any one party's raw seat total.

That same process has previously allowed parties with fewer seats to assemble coalitions when they secured enough external support, so finishing first in seats does not by itself lock in the right to govern if the recommendations go elsewhere.

When you next hear that someone won an Israeli election, check whether the report distinguishes the seat total from the recommendation count that actually decides who receives the mandate, and put two outlets from different regions side by side so you can see which facts they share and where interpretation begins.
