in the real world, skip the opening speeches and follow the slower path from a target to a
national law and a budget line. Each country submits its own plan, known as a nationally determined
contribution, and there is no central enforcement body that can find or punish a government that
falls short. The system instead relies on regular reporting, peer review among governments,
and domestic political pressure from voters and courts, which means a promise made in
a conference hall has to survive a finance ministry, a parliament, and a courtroom before
it changes what comes out of a smoke stack or a tailpipe.
The last three major summits showed a consistent pattern. When a country's plan is backed
by specific legislation and funding streams, emissions trends bend. When the plan remains
a high-level pledge, progress stalls. Peer review lets governments compare notes, but it does
not impose penalties for missed targets, so domestic budget fights can delay the translation of
an international number into a binding national rule for years. When a headline offers a new
temperature goal, the practical check is whether that same government has already passed the laws
