puts function as paid limit orders that compensate you for waiting to buy a stock you already want
to own. It works like leaving a bid on a used car at a price you have already decided as fair,
and the dealer pays you a fee every month your offer stays live. If the car never comes down to your
number, you keep the fee for waiting, and if it does, you buy at the price you named. In the options
play growth lab example, a rank list of names shows the put strike, the premium collected, and the
probability of assignment so you can compare the effective purchase price against your target
entry, then filter that list by probability of profit and annualize return before placing the
order. You open the position by selling the put and posting cash equal to the strike times 100
shares. If the stock stays above the strike you keep the premium as income and repeat the process,
while assignment simply delivers the shares at your chosen net cost. Implementation starts in any
options approved brokerage by scanning the chain for 30-45-day expires, where implied
volatility sits above the 20-day average, then confirming the premium covers at least
1% of the strike. Select a name trading near your target entry price, sell the 30-day put
at the strike one standard deviation below the current price, and record the premium received
as a percentage of the strike. That single step turns waiting time into income while enforcing
discipline on the entry you are willing to pay.
The approach worked well in the 2022-2023 rate hike period, when elevated volatility-produced
richer premiums, yet it fails when a sudden gap down leaves you assigned shares well below
your original target, and forces an immediate unrealized loss.
