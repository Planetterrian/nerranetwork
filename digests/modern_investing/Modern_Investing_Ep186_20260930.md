### Investor Education: How to Read an Earnings Report
Imagine you bought a consumer stock last week after seeing a headline about resilient same-store sales. Your order filled at one hundred eighty dollars. The actual earnings release shows revenue up four percent but operating margins compressed by eighty basis points because of higher energy costs. Revenue versus earnings tells you whether growth came from higher prices or more units sold. EPS and the forward P/E ratio show whether the market already prices in the next two quarters of expected profit. Forward guidance from management often moves the stock more than the headline beat or miss. Check the balance sheet for rising debt or falling cash balances that could limit future flexibility. Free cash flow after capital expenditures reveals whether reported earnings translate into actual cash the company can return to shareholders. The quickest check is whether the company beat or missed both revenue and EPS consensus while raising or lowering its full-year outlook. Management commentary in the release often highlights risks such as supply chain issues or currency movements that quantitative numbers alone may not capture. Balance sheet red flags include rising short-term debt relative to cash reserves or declining working capital that could constrain dividend growth.
