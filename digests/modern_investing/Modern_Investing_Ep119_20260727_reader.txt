Once you have the entry discipline, the next layer is making sure any single position can't hurt the whole book.

Let's move into position sizing.

Imagine you allocated one thousand dollars to a single energy name last week when oil spiked on Middle East headlines.

Your order filled at the opening print, but the position size left no room for the seven percent reversal that followed.

The mechanism works through simple arithmetic.

A two percent portfolio allocation limits any single loss to roughly twenty dollars on a one thousand dollar account even if the stop is hit at ten percent below entry.

A ten percent allocation turns that same ten percent drop into a one hundred dollar hit that compounds across correlated holdings.

Professionals always calculate the maximum acceptable loss in dollar terms first.

Then they back into share quantity using the distance to their chosen stop, whether fixed, trailing, or volatility-based.

The biggest mistake with position sizing is letting a promising setup dictate allocation instead of the other way around.

Instead, always size every new position so that a full stop-out equals no more than one percent of total portfolio value.

Correlation math adds another layer.

When energy names move together on the same geopolitical headline, even properly sized individual stops can still produce a portfolio-level drawdown that exceeds the one percent rule if three or four holdings trigger simultaneously.

The Kelly Criterion simplified for retail use suggests betting no more than the edge divided by the odds against.

In practice that means shrinking position size further when volatility spikes above the twenty day average.

Trailing stops adjust automatically as price moves in your favor.

Yet they still require an initial hard stop to define the maximum loss before the trail begins.

Portfolio drawdowns become psychologically easier to manage when each position is capped at one percent risk.

The investor can review the setup calmly rather than reacting to a single oversized loss.

The concrete misconception to avoid is treating every new idea as equally important.
