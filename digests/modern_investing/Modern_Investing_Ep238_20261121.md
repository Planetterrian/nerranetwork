### Strategy Spotlight
Defensive stock selection during corrections uses beta below 0.8 and payout ratios under 70 percent to identify names that typically lose less than the broader market. The Motley Fool Canada article highlights Fortis (TSX: FTS) as one such name, trading near $85 with a capital plan supporting high-single-digit earnings growth and mid-single-digit dividend growth. The utility also carries a recent quarterly earnings beat and dividend increase, yet its price-to-earnings multiple sits above 24 times, which is on the higher side of its historical range. To apply it, screen the TSX for utilities or consumer staples with five-year beta under 0.8 and dividend coverage above 1.5 times using your broker's screener, then compare the current payout ratio to the sector median. Next, calculate how many shares a fixed dollar amount such as $7,000 would purchase at the current price and verify that the position size fits your overall allocation rules before executing inside a TFSA or RRSP. This approach worked in the 2022 rate-hike period when lower-beta utilities outperformed the TSX by roughly 8 percentage points on average. It fails when interest rates rise faster than expected and compress valuations across the entire defensive group at once. The technique is best used inside a TFSA or RRSP where the lower volatility reduces the urge to sell during drawdowns. ### Investor Education: How TFSA Contribution Room Actually Rebuilds
You contribute $10,000 from available TFSA room to a stock that later falls to $6,000. The $4,000 decline does not restore room because the Canada Revenue Agency only counts withdrawals, not unrealized or realized losses inside the account. The Motley Fool Canada article walks through the exact arithmetic: withdrawing the remaining $6,000 creates $6,000 of new room the following calendar year, while the original $10,000 never reappears. Selling the position and leaving the cash inside the account also fails to generate new room. A pro habit is to keep a running spreadsheet of every contribution and withdrawal date across all TFSA providers before replacing any sold position in the same year. The article notes that contribution-room tracking requires records from every provider because the Canada Revenue Agency does not automatically update statements with unused room. The common misconception is that selling a loser automatically frees up the full original contribution amount; the fix is to treat only actual withdrawals as the source of future room and to reallocate inside the account without cashing out when possible. The article illustrates the point with an iShares Core S&P/TSX Capped Composite Index ETF example at $57.76 per unit, showing that $6,000 would purchase 103 whole units for $5,949.28 before fees, leaving a small cash remainder for later deployment. Source: [fool.ca](https://www.fool.ca/2026/11/20/a-tfsa-loss-doesnt-create-new-room-heres-the-rebuilding-plan-id-use/)
