### Segment 1 — The Open
SpaceX continues to operate as a private company with a valuation shaped by tender offers rather than public markets, yet the question of how ordinary investors might one day participate remains relevant. This episode examines the current state of SpaceX exposure options alongside the broader, reusable process for evaluating any IPO. By the end, listeners will understand the realistic paths available today and carry a disciplined checklist for assessing newly public companies in either Canadian or U.S. accounts.

### Segment 2 — The Background
SpaceX was founded in 2002 and has grown into a major private aerospace firm whose Starlink satellite constellation now generates the majority of its revenue through broadband services, while the core launch business supplies launch capacity and government contracts. The company has raised successive rounds of private capital and conducted periodic tender offers that allow existing shareholders to sell shares to new investors at negotiated prices. These tenders have produced reported valuations in the range of $200 billion or higher in recent years, though exact figures fluctuate with each transaction and remain non-public. Elon Musk has repeatedly stated a preference for keeping the company private to avoid quarterly reporting pressures and short-term market demands. No formal IPO registration or pricing has been announced as of late May 2026, making any discussion of a public listing speculative. Retail investors therefore face two distinct questions: how to gain any exposure while the firm stays private, and how to approach an eventual public offering if one occurs. The second question applies far beyond SpaceX and forms the reusable framework that occupies the second half of this episode.

### Segment 3 — The Core Analysis
Traditional IPOs involve a company filing an S-1 registration statement with the SEC, selecting underwriters who market the shares through a roadshow, and ultimately pricing the offering after collecting indications of interest from institutional buyers. The first-day “pop” often occurs because the offering price is set conservatively to ensure the deal clears, leaving room for immediate trading gains once public supply and demand take over. Direct listings bypass the underwritten allocation process and allow existing shares to trade immediately on an exchange, while SPACs reverse-merge a private company into a publicly traded shell, each route carrying different costs, timelines, and allocation outcomes for retail participants. Retail investors rarely receive meaningful allocations at the IPO price because underwriters prioritize large institutions; most individuals must buy in the open market after trading begins, frequently at a premium to the initial offering price. Post-IPO lock-up agreements typically prevent insiders and early investors from selling for 180 days, after which a wave of supply can pressure the stock if those holders choose to exit. The S-1 itself contains the prospectus that details revenue recognition policies, customer concentration, capital structure, dilution from convertible securities or options, and the use of proceeds; disciplined readers focus on these sections rather than the summary marketing language. Historical studies of IPO cohorts show that, on average, newly public companies have underperformed broad market indices over the subsequent one-to-three years, with the effect most pronounced among firms that price at peak valuation multiples or operate in crowded sectors. Canadian investors face the additional layer of currency conversion and the choice between TFSA, RRSP, or taxable accounts, each carrying different tax treatment for foreign dividends and capital gains. Platforms that facilitate pre-IPO access, such as Forge Global or EquityZen, generally require accredited-investor status and impose minimum investments that place them out of reach for most retail participants. Funds or holding companies that carry SpaceX stakes exist but often trade at premiums or discounts to net asset value and may carry their own liquidity restrictions. Waiting for a public listing, if one materializes, therefore remains the most straightforward route for the majority of individual investors.

### Segment 4 — The Framework
A disciplined investor begins by confirming whether any IPO candidate meets basic criteria of revenue visibility, a credible path to sustained free cash flow, and a share structure that limits excessive dilution. Before committing capital, the same investor reviews the S-1 for related-party transactions, insider ownership percentages, and the size of the lock-up overhang relative to daily trading volume. Account selection follows: a TFSA or RRSP may suit longer-term Canadian holdings where contribution room exists, while a taxable account offers loss-harvesting flexibility if the position is held only briefly. Retail platforms such as Interactive Brokers, Questrade, or Wealthsimple allow post-IPO purchases in either CAD or USD, yet none provide allocation at the offering price. The investor then asks whether the current valuation already prices in optimistic growth assumptions and whether comparable public companies trade at materially lower multiples. Common mistakes include chasing the first-day pop without reviewing lock-up expiration dates, ignoring currency risk on U.S.-listed names, or treating an IPO as inherently different from any other equity purchase. The framework generalizes: apply the same revenue-quality and dilution screens to every newly public company, regardless of sector or headline appeal, and consider a waiting period of several quarters to observe post-lock-up trading behavior and earnings delivery. This approach treats IPOs as one subset of equity opportunities rather than a distinct asset class requiring special rules.

### Segment 5 — The Risks & The Other Side
The strongest bull case for any high-profile listing rests on the possibility that early revenue traction and technological leadership translate into durable market share, yet historical base rates show many such companies later face margin compression or competitive response once public scrutiny increases. The bear case highlights the documented tendency of IPOs to underperform in the years after listing, driven by both initial over-optimism and the eventual release of locked-up shares. Private-market platforms carry their own frictions: accreditation requirements, high minimums, wide bid-ask spreads, and the risk that the platform itself faces liquidity or regulatory issues. Even if SpaceX eventually lists, the timing, pricing, and lock-up terms remain unknown, so any current exposure through indirect vehicles carries basis risk relative to a future public share price. Currency movements between CAD and USD add another layer of volatility for Canadian investors holding U.S. securities. None of these considerations constitute a prediction; they simply illustrate that the range of outcomes is wide and that past cohort performance offers only probabilistic guidance rather than certainty.

### Segment 6 — The Takeaways
Rule one: treat any unannounced IPO as speculative and evaluate exposure routes on their actual accessibility and cost rather than anticipated future liquidity. Rule two: read the S-1 for revenue concentration, dilution mechanics, and lock-up size before considering any post-IPO purchase. Rule three: default to waiting several quarters after listing to observe trading behavior once insiders can sell. Rule four: apply the same account-type and position-sizing discipline used for any equity, recognizing that IPOs carry no special exemption from ordinary market risk. Watch for the next concrete filing of an S-1 by a company with meaningful private-market visibility; that document will provide the first verifiable data set on which to test the framework.
