You already own a hundred shares of CIBC and you want extra cash without selling the position.

Sell a thirty-day call struck at one seven zero dollars for a premium of one dollar and eighty cents, collected immediately, which lowers the effective cost basis to one hundred fifty-seven dollars and twenty cents and caps the upside if shares rally through that strike by expiry.

The buyer pays you for the right to take those shares at the strike; if the stock stays below it, you keep the shares and the premium — think of it as renting out a parking spot on land you still own.

A pro habit is to read open interest and the bid-ask spread on the chain before you send the order, because a wide spread on a low-volume strike can erase most of the income edge.

Covered calls are not free money: they reduce but do not eliminate losses if the stock falls hard, and they clip you in a strong rally.

Size so the premium offsets no more than two to three percent of potential downside before you would sell the shares anyway.

Inside a T F S A or a Registered Retirement Savings Plan, or R R S P, the premium does not trigger an immediate tax bill, which is why this is a Canadian-account mechanic rather than a taxable-account afterthought.

Delta on that one-seven-zero call typically sits around zero point two five, so the position behaves like seventy-five shares of long stock plus twenty-five shares of short stock for risk purposes.

Time decay works for the seller: roughly two-thirds of the premium erodes in the final two weeks if the shares stay range-bound.

A protective put struck at one hundred fifty dollars for two dollars and fifty cents, paired with that call, builds a collar that floors you near one hundred forty-seven dollars and fifty cents and still caps the upside at the short strike.

Covered structures require the shares or the cash to secure the put, which is the difference from naked options: less leverage, fewer margin calls.
