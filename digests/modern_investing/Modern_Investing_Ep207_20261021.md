### Investor Education: Options Basics for Income and Protection
Imagine you already own 100 shares of CIBC at the current price near $159 and want to generate extra cash without selling. You could sell a covered call at the $170 strike expiring in 30 days for a $1.80 premium. That premium is collected immediately and lowers your effective cost basis to $157.20, but it also caps upside if the shares rally above $170 by expiry. The mechanism works because the buyer of the call pays you for the right to buy your shares at the strike; if the stock stays below $170 you keep both the shares and the premium. A pro habit is to check the option chain’s open interest and bid-ask spread before placing the order—wide spreads on low-volume strikes can erase most of the income edge. The common misconception is that covered calls are “free money” with no downside; in reality they reduce but do not eliminate loss if the stock falls sharply, and they limit gains in strong rallies. The one-sentence fix is to size the position so the premium income offsets no more than 2–3% of potential downside before you would normally sell the shares anyway. Canadian investors can execute this strategy inside a TFSA or RRSP without triggering immediate tax consequences on the premium received. The delta of the $170 call is typically around 0.25, meaning the position behaves like 75 shares of long stock plus 25 shares of short stock for risk purposes. Time decay works in the seller’s favor, with roughly two-thirds of the premium eroding in the final two weeks if the stock remains range-bound. Protective puts function differently: buying a $150 put for $2.50 creates a collar when paired with a covered call, capping both downside below $147.50 and upside above $170. The key difference from naked options is that covered strategies require owning the underlying shares or cash to secure the put, which limits leverage and margin calls. Investors should compare the annualized yield from the covered-call premium against the stock’s dividend yield—in CIBC’s case the 2.5% dividend can be supplemented by 4–6% annualized call income depending on volatility levels. This approach suits intermediate investors who already hold bank names for long-term dividend growth and want to monetize time decay without increasing directional risk.
