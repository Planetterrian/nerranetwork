Imagine you allocated one thousand dollars to a data-centre equipment name last week after reading about accelerating infrastructure spend. Your order filled at the open, yet the position size represented eight percent of the overall TFSA rather than the two to three percent range that keeps single-name drawdowns manageable.

The mechanism that actually determines survival is the interaction between position size and stop distance: a seven percent stop on an eight percent allocation creates a zero point five six percent portfolio hit, while the same stop on a two percent allocation limits damage to zero point one four percent.

Professionals always calculate the dollar risk first, then back-solve for shares so that any single stop breach stays inside a preset daily or weekly loss budget. What most retail investors don't realize is that widening the stop to avoid getting shaken out simply increases the position size needed to keep dollar risk constant, which magnifies correlation risk across the rest of the book.

The concrete misconception to avoid is treating stop placement as an afterthought once the entry price is chosen; fix it by deciding the maximum portfolio loss you will accept on the idea before you ever click buy.
