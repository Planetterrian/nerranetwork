### Investor Education: Warren Buffett’s Investment Philosophy
Imagine you bought a Canadian bank stock last month after a modest earnings beat, expecting quick mean reversion to its 200-day moving average. The order filled at the quoted price, yet the actual mechanics involved checking the company’s economic moat, free-cash-flow consistency, and margin of safety before entry. Buffett evolved from Benjamin Graham’s cigar-butt bargains to Charlie Munger’s quality compounders by focusing on businesses with durable competitive advantages that can reinvest capital at high returns for decades. Key principles include staying inside your circle of competence, demanding a margin of safety on valuation, and recognizing that most investors are better served by low-cost index funds rather than stock picking. His notable wins like Coca-Cola and American Express were offset by mistakes such as the initial IBM purchase that ignored shifting technology dynamics. Modern DIY investors can apply this by running a simple checklist on any TSX name: does it have pricing power, predictable earnings, and a balance sheet that survives a recession? The biggest misconception is treating Buffett’s style as simple value hunting; instead, always demand evidence of a long-term moat before committing capital.
