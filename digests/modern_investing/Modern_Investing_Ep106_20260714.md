### Strategy Spotlight
A covered-call ETF strategy lets investors collect premium income on existing holdings by selling out-of-the-money calls each month. Today’s oil spike and chip rebound create conditions where volatility premiums are elevated, making the income layer more attractive than in low-vol environments. To implement, screen for sector ETFs trading near their 50-day moving average with options volume above the 20-day average, then sell the 30-delta call expiring in 30-45 days on platforms such as Questrade or Interactive Brokers. The approach has historically delivered the best risk-adjusted results in sideways or mildly bullish regimes when realized volatility exceeds implied volatility by at least 3 points. The main risk is capping upside during sharp rallies, so position size should stay below 15% of the TFSA. ### Investor Education: How Index Funds Changed Investing Forever
Imagine you bought an S&P 500 ETF in 1976 when Jack Bogle launched the first index fund at Vanguard. Your order filled at the closing NAV, but the real story is what happened in the decades after: most active managers failed to beat the index after fees, a mathematical reality driven by the fact that active funds as a group must underperform the market by their average expense ratio. The 2%+ MER common in Canadian mutual funds versus 0.05-0.25% for index ETFs creates a compounding gap that grows to tens of thousands of dollars over 30 years on a $100,000 portfolio. Professionals always compare total cost of ownership including trading spreads and taxes before choosing active versus passive. The biggest misconception is that paying higher fees buys superior stock selection. Instead, always calculate the fee drag over your expected holding period first.
