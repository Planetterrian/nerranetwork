### Investor Education: How Normal-Course Issuer Bids Tighten Per-Share Metrics
Toronto-Dominion Bank's new authorization to repurchase up to 61 million shares for C$10 billion builds on the 47.2 million shares already retired under the prior program. When a bank reduces its share count while earnings remain stable, EPS and book-value-per-share both rise mechanically even if net income is flat. The mechanism works because the same earnings stream is now divided across fewer shares, but only if the buyback price stays below the intrinsic value the market later assigns. A concrete check is to divide the announced repurchase cap by the current shares outstanding to see the maximum percentage reduction. The pro habit is to compare that percentage against the bank's CET1 ratio target; if the ratio stays comfortably above regulatory minimums after the buyback, the capital return is credible rather than cosmetic. The common misconception is treating every buyback announcement as automatic per-share accretion; the fix is to verify the repurchase price relative to tangible book value before assuming the metric improvement will hold. Investors can run this calculation on any TSX bank filing by pulling the most recent quarterly share count from SEDAR+ and subtracting the announced repurchase ceiling to arrive at the post-buyback float. This same arithmetic applies to the 36% of workers earning above $300,000 who still live paycheck to paycheck, because even high earners benefit when corporate capital returns lift per-share ownership without requiring additional cash outlay.
