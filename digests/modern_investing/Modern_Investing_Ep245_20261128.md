### Strategy Spotlight
Dividend yield comparison in a TFSA requires adjusting U.S. holdings for the 15% withholding tax that cannot be recovered through foreign tax credits. The Motley Fool Canada analysis walks through two insurance names to show the cash-flow difference. Manulife Financial at $60 per share pays a quarterly dividend of $0.485 for a 3.23% yield that arrives in full inside a TFSA. MetLife at a 2.4% gross yield delivers only 85% of that amount after the 15% U.S. withholding, so an investor needs roughly 3.8% gross yield on the American name to match the net Canadian payout on the same $10,000 allocation. To implement the technique, open the brokerage account, pull the current dividend and price for each candidate, apply the 15% reduction to any U.S. ordinary dividend, then compare the net cash received per $10,000 invested. The method works when income is the priority and currency swings are secondary; it failed for many in 2025 when U.S. growth stocks outpaced the after-tax yield gap. Check the exact withholding rate on your broker's tax report before committing capital, and remember that Manulife's Asia earnings growth and record core earnings of $1.24 per share in the latest quarter provide the earnings support behind the distribution. The same article notes that Enbridge now yields 5.9% after pulling back below $66, with 31 consecutive years of dividend increases and $38 billion in secured capital projects expected to lift distributable cash flow about 5% annually. BCE's larger prior cut makes it a cautionary case for yield chasing without checking payout sustainability. The technique therefore favors Canadian names when the goal is maximum after-tax cash inside a TFSA rather than total return. ### Investor Education: How Co-Founder Sales in IPOs Set Price Anchors
You bought Anthropic shares at the open after the $83 IPO and watched the price fall below that level within days. The mechanism is straightforward: the seven co-founders each sold 3,378,378 shares at exactly $83 as part of the offering, locking in nearly $2 billion total while retaining 98.6% of their holdings. Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela each sold $280 million worth, matching the identical block size sold by every other founder. That coordinated sale was disclosed in the offering documents and went directly to underwriters rather than the open market, yet the stock has since traded below the $83 price the insiders accepted. The identical share count across all seven founders points to a pre-planned structure rather than individual decisions, creating a single visible reference point for new shareholders. Any sustained trade below that level signals the market has rejected the valuation the insiders locked in. The pro habit is to note the exact insider sale price and share count on the first trading day, then set an alert 5% below it so you see when the anchor breaks. The common misconception is treating every IPO insider sale as routine noise; the fix is to treat the disclosed sale price as the first real market test of demand and to size any post-IPO position accordingly rather than assuming continued momentum.
