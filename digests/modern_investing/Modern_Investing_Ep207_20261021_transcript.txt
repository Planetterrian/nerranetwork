You already own 100 shares of CIBC and you want extra cash without selling the position.
Sell a 30-day call, struck at $170 for a premium of $1.80, collected immediately, which
lowers the effective cost basis to $157.20, and caps the upside of shares rally through
that strike by expiry.
The buyer pays you for the right to take those shares at the strike.
If the stock stays below it, you keep the shares in the premium.
volume. Think of it as renting out a parking spot on land you still own. A pro habit is
to read open interest and the bid-ask spread on the chain before you send the order, because
a wide spread on a low volume strike can erase most of the income edge. Covered calls are
not free money, they reduce but do not eliminate losses if the stock falls hard, and they clip
you in a strong rally. Size so the premium offsets no more than 2-3% of potential downside
before you would sell the shares anyway. Inside a TFSA or a registered retirement savings plan, or RRSP, the premium does not trigger an immediate tax bill,
which is why this is a Canadian account mechanic rather than a taxable account afterthought.
Delta on that 170 call typically sits around 0.25, so the position behaves like roughly 75 shares of long stock for risk purposes.
The 100 shares count fully and the short call takes away about 25.
Time decay works for the seller, roughly two-thirds of the premium erodes in the final two
weeks if the shares stay range bound.
A protective put struck at $150 for $2.50, paired with that call, builds a collar that
floors you near $147.50 and still caps the upside at the short strike.
Covered structures require the shares or the cash to secure the put, which is the difference
from naked options. Less leverage, fewer margin calls. Compare the annualized yield on the premium
