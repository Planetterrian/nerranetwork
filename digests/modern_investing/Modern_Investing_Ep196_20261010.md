### Investor Education: Bid-Ask Spreads in Low-Volume ETFs
Imagine you bought an ETF tracking small-cap Canadian names last week when volume was thin. Your order filled at a price three cents above the previous close because the market maker widened the spread to protect against low liquidity. The mechanism works through the difference between the highest bid and lowest offer; in calm markets that gap might be two cents, but it can jump to fifteen cents in the first thirty minutes of a volatile session. What most retail investors do not realize is that the spread cost is paid on every round-trip trade, so a one percent annual fee plus repeated spreads can erase the entire expected excess return over an index fund. The pro tip is to check the average daily volume and the current bid-ask quote in your platform’s order ticket before placing a market order on any ETF under fifty million dollars in assets. The concrete misconception to avoid is assuming every ETF trades as tightly as the S&P 500 trackers; always compare the spread to the management fee before committing capital. Broad market funds such as the iShares S&P/TSX Capped Composite Index ETF keep these costs lower because they attract consistent liquidity. Hidden execution costs inside funds that use options can compound the problem further. Source: [fool.ca](https://www.fool.ca/2026/10/09/the-fees-that-quietly-eat-into-a-small-investment/)
