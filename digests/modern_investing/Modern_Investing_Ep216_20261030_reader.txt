You bought Celestica at the open after an eight percent rebound on AI news, and you parked a mental ten percent stop because the stock had already dropped twelve percent the prior session.

Do you leave that stop in your head, or do you put it on the book before the next tape? Here is how I would think about it.

A hard stop triggers on the exchange book; a mental stop waits for your next login.

During a thirty-seven percent monthly drawdown the bid-ask spread widened to forty cents while volume spiked above the twenty-day average, so a hard stop at one hundred fifty-two dollars would have filled near the low while a mental stop left you exposed to the next gap.

The bounce that followed came after third-quarter numbers and a hiked sales outlook, which is exactly when a mental stop feels clever in hindsight and sloppy in the gap.

Set the stop at the most recent swing low on the daily chart plus one average true range, then enter it as a stop-limit order with a two percent buffer so slippage does not turn a ten percent risk into thirteen percent.

The misconception is treating the stop as a prediction that the thesis is broken rather than a pre-committed exit that removes emotion once the position is live.

A stop that is not on the book is just a hope.
