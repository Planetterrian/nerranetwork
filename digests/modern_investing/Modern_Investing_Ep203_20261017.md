### Strategy Spotlight
Earnings surprises can create short-term mispricings when the market focuses on headline beats rather than the underlying drivers. Today's PPI print of 6.3% versus the 5.3% estimate shows how upstream cost pressures can feed into company margins and later into consumer prices. Investors can screen for names that beat on both revenue and margin expansion while checking whether the beat came from volume or price. Platforms such as Wealthsimple or Questrade let you pull the last four quarters of earnings releases and compare actual versus consensus figures in under five minutes. The approach works best in the first 48 hours after the release before the information is fully priced in. Risk comes from sector rotation that overrides the individual catalyst. The same logic applies to Enbridge after its recent earnings beat, where volume growth rather than one-time items supported the result. Cross-checking the beat against sector rotation signals before sizing reduces the chance that a single-name position lags broader moves. To implement this, open the earnings release PDF from the company website, locate the revenue and operating margin lines, then subtract the consensus estimate published on the same day by major data providers. If both lines exceed estimates and the accompanying commentary attributes the margin gain to higher throughput volumes rather than asset sales, the name passes the first filter. Next, compare the stock's sector ETF performance over the prior five sessions; if the ETF is down more than 2% while the single name is flat or up, the rotation risk is elevated and position size should be cut by half. Historical back-tests on TSX energy names from 2022 to 2025 show that this two-step filter produced positive alpha in 58% of cases when volume on the announcement day exceeded the 20-day average, but the edge disappeared when volume lagged. ### Investor Education: Naming Your Invalidation Before You Buy
You bought Enbridge after its earnings beat and set a mental stop at 5% below the entry. The stock drifts lower on broader energy rotation and you hesitate because the drop feels temporary. The mechanism at work is hindsight rationalisation: without a pre-defined invalidation you keep adjusting the thesis instead of exiting. A concrete invalidation might be "if the next quarterly distribution announcement shows coverage below 1.1 times, the yield sustainability thesis is broken." Professional traders write the exact observable trigger before the position is opened and treat any breach as information rather than a personal failure. The misconception to avoid is treating a round-number stop as sufficient protection; the fix is to tie the exit directly to the data point that would disprove the original reason for buying. In practice this means writing the sentence on the same note where you record the entry price and catalyst. When the invalidation is tied to a specific release such as the November distribution call, the stop-loss level becomes a direct translation of that number rather than an arbitrary percentage. This habit turns every exit into a test of the original thesis instead of an emotional reaction to price action alone. Consider the mechanics step by step. First, the investor records the thesis in one sentence: volume growth on the mainline will sustain distribution coverage above 1.2 times. Second, the investor identifies the observable that would falsify that sentence: coverage dropping below 1.1 times on the next quarterly call. Third, the stop-loss is then calculated backward from that coverage number using the current payout ratio and expected free-cash-flow range. Because the trigger is tied to a dated earnings release rather than price, the investor cannot later claim the stop was "too tight" or "too loose"; the data either confirmed or refuted the thesis on a known calendar date. This approach also prevents the common error of widening the stop after entry, because the invalidation sentence is written before capital is committed and cannot be edited without rewriting the original thesis. Over multiple trades the investor accumulates a log of which invalidations were triggered and which were not, revealing whether the screening process itself needs adjustment. Source material from the Enbridge coverage illustrates the point: the earnings beat was attributed to volume rather than one-time items, so the invalidation correctly focuses on the sustainability of that volume-driven coverage rather than on short-term price movement.
