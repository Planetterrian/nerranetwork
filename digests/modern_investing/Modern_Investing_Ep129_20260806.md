### Strategy Spotlight
Cash-secured puts let an investor collect premium income while committing to buy a stock only at a chosen strike price. The approach turns a potential purchase into an income-generating position rather than a plain limit order that earns nothing if unfilled. Today’s options discussion highlights that a short put always returns less loss than an unexecuted limit order because the premium offsets part of any decline. Implementation requires checking the options chain on a platform such as Interactive Brokers or Questrade, selecting a strike 5–10% below the current price with at least 30 days to expiration, and ensuring cash equal to the strike times 100 shares sits in the account. The strategy has performed best in sideways or mildly bullish markets where implied volatility remains elevated; the main risk is opportunity cost if the stock rallies sharply and the premium is left on the table. A second risk appears when the underlying drops sharply below the strike: the investor must purchase shares at the strike while the market price sits lower, though the collected premium still reduces the net cost basis compared with buying outright at the higher level. Platforms display the exact premium, open interest, and implied volatility so investors can compare income against a simple limit order in under two minutes. Historical back-tests show the edge concentrates in names with stable support levels and moderate volatility, where the premium provides a measurable buffer without requiring perfect timing. ### Investor Education: Technical Analysis Mechanics
Imagine you bought a materials ETF last week after the TSX posted a strong close on rising gold prices and your order filled at the quoted ask. The actual path from click to fill involved the bid-ask spread widening from 3 cents to 12 cents within the first 15 minutes as volume spiked, plus a 0.4% slippage from the displayed price because your market order hit successive offers. The 50-day and 200-day moving averages act as dynamic filters because a cross above both on above-average volume has historically preceded positive 20-day returns in 62% of TSX materials cases since 2015, while the same cross on below-average volume shows no edge. RSI (14-day) serves as a momentum gate: entries above 60 cut the win rate by roughly 15 percentage points in back-tests because momentum often exhausts once the oscillator leaves the 40-60 neutral band. MACD histogram turns provide an earlier signal than simple moving-average crosses, yet they generate more false positives when volume fails to confirm the shift. Support and resistance levels drawn from prior swing highs and lows function as probabilistic zones rather than hard barriers; a break accompanied by volume 50% above the 20-day average raises the probability of continuation to roughly 65% in broad equity indices. Chart patterns such as head-and-shoulders or flags carry statistical backing only when they coincide with volume expansion and alignment with the prevailing trend; isolated pattern recognition without those filters performs no better than random in large-sample studies. The professionals always verify that any breakout or reversal occurs with volume above the 20-day average before sizing up, because low-volume moves frequently reverse within two sessions. The misconception to avoid is assuming every visually compelling chart formation will repeat; instead require both a moving-average alignment and volume confirmation above the 20-day average before treating any setup as actionable.
