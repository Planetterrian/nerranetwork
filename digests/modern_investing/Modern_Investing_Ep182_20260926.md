### Strategy Spotlight
Volatility often moves inversely to market direction, with spikes frequently marking bottoms rather than tops. Today's article from OptionsPlay highlights how the VIX jumped to nineteen around the last FOMC meeting before reversing to fifteen the next day, a pattern that has historically signaled short-term bottoms. To apply this, investors can monitor the VIX relative to its twenty-day average on platforms like TradingView or Thinkorswim, entering positions only when a spike reverses with volume confirmation above the average. This approach worked well after the March 2020 COVID crash when volatility peaked above eighty before equities bottomed. The risk lies in false reversals during prolonged bear markets where elevated volatility persists. Canadian investors can track this in a TFSA by pairing VIX alerts with sector ETFs like XIU. Always require both the volatility reversal and a volume filter before committing capital. The inverse correlation means market crashes tend to coincide with elevated implied volatility while market tops align with low volatility levels. The pro tip is to check the VIX term structure on your broker platform before entering, looking for contango that signals the spike is temporary.
