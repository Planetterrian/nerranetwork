### Strategy Spotlight
Sector rotation confirmation requires checking whether money is flowing into or out of an entire group before sizing any single name inside it. BMO Capital Markets data shows the housing correction has hit recent buyers hardest while leaving almost half the country comparatively unscathed, creating uneven pressure on bank mortgage books. Canadian home prices have fallen 20.5% nationally from their early-2022 peak, close to the 26% decline the US saw during the 2008 housing crash. Prices surged roughly 50% in the final two years before the peak, so the correction has hit a relatively small pool of recent buyers hardest. The decline is also concentrated in two provinces. Canada’s real GDP grew at an annualized pace of about 1.7% over the four years since prices began falling, only slightly below this century’s 2.0% average. Variable-rate mortgages reset sharply higher as the Bank of Canada tightened, squeezing heavily indebted households faster than in the US. Investor-owned condos, particularly small units in Toronto and Vancouver, have been another pressure point, and the Bank of Canada has flagged a large stock of unsold small condominiums in both cities. An investor can implement this by pulling sector ETF flows for the financials group on a platform such as Wealthsimple or Interactive Brokers, then comparing the 20-day average volume in XFN against the broader TSX. When financials volume exceeds its average while the broader index lags, the setup meets the rotation test. The same check fails when individual bank names weaken on low volume even if the sector headline looks stable. This filter has kept dividend names out of weak rotation environments in past cycles where single-name yield support proved insufficient. The technique also accounts for the fact that a slowdown in immigration has added to the drag, removing a key source of housing demand just as a wave of rental supply comes to market. ### Investor Education: How Housing Corrections Transmit to Bank Credit Provisions
A listener holding TD or Royal Bank shares after the latest housing update would have seen the stock open lower on the 20.5% price drop headline, expecting the move to reverse quickly on resilient GDP numbers. In practice the bid-ask spread on the bank ETF widened from roughly 3 cents to 12 cents in the first hour as market makers adjusted for uncertain mortgage loss provisions, exactly the mechanism that turns a macro headline into an execution cost. BMO Chief Economist Douglas Porter wrote that this is arguably the most significant home price correction ever for Canada, and yet the economy has managed to avoid serious collateral damage from the deep decline. The pain, while deep, has been unusually contained because the correction has hit a relatively small pool of recent buyers hardest. Porter cautions that part of that resilience is an illusion: rapid population growth flattered the headline numbers, while real GDP per capita fell more than 2% peak-to-trough during the 2022 to 2023 stretch. The pro check is to compare the quarter-over-quarter change in each bank’s allowance for credit losses against the national home-price index before adding to the position. Canadian banks are the most direct trade on whether this stays a correction or becomes a credit event. Mortgage books at Toronto-Dominion and Royal Bank of Canada are the market’s main concern, and traders will watch provisions for credit losses each quarter for signs of stress. Bank of Montreal itself sits on both sides of the story, as the source of the warning and as a lender exposed to the same housing market. The common misconception is that a contained GDP impact automatically protects bank earnings; the fix is to track the per-capita GDP decline and the variable-rate reset schedule instead of headline growth alone.
