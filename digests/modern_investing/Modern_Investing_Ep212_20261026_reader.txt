A retired Albertan, in a Globe and Mail piece, weights several exchange-traded funds, or ETFs, by the dates and dollar amounts of his Tax-Free Savings Account, or TFSA, deposits instead of measuring himself against a static Toronto Stock Exchange or Standard and Poor's five hundred print.

Custom benchmarking is that rebuild: each contribution is treated as its own bucket that earns the return of the mix you would have held from the day the cash arrived, then the buckets are summed into a personal hurdle rate.

Pull the contribution log from your brokerage, assign each deposit to the allocation you would actually have owned that morning, and calculate the blended return against your real holdings.

Think of each deposit as a separate garden bed planted on its own date. You do not score every bed against the city's average rainfall for the year. You score it against the rain that fell after you put that soil in.

The method works best when money arrives in clumps around market lows or highs, because that is exactly when a plain index either flatters you or punishes you for timing you cannot undo.

It fails if you cherry-pick friendly ETF weights after the fact, or if you pretend rebalancing inside the account is free.

You added to your TFSA in uneven lumps last year, some of them when markets were already extended. Your holdings beat a static index. Do you call that stock-picking, or do you rebuild the hurdle from the days the cash arrived before you take the credit?

I'd rebuild it. A single broad index is not a fair picture of the cash flows you actually lived, and skipping the rebuild is how people credit or blame a ticker for results that were mostly the calendar of new TFSA room.

A useful habit is to export that log once a quarter, run the weighted math in a simple spreadsheet before you review any name, and look for drift between the mix you meant to hold and what is sitting there now.

Remember: cash has a birthday, and the hurdle rate should share it.
