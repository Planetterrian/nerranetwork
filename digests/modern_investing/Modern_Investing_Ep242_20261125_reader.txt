An investor sitting on a forty percent gain does not have to sell the shares to make a gift.

Tax-efficient charitable giving, as the Globe and Mail lays it out, lets you transfer appreciated securities in-kind to a registered charity so you never realize the capital gain, and you still deduct the full current market value on your return.

Picture a bottle that has aged well in the cellar: you can walk it to the dinner still sealed, or you can cash it at the shop, pay the cut, and show up with a thinner gift card — the in-kind transfer is the sealed bottle.

You see a non-registered position up forty percent and you want to give this year. Do you sell, pay the inclusion rate, and write a cheque, or do you instruct the brokerage to move the shares into the charity's account and claim the donation receipt at the higher value? I'd transfer the shares, because the inclusion rate never fires and the receipt is still the full market value.

The step is mechanical: you tell the brokerage to send the shares, not the cash, then you file the receipt; it only works in a non-registered account, it fails if the position is underwater, and it fails if the charity cannot take an electronic transfer.

Plan the size against the annual donation limit and against the capital-gains inclusion rate you would have paid on a sale, and you can line the gift up with Tax-Free Savings Account, or TFSA, contribution-room math so the after-tax hit is spread across accounts instead of dumped into one year.

Confirm the charity can receive the transfer before you start the move; done right, the charity keeps the whole market value and you never book the tax that a cash sale would have created.
