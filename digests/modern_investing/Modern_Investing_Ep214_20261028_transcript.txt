Here is how I think about it.
Dealers widen credit spreads when they anticipate slower future cuts, because the present value
of the issuer's cash flows falls, and the risk of refinancing at higher rates increases.
The professional habit is to check the option-adjusted spread on that bond every time the
Fedwatch probability of a December hike moves more than 10 percentage points. If the spread
has already widened 15 basis points or more, you reduce size before the statement rather
than after. The common misconception is that an unchanged policy rate means unchanged
bond prices. The fix is to treat the forward curve and the credit spread reaction as the
actual trade signal, not the headline decision. Any statement language that shifts the expected
path of the federal funds rate changes the discount rate on every corporate cash flow
beyond the next meeting, and investors who watch only the spot rate miss the repricing
in that 90 minute window when the curve and the credit spread matrix adjusts together.
