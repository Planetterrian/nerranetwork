### Investor Education: How Tariff Announcements Move Canadian Small-Cap Liquidity
Imagine you own shares in an Ontario cosmetics exporter that ships one-fifth of its output to the U.S.; you place a market order the morning after the tariff headline. Your fill arrives 12 cents lower than the previous close because the bid-ask spread on that low-float name just widened from 3 cents to 18 cents in the first 45 minutes of trading. The mechanism at work is reduced market-maker inventory when policy risk spikes—dealers simply pull bids rather than absorb inventory they cannot hedge quickly. Most retail investors do not realize the spread expansion is largest in the first hour and often reverses by midday once algorithmic liquidity returns. The professionals always check the average daily volume versus the 20-day average before entering any position larger than 1% of portfolio value. The biggest mistake with tariff-driven volatility is treating the initial price drop as a permanent re-rating instead of a liquidity event. Instead, always wait for the spread to compress back below its 10-day average before adding to the position. In practice this means pulling up the Level 2 order book on your platform and confirming that at least three market makers have restored tight quotes before executing. The same dynamic appears in other low-float Canadian names whenever U.S. policy headlines surface, so the discipline of waiting for spread normalization applies across the entire small-cap exporter universe.
