Covered call strategies on broad indexes generate income by selling call options against existing equity exposure.
Today's market conditions favor this approach when volatility supports higher premiums without
capping too much upside.
The TAP Alpha Innovation 100 Growth and Daily Income ETF implements the strategy by selling
zero days to expiration NASDAQ 100 calls each session against holdings in the Invesco NASDAQ
100 ETF.
Investors can access this through any brokerage that offers ETF trading, monitoring the daily
option strikes and resulting distribution rate. Historically these structures have performed
best in range bound or moderately bullish markets, where premium collection offsets
foregone upside. The risk is that strong rallies push the index through the strikes,
leaving the fund with only the premium rather than full participation.
A Canadian investor could allocate a portion of TFSA equity exposure to this ETF to boost yield
while retaining core NASDAQ 100 beta.
The 17.58% distribution rate has so far allowed the fund to maintain or exceed the total
return of a plain NASDAQ 100 ETF.
The fund resets its option exposure every trading session rather than overriding the full
portfolio with longer dated calls.
This daily reset gives the strategy repeated opportunities to select strikes based on current
market conditions.
The underlying NASDAQ 100 allocation comes through QQQM holdings that focus on the largest
non-financial companies listed on the exchange.
X distribution dates cause the net asset value to fall by the amount paid out, so the option
premiums must replenish that payout to avoid gradual erosion of share price.
The approach differs from funds that sell one month at the money calls and risk watching
the index rally through those strikes for weeks.

The mechanism at work is that most active managers must overcome higher expense ratios and
trading costs before they can beat the benchmark, and speed of the data shows the majority fail
to do so over five and ten year periods.
What most retail investors do not realize is that the tax efficiency of low turnover index
ETFs compounds the advantage inside registered accounts where capital gains
distributions are not an issue. The pro tip is to reserve active management for
pockets where the benchmark itself is inefficient such as small cap or emerging
market segments where information asymmetry can still be exploited. The
concrete misconception to avoid is treating every active fund as a potential
outperform. The fix is to compare any active choice against the relevant low-cost
index ETF on a net of fees after tax basis before committing capital.
Active strategies can still add value through factor tilts that emphasize value or momentum
characteristics not fully captured by broad indexes.
Risk management techniques such as tax loss harvesting become more practical when an active
manager can adjust holdings without triggering unwanted distributions.
small cap segments often show larger gaps between active and passive results
because information about those companies travels more slowly.
The decision between approaches ultimately depends on the specific market segment
and the investor's time horizon rather than a blanket preference for one style.
