### Strategy Spotlight
Covered-call strategies on broad indexes generate income by selling call options against existing equity exposure. Today's market conditions favor this approach when volatility supports higher premiums without capping too much upside. The TappAlpha Innovation 100 Growth & Daily Income ETF implements the strategy by selling zero-days-to-expiration Nasdaq-100 calls each session against holdings in the Invesco NASDAQ 100 ETF. Investors can access this through any brokerage that offers ETF trading, monitoring the daily option strikes and resulting distribution rate. Historically these structures have performed best in range-bound or moderately bullish markets where premium collection offsets forgone upside. The risk is that strong rallies push the index through the strikes, leaving the fund with only the premium rather than full participation. A Canadian investor could allocate a portion of TFSA equity exposure to this ETF to boost yield while retaining core Nasdaq-100 beta. The seventeen point five eight percent distribution rate has so far allowed the fund to maintain or exceed the total return of a plain Nasdaq-100 ETF. This daily reset gives the strategy repeated opportunities to select strikes based on current market conditions. The underlying Nasdaq-100 allocation comes through QQQM holdings that focus on the largest nonfinancial companies listed on the exchange. Ex-distribution dates cause the net asset value to fall by the amount paid out, so the option premiums must replenish that payout to avoid gradual erosion of share price. The approach differs from funds that sell one-month at-the-money calls and risk watching the index rally through those strikes for weeks. ### Investor Education: Passive Indexing Versus Active Management
Imagine you bought an S&P 500 ETF in your TFSA last year and it returned roughly the index gain after fees. The mechanism at work is that most active managers must overcome higher expense ratios and trading costs before they can beat the benchmark, and SPIVA data shows the majority fail to do so over five- and ten-year periods. What most retail investors do not realize is that the tax efficiency of low-turnover index ETFs compounds the advantage inside registered accounts where capital-gains distributions are not an issue. The pro tip is to reserve active management for pockets where the benchmark itself is inefficient, such as small-cap or emerging-market segments where information asymmetry can still be exploited. The concrete misconception to avoid is treating every active fund as a potential outperform; the fix is to compare any active choice against the relevant low-cost index ETF on a net-of-fees, after-tax basis before committing capital. Active strategies can still add value through factor tilts that emphasize value or momentum characteristics not fully captured by broad indexes. Risk management techniques such as tax-loss harvesting become more practical when an active manager can adjust holdings without triggering unwanted distributions. Small-cap segments often show larger gaps between active and passive results because information about those companies travels more slowly. The decision between approaches ultimately depends on the specific market segment and the investor's time horizon rather than a blanket preference for one style.
