### Investor Education: How to Read an Earnings Report
You hold Celestica shares ahead of its next quarterly release and place a limit order to add on any dip below the recent low; the report shows revenue up 12 percent yet free cash flow down 8 percent because capital spending on new facilities rose sharply. The mechanism at work is the difference between accrual accounting and cash reality: revenue can be recognized before cash arrives, while heavy capex drains the balance sheet even when the income statement looks healthy. A professional habit is to open the cash-flow statement first, subtract capital expenditures from operating cash flow, and compare the resulting free-cash-flow number to the same quarter last year; a drop of more than 10 percent despite revenue growth signals the company is spending ahead of returns. The common misconception is that a revenue or EPS beat automatically means the stock will rise; the fix is to require both an earnings beat and year-over-year free-cash-flow growth before adding to the position. Celestica’s positioning in AI data-center supply chains makes this cash-flow check especially relevant because the company’s recent expansion announcements have coincided with elevated capital expenditures that have not yet translated into proportional free-cash-flow gains. Investors who skip the cash-flow reconciliation often discover later that reported earnings growth was funded by balance-sheet expansion rather than operating leverage, leading to valuation compression once the market prices in the higher reinvestment requirement. The same discipline applies when reviewing any TSX-listed technology name whose growth narrative rests on multi-year infrastructure contracts.
