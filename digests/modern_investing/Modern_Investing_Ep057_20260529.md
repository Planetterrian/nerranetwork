### Strategy Spotlight
Return on invested capital (ROIC) measures how efficiently a company turns capital into profits after accounting for the cost of that capital. In today’s environment of constrained consumer spending and selective AI demand, focusing on high-ROIC businesses helps filter names that can compound even when macro data weakens. The approach starts with screening for companies posting ROIC above 15% over five years, then checking free-cash-flow consistency and competitive positioning. Platforms such as Wealthsimple or Questrade allow quick ROIC pulls via their fundamental data tabs; cross-check against the most recent quarterly filings. This screen worked best during 2018–2019 and 2022–2023 periods when broad multiples compressed yet quality compounders still delivered. The main risk is paying too high a multiple for already-recognized ROIC leaders, so always pair the metric with a margin-of-safety check on forward earnings. ### Investor Education: Warren Buffett's Investment Philosophy
Imagine you bought a consumer staple ETF last week when recession headlines first hit; your order filled at the quoted price, yet the real mechanics between click and execution often include hidden costs that erode the margin of safety Buffett demands. Buffett evolved from Benjamin Graham’s cigar-butt bargain hunting to Charlie Munger’s emphasis on quality compounders with durable economic moats—franchises that can reinvest at high returns for decades. Key principles include staying inside a defined circle of competence, insisting on a margin of safety before purchase, and treating index funds as the default for most investors who lack time or edge. His notable wins such as Coca-Cola and Apple illustrate the power of long-term ownership of predictable cash-flow machines, while mistakes like the 2015–2016 energy foray show even legends can misjudge cycle timing. Professionals always verify whether a business can earn above its cost of capital for ten-plus years before committing capital. The biggest misconception is that Buffett-style investing means only buying cheap assets; instead, always demand both quality and price discipline before entering any position.
