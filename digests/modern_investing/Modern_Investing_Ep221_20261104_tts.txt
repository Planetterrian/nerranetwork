Cash-secured puts function as paid limit orders that compensate you for waiting to buy a stock you already want to own.

It works like leaving a bid on a used car at a price you have already decided is fair, and the dealer pays you a fee every month your offer stays live — if the car never comes down to your number, you keep the fee for waiting, and if it does, you buy at the price you named.

In the OptionsPlay Growth Lab example, a ranked list of names shows the put strike, the premium collected, and the probability of assignment so you can compare the effective purchase price against your target entry, then filter that list by probability of profit and annualized return before placing the order.

You open the position by selling the put and posting cash equal to the strike times one hundred shares; if the stock stays above the strike you keep the premium as income and repeat the process, while assignment simply delivers the shares at your chosen net cost.

Implementation starts in any options-approved brokerage by scanning the chain for thirty- to forty-five-day expiries where implied volatility sits above the twenty-day average, then confirming the premium covers at least one percent of the strike.

Select a name trading near your target entry price, sell the thirty-day put at the strike one standard deviation below the current price, and record the premium received as a percentage of the strike — that single step turns waiting time into income while enforcing discipline on the entry you are willing to pay.

The approach worked well in the two thousand twenty-two to two thousand twenty-three rate-hike period, when elevated volatility produced richer premiums, yet it fails when a sudden gap down leaves you assigned shares well below your original target and forces an immediate unrealized loss.

A market buy for two hundred shares of a T S X listed name at the open, on a morning when the index is already down more than two hundred fifty points, is where the displayed quote stops being the price you get.

The bid-ask spread can jump from three cents to eighteen cents within the first two minutes because market makers pull quotes or widen them to protect against adverse selection when order flow spikes and inventory risk rises, so the fill lands several cents worse than the last printed price even when the underlying index move is only zero point eight percent.

Liquidity providers also cut posted size at the best bid and offer on those sessions, which is why investors who fire market orders in the first minutes often see effective prices fifteen to twenty-five cents away from the previous close.

The spread just went from three cents to eighteen cents and your size is two hundred shares — do you hit market, or sit on a limit at the mid?

Enter as a limit at the mid-point of the current spread, refresh it every thirty seconds rather than accepting the first market fill, and on high-volume days that habit can save ten to twenty basis points per round turn.

The misconception is that a market order always executes at the displayed quote; the fix is to read the level-two ladder, size the limit no larger than the visible depth at your target price, and check whether large sellers are stacked at the offer before you submit.
