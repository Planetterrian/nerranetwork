### Investor Education: Reading Implied Volatility During Earnings Season
Imagine you bought an options spread on a stock the day before its earnings release and the fill came in at a mid-price that already reflected elevated IV. What actually happened between your click and that fill price is that market makers widened the spread to account for the binary event risk, moving the bid-ask from perhaps 5 cents to 20 cents in the final hour of trading. The mechanism is straightforward: implied volatility is the market’s expected range priced into options; when IV rank sits in the red zone on an earnings screener it means the premium is expensive relative to the stock’s own history, so selling premium becomes statistically attractive while buying premium requires an even larger move to overcome the inflated price. A pro tip most retail traders miss is to check the IV rank column first, then cross-reference the expected move (usually shown as a percentage on the options chain) against the stock’s average post-earnings move over the past eight quarters; if the priced move exceeds the historical average, the edge shifts toward credit spreads rather than long calls. The biggest misconception is treating all high-IV names as “cheap lottery tickets.” Instead, always compare the current IV rank to the stock’s own 52-week range and only sell premium when rank exceeds the 70th percentile.
