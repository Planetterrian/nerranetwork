With rates moving and volatility in FX, let us look at a strategy Canadian investors are using inside tax advantage to counts to generate income.
Covered call ETFs sell call options against equity holdings to generate extra income while retaining ownership of the underlying shares. Two such vehicles now yield above 25%, which makes the income look attractive in a sideways or mildly bullish market. The sold calls cap upside participation and the underlying holdings can still decline sharply if sector rotation accelerates.
Canadian investors can place this inside a TFSA by choosing a covered call ETF that tracks broad US or Canadian indices. They then monitor the call strike relative to the current price to see how much upside is being traded away. The approach has historically performed best in low volatility regimes where collected premiums exceed modest draw downs.
It tends to lag in strong trending bull markets once the calls are exercised away. A same-day volume check above the 20-day average remains essential before treating any rebound in these ETFs as confirmed. The quiet change in risk comes from the fact that elevated yields now come with greater potential for the underlying assets to fall outside the covered range during sector rotation.
Investors should therefore pair any allocation with the review of the ETFs holdings concentration and recent drawdown history before committing capital. Before placing any orders in these vehicles, it is worth understanding exactly how fills happen on volatile days. Imagine you place a market order for a US equity ETF right after the open on a day when mortgage rate data surprises the market.
Your order fills at $48.72 even though the last quoted price on your screen was $48.55. The bid asks spread widened from $0.3 to $0.14 within the first 90 seconds as liquidity provider stepped back. Your market order crossed the wider ask while quoted depth on the bid dropped from 12,000 shares to fewer than 3,000.
Professionals always check the depth of book and recent trade prints before sending a market order on news days. Retail investors who skip this step routinely pay 20-40 basis points more than necessary.
