### Investor Education: Position Sizing and Stop Losses
Imagine you allocated $1,000 to a single energy name last week when oil spiked on Middle East headlines; your order filled at the opening print, but the position size left no room for the 7% reversal that followed. The mechanism works through simple arithmetic: a 2% portfolio allocation limits any single loss to roughly $20 on a $1,000 account even if the stop is hit at 10% below entry, whereas a 10% allocation turns that same 10% drop into a $100 hit that compounds across correlated holdings. Professionals always calculate the maximum acceptable loss in dollar terms first, then back into share quantity using the distance to their chosen stop—fixed, trailing, or volatility-based—before placing the order. The biggest mistake with position sizing is letting a promising setup dictate allocation instead of the other way around. Instead, always size every new position so that a full stop-out equals no more than 1% of total portfolio value. Correlation math adds another layer: when energy names move together on the same geopolitical headline, even properly sized individual stops can still produce a portfolio-level drawdown that exceeds the 1% rule if three or four holdings trigger simultaneously. The Kelly Criterion simplified for retail use suggests betting no more than the edge divided by the odds against, which in practice means shrinking position size further when volatility spikes above the 20-day average. Trailing stops adjust automatically as price moves in your favor, yet they still require an initial hard stop to define the maximum loss before the trail begins. Portfolio drawdowns become psychologically easier to manage when each position is capped at 1% risk, because the investor can review the setup calmly rather than reacting to a single oversized loss. The concrete misconception to avoid is treating every new idea as equally important; instead, always rank setups by conviction and allocate risk dollars accordingly so that only the highest-conviction names receive the full 1% allocation.
