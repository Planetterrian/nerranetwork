Tax-loss harvesting with exchange-traded funds, or ETFs, lets you book a capital loss without stepping out of the market, and Barron's reports the swap approach generated billions in reported tax losses last year.

You open a taxable account at a Canadian broker, find two ETFs with overlapping holdings but different tickers, sell the loser to book the loss, then buy the replacement immediately.

Think of it as selling last year's winter coat on a down day and walking into the next shop for a different brand that still gets you through January — you never stand outside in the cold, but the old coat's loss is the receipt the tax office will accept.

The step works best when the price gap is small and the swap happens inside the same trading day, because overnight drift can undo the economic equivalence you just tried to keep.

You see a US-listed broad-market ETF in a non-registered account sitting at a loss, and a similar fund tracks almost the same index. Do you swap the same day, or wait for a wider dislocation? I'd execute the same day — drift is the enemy of a clean harvest.

The technique has produced consistent tax savings in years with mixed equity returns, and it is particularly useful for Canadian investors holding US-listed ETFs in non-registered accounts where capital gains are taxable at the inclusion rate.

It fails when both ETFs move in lockstep, or when Canada's superficial-loss rules disallow the claim if you, or an affiliated person, replace the position within thirty days, and the United States wash-sale rule is the parallel trap on that side of the border.

Before you click, confirm the replacement has similar beta and sector exposure so you keep the intended market position, then record the new adjusted cost base, or ACB, in the account software so the next sale is not a guess.

Remember: book the loss, keep the exposure.
