### Strategy Spotlight
The Wheel strategy combines selling cash-secured puts to acquire shares at a target price and then selling covered calls against those shares to generate income in a repeatable loop. Today's options-focused content highlights how screeners can automate both legs instead of manual contract selection, which reduces emotional bias and improves consistency for income-oriented accounts. Canadian investors can implement this in a TFSA using Interactive Brokers' options chain tools by filtering for high-probability short puts on stable names and matching covered calls with similar deltas. The approach has historically performed best in sideways or mildly bullish markets where implied volatility remains elevated but underlying trends are stable, though it carries assignment risk if the stock gaps sharply lower. Risk management requires position sizing at 1-2 percent of portfolio capital per trade and strict adherence to defined entry criteria rather than chasing premium alone. Pairing the two screeners into one system turns the Wheel into a mechanical income engine rather than a series of ad-hoc decisions. Screeners also allow users to set repeatable parameters such as minimum premium thresholds and delta ranges so the same criteria apply across multiple cycles without re-evaluating each contract manually. This systematic pairing directly addresses the common failure point where investors select puts and calls independently and end up with mismatched risk profiles. ### Investor Education: Market Crashes Through History: Lessons for Today
Imagine you bought a broad TSX ETF last week when the central-bank task-force news first surfaced and your order filled at the prevailing ask; the actual path from click to execution can reveal how liquidity evaporates during stress. In 1929 the Dow fell 89 percent over three years after margin-fueled speculation collapsed, with recovery taking until 1954. Black Monday in 1987 saw a 22.6 percent single-day drop triggered by program trading and portfolio insurance, yet the index regained its level within two years. The dot-com bust erased 78 percent from the NASDAQ between 2000 and 2002 after speculative valuations detached from earnings, with full recovery stretching seven years. The 2008 crisis produced a 57 percent S&P 500 decline amid housing leverage and bank failures, requiring four years to return to prior highs. COVID's 34 percent plunge in March 2020 lasted only 23 trading days before new highs, aided by instant fiscal and monetary support. What most retail investors miss is that every crash featured a preceding period of compressed volatility and rising leverage; the professionals always check margin-debt levels and VIX term-structure before sizing new positions. The biggest mistake with crash preparation is assuming the next event will match the last one. Instead, always maintain a written re-entry plan tied to fixed-percentage drawdown thresholds rather than trying to time the bottom.
