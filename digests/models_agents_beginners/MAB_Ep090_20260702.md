For students or anyone who has ever felt “I’m not a chess person,” it removes the barrier of looking dumb while learning. It also hints at future learning tools that could make subjects like math or history feel like custom video games built just for you. You can try it yourself in under a minute: open your browser, go to https://game-seven-chess.netlify.app/, and start playing—the AI handles everything else. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you take a photo with your phone, the image file quietly stores little bits of hidden information like the date, location, and camera settings? Now imagine that same idea, but instead of storing the date, the system hides a secret pattern that only special software can read later. That hidden pattern is what Google’s SynthID does with pictures, videos, audio, and even text made by AI. It adds the pattern during creation so that later, when someone checks the file in Google Search or the Gemini app, the software can say “this was made by AI” without changing how the content looks or sounds to regular people. The pattern is designed to survive edits like cropping or filters, a bit like how a watermark on paper money still shows up even if the bill gets wrinkled. Over 100 billion images and videos already carry this hidden mark, and the same system now works for 60,000 years’ worth of audio clips. The result is a quiet way to keep track of what is real and what was generated, without anyone needing to be a tech expert to check. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/GoogleAI/status/2072318809277390940)

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Report sketchy AI behavior in one click**
A new website called Flare lets anyone flag when an AI chatbot starts giving dangerous advice, like instructions for building something harmful or leaking private information. It works like a neighborhood watch for AI: you describe what happened, and the report goes to people who study AI safety. This is useful because as chatbots get more powerful, having an easy way to speak up helps keep them helpful instead of risky. Anyone with a phone or laptop can use it—no coding required. Go to the Flare site mentioned in the Wired article, describe the odd or unsafe thing the AI said, and submit. You may need a parent’s help if the site asks for an account.
