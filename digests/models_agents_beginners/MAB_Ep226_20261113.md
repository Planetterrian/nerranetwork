# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **AI shopping assistants are accidentally sending people to fake stores — here's why it happens and how to stay safe.**
Today's biggest story shows how even helpful AI tools like ChatGPT and Gemini can point shoppers toward risky websites. We'll break down why that happens and what it means when you ask an AI for everyday help. Then we explore a brand-new fast version of Google's Gemini model you can try right now, plus a few quick warnings and cool experiments.
---
### The Big Story
Imagine you're texting a friend for the best place to buy new sneakers online, and instead of a trusted store they send you a sketchy link that looks real but isn't. That's basically what happened in a new study where researchers asked ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Perplexity questions about 151 brands like Lululemon and Birkenstock. The AI chatbots sometimes suggested fake or risky websites that could be selling counterfeits or might even be dangerous. Perplexity gave risky links in 15 percent of its answers, ChatGPT in 8 percent, and Gemini in just 4 percent. The security firm Podqi ran the test on 6,795 AI-generated answers and found that roughly 9 percent of all responses exposed users to potentially unsafe sites. Smaller brands faced roughly twice the risk of risky-link exposure compared with big-name companies. Questions about regional shipping or international delivery made the problem even worse because the models sometimes invented websites that did not actually exist for those regions. When risky sites did appear, ChatGPT issued a warning only 24 percent of the time, while the other two tools warned even less often. The study also showed that in 57 percent of the answers containing a risky link, the chatbot actively suggested the site as a place to shop rather than just mentioning it in passing. One documented case had Perplexity naming an incorrect page as the official Stüssy site and describing it as a source of authentic products. This matters because more and more people — including teens shopping for clothes, games, or school supplies — are turning to AI for quick recommendations instead of searching themselves. It shows that AI is really good at sounding confident even when it's pulling from messy web data it hasn't double-checked. For you, it means the next time you ask an AI where to buy something, treat the answer like a suggestion from a helpful but forgetful friend — always open the link yourself and check the URL before clicking or entering any info. You can try this right now by opening the Gemini app or ChatGPT on your phone, asking "where can I buy authentic Stüssy clothes online," and then comparing the suggested sites to the real brand's official page. Source: [digit.in](https://www.digit.in/news/general/shopping-with-ai-chatgpt-gemini-and-other-ai-chatbots-can-lead-you-to-fake-websites-study-finds.html)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're scrolling through your phone's suggested videos on YouTube or TikTok, the app looks at what you've watched before and guesses what you might like next? Now picture that same guessing game but with millions of web pages instead of videos. An AI chatbot doesn't actually "shop" or visit stores itself — it looks at patterns in the huge pile of text it was trained on and predicts which words and links usually appear together when people ask about a brand. If lots of old web pages mention a certain domain next to a brand name, even if that domain is now fake or abandoned, the model can still suggest it because the pattern feels familiar. The tricky part is that the AI has no way to check in real time whether the link still works or belongs to the real company — it's just completing the sentence based on what it saw during training. That's why smaller or regional brands cause more mistakes: there are fewer trustworthy pages about them, so the model has less clear patterns to follow. The study showed that when risky sites appeared, the chatbots warned users only some of the time, which is like a friend recommending a restaurant without checking if it's still open. Once you see it this way, you realize the AI isn't trying to trick you — it's just doing autocomplete on a massive scale and sometimes the autocomplete is outdated or wrong. The same pattern-matching process explains why the error rate jumped for questions about shipping to specific countries: fewer reliable pages existed in the training data, so the guesses became less reliable. Researchers counted 718 risky domains out of 4,437 they reviewed, split between outright fake sites and dead domains that scammers could later claim. Understanding this helps you know exactly where the AI's confidence comes from and why it can still be wrong.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Google just dropped a faster, lighter Gemini model you can use today**
Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a new version built for speed on everyday tasks like coding help, answering tricky questions with multiple parts, and agent-style workflows. It runs about 2.5 times faster than the previous Flash model while still handling complex requests, and it's already live for regular users in the Gemini app and Google Search. The model also appears in Google Sheets for Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers and is available to developers through Google AI Studio and Android Studio. On the independent Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index it surpasses the earlier Argon model at its default thinking level. Teens who like quick homework help, creative writing prompts, or game ideas will notice it feels snappier than older versions. It is especially good at breaking down questions that have several parts, contain typos, or include highly specific requests. Open the Gemini app on your phone (or go to gemini.google.com), sign in with a free Google account if you haven't already, and try asking it a multi-part question like "Explain photosynthesis in simple steps and then give me a 3-sentence story about a plant that learns to talk." Watch how cleanly it breaks the answer into parts and keeps each section accurate even when the request has two very different goals. Source: [theverge.com](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1032505/google-search-ai-mode-gemini-4-hydrogen)
