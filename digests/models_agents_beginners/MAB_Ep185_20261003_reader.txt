Picture a helper on your laptop that can open every folder you own.

Apple is adding new limits to a Mac permission called Full Disk Access, Ars Technica reports.

A Mac is Apple's computer, and Full Disk Access is like handing an app the master key to the whole house.

With that key, an app can reach every file, message, email, and browsing record, without asking again and again.

Apple announced the change on October second, after seeing how capable these tools have become with private data.

The tools in question are AI agents, which are artificial intelligence programs built to finish multi-step jobs on their own.

Think of an intern who does not just answer one question, but walks the office and handles a whole stack of tasks.

Sorting documents or summarizing long chats are the kind of jobs these agents try to complete.

Because those jobs often touch private data, the agent often asks for that broad master-key permission so it can work smoothly.

Here is the risk. A poorly designed or misused agent could copy sensitive information, or make unintended changes across the entire system.

That is less like a spell-checker fixing one sentence, and more like someone rearranging every drawer in your bedroom.

Apple is adding extra confirmation steps and clearer warnings, so the permission is granted only when you truly mean to allow it.

The new rules are like a second lock on the front door, plus a sign that says you are about to give away the only key.

This update matters to anyone who uses a Mac and cares about privacy, especially as more everyday apps add AI helpers that promise to do things for you.

Mac users will start seeing the new prompts right away when an app asks for broad access.

You can check this yourself in about thirty seconds, for free, on the computer you already have.

Open System Settings, click Privacy and Security, then scroll to Full Disk Access.

Look at the list, and turn off any app you no longer trust.

If a name looks unfamiliar, that is your cue to pause, the same way you would pause before lending a stranger your house keys.

A robot vacuum makes the next part easier to see.

A vacuum only cleans well if it knows where the furniture sits and which doors are open, or it gets stuck.

Now stretch that picture. The vacuum can also open drawers, read your notes, and decide what to throw away.

An AI agent works in a similar chain. You give it a goal, such as organize my photos, and it tries to finish the whole job, not one tap.

Step one is the goal. You say what you want, the way you tell a friend, please tidy the living room.

Step two is the map. The agent looks across many files and apps, because a big job rarely lives in a single folder.

Step three is action. It sorts, summarizes, moves, or replies, taking several steps in a row without you clicking each one.

Step four is permission. Someone has to say yes before it can open those drawers, or it might wander into places it should not.

The extra Apple locks are there so the helpful robot stays helpful, instead of accidentally making a mess.

Once you see an agent as a helper that needs a map and a key, the whole idea sits still in your head.

Planet Labs helped Google build a small satellite to see how its AI chips handle the extreme conditions of orbit.

The satellite went up on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare mission, which is a bit like carpooling on a rocket.

Many small payloads share one launch, the way several classmates share one van to a field trip.

On board are Google TPUs, special chips of the same kind used in data centers to train and run large models.

A data center is a giant building packed with computers. These chips are the engines that help chatbots and image tools think.

The goal is to gather data on how the chips survive the physical stress and temperature swings of spaceflight.

Picture packing your gaming laptop for a camping trip where it is freezing at night and blazing at noon, then asking whether it still works.

One big advantage of low Earth orbit, a path relatively close to the planet, is near-constant sunlight.

The project says that sunlight could generate up to eight times more solar power than systems on the ground.

That is like a solar panel that almost never waits for night.

This is still early research. It shows one possible future where some of the heavy computing that powers chatbots and image tools might happen above the clouds, instead of in giant data centers.

If you are into space, climate tech, or big what-if ideas, this project is worth following.

Here is a free try-this you can do on your phone in a minute, with no account required.

Open X, search Google AI, and look for the post from October second about Project Suncatcher.

Read the short announcement and notice what they chose to measure first, chips under stress, not a finished space chatbot.

Ten popular chatbots recommended unlicensed gambling sites, a Dutch report found.

Researchers asked ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Grok, and six other chatbots questions in Dutch about casinos.

Every single one named at least one site that does not have a proper Dutch license.

The study, run by Keurmerk Verantwoorde Affiliates, tested nine questions per tool.

Some chatbots even presented missing identity checks or large bonuses as advantages.

The watchdog warned that Dutch consumers should be able to trust accurate information when they ask about legal gambling options.

Treat chatbot answers about money and rules like a stranger's restaurant tip. Check the official list before you go.
