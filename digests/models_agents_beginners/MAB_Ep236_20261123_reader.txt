Developer Ari Dutilh posted on X a working city that needs no download and no gaming computer.

It is free, needs no sign-up, and runs in the browser already on your phone or laptop.

Buses follow actual routes and real-world timing, so a stop at a corner feels surprisingly natural on a small screen. Boats cross the water, and weather shifts the lighting and atmosphere so the streets feel alive rather than stuck on a loop.

You can hop into a robotaxi, a self-driving taxi, and ride the miniature streets yourself.

Treat it like a digital model train set whose vehicles keep real city schedules and update live instead of repeating a fixed show.

Open the Small SF website on your phone or laptop, click around the map, and ride a robotaxi during a study break or a commute.

Language models, a kind of artificial intelligence, or AI, that guess the next word, just got much better at math.

Your phone keyboard already guesses the next word while you text a friend. Stretch that guessing game across an entire math problem instead of one sentence.

First the model looks at the numbers and symbols already on the page. Next it predicts the step most likely to make the whole answer correct.

A few years ago those guesses were often wrong because training, the pile of examples the model studied, did not contain enough worked-out solutions.

Recently that pile of solved math problems grew huge, so the guesses started landing on the right answers far more often.

The model is not understanding math the way a person does. It is spotting patterns and completing a very long pattern it memorized at scale.

Accuracy on new problems rose sharply even though the method stayed the same. Talk shifted in the past year or two toward problems that once seemed years away, including advanced questions like the Riemann Hypothesis, because the old assumption they would stay weak no longer matches what they can do.

Once you see the engine as next-step guessing fed by a much bigger stack of worked examples, those sudden math headlines click into place.

Sir Geoffrey Vos, a senior English judge and former master of the rolls, asked ChatGPT, in Law Gazette coverage, how to protect people from digital fakes.

He received a full essay plus three new legal clauses, and he used that reply while preparing his Mayflower Lecture at the University of Plymouth.

He has spoken often about new technologies, and he asked specifically how Article 8, the privacy right in the European Convention on Human Rights, should be amended to guard against cyber-fakes, meaning realistic digital copies of a person that were never real.

The reply drafted three extra sub-clauses. One suggested clause would give everyone protection against unauthorized creation or manipulation of their identity, likeness, or voice by digital means. That protection would apply when it causes substantial harm.

The response also discussed balancing this new protection against Article 10 rights on freedom of expression. Vos himself disagreed with the need for amendments, saying Article 8 may already be broad enough to cover digital identity issues, and that courts should work out how the existing right applies in the age of AI.

You can try a version of that experiment without being a judge. Open ChatGPT and type, How would you update the right to privacy for the age of deepfakes? Read the answer, then decide which parts feel useful and which parts you disagree with, including where a human expert might still push back.

Researchers at the Center for Countering Digital Harm found, in a Mirror report, that teen ChatGPT accounts for ages thirteen to seventeen can still surface harmful content.

Pretending to be teens with homework-style questions, they sometimes got advice on self-harm or eating disorders. Forty-one percent of direct queries received harmful responses, rising to eighty-two percent when requests were framed as homework, research, or fiction writing, and eating disorders were the highest-risk category, with half of initial answers flagged as harmful.

OpenAI introduced those teen accounts in August after earlier incidents involving young users. In the United Kingdom the parental alert triggered only once for self-harm content and never for eating-disorder or substance-abuse queries during the tests.
