A researcher named Ethan Mollick asked an AI system called Fable, or Opus, to build a full two-dimensional starship battle.

Two-dimensional just means you look down on the fight like a board game, not from inside a cockpit.

He wanted the ships to obey real physics, not the swooping movie trick where a craft stops on a dime.

Claude, a chatbot you may already use for writing, helped make the whole thing.

In the finished browser game, every ship has to watch fuel, heat, and which way it turns.

Nothing in space eases off the gas for you.

Picture sliding a car across a frozen parking lot.

Once you start moving, you keep going until you spin the engine around and burn fuel to slow down.

The game tracks that remaining shove with something called delta-v, which is just how much push your engines can still give.

Think of it like a gas tank for motion, except you never get a refill in the middle of a fight.

There is also a heat system.

After a battle, the ship has to stretch out radiators to cool off, the way you might stick your arms out after running.

If you hide those radiators during an attack, you stay safer, because they are easy to hit when they are hanging out.

And if you brake toward an enemy, you show the weaker side of your armor, like turning your back in dodgeball to stop.

That is the kind of tactic strategy-game fans and space-story readers can feel in their hands.

It turns orbital mechanics, the real rules of how things move in space, into something you can poke at instead of only reading in class.

You can try the exact game on the Orbital Declaration website.

Click any ship on the left to pick it.

Right-click an enemy to set an intercept course.

Then tap the order buttons on the right, and watch how each move spends fuel and redraws the path.

A navigator line at the top tells you what the ship is doing now and what it will do next.

The plotted route uses bright lines for burns, dashed lines for turns or coasts, and blue-grey lines for braking.

It is like a GPS preview, except every glowing stretch is fuel you will never get back.

The orders you can give are Intercept, Keep range, Match speed, Hold, Retreat, and Manual.

Each one shows its delta-v cost before you confirm, so you see the price tag first.

For weapons, press W to set them free, and press L to fire a salvo of two shots.

Hit Space to pause, or press one through four to speed up time when you are not deciding anything.

Damage control lets you send repair parties to the parts that got hit.

Repairs stop if the ship pulls harder than one point two g, which means more than one point two times the gravity you feel standing on Earth.

Crew can get hurt above three g.

Throw a ball on Earth and it comes back down, because gravity and air steal its speed.

In space there is almost no air, and gravity is tiny, so a moving ship just keeps going in a straight line until something shoves it again.

Now think about your phone battery.

Every time you open an app, the number drops, and you cannot wish that energy back without plugging in.

Delta-v works the same way.

It is the total push still sitting in the engines, and every burn or turn spends some forever.

That is why you do not chase a target like a kid sprinting after a bus.

You plan ahead and match speed, more like jogging alongside a friend until you can hop on.

Most space shooters let you tap the brakes whenever you want.

This one does not, because the math is quietly enforcing real orbital rules under every click.

After you give an order, the path redraws.

Coasting shows up as the cheap, dashed stretch.

Burns show up as the expensive, bright ones.

That is the moment space travel stops feeling like a dogfight and starts feeling like careful planning with long, quiet slides in between.

Once those lines make sense, you are not memorizing a textbook sentence.

You are watching the rule happen.
