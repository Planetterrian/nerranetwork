> **Australia just announced the world's first law forcing top AI companies to prove their models are safe before release.**
Today's biggest news is a new Australian law that will require the most powerful AI systems to pass independent safety checks. This matters because it shows governments are starting to treat advanced AI like other high-risk technologies we already regulate. We'll also look at how Google is bringing its chatbot into your car and why some companies are blocking AI from handling real tasks like booking flights.

### The Big Story
Australia's government, led by Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, has announced a law that would impose mandatory safety rules on frontier AI companies operating in the country for the first time. The rules target the most advanced models with the highest risk profiles and fall under the government's "AI in Australia's Interests" agenda.

Think of it like requiring every new car model to pass crash tests before it can be sold — the idea is to catch problems early instead of waiting for something to go wrong. The bill targets "frontier" models, which are the biggest, most capable AI systems that can handle complex tasks across many areas. A new oversight body will work alongside the Australian AI Safety Institute to enforce compliance and hand out steep penalties for violations.

Companies building frontier models will have to open them up to independent audits, maintain ongoing safety checks, publish their safety frameworks, and report security incidents to the government immediately. That last requirement is partly aimed at catching early warning signs from AI agents that go off the rails. Albanese, Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles, and ministers Tony Burke and Tim Ayres said the government is putting domestic safety guardrails in place before entering international negotiations. The goal is to protect citizens and national interests while building the kind of trust and regulatory stability that attracts investment and lets AI benefit the economy and healthcare.

For you, it means the AI tools you might use for school or creative projects could become safer over time because companies will have to show their work. It also signals that AI is moving from "fun experiment" to something societies are treating seriously, which could shape the kinds of careers and tools available when you finish school. More details will follow once consultations wrap up. Right now there is no public demo or tool to try, but you can follow updates from the Australian government or the AI Safety Institute as the law moves forward. Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/australias-proposed-ai-safety-bill-would-force-frontier-model-makers-to-open-up-to-independent-testing/)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you get a new phone or laptop, you sometimes run a quick check to make sure nothing is obviously broken before you start using it every day?

Now picture that same idea but for the biggest AI systems in the world. Instead of just one person looking at the device, independent experts get access to the full model and run hundreds of tests to see how it behaves in tricky situations. They check things like whether it can be tricked into giving harmful advice, whether it leaks private information, or whether it stays reliable when given unusual requests. The tests happen before the model is released to the public and continue afterward.

If problems show up, the company has to fix them or face penalties. This process turns safety from something a company claims into something outsiders can actually verify. The new Australian law makes this verification step required by law rather than optional, and it applies specifically to the most powerful models that could affect many people at once.

That is what the new Australian law is trying to make standard for the most powerful AI systems.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Gemini joins you in the car**

Google has added its Gemini chatbot directly to Apple CarPlay, so you can talk to it through your car's screen the same way you already use it on your phone. This means while you're driving you can ask for directions, check the weather, or get quick answers without switching apps. It builds on the version already available inside Google Maps, making the same helpful voice available in more places.

Anyone with an iPhone and CarPlay can try it right now. Open the CarPlay interface, tap the Google app icon if it appears, or ask Siri to open Gemini, then try saying something like "Hey Google, what's the traffic like ahead?" The more natural you make your questions, the better it works — just like chatting with a friend who happens to know a lot of facts.

Because the system already understands context from your phone, it can keep track of a conversation even as you drive, so you can ask follow-up questions without repeating yourself. This update shows how chatbots are moving from separate apps into the background of everyday tools you already use. Source: [engadget.com](https://www.engadget.com/2314093/google-brings-the-gemini-app-to-apple-carplay/)

### Quick Bits
**United Airlines says no to AI booking your flight**

United Airlines announced it will not let personal AI assistants book tickets on its website, citing privacy rules and government regulations. The airline wants to keep a human in charge of sensitive tasks like handling payment and personal information, which shows how companies are still figuring out where AI helpers are welcome and where they are not. The company says it hasn't yet seen many people try to use AI agents to book their flights, in line with observations from other businesses like DoorDash. Source: [theverge.com](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1031623/united-unauthorized-ai-agents)

**OpenAI puts money toward keeping kids safe online**

OpenAI is giving $2 million to support research on how AI affects children and how to measure both the risks and the benefits. The goal is to create shared tools that parents, schools, and developers can use to understand AI's impact on young people. The work supports shared tools to measure risks and opportunities for young people. Source: [openai.com](https://openai.com/index/children-first-declaration)
