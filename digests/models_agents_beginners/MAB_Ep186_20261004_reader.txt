OpenAI, the company behind the ChatGPT chat app, just introduced helpers called dots, Top News reports.

A regular chatbot sits still, like a cashier waiting for you to walk up to the counter.

A dot is different. It runs on its own cloud computer, which is a machine that lives on the internet instead of on your phone.

You set the goals. It keeps chasing those goals even when you are not typing.

Picture hiring a very fast assistant for the afternoon, then leaving the room.

That assistant can still dig up facts, sort your notes, or double-check details while you go somewhere else.

Each helper is powered by OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra model, the language brain that reads, writes, and plans inside the dot.

A dot can also connect to thousands of apps through plugins, which are extra doorways into other tools.

Think of plugins as a huge key ring. The helper is not stuck talking through one chat window.

Most AI today only reacts when you type. Dots are built to act in the gaps between your messages.

For a student, that can look like sources for a report still being gathered while you are at practice.

For anyone exploring future jobs, it is AI sliding from answer machine to background teammate.

Dots are starting with ChatGPT Pro and Business Premium users in some countries, and those are paid plans.

Most people cannot try them yet. The design still hints at where free tools may head next.

Your phone already does a quiet version of remembering you.

It notices you usually text after dinner, or that you hunt for skate videos on weekends, without you announcing those habits out loud.

Stretch that same habit across every conversation you have with an AI.

Ask about homework, share a story, or correct a wrong answer, and the model files a little note and starts connecting those notes together.

Step one is the raw chat. Step two is the quiet filing. Step three is the web of links between those files.

Over weeks or months, the small notes stack up, like a scrapbook that nobody posed for.

What comes out is a detailed picture of what you care about, how you write, and what you might ask next.

It is not mind reading. It is pattern memory, built from talks you already had.

Once you can picture that scrapbook, the mystery drops away, and you can decide what you still want to share.

If you actually want to build something with your hands, PLY is a free web tool that designs skate ramps.

You can sketch a ramp, a vert, or a full park, and it gives you a materials list, building plans, and cost estimates.

It also tries to waste as little wood as possible, which is the difference between a daydream drawing and a real build.

Think of it as the most organized shop-class partner you have ever had, except it never loses the measuring tape.

Go to the demo shared by Linus Ekenstam on X, watch the short video, then describe a simple ramp shape and see the plans it creates.

You can also look at the scrapbook itself with Lumo AI Paper Trail, a free privacy tool from Proton.

It takes the data export from your ChatGPT or Claude account. Claude is another AI chat app, in the same family of products as ChatGPT.

A data export is just a download of your past conversations. You upload that file, and Lumo turns it into a clear report of what the AI thinks it knows about you.

You do not need to be technical. The report even explains how each piece of information was collected.

Head to Proton's Lumo site, choose the free AI Paper Trail option, and run your own export. You should see results in a few minutes.
