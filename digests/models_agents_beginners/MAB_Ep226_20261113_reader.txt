ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Perplexity, in a Podqi study covered by Digit, were asked shopping questions about one hundred fifty-one brands like Lululemon and Birkenstock.

These are the chatbots a lot of people already type into when they want a fast recommendation, the way you might text someone for a store.

Picture this. You ask a friend where to buy new sneakers, and they send a link that looks official but is not.

That is basically what happened here. The chatbots sometimes suggested fake or risky websites that could be selling counterfeits, meaning fake copies of real products, or that might even be dangerous.

Perplexity gave risky links in fifteen percent of its answers, ChatGPT in eight percent, and Gemini in four percent.

The security firm Podqi ran the test on six thousand seven hundred ninety-five AI-generated answers.

Smaller brands faced roughly twice the risk of a risky link compared with big-name companies.

Think of a huge chain versus a tiny local label. There is just less solid information floating around about the small one, so the guess gets shakier.

Questions about regional shipping or international delivery made the problem even worse, because the models sometimes invented websites that did not actually exist for those regions.

When risky sites did appear, ChatGPT issued a warning only twenty-four percent of the time, while the other two tools warned even less often.

In fifty-seven percent of the answers containing a risky link, the chatbot actively suggested the site as a place to shop rather than just mentioning it in passing.

YouTube and TikTok already play a guessing game with you. The app looks at what you watched before and predicts what you might like next.

An AI chatbot does a similar guessing game, except the pile is millions of web pages instead of videos, and it does not actually shop or walk into a store.

It looks at patterns in the huge pile of text it was trained on, meaning the writing it studied beforehand, and predicts which words and links usually appear together when people ask about a brand.

If lots of old pages mention a certain web address next to a brand name, even if that address is now fake or abandoned, the model can still suggest it because the pattern feels familiar, the way autocomplete finishes a sentence you have typed a hundred times.

Here is the tricky part. The AI has no way to check in real time whether the link still works or belongs to the real company. It is just completing the sentence based on what it saw during training.

Smaller or regional brands cause more mistakes because there are fewer trustworthy pages about them, so the model has less clear patterns to follow, like trying to cook a rare dish when only two messy recipes exist.

The same pattern-matching process explains why mistakes jumped for questions about shipping to specific countries. Fewer reliable pages existed in the training data, so the guesses got worse.
