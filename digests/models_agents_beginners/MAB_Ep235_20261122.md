### The Big Story
Imagine you're playing a racing game and the world around you reacts exactly the way real streets, water, and objects would. That's what PAN 2 does. It's an open-source world model, which means a free AI system that can generate entire scenes and predict how they will change based on your actions, like a driver stopping at a red light or a police chase through a wet alley.

The person sharing the videos described driving through what feels like Miami, complete with realistic handling on wet surfaces. One clip shows a driver actually waiting at a red light, something you would never see in most video games where traffic rules are often ignored for action. Another shows a police car chasing a motorbike down a narrow back alley before smashing into a stack of boxes, with the boxes tumbling in a way that matches real-world expectations.

Think of it like a super-advanced version of those choose-your-own-adventure books, except the story keeps going on its own with realistic rules for gravity, speed, and crashes instead of just text choices. The creator shared videos of driving straight into the ocean and watching what happens, or chasing a motorbike and smashing into stacked boxes, and the results look surprisingly natural.

This matters because tools like this are moving AI from just answering questions to building whole interactive worlds that anyone can experiment with. For students who love games or want to make their own stories and videos, it shows how AI is becoming a creative playground rather than just a homework helper. In the future, this kind of technology could let you build game levels or test ideas without needing expensive software or coding skills.

Right now you can watch the short demo videos shared on X by searching for the posts from Linus Ekenstam to see the red-light scene and the chaotic chase for yourself. The project being open-source also means developers are already sharing ways to try similar experiments as they become available. Because the model runs locally on capable hardware, people experimenting with it can pause scenes, change starting conditions, and immediately see how small changes affect the outcome, giving a hands-on feel for how AI predicts physical behavior. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/LinusEkenstam/status/2124414718286897478)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're watching a movie or playing a game, your brain automatically guesses what will happen next, like "that car is going to turn left" or "those boxes are about to fall over"?

World models do something similar but with pictures instead of words. They look at the current scene, break it into tiny pieces of information about where everything is and how fast things are moving, then predict the most likely next frame.

Step by step, the AI first studies thousands of real driving videos so it learns basic rules like "cars usually stop at red lights" and "wet roads make things slide." Then, when you give it a new starting scene, it generates what comes after by checking its learned rules against the exact positions and speeds in that moment.

If something unexpected happens, like a sudden crash, it adjusts the prediction on the fly using the same pattern-matching process. The result feels smooth and believable because the model is constantly rebuilding the scene from its best guess of the rules, not just copying old footage.

This is why the red-light and chase scenes look so natural even though no human filmed them. The system does not store every possible outcome in advance. Instead it rebuilds each new frame from scratch using the patterns it has seen before, which is why the boxes fall differently every time depending on exactly where the car hits them.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**A 35-year-old game console now runs ChatGPT**

The original Super Nintendo from 1990 just got an upgrade that lets it run a tiny version of ChatGPT. Researchers used a special add-on chip called the Super FX 2 to speed things up enough for the old hardware to handle simple AI conversations. The chip runs at 21 MHz and uses RISC architecture to push a 2.2 million parameter chatbot model fast enough for real replies.

It's exciting because it shows how far AI has come that even ancient game systems can now chat with you. Retro gaming fans and anyone curious about old tech will love seeing this mash-up of 1990s hardware and 2020s AI. The demo proves that smaller AI models can run on very limited hardware when given the right acceleration, opening the door for people to experiment with AI on devices they already own instead of needing the newest computers.

You can watch the full demo video on Tom's Hardware to see the SNES actually replying to questions in real time. Try searching for the article title on that site and play the embedded clip to hear how the old console sounds when it answers basic questions. Source: [tomshardware.com](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/35-year-old-snes-runs-chatgpt-accelerated-with-a-super-fx-2-chip-21-mhz-of-risc-muscle-speeds-2-2m-parameter-chatbot-replies)

### Quick Bits
**Teachers get quiet permission to use AI for marking exams**

Some schools in the UK are now officially allowing teachers to use AI chatbots when grading student coursework, even though critics are calling the move "scandalous." The change could speed up feedback for students but raises questions about how much human judgment should stay in the process. Officials have given the permission quietly, which has led to public debate over whether the practice should be announced more openly to parents and students. Source: [dailymail.com](https://www.dailymail.com/news/article-16312021/Teachers-given-permission-AI-marking-exams.html)

**Expert predictions about AI and hard math problems are already outdated**

Just last month, top forecasters and even mathematician Terrance Tao guessed it would take years or decades for AI to help solve the Riemann Hypothesis, one of math's biggest open problems. Metaculus predicted May 2035, LEAP Superforecasters gave only a 10 percent chance of another Millennium problem being solved in 2026, and Tao suggested some combination of humans and AIs around 2050. New progress has already made those timelines look too slow, showing how fast AI abilities are moving in technical fields. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/emollick/status/2124348911553061167)
