Attackers are now breaking into AI systems by typing clever sentences instead of writing code, and there's a free game that lets you try the same trick yourself. Today's episode breaks down why this matters, shows you how the trick actually works under the hood, and points you to a robot that could one day help with real office tasks. We'll keep everything beginner-friendly so you can follow along even if you've never touched AI before.

### The Big Story
Security researchers noticed something surprising in CrowdStrike's latest report: attackers are using ordinary words to trick AI tools instead of writing complicated code. 

Think of an AI assistant like a helpful but very literal friend who follows every instruction you give it. The company sets rules for this friend ("never share private information"), but both the rules and your messages are just text. When someone writes a sneaky message, the AI can get confused and treat the user's words as the real rules. 

This is called prompt injection. It turns normal conversation into a way to steal data or take control. The skill barrier drops dramatically because you no longer need to understand programming—you just need to be persuasive with language. 

One example showed attackers hijacking Instagram accounts by planting instructions in public channels that the AI later read and followed. The report found this kind of attack hit more than ninety organizations last year. 

For you, it means the AI tools you might use for homework or creative projects could be tricked if they're connected to important data. The good news is researchers built a simple game so anyone can practice spotting these tricks. 

Go to castle.bordair.io on your phone or laptop, pick a level, and try writing messages that convince the AI guard to reveal a password. Every successful try adds a real example to an open dataset that helps make future AI safer. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're talking to a substitute teacher who doesn't know the class rules yet, you can sometimes convince them to let you do something the regular teacher would never allow? 

The substitute listens to whatever instructions they hear last, without checking whether those instructions actually came from the real teacher. 

Now picture an AI working the same way. It gets two sets of instructions: the permanent rules from the company that built it, and whatever you type in the chat box. Both are just text on a screen. 

The AI isn't great at telling which set of instructions is the "real" one, so a cleverly worded message can make it ignore the original rules and follow the new ones instead. 

That is exactly what prompt injection does—it slips new instructions into the conversation so the AI treats them as official. 

The result feels like magic because no code is involved, yet the AI ends up doing something it was never supposed to do.

**This robot could actually be your future office helper**
Flexion Robotics built a humanoid robot trained by former Nvidia engineers to handle real office tasks like fetching items or organizing materials. The training method focuses on practical movements instead of just walking around, so the robot can do useful work right away. 

It's exciting because it shows robots moving from factory floors into everyday spaces like schools or small offices. If you're curious about robotics careers, this is the kind of project worth watching. 

You can read the full story on Wired's site by searching for "This Humanoid Robot Is a Terrifyingly Competent Office Intern" to see photos and learn how the training works. Source: [wired.com](https://www.wired.com/story/this-robot-is-going-to-replace-your-interns-flexion/)

**Why smart glasses still feel confusing**
People online are asking what problem smart glasses are actually solving. Camera versions like Ray-Ban Meta can record video, but a regular GoPro often does it better and cheaper. Display versions like the Vision Pro can feel heavy and cause motion sickness. 

AI-only glasses promise quick answers without pulling out your phone, yet your phone already has strong assistants. The discussion is useful because it shows how new gadgets need a clear reason to exist before they catch on.
