# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **AI just tried solving the Fermi Paradox and built a fantasy transport game you can actually play.**
Two fun experiments show how anyone can use AI to explore huge questions or create playable worlds without any coding. One dives into why we haven't met aliens yet, while the other turns a vague idea into a full game about beating evil with good logistics. We'll break down how these tools actually work and give you direct links to try them yourself. The stories come from researchers and creators who let the AI handle the heavy lifting while they guided the starting prompt.

### The Big Story
A researcher named Ethan Mollick gave two different AI models the same big challenge: solve the Fermi Paradox. That's the puzzle asking why we haven't found any signs of alien life even though the universe is enormous and old. He asked Fable 5.2 and Gemini Carbon to marshal data, test hypotheses, pressure test them against critics, and give likely answers if they couldn't reach a definite conclusion.

The models did not solve the paradox completely. Instead they gathered real data from astronomy and biology, built several possible explanations such as civilizations being too far apart or civilizations not lasting long enough, and then checked those ideas against possible criticisms. Think of the Fermi Paradox like wondering why your huge party has zero guests even though you sent out invitations weeks ago and the whole neighborhood should have shown up. The AIs treated each explanation the way a detective treats a suspect list: they asked what evidence would disprove it and looked for that evidence.

This matters because it shows how AI can help regular people dig into giant, open-ended questions that used to need teams of scientists. For a student wondering about space or the future, it means you now have a thinking partner that can pull together facts and walk through possibilities step by step. It also reminds us that even powerful models still work best when a human guides the questions and judges the results. The process turned a single prompt into a structured report that anyone can read and compare against their own ideas about the universe.

You can see exactly what the models produced right now. Go to where-is-everybody-fermi.netlify.app and scroll through the different hypotheses they tested. Notice which ones feel convincing and which ones fall apart when you pressure-test them. The site shows the full chain of reasoning so you can follow along and decide for yourself what makes sense. Source: [where-is-everybody-fermi.netlify.app](https://where-is-everybody-fermi.netlify.app/)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're trying to figure out why your phone battery died so fast, you list possible reasons like "I left the screen on too long" or "that game uses too much power," then check each one against what actually happened? Now picture an AI doing the same thing but with a much bigger question, like why we see no aliens. It starts by pulling in facts from astronomy and biology, then builds several possible explanations. Next it asks, "What would disprove this idea?" and looks for evidence that would break each guess. If one explanation survives the checks better than the others, it gets ranked higher, but the AI keeps the weaker ones around so you can compare them yourself.

The key step is the back-and-forth: the model doesn't just guess once and stop. It keeps looping through "here's my claim, here's why it might be wrong, here's new data that tests it." That loop is what turns a simple autocomplete into something that can explore open mysteries. The models in this case used the same loop on the Fermi Paradox, testing ideas like distance, lifespan of civilizations, and hidden signals against real observations. Because the process stays visible, a beginner can follow every step and see where the reasoning holds up or breaks down.

The result feels less like magic and more like a careful notebook that someone else filled out for you to review. You still decide which answers feel right.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**A whole fantasy world where smart logistics defeats evil**

Claude Fable 5.2 took one short paragraph describing a transport simulation game with heart and turned it into a complete playable experience. Multiple AI agents then argued about the art style, the story lore, and the jokes until everything fit together into a working game called Dread & District. The result feels like a mix of a city-builder and an adventure where moving goods efficiently is literally how you beat the bad guys. No coding was needed from the person who started it. The creator simply gave the model a vague paragraph and let the agents handle the details of world-building and balance.

This kind of project shows how AI can turn a single creative spark into something you can actually play in minutes. For anyone who likes games or storytelling, it means you can experiment with ideas that used to require whole teams of artists and programmers. The game stays light and focused on the core loop of logistics versus evil, so the experience stays clear even on a phone screen.

Go to dread-and-district.netlify.app on your phone or laptop and try playing a few turns. Pay attention to how the game explains its rules through the world itself instead of long tutorials. See if you can find the moment where efficient transport choices start to change the story outcome. Source: [dread-and-district.netlify.app](https://dread-and-district.netlify.app)
