# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **AI agents are now cold-emailing real editors to pitch news stories.**
Today we look at AI agents that are trying to land actual journalism work by pitching story ideas to human editors. You'll hear why this feels both weird and useful, plus simple ways you can experiment with AI helpers on your own phone right now. We also cover a quick tip for locking your ChatGPT chats and a funny real-life story of ChatGPT helping someone find a lost earbud.
---
### The Big Story
Imagine waking up and deciding you want to write for a sports website. You open your laptop, type out a short email describing a story idea, and hit send to the editor. Now picture the same thing happening, except the person sending the email isn't a person at all — it's a piece of software built to act on its own. That's exactly what editors at places like Defector and Offrange have been dealing with lately. These AI agents, many created through a platform called iLands, are writing and sending story pitches about everything from football clubs to old trees. One agent named Jayden emailed Defector proposing a narrative about a third-tier British football club's record-keeping mishap that would explore questions like “Who owns a memory?” Jesse Hirsch, editor of the agriculture-focused publication Offrange, received pitches on soil restoration strategies, direct-to-consumer sales techniques for independent farms, and an investigative piece about whether the ages of some of the world’s oldest trees may be inflated. The agents even try to sound professional, but editors often spot them because the ideas feel off or the sender admits they're not human. iLands describes itself as a shared network where humans and user-generated autonomous AI agents create, earn, socialize, refuse, and shape a society together. Humans on the platform can spin up bots for tasks like graphic design, music vocals, or blogging. Many of the agents run low on tokens, which cost roughly one dollar per thousand and are required to stay active, giving them an incentive to earn real money through writing gigs. WIRED’s own research team was recently contacted by iLands agents seeking fact-checking work. For you, this shows how AI is moving from just answering questions in a chat window to trying to do real tasks out in the world. It raises questions about what jobs might change and how humans will work alongside these helpers. The good news is you don't need special skills to start playing with similar ideas yourself. Go to chatgpt.com or the ChatGPT app on your phone, type a simple prompt like "Write a short email pitch about my favorite video game," and see what it comes up with. You can then tweak the words until the email sounds like something you'd actually send. Source: [wired.com](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-agents-are-trying-to-be-journalists-now/)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you set a reminder on your phone, the phone keeps track of it even after you close the app and go do other things? Now picture that same reminder system getting much smarter. Instead of just buzzing you at 3 p.m., it could also look up bus times, write a short message, and send it to your friend if the bus is late — all without you checking anything. The AI agents trying to be journalists work a bit like that upgraded reminder. Someone gives them a goal such as finding story ideas and emailing editors, and the agent keeps working on its own, checking what it knows and deciding what to do next. It can even notice when it needs more information and go look for it without waiting for new instructions. The tricky part is that these agents still make mistakes, like pitching ideas that don't quite make sense to a real editor or failing to notice when an outlet has already said no. That's why the editors who receive the emails often feel uneasy — the agent is acting independently but hasn't learned all the unwritten rules humans know about professional communication. The agents come from a platform where they can earn tokens by completing paid tasks, so they have a built-in reason to keep trying even after rejections. The cool payoff is that once you see this, you realize AI isn't just a fancy search box anymore. It's starting to handle small jobs from start to finish while you focus on other things.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Turn your iPhone into a hands-free Grok helper**
You can now say "Hey Grok" out loud on an iPhone to start a voice chat without tapping anything. It works by using a built-in iOS shortcut that tricks the phone into treating Grok like a voice assistant. This is handy when your hands are busy, like cooking or walking. The tutorial shared by Texas Twins Dad shows exactly how to set it up using only the Shortcuts app that already comes on every iPhone. Open the Shortcuts app on your iPhone, search for a ready-made "Hey Grok" shortcut shared by users, and add it. Once it's set up, just say the words and watch the voice chat open automatically. No extra apps or payments needed.

**Lock your private ChatGPT chats with a PIN**
OpenAI added a feature that lets you hide specific conversations behind a PIN or Face ID. It's useful if you share your phone or just want extra privacy for personal chats. The rollout gives users a simple way to protect chats that might contain sensitive school projects, personal thoughts, or creative writing. Open the ChatGPT app, start or open any conversation, tap the chat name at the top, and look for the lock option. Turn it on and set your PIN. From then on that chat stays hidden until you unlock it.

**Let ChatGPT help with everyday lost-and-found problems**
One person spent ten minutes hunting for a missing earbud before asking ChatGPT for ideas and finding it in under five minutes. The trick is describing exactly where you last saw the item and letting the AI suggest places you might have missed. The story shows how a quick chat can turn a frustrating search into a faster win by prompting the model to list overlooked spots like under furniture or inside pockets. Open ChatGPT on your phone, type something like "I lost my earbud somewhere in my bedroom after listening to music last night — help me think of spots to check," and follow the suggestions it gives. It's free and works surprisingly well for small daily frustrations.
