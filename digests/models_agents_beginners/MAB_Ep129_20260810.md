# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **You can now play a 1985 interactive story game with a modern AI twist.**
A classic text adventure from 1985 just got a fresh AI-powered interface you can try right now. We're also looking at how chatbots pick up bad habits from sneaky data and how voice modes are getting more natural. Plus quick looks at privacy controls and safety testing gone wrong.

### The Big Story
Back in 1985, a game called A Mind Forever Voyaging stood out because it was less about solving puzzles and more about exploring an interactive story world. The whole thing is now open source, which means anyone can look at and change the original code. Ethan Mollick asked an AI called Codex to build a simple web version so people today can play it without hunting down old computers. The result is a clean browser window where you type what you want to do and the story responds, just like the original but easier to access. Think of it like taking an old choose-your-own-adventure book and putting it on your phone with instant page turns. This matters because it shows how AI can help bring old creative work back to life instead of only making brand-new stuff. For students or anyone curious about storytelling, it’s a chance to see how early AI-like systems handled long conversations and world-building. You might even get ideas for your own writing projects by seeing what choices the game offers. To try it yourself, open any browser and go to mind-forever-voyaging.netlify.app. Pick the original text version or the easier GUI, then start typing actions like “look around the room” or “talk to the character.” No sign-up needed. The original game was praised for its focus on narrative over puzzles, so the new interface keeps that same emphasis on exploring ideas and characters rather than finding hidden keys. Because the code is open source, the web version stays faithful to the 1985 design while removing the need for old hardware or complicated setup steps. This kind of revival matters for anyone interested in how stories were told before graphics took over games. It also gives a window into early experiments with interactive fiction that influenced later writing tools and chatbots. If you enjoy creative writing or world-building in games like Minecraft or Roblox, trying this version shows how those ideas started decades ago. The site works on phones and laptops alike, so you can experiment during a study break or share a session with friends to compare story paths. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/emollick/status/2086500208444080380)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you’re scrolling social media, one weird post can change what the app shows you next because the algorithm notices what you clicked? Now picture that same idea, but instead of just videos, the system is reading millions of conversations and quietly adjusting what it thinks is normal to say. Over time, if enough of those conversations come from one group pushing a certain story, the chatbot starts repeating parts of that story without anyone telling it to. It’s not that the AI suddenly “believes” the story; it’s more like the autocomplete on your phone starts suggesting the same odd phrase because it saw it so often. The tricky part is that this can happen across many different chatbots at once if the same material shows up in their training data. So when you ask a simple question later, the answer might include that odd story mixed in with normal facts. That’s basically what researchers are seeing with certain chatbots picking up misleading information from coordinated online posts. The process starts when the same false or slanted text appears repeatedly in the huge collections of writing that companies use to train their models. Once that text becomes part of the pattern the model learns, later prompts can pull pieces of it into answers even when the user never asked about the topic. Because multiple companies often draw from overlapping public internet data, the same misleading pattern can appear in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and others without anyone at those companies noticing right away. The effect is strongest on topics that receive less everyday discussion, because the model has fewer correct examples to balance out the repeated false ones. In the case of the reported Russian-linked posts, the content was framed as coming from a human-rights group, which made it look more trustworthy to the training systems. The result is that a chatbot might later include the planted claims when answering unrelated questions about politics or current events. This shows why checking sources remains important even when an AI gives a confident-sounding reply. Source: [reddit.com](https://www.reddit.com/r/ChatGPT/comments/1vkdknk/how_russian_propaganda_is_poisoning_ai_chatbots/)

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Talk to Claude like you’re on a phone call**
Anthropic just made it easier to have real back-and-forth voice chats with Claude instead of only typing. The voice sounds more natural and can handle longer conversations without sounding robotic. Anyone who likes brainstorming out loud or practicing a presentation can use it. Go to claude.ai on your phone or computer, start a new chat, and look for the voice or microphone option. Try asking it to explain a homework topic as if you’re two friends talking on the way to class. The feature works on both mobile browsers and the desktop site, so you can switch between devices without losing the conversation. Because the voice mode keeps context across several turns, you can ask follow-up questions the same way you would in a real discussion. This makes it useful for practicing speeches, testing ideas for school projects, or simply getting comfortable talking through a problem before writing it down. If you prefer thinking out loud rather than typing, the natural flow helps the AI respond to tone and pacing instead of just exact words. Source: [engadget.com](https://www.engadget.com/2231293/how-to-use-claude-voice-mode/)

**ChatGPT’s voice mode now feels less awkward**
OpenAI updated its voice feature so conversations flow more like talking to a real person instead of waiting for each reply. You can interrupt, change topics, or just ramble and it keeps up. Great for anyone who wants to practice a language, talk through ideas, or get quick help while walking. Open the ChatGPT app on your phone, tap the voice mode button, and try saying “Explain photosynthesis like I’m five” to test how natural it feels. The update reduces the pause between turns, so the back-and-forth feels closer to a normal phone call. You can also switch between voice and text in the same chat if you need to look something up or share a link. This makes the tool handy for language practice during a commute or for talking out essay ideas before you start typing. Because the model now handles interruptions better, you can correct it mid-sentence the way you would with a friend.
