# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **AI just read words from a 2,000-year-old scroll that no human could see.**
Researchers used AI to recover a lost passage from an ancient scroll buried for two millennia. The same kind of technology is also being used to invent brand-new burger recipes. Today we explore how these tools work, why they matter for creative work, and what you can try right now to see similar magic in action.
---
### The Big Story
Imagine a scroll so damaged that the ink has faded and the paper has crumbled into pieces no one can read. For two thousand years, that text stayed hidden until AI stepped in. Researchers trained a model to look at tiny patterns in the remaining fragments and predict what letters and words once sat there. One newly recovered line talks about people who “strained ourselves to the utmost through research and learning” and gained “practical wisdom.”  

Think of it like putting together a giant jigsaw puzzle where most pieces are missing and the ones you have are faded. The AI doesn’t just guess randomly; it has studied millions of other ancient texts so it knows how words and ideas usually fit together. That lets it fill in the blanks with surprising accuracy.  

This matters because history isn’t just for museums. The same approach could help translate old documents in libraries around the world, uncover lost stories, or even help students explore primary sources that used to be locked away. For anyone interested in history, writing, or archaeology, it opens doors that used to require years of expert work.  

On a personal level, it shows how AI can act as a super-powered detective for anything that’s hard to see or read. If you’ve ever wished you could understand an old family letter or a faded poster, this is the same idea scaled up.  

Right now you can’t try the exact scroll tool, but you can experiment with similar image-and-text AI on your phone. Go to the free version of Google’s Gemini app, upload a photo of any handwritten note or old printed page, and ask it to transcribe or translate what it sees. Watch how it handles messy or incomplete text. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/emollick/status/2070729413520396475)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you’re drawing with a friend and you both start with random dots on paper, then slowly add more color and shape until a picture appears?  

First you might add a few broad strokes that suggest a burger bun. Then you refine the edges so it looks round instead of blurry. Next you layer in details like sesame seeds or a patty. At each step the picture gets clearer because you’re removing noise and keeping what looks right.  

Now imagine the same process but the “drawing” is made of words instead of lines. The AI starts with pure noise and gradually turns that noise into sentences by deciding, one tiny step at a time, which word fits best at each position. It keeps doing this until the whole paragraph reads like a real recipe.  

That’s how a diffusion model works when it invents new burgers. It never copies an existing recipe; it builds one from scratch by repeatedly cleaning up random guesses until something delicious-sounding appears.  

The cool part is that the same step-by-step cleanup trick can be used for pictures, music, or even code. Once you see it as “start messy, get cleaner,” the whole idea stops feeling like magic and starts feeling like a really patient artist.

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**AI just invented a brand-new burger (and the classic Big Mac too)**
A model trained only on burger recipes figured out the Big Mac on its own and then created fresh combinations aimed at being extra tasty, more sustainable, or healthier. It didn’t copy existing menus; it explored what makes a burger good and invented new ones. This shows how AI can help with creative hobbies like cooking, game design, or even writing stories.  

Anyone who likes food or experimenting in the kitchen should try playing with similar creative tools. Head to the free ChatGPT website, start a new chat, and type: “Act as a burger chef and invent three brand-new burgers optimized for maximum deliciousness using only common ingredients.” Ask it to explain why each one works. You can tweak the prompt to focus on vegetarian options or low-cost ingredients next.

**AI is sneaking into science journals with fake images**
Some AI-generated pictures are now so realistic that they’re appearing in published research papers, which can mislead readers and damage trust in science. The problem is getting big enough that journals are starting to add extra checks. It’s a reminder that not everything you see online was made by a human with a camera.  

If you care about spotting real versus generated images, try this quick test: go to the free Bing Image Creator (bing.com/images/create), type a simple prompt like “a scientist looking through a microscope in a lab,” then compare the result side-by-side with a real photo from a news site. Notice which details look slightly off. Source: [livescience.com](https://www.livescience.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/ai-images-are-more-convincing-than-ever-infiltrating-journals-and-undermining-trust-in-science)
