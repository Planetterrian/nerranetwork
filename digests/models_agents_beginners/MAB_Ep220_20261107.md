> **A new study reveals AI already handles up to half the work scientists do when running real research projects.**
Researchers at Anthropic examined more than two hundred thousand actual sessions where scientists used Claude for lab and analysis work. The same company also described how its AI uses quiet periods to reorganize what it has learned, much like the brain sorts memories overnight. ### The Big Story
Picture a high-school science fair project where you have to gather measurements, run calculations, write up the results, and decide what the numbers actually mean. Anthropic researchers tracked 207,000 real sessions in which working scientists used Claude and Claude Code on exactly those kinds of tasks. They discovered that current AI could already take over between 27 and 52 percent of the total researcher time needed to finish a study, with the middle value across all fields landing at 38 percent. Most of that help shows up in two places: analyzing data, which accounts for roughly one-third of ordinary Claude sessions and nearly half of Claude Code sessions, and writing the final report. Computer science, mathematics, and statistics researchers turn to the tool far more often than researchers in health or lab-based physical sciences, because those formal fields contain more of the tasks AI currently handles well. When the same scientists were asked in a survey which parts of research matter most, they ranked coming up with fresh ideas at the top, yet right now they ask Claude for idea generation less often than they ask it to crunch numbers or polish sentences. On a special version called Claude Science the share of idea-generation requests doubles, showing that purpose-built tools can shift how people use AI. The study also found that tasks AI is theoretically strongest at receive two to four times more real use than their share of a researcher's day would predict, while tasks involving physical work receive almost none. In fields where many tasks line up with AI strengths, overall usage rises; in fields where fewer tasks match, usage stays low. Using a simple model, the researchers estimate that widespread adoption of today's capabilities could roughly double research output in computer science and statistics while producing smaller but still noticeable speed-ups in chemistry or anthropology. For any student thinking about a future in science, the numbers suggest that the daily rhythm of lab work is already changing, with more time freed for the parts of research that still need human judgment. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how, after a busy day, your mind keeps replaying moments from class or lunch even after you close your eyes? That quiet replay helps your brain decide which details are worth keeping and which ones can fade. Anthropic engineers are testing something similar inside Claude. Instead of leaving every conversation as one long, messy transcript, they give the model stretches of idle time when no one is chatting with it. During those stretches the AI reviews its own earlier logs, spots mistakes it made, and rewrites scattered notes into shorter, cleaner summaries it can read later. The team calls this step “dreaming” because it happens when the model is not actively answering anyone, exactly the way your brain does most of its organizing while you sleep. The goal is to let Claude carry useful patterns forward across many separate conversations instead of starting from scratch every time a new chat begins. Early tests show the method already helps the model stay consistent on longer projects that stretch over several days. The same basic loop—capture experience, then replay and tidy it up—appears in both human memory research and this new AI design. Source: [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory)

### Quick Bits
**When people feel overwhelmed, some are turning to ChatGPT for support**
Researchers in Israel tracked how often people under emotional stress typed their feelings into ChatGPT instead of using traditional hotlines or talking with friends. The pattern shows AI chatbots are sliding into daily emotional life even when the topics are deeply personal. Source: [jpost.com](https://www.jpost.com/health-and-wellness/mind-and-spirit/article-913917)
