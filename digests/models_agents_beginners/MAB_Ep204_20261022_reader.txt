Anthropic put out a research preview of Claude Duet, The Decoder reports, and it is an agent rather than a regular chatbot.

An agent is a helper that can take steps on its own, instead of waiting for you to type a question.

A research preview means this is an early test, more like a lab version than a finished school project.

The system runs on cloud computers, machines in big buildings that stay on when your own devices are off.

Picture a really organized classmate working from a library you never visit, only texting when they need a decision.

Duet connects to outside services like Gmail, Slack, and Stripe, so it can schedule meetings or prepare billing.

It always asks for your approval before payments or sending messages.

Bank transfers are blocked entirely, like a door that will not open no matter who knocks.

The helper is built on Claude Opus five point five, the model that does the thinking underneath.

A model here is a trained system that learned patterns from a huge amount of text, then uses those patterns to decide what to do next.

It comes as a standalone app on Apple phones, on Android, and on Claude's website.

Pro and Max subscribers in the United States can join a waitlist for access.

Team and Enterprise plans, the ones companies use, are not included yet.

Every morning the agent sends a short summary of the most important items from connected apps.

After that it only reaches out again when something needs attention.

That morning ping is like a coach sliding a tiny game plan under your door, then leaving you alone until the next play actually matters.

Users assign tasks by text or voice in a single ongoing thread.

That thread can cover scheduling, dashboards, financial forecasts, prototypes, and web apps.

One thread is like a group chat that never starts over, so the helper already knows the whole season.

The agent works on its own virtual machine with a browser.

A virtual machine is a computer made of software, sitting in the cloud, with its own screen and memory.

Your laptop or phone does not need to stay powered on.

It also reaches Docusign and Shopify through more than six thousand connectors and plugins from the Claude Marketplace.

Connectors work like those little adapters in a charging drawer, each one letting the helper plug into a different app.

That is how it tracks unpaid invoices or gets payroll ready.

For purchases it uses Stripe Link with a virtual card, so your real card number is never shared.

Think of that virtual card as a single-use lunch ticket. The cafe sees the ticket, never your actual wallet.

This setup responds to similar products from xAI with Grok Bot in August, Meta with Muse in September, and OpenAI with Dots in September.

For a student it could notice a project deadline in a connected calendar and send a reminder without being asked.

For someone running a small side project it could watch for unpaid invoices while that person focuses on the actual work.

Right now access is limited to the waitlist for eligible United States subscribers, and the agent still requires human approval on any risky step.

You can join the waitlist on Claude's website if you have a qualifying plan.

Or open the regular Claude app today, paste a list of your real deadlines, and ask it to plan a study schedule so you can see how multi-step tasks already feel.

Leaving Minecraft running while you grab a snack is the closest everyday picture of this kind of helper.

The world keeps simulating rain, mobs, and daylight even though your hands are off the keyboard.

An agent gets that same kind of independence, but the world it tends is your mail, your calendar, and the jobs you already described.

First, you connect the apps you actually use, and you talk in that long thread so it keeps memory of your preferences.

Second, every few hours it walks through those apps the way you might skim your inbox between classes.

Third, it sorts what can wait from what needs a human, then it only taps you for the human part.

A normal chatbot is like a vending machine. Nothing happens until you walk up and press a button.

The companies writing the rules still make it ask before anything risky, because one wrong automatic payment could wreck trust fast.

The always-on work is the computer's job. The safety rails are still written by people, on purpose.
