Niantic, the company behind Pokémon Go, sold that game last year, Wired reports, and now builds three-dimensional digital replicas of real places from its collection of environment data.

Three-dimensional means the copy has height, width, and depth, like a street you could actually walk through. Picture a dusk walk in two thousand sixteen with your phone out to catch a Snorlax.

The app scanned the buildings and streets while hundreds of millions of players did the same thing. That pile of scenery is now fuel for a new kind of artificial intelligence system, or A I.

A world model looks at visual inputs like photos or video frames. It then predicts the next visual state of a scene, including physics, which just means the rules of how things move.

It tries to get falling objects and swinging doors to behave the way they do in real life. Brian McClendon is the chief technology officer at Niantic Spatial, the person who leads the tech there.

He wants to reconstruct the real world in software so robots can rehearse inside safe digital copies instead of the physical room. Treat those copies like a practice gym that matches your actual street.

The robot can stumble, retry, and learn without scraping a real wall. A few photos of a kitchen or hallway can unlock millions of practice sessions for that robot.

It is like handing a student two textbook pages and then letting them drill millions of practice problems. Driverless cars use the same trick to learn split-second reactions to obstacles without ever leaving a computer.

That is the same idea as a flight simulator that trains a pilot before anyone leaves the ground. More reliable home robots could follow by two thousand twenty-seven, even if you never open the software yourself.

A language model is an A I that predicts the next word in a sentence. A world model predicts the next image after it sees the current one.

Show it one photo of a kitchen. It tries to guess the next picture if a chair slides or a cupboard door moves.

It picks up those rules the way you learned that a ball rolls downhill, by seeing the pattern again and again. Think of a flipbook. Each page is a guess at the frame that should come after the last one.

The model plays that guessing game on huge piles of examples until the next picture looks physically right. A robot can then rehearse walking through a kitchen it has never physically visited, because the model already expects what happens if it bumps a chair.

All of that rehearsal stays inside the computer, so no real mug gets smashed. Later the robot carries those lessons into the actual room.
