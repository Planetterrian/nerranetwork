Apple is preparing a new seven-inch HomeView smart home display, Analytics Insight reports. It is meant to support outside chatbots on top of Apple's own voice assistant Siri. A chatbot is a kind of artificial intelligence, or AI. That means software you talk to in ordinary words, the way you text a person. Siri is the talking helper already built into Apple phones and speakers.

Picture HomeView as a small talking tablet that lives on the kitchen counter instead of in your pocket. Seven inches is about the size of a paperback book held in your hands.

Early test software for the upcoming iOS twenty-seven point three update contains a brand-new section labeled Conversational Apps. iOS is the software that runs iPhones. This test copy looks built so the screen can show and run chat services from companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic.

OpenAI makes ChatGPT, the chatbot many people already type questions into. Anthropic makes Claude, another chatbot that replies in everyday language. Conversational Apps is like being allowed extra chat apps on a phone, instead of only the built-in messages app.

The same test code also lists two-way audio routing, conversation session management, and the ability to handle interruptions so voice chats can feel natural. Two-way audio is like a phone call. You talk, and the device talks back, instead of a walkie-talkie that only goes one way at a time.

Session management is the device keeping track of which chat you are in. It is like a group text that stays in its own thread so messages do not spill everywhere. Handling interruptions means you can cut in while it is speaking, the way you jump in when someone at the table is talking.

Security features are being added too. If the face check fails three times, the system asks you to prove it is you with your iPhone. Keep getting locked out, and the waits stretch, growing as long as eight hours. The face check is like unlocking your phone with your face. The growing wait is like a timeout that lasts longer each time someone keeps guessing wrong.

HomeView is scheduled to reach stores on November thirteenth in selected countries including the United States, Britain, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India, Singapore, and South Africa. The starting price is two hundred ninety-nine dollars in the United States.

The feature that would actually run third-party chatbots is still described as not fully wired up. Third-party just means chatbots from other companies, not Apple's own. No one can say exactly when or whether that part will appear.

For families this could mean choosing the AI voice that explains homework clearly. It could also mean one that remembers the right smart-light settings, instead of being stuck with one company's style. It is like being allowed to pick which teacher to ask, instead of having only one person at the front of the class.

Students might ask one chatbot for step-by-step science help while parents ask another for quick calendar summaries.

You cannot try HomeView yet. You can already compare how different chatbots answer the same question. Open both ChatGPT and Claude in your browser. Type explain photosynthesis like I am twelve, and watch how each one teaches the same idea.

Your phone keyboard already guesses the next word while you type. That tiny trick is the whole engine behind these chatbots, stretched until it looks like a real conversation.

First, the system studies huge collections of books, websites, and chats. It learns which words usually follow which other words, the way you learn song lyrics by hearing them over and over.

Next, you ask a question, and it does not think the way a person does. It just keeps picking the most likely next piece until a full answer sits there.

Then each little chunk it reads or writes is called a token, roughly one word or part of a word. Picture tokens as Lego bricks. The model snaps one brick on, then the next, until the sentence is a whole build.

Last, the more tokens it can handle at once, the longer the conversation or document it can keep track of without dropping earlier details. Treat that limit like a backpack. Fill it with too many bricks and something old has to fall out.

That is why a chatbot can suddenly lose the thread if your chat gets really long. The backpack is full, so the early stuff is gone. What you are talking to is an autocomplete engine that got so good at guessing it can write essays, answer science questions, or even help plan a project. The mystery is mostly speed and scale, not a brain hiding in the computer.

OpenAI co-founder Andrej Karpathy used the AI chatbot Claude, The Decoder reports, to sort a mountain of old comments. He ran through forty-three million Hacker News comments and turned them into a clean list of six thousand four hundred fifty-four recommended videos. Hacker News is a long-running site where people in tech share links and leave comments.

Forty-three million comments is far more than anyone could read by hand in a weekend.

He built a whole video site in that one weekend. The result is a hand-picked feed that he says beats YouTube's own suggestions for finding interesting tech talks and tutorials. It is like having a super-fast helper who can read a mountain of notes while you just describe what you want. One person with no coding team did it by chatting with an AI instead of writing code from scratch.

You can try a tiny version of that same weekend project right now. Open Claude or ChatGPT on your phone or laptop, and paste a short list of video titles you like. Type group these into beginner, intermediate, and advanced tech talks and give each group a short reason why it fits. The AI will sort them and explain its choices.

One shopper on Reddit takes a quick photo of the entire wine shelf and asks ChatGPT which bottle would go well with curry, or whatever they are cooking. The chatbot reads the labels, prices, and grape types from the picture and gives a recommendation plus reasons why it might taste good.

It works even when the photo is a little blurry. It often matches what a wine expert would pick, but much faster. It is like standing in the aisle with someone who can read every label at once, then tying those bottles to the meal in your cart.

Next time you are at the store, open the ChatGPT app on your phone and snap the shelf. Type which of these would taste good with pasta, and you might find a new favorite without guessing. The same trick works for any meal. Just add the dish you are planning and let the chatbot suggest based on the actual bottles in front of you.
