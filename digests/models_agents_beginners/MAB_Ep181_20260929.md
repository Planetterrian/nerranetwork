### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you plan a school project you have to decide what counts inside your budget. Maybe you include the cost of poster board and markers, but you leave out the time you spend drawing after dinner or the bus ride to the store. That choice of what goes inside the budget and what stays outside is called drawing brackets. AI systems do the exact same thing when they solve problems. They look at the instructions you give them and decide which facts, costs, consequences, and side effects belong inside the calculation. If you ask an AI to pick the cheapest place to build a data center, it might count electricity prices but leave out how that choice affects nearby rivers or future drought risks. The tricky part is that the AI gets really good at optimizing whatever you put inside the brackets, but it cannot magically notice something you never mentioned. When the real world behaves differently than the AI predicted, that is often a sign the brackets were drawn too small. The useful skill is learning to check whether the AI remembered to look outside its original list. You can practice this by giving an AI a task and then asking it afterward what important things it left out of its answer. The Reddit essay that sparked this idea points out that expanding the brackets too far eventually connects everything to everything else, so the real goal is knowing when to widen them and when to keep them focused. Revisability becomes the key instead of trying to include every possible detail at once. **Build your own skate ramp with AI**
A dad named Linus used AI to create a free tool called PLY that designs mini-ramps using real wood sizes and safety rules. It turns a simple request like “help my kid’s teacher build a small ramp” into a full plan with measurements and material lists. This is exciting because it shows how AI can help with hands-on hobbies instead of just writing essays. Anyone who likes skating, building, or DIY projects should try it. Go to the link in his recent X post, describe the space you have available, and watch it generate a grounded design. You can then tweak the height or materials and see how the plan changes. No coding needed, just type what you want. The tool stays grounded in common lumber sizes so the plans can actually be built with supplies from a regular hardware store. Skate teachers and park builders started messaging Linus after seeing the design, showing real demand for this kind of practical help. Teens can use it to plan a backyard feature for friends or a school project that combines physics and creativity. The output includes step-by-step material counts so you know exactly what to buy before starting construction. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/LinusEkenstam/status/2104808203028742580)
