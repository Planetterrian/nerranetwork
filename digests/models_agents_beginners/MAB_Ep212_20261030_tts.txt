Gemini, Google's chatbot, can now build a Google Doc, a slide deck, a PDF, or a spreadsheet, then keep changing that file when you send another message.

A PDF is that familiar file you open when someone sends a flyer or a form.

Google Docs is the online writing app many people already use for homework.

The Verge reports the new options also cover Google Sheets, slide decks, Markdown files, TXT files, and code.

Markdown is a simple writing format that uses plain typed symbols for headings and lists.

A TXT file is just basic unformatted text, and code means computer instructions.

PDF preview and editing work on the web and on the mobile apps.

The other file types currently work only on the web.

Picture a group chat where one person can actually produce the poster while everyone else is still talking.

You used to copy words out of a chatbot and paste them into another tab, like carrying water in a cup instead of turning on a tap.

Now the file lives on the same bench as the conversation, so you never lose your place.

Talk to Gemini the way you would talk to a classmate who both writes the first draft and then fixes the heading when you ask.

People already use Google tools for homework, group projects, and creative work, so this sits inside a workspace they already know.

A student can brainstorm an essay, generate the outline, and turn it into a formatted document in one conversation.

Anyone making posters, resumes, or presentations gets a faster back and forth.

The feature rolled out starting today and is available to all Gemini users at no extra cost.

To try it, open Google's Gemini website, start a new chat, and type this: Create a one-page Google Doc about why bees are important with a simple title and three bullet points.

Then send a follow-up like, Add a picture description at the bottom, and watch the file change instead of starting over.

You can also ask for a short slide deck for a class presentation, then say, Change the second slide to have two columns instead of one.

Checking your own work after a test is a habit most people already have.

Claude, another chatbot, now does a version of that glance after every single reply it gives.

A post on X described the system as self-assessment, added to Claude in June.

Right after it answers, the model looks at the whole conversation and asks whether the reply could be harmful or against the rules.

It then writes a short note about what it just said.

Because Claude has already read every message in the chat, it can spot patterns that simpler filters might miss, such as someone trying to get advice on something unsafe.

Treat those older filters as a locked door with a fixed list of forbidden rooms.

Self-assessment is closer to a student rereading a text before hitting send, using judgment about the whole thread rather than a static list of bad topics.

Claude now gives this preliminary safety assessment right after it responds.

The technique has already surfaced misuse that earlier safeguards were not designed to catch.

The check happens inside the same model that just answered, so it stays efficient even as conversations grow longer.

As the underlying model improves, these self-checks are expected to get better too, since they rely on the model's own growing judgment rather than outside rules written by humans.

The extra safety layer runs automatically in the background without slowing down normal conversations.

You already know that feeling of sending a message and then noticing a tone problem two seconds later.

Claude is being taught to take that second look on purpose, every time, and to leave a brief note on the fridge about what it found.

Once you see it that way, a safety pass stops sounding like magic and starts sounding like a careful habit built into the reply itself.

A free artificial intelligence video tool can turn a short typed description into a polished clip that looks like a tiny production team made it.

You describe what you want, a quick explainer for a school project, a fun birthday message, or a short story scene, and the tool handles the visuals, the voice, and the timing.

Calling that description a prompt just means the sentence you type to tell the system what to make.

Teens who want content for YouTube, TikTok, or class presentations can skip learning complicated editing software.

Ordering a dish out loud and letting the kitchen plate it is the same idea: you name the scene, and the generator cooks the shots.

Analytics Insight has a free step-by-step guide for choosing a free generator, writing a clear prompt, and reviewing the output.

Follow that guide in your browser, then try typing a thirty-second animated video explaining how photosynthesis works with friendly cartoon characters.

Photosynthesis is how plants turn light into food, and you should notice whether the clip keeps a simple beginning, middle, and end.

You can also try a fifteen-second TikTok-style clip about your favorite game, or a simple how-to video for a hobby you enjoy, and test several ideas in one sitting because it is free to start.
