AI just designed brand-new proteins to fight deadly scorpion venom.

Privacy locks in ChatGPT, a helper that can read several open tabs, Gemini in the car, and a Deep Dive are up after the lead.

Anthropic's Claude, an artificial intelligence assistant, created hundreds of thousands of protein designs meant to stick to scorpion neurotoxins and shut them down, The Decoder reports.

A neurotoxin is a venom that attacks your nerves, the body's wiring that carries messages.

Researchers then tested two hundred eleven of those designs in real labs.

Seventeen of them actually grabbed onto the venom proteins the way the team hoped!

The toxins came from scorpions in Mexico, North Africa, and the Middle East.

They included Cn2 from a Mexican scorpion, Ts1 from a Brazilian species, AaH II and AaH I from North African species, and LqhIII from a Middle Eastern species.

Picture a lock on a nerve cell, and the venom as a stolen key that jams the lock and stops the cell from working.

Claude was inventing brand-new keys from scratch, shaped to block that stolen key, instead of copying keys that already exist in nature.

Two contract research organizations, Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience, measured how well those new keys stuck.

A contract research organization is an outside lab you hire to run careful tests for you.

They used surface plasmon resonance, or SPR, a lab technique that tracks how tightly one molecule sticks to another.

Think of SPR as a super-sensitive handshake meter.

It does not just ask whether two pieces touch. It asks how hard they hold on.

Claude did not throw random shapes at the wall.

It used what it knows about how molecules fit together to propose shapes that might stop the toxins from harming nerve cells.

Developing medicines or antidotes usually takes years of trial and error in labs, like tasting thousands of recipes before one works.

Tools like this could speed up the early idea stage dramatically, the part where scientists decide what is even worth mixing in a real test tube.

If you care about biology or medicine, this is AI as a lab partner, not just a homework helper.

Future doctors and researchers could use similar tools to tackle rare diseases or new health threats faster.

These designs are still early-stage research and not ready as medicine, and that honesty matters.

The full set of tested designs and lab results has been released publicly so other scientists can build on them.

The data lives on Hugging Face, a public library of research files, under the dataset name Anthropic scorpion toxin binder design.

The technical report is titled De novo design of protein binders against scorpion toxins with Claude, and de novo just means designed from scratch.

You can read that report on Anthropic's site, and you can open the dataset on Hugging Face right now.

Search for Anthropic scorpion toxin binder design, then look at the spreadsheet of sequences and the stickiness numbers from both labs.

You do not need to understand every column on the first pass.

Just notice that a computer's guesses became real molecules that real instruments could measure.

A protein is a long chain built from twenty kinds of building blocks called amino acids.

Picture those building blocks as beads on a friendship bracelet.

The order of the beads decides what shape the chain folds into, and the shape decides what job it can do.

Your phone already tries to finish your sentences when you text, based on what you typed and what usually comes next.

Claude did a version of that guessing game, except the "words" were amino acids, and the "sentence" was a whole protein.

It studied the shape of the scorpion toxin, then proposed huge numbers of bracelet patterns that might nest against that toxin like a puzzle piece.

Then it trimmed the list using rules about how amino acids like to sit together or keep apart, the way classmates pick seats at lunch.

That filter is similar to your phone refusing to suggest nonsense words.

None of the working designs existed in nature before.

The AI invented them on its own, then humans put the best candidates into actual lab equipment.

The ones that worked got short names such as Cn2-B1, like code names for new characters in a game.

The public spreadsheet lists each design's bead-by-bead sequence plus the binding measurements from both labs.

Binding just means sticking, the way two magnets click or fail to click.

So when someone says an AI designed a protein, you can picture a bracelet-maker who has seen countless bead patterns, studies one lock, and proposes new bracelets that might fit it.

The lab is still the boss.

The model suggests. The instruments decide.

Once you can see those two jobs as separate, the whole story feels a lot less like magic and a lot more like a very fast sketch artist handing ideas to a careful builder.

OpenAI is rolling out locked chats in ChatGPT, a way to move sensitive conversations into a hidden section protected by a four-digit PIN, or by your phone's fingerprint or Face ID.

A PIN is just a secret number lock, like the code that wakes your phone.

Once a chat is locked, it disappears from your normal history and from search results, so someone who borrows your phone will not stumble into it.

That is useful if you journal, draft a personal story, or talk about private topics with the AI.

Treat it like a diary with a clasp, sitting next to your ordinary notebooks.

The feature is available on free and paid accounts and is rolling out gradually, according to Tech Times.

Open ChatGPT, tap the three-dot menu on any chat, and look for the Lock option.

If it appears, you can move an existing chat, or start a new one inside the Locked chats area under Projects, which is a folder space inside the app.

The same PIN works across all your devices once you set it up, and phones that support it can add a face or fingerprint unlock.

Files you upload inside a locked chat stay protected too.

Try it with something low-stakes first, like a messy brainstorm you would not want on the main list, and notice how the thread vanishes from everyday view until you unlock it.

The updated ChatGPT Chrome extension can now read content from multiple open tabs at once.

A browser extension is a small add-on that lives in your browser and gives a site or an assistant extra powers.

Instead of copying and pasting like you are ferrying notes between rooms, you can ask it to pull details from several pages and organize them.

It can compare products, fill forms, or summarize what you are looking at, which is handy for school research, shopping, or planning a project.

Go to the Chrome Web Store, search for the official ChatGPT extension by OpenAI, and install it if you have not already.

Then open a few tabs on one topic and try a line like, compare the prices and shipping times on these pages.

Watch whether it actually uses details from more than one tab, not just the page in front of you.

The extension also adds a sidebar so you can keep chatting while you browse, and it can both answer questions from those tabs and take actions inside them.

Think of it as a study buddy who can see every textbook you already have open on the desk, instead of making you read each one aloud.
