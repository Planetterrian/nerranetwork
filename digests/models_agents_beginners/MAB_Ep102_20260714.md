# Models & Agents for Beginners
> **An AI just rebuilt a 2,700-year-old Greek ship list as a beautiful website — and caught real translation mistakes.**
Today we're looking at a creative AI experiment that turned an ancient poem into an interactive site while spotting errors humans missed. You'll see how AI can help explore history in fresh ways. We'll also break down one key idea behind how these tools actually work.
---
### The Big Story
Ethan Mollick tried something fun with an AI called Fable. He asked it to build a website showing the famous "Catalog of Ships" from Homer's Iliad — the long list of Greek warriors and their fleets heading to Troy. Fable created a clean, easy-to-browse site at catalogue-of-ships.netlify.app that looks polished and organized.

Think of it like asking a super-organized friend to turn a dusty old book chapter into a clickable museum display. The AI didn't just copy the text; it structured everything so you can explore different ships and leaders quickly. On top of that, Fable noticed two mistakes in an older English translation of the Greek by Samuel Butler, something even careful readers had missed for decades.

This matters because it shows how AI can become a helpful partner for creative and school projects. Instead of just summarizing homework, it can build shareable tools that make old stories feel alive again. A student studying mythology or history could use similar requests to turn boring reading lists into something visual and fun to share with classmates.

For you personally, it hints at future tools that could let anyone turn research into interactive maps or timelines without learning design software. Right now you can visit the site Mollick shared and click through the ships yourself to see the result. Then try the same idea with a story you like — open any chatbot and ask it to turn a short book passage into a simple webpage layout you can view in your browser. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're reading a long group chat, your brain keeps track of who said what and what the main topic is, even if people jump around?

Now picture an AI doing something similar but with every single word at once. It breaks the text into tiny pieces and checks how each piece connects to the ones before and after it, almost like drawing invisible lines between ideas. When the AI built the Iliad website, it used those connections to decide what should become a heading, what should be a list, and where a map or table would help.

The same process let it compare the old translation against the original Greek patterns it had learned. Instead of guessing, it spotted places where the English didn't line up with the structure it expected. That's why it could flag real mistakes instead of just making up new text.

The payoff is that these connection-drawing skills turn raw words into something you can actually click and explore.
