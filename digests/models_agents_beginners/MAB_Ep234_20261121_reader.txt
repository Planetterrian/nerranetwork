Gemini Neon, described in posts on X, is a new feature in the Gemini app that builds an app from a spoken description.

You say what you want out loud. The artificial intelligence, or AI, puts a real app on your screen, with buttons that do real things.

One request people are using is, make me an app that follows homework due dates and fires reminders.

It is close to describing a Minecraft house to someone who then places every block so the doors actually open.

The AI does not get tired, and it can make the app save your data or hook it up to other tools.

For a science fair or group presentation, you spend time on the idea, not hours learning to code a simple tool. Building your own study planner or a rough game becomes much faster to test.

A lot of jobs will soon mean saying what you want an AI to make, rather than writing every line yourself. Learning to explain your ideas clearly is the skill that counts most.

Open the Gemini app on your phone or laptop and start talking with your voice. Say build me a task list that lines up jobs by due date, and watch what it builds. You may need a free Google account to start, and you do not need any coding.

Get more specific and you can ask for an app that uses the weather to suggest things to do outside. Or one that sorts school-trip photos into a short slideshow.

Starting from your voice, even someone who has never written code can test whether an app idea is worth pursuing. Describe a problem you want solved, and the AI takes steps that once took weeks of study.

You still need to understand what the app actually does.

A chatbot, the AI you message back and forth, does not remember like a person, Analytics Insight explains. Threads can run for hundreds of messages.

Take a pop quiz that reprints every earlier answer, then adds one new question at the bottom.

You send a new line, and in the background the app copies the full old thread and stacks your new words on top. That rebuilt packet goes to the model, the AI that writes the reply, every single time.

The model is trained to find patterns in large amounts of text, so it can grab the useful bits without getting lost.

If the packet gets too thick to handle in one pass, the app may peel off the oldest pages or replace them with a short recap. That is why some chats slow down, or forget older details, after they run long.

When a reply uses something from ten messages ago, the model just took that reprinted test again. It keeps sending the full history in, so the AI has what you would have after reading the chat from the start.

The whole trick is that reprint. Once you see it, the long memory stops feeling like a superpower.

OpenAI, as Engadget reports, opened shared workspaces called Spaces to regular users.

You drop in documents, notes, or files, then invite classmates or friends to work in the same chat. Everyone sees the same AI helper and can toss in ideas at the same time.

Treat it like a shared class folder that can also answer questions about whatever you put inside.

Try this as a mini challenge. Go to the ChatGPT website, look for Spaces in the sidebar, and create a new one.

Upload a homework sheet or a story you are writing, invite someone to join, and ask the AI questions together. Notice how every file and every AI reply stays in one place, so nobody hunts through separate chats or forwarded messages.

When someone adds a new file, the AI can use it right away to answer the whole group.

That helps when teammates live in different time zones, or when a writing group wants to brainstorm without losing earlier ideas.

Everyone gets answers based on the same documents, so one person does not get different suggestions than another. The feature is aimed at Plus users, which is a paid plan, so you may need a parent's help if you are under the signup age.

Once you are inside, the steps stay simple.

Google's search homepage now shows your past AI chats on the side, Ars Technica notes, and the old search button is gone. You can see earlier conversations while you search the web as usual, so jumping between search and an AI chat feels smooth. Switching is closer to flipping tabs than opening a blank page.
