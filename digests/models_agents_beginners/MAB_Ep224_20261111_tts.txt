An A I model is software that predicts what should come next, like autocomplete on your phone, except it has gotten good enough to gather facts and argue through a problem.

The challenge was the Fermi Paradox, the puzzle of why we have found no signs of alien life even though the universe is enormous and old.

He asked Fable five point two and Gemini Carbon to gather data, test possible explanations, pressure-test those ideas against tough critics, and give likely answers if they could not reach a definite conclusion.

Pressure-test just means trying to break your own idea on purpose, the way you double-check a math answer by working it backwards.

The models did not solve the paradox completely.

They gathered real data from astronomy and biology, then built several possible explanations, such as civilizations being too far apart, or civilizations not lasting long enough.

Picture a huge party where you sent invitations weeks ago, the whole neighborhood should have shown up, and the door never opens. That empty-room feeling is the Fermi Paradox in everyday clothes.

The A I S treated each explanation the way a detective treats a suspect list. They asked what evidence would knock a suspect out, then looked for that evidence.

You know that slow dread when your phone dies by lunch, and you start listing reasons. Maybe the screen stayed on. Maybe one game drank the battery. Maybe the charger is quietly failing. You do not stop at the first guess. You check each reason against what actually happened.

That same habit, stretched across a much bigger mystery, is how these models worked the alien question. They did not blurt one clever sentence and freeze.

First they built several possible explanations. Next they asked what would disprove each idea, then hunted for evidence that could break the guess.

If one explanation survived the checks better than the others, it got ranked higher. The weaker ones stayed on the page anyway, so you could compare them yourself, like keeping every draft of an essay instead of shredding the messy ones.

The key move is the loop. The model says here is my claim, here is why it might be wrong, here is new data that tests it, then it goes around again.

That loop is what turns simple autocomplete into something that can explore an open mystery, including ideas like hidden signals set against real observations.

Because every pass stays visible, a beginner can follow the chain and see where the reasoning holds up or cracks. The result feels less like a magic trick and more like a careful notebook someone else filled in for you to review.

You still pick which answers feel right. Once you can see the loop, the giant question stops looking like a locked door.
