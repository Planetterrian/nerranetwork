### The Big Story
A major newspaper just published a clear guide for people who have never thought about using AI agents before. The article walks readers through the basics of these tools even if the idea has never crossed their mind. An AI agent is like a digital helper that can take a goal you give it and break it into steps, then use tools such as web browsers or apps to get the job done. Think of it like asking a really organized friend to plan your whole weekend: instead of just suggesting ideas, the friend books the tickets, checks the weather, and texts you reminders. This matters because agents are moving from “chat with a robot” to “the robot actually handles parts of your homework, creative projects, or daily errands.” For students, that could mean an agent helping organize research for a paper or pulling together study notes from different websites. For anyone curious about future jobs, learning to work with these helpers now is like learning to use a smartphone when they first came out. The guide explains that you don’t need special skills to start—you can begin with the same chatbots you already know, just by giving them clearer, multi-step instructions. You can try this right now by opening ChatGPT or Claude on your phone or laptop and typing a request like “Help me plan a simple study schedule for my next three tests, including short breaks and what to review each day.” The more specific you are about what you want done, the better the agent works. The article also points out that agents can feel overwhelming at first because they seem to act on their own, but the key is starting small with one clear goal at a time. Once you see an agent complete even a simple task like gathering links for a school project, the idea stops feeling mysterious and starts feeling useful. Many readers who have never tried agents before will find the step-by-step approach reassuring because it shows you can experiment safely without breaking anything. The piece emphasizes that the real power comes from learning how to give instructions that match what you actually need, rather than expecting the agent to read your mind. Over time, this skill of directing an agent becomes as normal as searching the web or sending a text. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you’re playing a video game and you give your character a quest, the game doesn’t just stand there—it figures out the path, uses items from your inventory, and keeps going until the quest is complete? Now picture that same idea, but the “character” is an AI and the “game world” is the internet plus apps on your phone. The AI gets a goal from you, decides which tools it needs (maybe searching the web or opening a calendar), takes the first step, checks what happened, and keeps going until the goal is reached or it needs to ask you something. That loop of “decide, act, check, repeat” is what makes an agent different from a regular chatbot that only answers one question and stops. The reason this feels new is that earlier chatbots waited for every single instruction, while agents are built to keep working on their own for a while. Once you see it that way, the whole idea stops feeling like magic and starts feeling like a very fast, very patient digital teammate. The process works because the agent keeps a running record of what it has already tried, so it does not repeat mistakes or ask you the same question twice. It can also switch between different tools without you having to tell it every time, which is why the same agent can look up information, write a short summary, and then save that summary in a note for later. The important part is that the agent only continues as long as it has a clear next step; if something goes wrong or the goal becomes unclear, it pauses and waits for your input instead of guessing. This back-and-forth between action and checking results is what turns a simple chatbot into something that can handle longer jobs like planning a weekend or organizing study materials. The more you watch an agent work through its steps, the easier it becomes to understand why people describe it as a teammate rather than just another search box.

**OpenAI’s new “dots” that stay on even when you close the app**
OpenAI turned some of its older coding tools into a new always-on helper called dots. It runs in the background and can keep working on things you started earlier, like research or writing projects. This is cool because most chatbots forget everything the moment you close the tab, but dots is designed to feel more like a persistent assistant that remembers where you left off. You can try it by going to the ChatGPT website or app and looking for the dots feature if it’s available in your region—start by asking it to keep track of a simple ongoing task like “help me brainstorm ideas for my next school project every day this week.” The feature comes from turning existing coding tools into something that stays active across different sessions, so you do not have to repeat instructions every time you open the app. Because it was built from tools that already helped with code, dots can handle structured tasks like keeping lists or tracking progress without losing context. The executive behind the project has said he never wants to meet someone else’s AI agent, which shows how much thought went into making this one feel reliable and personal. Once dots is running, you can check back later in the day and see what it has added to your project without having to restart the conversation from scratch. This background memory is what makes the tool feel more like a real helper than a one-time chat.
