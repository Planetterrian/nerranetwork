PAN 2, which Linus Ekenstam posted on X, is a free open-source world model.

Open-source means anyone can use it and inspect how it works.

A world model is an AI that builds a whole scene, then predicts how that scene changes when you act, like turning the wheel or hitting the brakes.

Think of it like a racing game that draws the next second of street after you turn the wheel.

It is not loading a track someone already built.

One clip shows driving through what feels like Miami, with handling on wet surfaces that matches real streets.

Your brain already guesses the landing when a skateboarder leaves a ramp in a YouTube clip.

World models do a similar kind of guessing, except they work with pictures instead of words.

They look at the current scene and break it into tiny pieces of information about where everything is and how fast things are moving.

Then they predict the most likely next frame, meaning the next still picture in the sequence.

First, the AI studies thousands of real driving videos and learns basic rules.

Cars usually stop at red lights, and wet roads make things slide.

Next, you give it a new starting scene.

It generates what comes after by checking those learned rules against the exact positions and speeds in that moment.

If something unexpected happens, like a sudden crash, it adjusts the prediction on the fly.

It uses the same pattern-matching, looking for the closest match to situations it has seen.

It does not store every possible outcome in advance, and it is not copying old footage.

It rebuilds each new frame from scratch using the patterns it has seen before.

That is why the boxes fall differently every time depending on exactly where the car hits them.

Smooth, believable motion is just that guess, rebuilt again and again from the rules.

So the trick has a plain name now.

It is next-frame guessing, and you have been doing a version of it every time you watch a clip.

The original Super Nintendo from nineteen ninety, Tom's Hardware reports, just got an upgrade that lets it run a tiny version of ChatGPT.

Researchers used a special add-on chip called the Super FX 2 to speed the old hardware enough for simple AI conversations.

The chip runs at twenty-one megahertz, a measure of how fast it ticks.

It uses RISC, a stripped-down chip design that does fewer kinds of jobs but does them quickly.

That is enough to run a two point two million parameter chatbot.

Parameters are the little settings that shape its answers, and this tiny model is still fast enough for real replies.
