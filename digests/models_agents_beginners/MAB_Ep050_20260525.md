### The Big Story
Google just showed off a new version of its Gemini AI that can take almost any kind of input and turn it into video. Instead of just answering questions in text, this model can create moving pictures from photos, drawings, or even simple descriptions. Think of it like having a magic camera that doesn't just capture what you point it at — it can invent entire scenes based on whatever you give it. The journalist who tested it used the tool to make videos of his kid's stuffed deer going on vacation, copying a style from a Google ad. This works because the model connects different types of information, like images and motion, in one system instead of needing separate tools for each step. For students and creators, this changes things because you no longer need expensive software or years of training to make short videos for school projects or fun posts. It could let anyone turn a doodle into an animation or bring old photos to life without hiring help. The personal side is that tools like this lower the barrier for trying creative ideas right now, even if the results still have limits like weird movements or short clips. You can already experiment with similar features in the free Gemini app on your phone or at gemini.google.com by uploading an image and asking it to create a short video clip from it. Start simple: upload a photo of something in your room and type "make this object dance." Source: [theverge.com](https://www.theverge.com/tech/936507/gemini-omni-hands-on-deepfake-ai-video)

### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're doing a math problem in class, sometimes you just guess the answer quickly and sometimes you actually write out every step so you catch mistakes? Now imagine an AI that can do the same thing — it has a fast mode that just blurts out an answer and a slower "thinking" mode that shows its work. The fast mode is like rushing through homework and accidentally writing that France and Germany have totally different average heights when the data was actually identical. The thinking mode is like slowing down, checking the numbers twice, and realizing the labels were just swapped to trick it. That's basically what happens when you leave an AI tool on its default setting instead of switching to a thinking version. The default often grabs patterns it has seen before, like stereotypes about countries, and applies them even when the facts don't match. Switching modes makes the AI pause and reason more carefully, the same way showing your work helps you spot errors before turning in the paper. So next time someone says "model selection," you can tell them it's basically choosing whether the AI rushes or takes its time. Not so scary, right? Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/why-you-shouldnt-leave-model-selection-on-default-in-copilot-gemini-and-other-ai-tools)

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Robots Cooking Meals for People Who Need Them:** Wired
A nonprofit in San Francisco's Tenderloin neighborhood started using robots to prepare meals because they didn't have enough human volunteers. The robots handle chopping, mixing, and portioning food so the group can still serve hot meals every day. This is cool because it shows AI and robots stepping in for real community work instead of just making art or chatting. Anyone interested in how technology helps people can follow updates from groups like this one. You can learn more by searching for the Wired article on robots in the Tenderloin and watching any linked videos of the machines in action.
