GPT-4 is a chat program from the company OpenAI that writes, answers questions, and helps with schoolwork.

For a full year it lived only in the cloud, which means it ran on huge distant computers you reach through the internet.

Ethan Mollick, who has tracked these programs for years, noted that GPT-4 held the top spot that whole time before smaller versions caught up.

The new ones are small enough to sit on a regular phone, and they still handle those same writing and question tasks.

A giant shared computer in a library is the old picture of this, the machine you had to visit and wait your turn to use.

What you carry now is closer to a super-smart notebook that works at your desk, on the bus, or with the wifi switched off.

You no longer need fast internet or a paid subscription just to get strong help with homework, story writing, or game ideas.

A student who wants instant feedback on an essay, or a quick coding hint, can get it without waiting on a faraway server.

There is no extra data cost when the answers are born on the phone itself.

Your questions and writing, sometimes called prompts, stay on the device instead of traveling to distant servers.

That can feel more private for everyday school or creative work, the way a locked diary differs from a letter you drop in the mail.

The helper also keeps working in places with spotty internet, even when the connection drops.

Open one of the free chat versions for a few minutes, and notice how quickly the answers arrive compared with tools that only live in the cloud.

Engineers are teaching a robot hand to spin a pen the way a kid learns to juggle.

Riding a bike is the closest everyday version of the same idea.

You wobble, you tip, you adjust your balance the next time, and slowly the corrections get smaller.

The robot hand practices that loop thousands of times inside a computer game before it ever touches a real object.

Engineers build a pretend version of the hand inside a simulator, which is a practice field made of math instead of metal.

In that pretend world, the hand tries spinning a pen or turning a cube over and over.

Every time it drops the object, the program gives it a low score, like missing a jump in a video game.

Every time it succeeds, the score goes up, so the good finger movements get practiced more.

After millions of these tiny experiments, the hand figures out which motions work best.

Those instructions are then copied onto the real metal-and-plastic hand, the way you might learn a dance in a game and then step it out on a real floor.

The physical hand can copy the moves without ever having practiced in the real world first.

It still makes small corrections on its own, the same way you lean left or right while the bike is already rolling.

That whole process is called reinforcement learning, which just means learning from a score instead of from a teacher spelling out every step.

It is also why the hand can do delicate tricks instead of only opening and closing.

The hand has twenty independently moving joints, like twenty tiny hinges that each get their own little decision.

The training lets it copy human motions captured by a camera or a special glove.

The open-source code for this setup is available, so anyone curious can look at how the pretend practice connects to the real hardware.

Meta released two fresh chat models called Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, The Verge reports.

They add something called active memory and active focus.

Active memory is the model remembering what you said earlier in the same chat, like a classmate who still has your first sentence ten minutes later.

Active focus is the model staying on the topic even when the conversation gets long, instead of wandering off the group project and into lunch plans.

The models are free to try on Meta's platforms right now, and they aim to match the strongest current systems while running on Meta's own services.

That gives you another free option beside paid tools, especially for longer creative work like writing stories or outlining school reports.

Open Meta's AI website in any browser, start a new chat, and ask it to help you plan a weekend trip.

Midway through, change the destination, and watch whether it keeps your original plans without you repeating everything.
