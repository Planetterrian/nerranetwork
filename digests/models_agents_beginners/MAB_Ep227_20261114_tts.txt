Someone with no coding history at all decided in August, a Reddit post says, to make a racing game.

He asked Chat G P T, the chatbot you type questions into, for help. It pointed him to Godot, a free game engine that works on computers and phones.

Think of Godot as a giant box of LEGO made for video games. You snap pieces together instead of carving every brick by hand.

Across the next two months, weekend tinkering became a daily habit that now takes six to eight hours after work.

The coastline alone ate two full days of a loop that feels like art class. He would launch a round, say which bit looked off, receive fresh code, and try again.

The water uses shaders, which are tiny recipes that tell every pixel how light should sit on a surface, like a makeup tutorial for the ocean.

Foam is painted on with generated textures, the wrapping paper that sits on top of a shape. Splash motion was built in Blender, a free tool for making three D movement, then imported into Godot as moving textures.

He first tried a recording of a real engine, and it felt off inside the game, so Chat G P T helped him invent a custom synth. A synth is like a keyboard that invents a noise from scratch, note by note, instead of playing a tape of a real motor.

Background audio came from ElevenLabs or Adobe Firefly, which are tools that can invent voices, whooshes, and room tone from a description.

The vehicle is a ready-made stock model that carries a free license, meaning anyone is allowed to use that three D car. He built buildings and props from typed descriptions or from pictures the A I drew as references.

A few scenery pieces arrived as full three D models from Tripo A I. Codex, an A I helper for code and files, then helped slim down both the meshes and textures so Godot could run them.

Meshes are the chicken-wire frames under a three D object. Slimming them is like shrinking a stuffed backpack until a laptop can carry it.

He began on G P T five point six Sol with a twenty dollar plan, then in November switched to G P T six Astra with a five hundred dollar plan.

He tests on an M one Pro MacBook Pro and an iPhone fifteen Pro, building both at once because the phone build has to run leaner. That is like mixing the same song twice, once for stadium speakers and once for tiny earbuds.

Attention sits on steering feel, how the car bumps into the world, and whether the look and the sound work together while moving. He plans sprints, circuit races, and rivals, and a story once those modes actually run.

A police chase comes first. Making a real game used to mean a long education or a crew, and that wall is a lot lower now.

If a homemade game has ever been on your wish list, this is proof you can start. Go to the Chat G P T website, start a new chat, and type that you have never coded before.

Researchers at Anthropic, writing on the company's Engineering Blog, put out ten practical rules for building safety checks on artificial intelligence, or A I.

Unlocking your phone with your face is a tiny gate. The phone is checking that the right person is about to do something that matters.

These rules are a much bigger version of that gate. Instead of guarding one phone, they guard against a model that might give bad advice or cause harm.

This part is a little tricky, but the simple version is enough. People who know the topic and people who know the machinery should both help write the tests, not one group alone.

That is like a group project where the history student and the science student both help write the quiz, so it is not just one person's blind spot.

Spell out the exact thing you are measuring before any test begins. The post borrows from psychology's replication reforms from a decade ago, including preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans.

A hypothesis is the guess you are checking. An analysis plan is how you will score the results. Preregistration means you show that plan first, like handing your teacher the quiz questions before anyone takes the test, so you cannot quietly change the rules after you see the answers.

A test should not fall over just because someone rephrases the question. Picture a student who memorizes the answer key's phrasing instead of actually learning the material.

Run the check more than one way, since scores can shift when items are multiple-choice versus open-ended. Same pop quiz, two formats. Bubble sheets can hide a guess. A blank page shows whether the idea is really there.

Clear proof should exist, before a new model ships, that it will not help a person harm themselves or other people. The evidence sits in the open, rather than a company simply asking the world to believe it.

Once you see the steps, a safety test stops feeling like secret lab magic and starts feeling like a checklist you could explain to a classmate.

Praxis one, an open-source model from Runway, was trained on millions of videos and can now control robots, The Decoder reports.

Open-source here means the recipe is public. Runway released open weights and source code under the Apache two point zero license, a permission slip that lets anyone use, share, and build on it.

Weights are the millions of little knobs the model learned, like the muscle memory in a dancer's body after watching a million clips.

It uses knowledge from video training for robot control and leads the World Action Model category on Nvidia's RoboLab one hundred twenty benchmark. A benchmark is a scored obstacle course. World Action Model means A I that turns what it sees into a physical next step.

It cannot yet plan complex action sequences on its own. Instead of writing thousands of lines of robot code, the model uses what it learned from watching things move to tell a robot arm or body what to do next.

That is learning a dance from YouTube, then your arms copy the next move, without yet choreographing a whole recital. It is free to download if you have a decent computer, and it is one path from tools that make cool videos to tools that can help robots do real tasks.

Go to Hugging Face, a site where people share A I models, search for Praxis one, and scroll the examples until you see a robot arm stacking blocks after only watching video.

Open A I launched Chat G P T Wallet on November thirteenth, two thousand twenty-six, so special agents inside Chat G P T can finish purchases after you approve them with Face ID or a fingerprint.

An agent here is a helper that can take steps for you, not just answer a question. Face ID is that face scan many iPhones already use as a lock, and a fingerprint works the same way if that is how your phone unlocks.

The feature is available for Chat G P T Pro subscribers, the paid higher tier, in the United States who hold eligible United States issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard, or Visa cards.

The A I never receives your card details, and neither does the store. After you approve, the payment network mints a stand-in with its own card number, security code, and expiration date, then charges that stand-in to your card.

Think of it as a one-time gift card number that pretends to be your real card, so the shop never sees the original.

Open A I built the feature with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa. At checkout, Chat G P T Wallet shows the merchant, the items, the total, and the selected card. Users keep existing card rewards and dispute protections for eligible claims.

Right now it is only for Chat G P T Pro subscribers in the United States. Open A I plans to let users delegate purchases to an agent in a future version that would use spending limits, purchase categories, and time periods.
