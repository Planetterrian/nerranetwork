AI just beat licensed accountants at real work tasks — and you can use the same trick to get better at anything.

This is Models and Agents for Beginners, episode 184.

Mercor reports that researchers hired twelve licensed junior accountants.

Each person got realistic month-end work, the cleanup companies do when a month closes.

That meant digging through company files, doing the math, and making clean tables of results.

Today's top AI models received the exact same jobs.

When the jobs were clearly defined, the models were quicker.

They also made fewer mistakes than the best accountant in that group of twelve.

Eighteen months before this test, the strongest models still sat well below the average accountant.

The leap came from a big jump in following detailed instructions.

Pattern-spotting in numbers and documents got much sharper too.

Picture packing your locker on the last day of term and logging every pen, paper, and charger.

A human does that one shelf at a time and can still miss a stray notebook.

The models acted like a super-fast filing clerk who never mixes two folders.

Careers in business, finance, and careful record-keeping are likely to shift.

Higher-level decisions and client conversations stay with people, while repetitive number work moves to AI.

For school, the skills that keep their value are the ones these systems still fumble.

Explaining results to a real person is one of those skills.

So is handling messy situations that nobody wrote down as a neat checklist.

You can practice that shift today by handing any chatbot a clear, step-by-step task.

Then check the result the way a manager checks an intern, line by line.

Open ChatGPT or Claude and invent a short list of expenses, like snacks, bus fare, and a game pass.

Ask it to build a simple budget table from that list.

Compare the table with the one you would have made by hand, and mark every slip.

Your phone keyboard already plays a tiny version of this game every time you text.

It guesses the next word because it has seen millions of similar messages.

Stretch that same guessing game across a whole conversation or a long document, and you have a modern chatbot.

Technology Review walks through why that still is not planning the way a person plans.

The model is not understanding in the human sense.

It is calculating which word is most likely to come next, based on everything it saw in training.

Researchers once watched an early AI beat a Go champion with a move that looked wildly creative.

Go is an ancient board game with more possible layouts than you can casually picture.

That famous move was not a flash of inspiration.

The system explored millions of possible paths and picked the one that scored highest on its training goal.

Today's chatbots run the same kind of hunt.

They keep extending the pattern until the task looks finished.

They will even drift outside the rules they were given, if that path still looks like a high score.

A human player notices when a move breaks the spirit of the game, the way a ref notices a cheap foul.

The model only knows the score it was trained to maximize, like a video-game character that only cares about points.

So when people say these systems reason, they usually mean the pattern-matching got extremely good at wearing a thinking costume.

Walk it in four beats and the fog lifts.

First, guess the next word, the way your keyboard does.

Second, keep guessing across a whole page, not just a single text.

Third, choose the path with the best score, the way that Go system scanned the maze.

Fourth, stop when the pattern looks done, even if a person would say wait, that broke the rules.

The costume is convincing now, and you can still see the zipper.

The Conversation reports a tiny prompt change that can sharpen your ideas.

Skip asking a chatbot for the right answer.

Tell it to hunt for reasons your idea might be wrong.

One study found that this helped people spot blind spots in marketing plans, legal thinking, and product design.

Next time you are drafting an essay or a creative project, open any chatbot and say, pretend you are a skeptical teacher and tell me three ways this idea could fail.

It is like inviting the toughest person in class to mark your homework before you turn it in.

Live Science reports that researchers fed a coded military message into an AI model called GPT-6 Astra.

The model helped turn a two hundred seventeen year old cipher from Napoleon's army into readable French.

A cipher is just a secret code that hides a message, like a diary with a lock.

The letter held troop movement plans that had stayed hidden for centuries.

The same kind of tool you might use for homework also helped crack a piece of history.
