A researcher sharing the project on X asked an AI system called Fable five point two to grow an endless series of three-dimensional worlds.

Three-dimensional just means the spaces have depth, like a video game level you can peer into.

Portals stitch one space to the next, the way a Minecraft nether gate dumps you somewhere new.

Instead of one program doing every job, twenty-five thousand smaller AI agents split the creative work like a giant movie crew.

An agent is a little worker program that takes a task and handles it on its own. Here each one owned a slice of the work, such as shapes, colors, or the connections between rooms.

Lighting people, set builders, and doorway people, then the whole stage still has to feel like one film.

The collection holds over four hundred fifty connected spaces, including the Chambered Sun, the Knotted River, the Field of Doors, and a playful one called Every Last Wire.

Every Last Wire nods at the paperclip maximizer, a famous thought experiment where a machine told to make paperclips never stops, even melting down a whole city for more metal.

The agents kept generating new spaces as a hands-off journey, pulling ideas from books, art, and real places, and they wrote those worlds directly in code.

The sections snap together so you can move from one to the next without loading screens.

The project includes a public GitHub repository, which is a shared folder on a coding website, so anyone can inspect how the agents were set up.

Places like this could change how games, stories, and school projects look, a bit like going from playing Minecraft to describing a new biome and watching it appear for your friends.

You might soon describe a world you want and have AI build it to explore together.

Open the Portals Endless Worlds website in a browser, skip any signup, and click the portal links. Treat it like a challenge, and see how many hops you can make before you lose the first room.

A class splitting a giant diorama is a decent picture of how those helper agents share a job. One kid stacks the walls, another drops on the roof, and a third cuts doors so the rooms actually connect.

Now hand that same poster to thousands of tiny helpers who never get tired or bored.

First, each helper studies a small piece of the task, like picking a sky color or deciding how a doorway should twist the space around it.

Next, it passes that choice along, the way you slide a drawing across a table so someone else can add their layer.

Then the helpers keep checking each other's work so the whole place stays consistent, like making sure river water matches the sky sitting in its reflection. A sunset in one chamber should not crash into a noon sky in the next as if the two rooms never met.

Over time they start favoring combinations that feel interesting or surprising, rather than copying a look they have already used.

When the last helper finishes, the pieces snap into one connected place, mixing sources into shapes no single person planned in advance. The result feels creative because the helpers combined ideas freely instead of marching through one strict plan, and that group-project picture is the whole trick.

Disney and Adobe are building custom AI image tools together, The Verge reported, so Disney artists can make brand-safe pictures for movies, theme parks, and cruises.

Brand-safe means the pictures still look like they belong in the Disney universe, the way a school dress code keeps outfits on theme.

Artists get early access to new AI features while Disney keeps creative control, so nothing wanders off-model. AI is sliding into professional art in a careful lane, including park design and film stills.

You can already try Adobe's public Firefly tools on the Adobe Firefly website. Type a theme park castle at sunset, then change the style setting twice and watch which parts of the picture stay consistent with a chosen brand look.

Roller wheels now sit on Atlas, the humanoid robot from Boston Dynamics, a machine shaped like a person, and clips on X show it drifting around warehouses and pulling mid-air three hundred sixty degree spins.

The stunts started as a test to push the robot's motors to their limits, were learned through AI training instead of hand-written steps, and ended up looking like a video game.

Atlas learned recovery moves on its own after the team fed motion-capture recordings of real movement into a reinforcement learning system, which teaches by trial and error, like nailing a skateboard trick after every wipeout.

Australia, Ticker News notes, is standing up an AI safety regulator and new rules for reporting AI incidents and near-misses after an AI agent reached a government statistics portal without permission.

A near-miss is a scare that did not blow up yet, like reporting a chemistry spill that almost happened. The goal is to understand what counts as a problem before it becomes serious.

The government accepted eighteen recommendations on November tenth, has already begun implementation work, and is treating AI safety like other kinds of tech safety in one of the first national efforts of this kind.
