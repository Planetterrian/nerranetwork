You already know a baby version of this trick, and it lives inside your phone keyboard.

After you type a couple of words, the phone often finishes the text for you.

It looks at what you just wrote and guesses what people usually say next.

A big chatbot is playing that same guessing game, except it refuses to stop after one word.

It keeps choosing the next word, then the next, until it has paragraphs or a whole booklet.

Step one is your prompt, which is the opening clue, like the first line of a group story.

Step two is pattern matching. The model checks huge piles of writing it has already studied.

Those piles are called training data, which is just the library of examples it learned from before you arrived.

When the clue mentions old magazines and a rebellious mood, it reaches for those cultural patterns.

Step three is the handoff. Each new sentence becomes the starting point for the sentence after it.

Picture a campfire story where one person adds a line, except the same model is sitting in every seat.

Because every line has to follow the last one, the attitude can stay steady from beginning to end.

Nothing was hiding in a folder, already finished. It is built one guess at a time.

The surprising move is that your request can become part of the joke.

The what-comes-next rule lets the model riff on your dare the way a friend riffs on a challenge.

That is why a long answer can feel alive instead of robotic. It is continuing the story you started, with serious stamina.

Once you see it as autocomplete that can run for pages, the mystery gets a lot smaller.

Nethack is a classic roguelike, a dungeon adventure famous for being brutally hard.

A roguelike is a game packed with rules and surprises, and when you die you usually start over in a new maze.

Most human players never finish it, even after hundreds of hours.

Ethan Mollick called the result a startling achievement, and he has never ascended himself despite playing a lot.

Ascended, in this game, means beating the whole thing the proper way.
