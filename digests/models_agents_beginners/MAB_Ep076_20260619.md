### The Big Story
Adobe has started testing new AI assistants inside five of its biggest creative programs: Photoshop for photos, Premiere for video, Illustrator for drawings, InDesign for layouts, and Frame.io for video review. Instead of hunting through menus, you can now type what you want in plain words—like "make the background blur" or "cut out the shaky parts"—and the AI tries to do it right inside the app you already use. Think of it like having a helpful friend sitting next to you who knows every button and shortcut, except this friend never gets tired and can suggest ideas you might not have thought of. The assistants also remember your recent work so they can keep helping across different projects without you repeating yourself. For students making posters, editing TikTok-style videos, or designing yearbook pages, this lowers the learning curve that used to stop a lot of people from finishing creative work. It also opens doors for future careers in design or media because the tools that professionals use are becoming easier to start with. You might feel more confident trying digital art or video editing knowing the AI can guide you step by step. Right now the feature is in public beta, so anyone with an Adobe Creative Cloud account can join the test by opening one of the apps and looking for the new AI Assistant panel. If you don't have an account yet, the free web versions of some Adobe Express tools give a taste of similar AI features you can try immediately. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're texting a friend, your phone sometimes suggests the next word, and if you keep accepting those suggestions it starts to sound more like you? Now imagine researchers do the same thing but with an entire AI model: they show it thousands of examples of helpful, honest answers in one area—like health questions—and reward it every time it stays truthful and safe. Next they check whether that good behavior carries over when the AI faces completely different topics it was never trained on, such as math or creative writing. The surprising part is that it often does carry over, almost like the AI learned a general habit of being careful instead of just memorizing one subject. This is called cross-domain transfer, and it works because the training focuses on the underlying pattern of "be helpful without lying or causing harm" rather than the specific words. So when someone says "alignment training," you can picture it as teaching the AI good habits in one small area and watching those habits spread to everything else it does. Not so scary once you see it as an upgraded version of your phone's autocomplete learning your style. Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/openai-researchers-show-small-doses-of-beneficial-trait-training-make-ai-models-broadly-safer-and-harder-to-manipulate/)

### Cool Stuff & Try This
**Check if AI already knows your favorite celebrity**
A new free website called "In the Weights" lets you search any person's name and see how strongly different AI models remember them from their training data. It gives a simple strength score so you can compare, for example, whether Taylor Swift is remembered more deeply than a lesser-known athlete. This is cool because it shows that AI isn't magic—it literally stores patterns from the internet it was trained on. Anyone curious about privacy or how AI "remembers" things should try it. Go to the site, type in a name like "Mozart" or "your favorite YouTuber," and look at the score. You can even compare two people side by side to see whose information is more deeply embedded. Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/website-in-the-weights-shows-whether-ai-models-know-who-you-are/)

### Quick Bits
**Robodogs learning to fetch with AI help**
Anthropic just shared videos of robot dogs being programmed by Claude to pick up objects. The AI wrote the instructions much faster than human teams did last year, even though the dogs still need practice. It's a fun peek at how AI might one day help control real robots around the house or in schools. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2067651700757086553)

**OpenAI testing whether good behavior sticks**
Researchers trained AI models to be truthful and helpful in one narrow area and then checked if that behavior appeared in unrelated tasks. Early results show the models became harder to trick into saying harmful things while still answering normal questions. This matters because it could make future chatbots safer for everyone to use. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2067722693714338044)
