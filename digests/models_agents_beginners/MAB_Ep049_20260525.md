> **AI just diagnosed millions of people with a disease that doesn't exist — here's why that matters to you.**
Researchers planted a completely fake illness online just to test whether AI would repeat it, and the results were eye-opening. This story shows how quickly wrong information can spread through chatbots millions of people use for health questions every day. We'll also look at a surprising new way scientists are making AI more accurate and why robots sorting packages nonstop is a bigger deal than it sounds.
---
### The Big Story
A Swedish researcher created a completely made-up eye condition called Bixonimania, complete with fake research papers and silly-sounding details, then posted it online to see what would happen. AI chatbots from companies like OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft quickly started telling users that Bixonimania was real, giving them made-up statistics and even advising them to see a doctor. Think of the internet like a giant library where anyone can add a book — except now the librarians (the AI systems) are reading every book without checking if it's true first. The experiment worked because the AI models simply absorbed the fake papers and repeated the information as if it were fact. This matters because over 40 million people turn to tools like ChatGPT for health questions daily, and nearly half of the answers those tools give can be misleading. If you're a student researching a project or a parent checking symptoms for a family member, you could get confident-sounding but completely wrong advice. The bigger issue is that once false information gets into the system, it can even sneak into real medical journals before anyone catches it. For you personally, this means treating any medical answer from an AI as a starting point for real research, not the final word. You can try this right now by asking ChatGPT or Gemini a simple health question you already know the answer to and then double-checking the sources it gives you against a trusted site like the CDC or Mayo Clinic. ### Explain Like I'm 14
You know how when you're texting with friends, sometimes one person repeats a rumor that started as a joke and suddenly everyone treats it like fact? Now imagine that same game of telephone, but instead of a few friends, it's happening with millions of pages of information scraped from the internet. The AI reads everything without a built-in "is this obviously nonsense?" filter, so it can pick up a ridiculous made-up story and present it with total confidence. Next it adds details that sound scientific because those details appeared near the original fake text. And that's basically what happened with the fake disease experiment — the models treated nonsense papers the same way they treat real ones. So next time someone says "AI hallucinated," you can tell them it's basically the world's fastest rumor mill that never learned to fact-check. Source: [the-decoder.com](https://the-decoder.com/ai-models-often-give-the-right-answers-but-point-to-the-wrong-sources/)

### Quick Bits
**Quantum computers just helped an AI get smarter with fewer changes**
Scientists ran an AI model on an IBM quantum computer and found it could answer questions correctly that the regular version got wrong. The improvement came from adding only a small number of extra settings rather than rebuilding the whole system. This hints that future AI might get better by borrowing power from quantum machines instead of just getting bigger. Source: [livescience.com](https://www.livescience.com/technology/quantum/scientists-trained-an-ai-model-using-an-ibm-quantum-computer-and-it-answered-questions-correctly-that-the-base-model-couldnt)

**Robots worked 24/7 sorting packages for over a week with no breaks**
Figure AI streamed their robots doing real warehouse work nonstop for eight days, proving these machines can handle long, repetitive tasks without constant human supervision. The livestream showed steady, practical movement instead of short flashy demos. It's a sign that robots are moving from science experiments toward tools that could one day help in factories, stores, or even homes.
