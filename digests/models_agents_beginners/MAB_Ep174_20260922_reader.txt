And that same “start small, share everything” spirit showed up in a totally different corner this week.

Someone spent free time training a tiny language model from scratch on Arabic text and dialects, then shared the whole project.

A language model is software that learns patterns in words so it can predict what should come next.

This one is called Horus Taleeq zero point two B Base, and it has zero point two billion parameters.

Parameters are the tiny knobs the model tunes as it learns, like millions of little volume dials.

The whole first training run cost just over one hundred dollars, about the price of a video game.

It ran for about thirty hours on one consumer graphics chip.

That chip was a single NVIDIA RTX PRO six thousand Blackwell Server Edition GPU, which stands for graphics processing unit, the kind of processor that also trains AI.

It learned from six billion training tokens.

Tokens are little chunks of text, like puzzle pieces of language the model reads one after another.

The creator stopped at pretraining, which is the first big reading stage, then released the model, the data sources, and the code.

Everything went out under the MIT License, a permission style that lets others study and reuse the work for learning.

If you are curious about languages or how models learn, go to the TokenAI site, search for Horus Taleeq zero point two B Base, and read the training details and data sources.

Notice how small the budget is next to the usual giant-lab story.

Creators who think in shapes got a new helper too.

Hi3D just released an agent built specifically for making three-dimensional models.

An agent here is software that can take steps and use tools, not just chat back.

It pairs a language model with three-dimensional tools, so you describe what you want, then refine geometry and textures.

You can even turn the result into something usable in games or three-dimensional printing.

It is aimed at people who want to go from idea to finished object without learning every button in complex software.

The agent includes reusable skills for optimization, animation, and turning high-poly models into playable demos.

High-poly just means a model with tons of tiny surfaces, like a statue with every wrinkle, which is too heavy for a game until you simplify it.

There is a Windows client, and the workflow walks you through generating, revising, and finishing a model.

If you are into art, game design, or just making things, open the Hi3D Agent page and describe a simple object, like a backpack or a tiny spaceship.

Watch how it guides you through those steps instead of dumping you into a blank toolbox.

Researchers copied a tiny trick from fruit flies to help AI learn new smells with almost no memory.

A fruit fly brain contains only about one hundred forty thousand neurons, yet the new Spi-Fly algorithm uses a spiking neural network modeled on that odor system.

A spiking network fires in quick bursts, more like real brain cells than a steady calculator.

The team at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology designed it for times when examples, memory, and computing power are limited.

It did especially well with only a few examples of each odor, and it learned new categories without substantially forgetting earlier ones.

Sometimes the smartest move is studying nature instead of just making models bigger.
