Let's look at one simple grammar idea.

Russian nouns have genders that affect endings.

Кошка ends in -а so it is feminine; собака is also feminine.

Медведь ends in a consonant so it is masculine.

Compare to English where “the cat” stays the same no matter what.

In Russian the word itself changes slightly with articles we don’t even have!

In Russian fairy tales animals often talk and help children like the clever fox or the wise bear in stories by Pushkin.

Russians love keeping cats as pets because they are seen as symbols of comfort and good luck in the home.

Now it is practice time.

For kids draw your favorite animal and write its Russian name underneath then show it to a grown-up and say the word out loud.

For adults look at a pet photo or zoo picture and describe it using three new words for example “Моя кошка” or “Рыба в воде.”

Now here's something really cool about one of today's words.

Did you know the Russian word кошка and the English word “cat” are actually cousins that both trace back to the same ancient root?

This word started in North Africa or the Middle East with the sound “qit” or “kit,” travelled through Latin “cattus,” then into many European languages, and finally reached Russian as кошка.

But be careful — the Russian word “кот” is the masculine form for a male cat while кошка is usually for a female cat or cats in general.

Next time you see a cat remember its Russian cousin кошка comes from the same ancient meow-sound root — that connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.
