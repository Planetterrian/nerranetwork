**Grammar Spotlight:**
Today we look at the verb чувствовать (to feel). In Russian the subject changes the ending of the verb.
- Я чувствую (I feel)
- Ты чувствуешь (you feel)
- Он/она чувствует (he/she feels)
English keeps the verb almost the same (“I feel, you feel, he feels”), but Russian changes the ending each time so the listener knows exactly who is feeling something.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: сердце**
This word started in the ancient Proto-Slavic language as *sьrdьce and travelled straight into modern Russian with almost no change. It is a true cognate with the English word “heart” — both come from the same very old root that meant the centre of the body and emotions. A fun “gotcha” moment: the Russian word looks nothing like “heart” on the page, yet they are linguistic cousins! Here is a simple memory trick: picture your heart as the “centre” of your chest, and both the Russian and English words grew from that same ancient idea of “centre.”

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russian families it is common to ask “Как самочувствие?” (How are you feeling?) when someone looks tired. Russians often bring warm tea and honey when a family member feels under the weather — a simple, caring tradition that shows love through small daily actions.
