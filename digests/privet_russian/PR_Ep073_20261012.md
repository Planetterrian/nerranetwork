### Word of the Day
**Russian:** билет
**Transliteration:** bee-LYET
**English meaning:** ticket
**Example sentence:** Я покупаю билет на поезд.
**English translation:** I am buying a ticket for the train.
**Memorable hook:** It sounds a little like “bill yet” — imagine the bill you pay yet for your ticket!
**Repeat after me:** bee-LYET… bee-LYET.

**Grammar Spotlight:**
Today we look at the verb “to go / to travel” in the present tense. Russian has different verbs depending on how you travel.
- Я еду на поезде. (I am going by train.)
- Мы летим на самолёте. (We are flying by plane.)
- Ты идёшь пешком. (You are walking.)
Unlike English, Russian often chooses a special verb for each method of travel instead of using one general “go.”

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: вокзал**
This word started life far from Russia as the name of a London pleasure garden called Vauxhall. English travellers and engineers brought the name to Russia in the 19th century when the first big train stations were built, and Russians turned “Vauxhall” into “вокзал” for any major railway station. It is a classic borrowing that feels completely Russian now. Be careful — it is a false friend with the English “Vauxhall” because today it only means a station, never a park. Here is a memory trick: picture a grand old station with a big “V” sign that Russians pronounced their own way.

**Cultural Corner:**
Russians love long train journeys, especially on the famous Trans-Siberian Railway that crosses the whole country. Families often bring big samovars of tea and play cards together for days, turning the trip itself into part of the holiday.

**Practice Challenge:**
For kids: Draw a simple map of your town and label the вокзал, аэропорт and your машина with the Russian words, then say “Я еду на машине!” out loud.
For adults: Next time you book travel, practise saying “Один билет, пожалуйста” (One ticket, please) in your head or to a friend.
