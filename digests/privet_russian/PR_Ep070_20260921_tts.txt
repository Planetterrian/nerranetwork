Our word of the day is молоко.

Molokó.

Break it down as mo-lo-kó, with the stress on the last part.

It means milk.

Imagine a moo-loco cow doing a silly dance while giving you milk.

Я пью молоко.

That means I drink milk.

Repeat after me: molokó… molokó.

Now that you know молоко, let's add more kitchen words.

Сыр means cheese.

Syr.

Repeat after me: syr… syr.

Я ем сыр.

That means I eat cheese.

Imagine “Sir” cheese sounds fancy, just like a knight’s title.

Хлеб means bread.

Khlyeb.

Repeat after me: khlyeb… khlyeb.

Хлеб вкусный.

That means the bread is delicious.

Think of “hleb” as the hearty base of every Russian meal.

Яблоко means apple.

Yábloko.

Repeat after me: yábloko… yábloko.

Я люблю яблоко.

That means I love an apple.

“Yabloko” sounds like “yummy block” — a tasty round fruit.

Суп means soup.

Sup.

Repeat after me: sup… sup.

Суп горячий.

That means the soup is hot.

“Sup” is super when it’s steaming on a cold day.

Мясо means meat.

Myáso.

Repeat after me: myáso… myáso.

Мясо в супе.

That means meat is in the soup.

“Myaso” sounds like “meaty oh-so” good.

Вода means water.

Vodá.

Repeat after me: vodá… vodá.

Я пью воду.

That means I drink water.

“Vodá” is what you add to make tea or soup.

Чай means tea.

Chay.

Repeat after me: chay… chay.

Чай горячий.

“Chay” rhymes with “chai” — the same cozy drink.

Вкусный means delicious.

Vkusnyy.

Repeat after me: vkusnyy… vkusnyy.

Сыр вкусный.

“Vkusnyy” tastes like “voo-kus” yum.

Remember поезде from a few lessons ago? It means on the train.

Remember большая? It means big.

Remember погода? It means weather.

In Russian the verb to eat changes depending on who is doing the action.

We say я ем for I eat.

Ты ешь for you eat.

Он or она ест for he or she eats.

This is different from English where the verb stays almost the same.

Now you can say Я ем сыр.

That means I eat cheese.

Or Ты ешь хлеб.

That means you eat bread.

The word чай started in northern Chinese dialects as chá.

It traveled along the Silk Road through Central Asia.

It entered Russian in the seventeenth century as the popular drink чай.

Be careful though.

The Russian word чай sounds exactly like the English chai yet it simply means the same beverage not a fancy latte.

Picture a steaming cup of chay arriving by caravan from China straight to a Russian samovar.

That picture helps the word stick.

Russians love sharing tea and simple snacks like bread with cheese or soup when guests visit.

Offering food is a warm way to show hospitality and start a friendly conversation.

It feels like opening your home with a steaming cup or bowl.

Guests often feel instantly welcome around the table.
