**Grammar Spotlight:**
We will learn about simple adjectives that describe size or appearance, like "большой" (big). In Russian, adjectives change slightly to match the noun's gender (masculine, feminine, or neuter), but for beginners we keep it easy. For example: большой медведь (big bear — masculine), большая птица (big bird — feminine), большое животное (big animal — neuter). Unlike English, where adjectives stay the same, Russian adds endings like -ой, -ая, or -ое so the words "agree" nicely — it's like the adjective wears a matching hat to the noun!

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: медведь**
Did you know that the Russian word "медведь" and the English idea of a "honey-eater" are basically the same word? They both come from an ancient way of describing bears without saying their scary name out loud! This word started in old Slavic languages where people cleverly combined "мёд" (honey) and "ведь" (eater) to create a safe nickname for the bear, avoiding direct words that might bring bad luck in folklore. It travelled through centuries of Russian storytelling and forest tales, staying unique to Slavic tongues while English kept its own "bear" from completely different roots. But be careful — the Russian word "медведь" sounds nothing like English "bear," creating a fun false-friend moment where you might expect a similar sound but get a whole honey-loving story instead! Next time you see honey or think of a bear, remember it's cousins with "медведь" — they both come from that clever "honey-eater" trick. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russian fairy tales, bears like the famous "Mishka" often appear as strong but kind forest friends who help or trick characters. Kids grow up hearing stories where the bear represents nature's power and winter hibernation, teaching respect for animals in the wild.
