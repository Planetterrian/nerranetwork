### Word of the Day
**Медведь** (myed-VYED’) — bear
English meaning: bear  
Simple example sentence: Медведь любит мёд.  
English translation: The bear likes honey.  
Memorable hook: Picture a big, furry “medved” lumbering through the forest like a walking teddy bear.

**Grammar Spotlight:**
Russian nouns have genders (masculine, feminine, neuter) that affect endings. Медведь is masculine, so it often ends in a consonant; кошка is feminine and ends in -а. Compare to English, which has almost no gender endings. Example: Большой медведь (big bear — masculine); Большая кошка (big cat — feminine).

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: Медведь**
Did you know that the Russian word медведь and the English word “bear” are actually cousins from the same ancient root? This word started in Proto-Indo-European as something like *bher-, travelled through Slavic languages, and became медведь in Russian around the 11th century, literally meaning “honey-eater.” But be careful — the Russian word “медведь” sounds nothing like “bear” yet both come from the same hidden root that also gave us “brown” in some languages! Next time you see a teddy bear, remember it’s cousins with медведь — they both come from that ancient “honey-eater” idea. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russian fairy tales the bear is often called “Михаил Потапыч” and acts like a wise but clumsy forest king. Russians have respected bears for centuries as symbols of strength and the deep woods.
