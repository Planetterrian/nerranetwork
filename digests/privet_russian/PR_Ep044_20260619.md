### Word of the Day
**животное**
Transliteration: zhi-VOT-no-ye  
English meaning: animal  
Simple example sentence: Это большое животное. (Eto bol'shoye zhivotnoye.)  
English translation: This is a big animal.  
Memorable hook: Imagine a zoo full of “zhi-VOT-no-ye” friends — every creature you see is a животное!  
“Repeat after me” prompt: Say it slowly with me: zhi-VOT-no-ye… now faster: животное!

**Grammar Spotlight:**
Russian nouns have genders (masculine, feminine, or neuter) that affect the endings of words that describe them. For animals, this means adjectives like “big” or “small” change slightly.  
Examples:  
- Большой кит (bol'shoy keet) — big whale (masculine)
- Большая птица (bol'shaya ptitsa) — big bird (feminine)
- Большое животное (bol'shoye zhivotnoye) — big animal (neuter)
Unlike English, where adjectives stay the same, Russian makes you match the ending to the noun’s gender — but once you learn the pattern, it feels like a fun matching game!

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: медведь**
Did you know that the Russian word медведь and the idea of a “honey-eater” are actually the same concept? They both come from the ancient Slavic words for “honey” (мёд) and “to know” (ведать), because people were afraid to say the real name of the bear out loud! This word started in Proto-Slavic, travelled through old Russian folk tales, and stayed in modern Russian as a gentle nickname. But be careful — the Russian word “медведь” sounds nothing like the English “bear,” yet both animals love the same sweet treat! Next time you see honey, remember it’s cousins with медведь — they both come from the same honey-loving root. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russian fairy tales, animals often act like wise teachers or tricky friends — think of the clever fox or the strong bear in stories by Pushkin. Kids grow up hearing these tales, which makes animal words extra fun and memorable when you visit Russia or talk with Russian-speaking family.
