In Russian, words like счастливый change their ending to match the person.

For a boy or a male speaker, we keep счастливый.

For a girl, it becomes счастливая.

The ending tells us who feels the emotion.

It works the same way with грустный or усталый.

Now you can describe exactly who feels happy, sad, or tired.

Russians ask Как дела to learn how someone really feels.

Sharing emotions openly is part of warm family talk.

Children learn early to name their feelings, just like we did today.

Draw faces for happy, sad, and tired, then label each with the Russian word.

Or tell someone at dinner Я счастливый or Я грустный, and say why in one short sentence.

Now, here is something really cool about one of today's words.

Want to know a secret about счастливый?

Did you know that счастливый and the English word happy are cousins from an ancient root?

It began as good fortune in Proto-Slavic, moved through Old Church Slavonic, and landed in modern Russian.

The similar-sounding chest has nothing to do with it, because счастливый comes from sharing a part of luck.

Next time you feel happy, remember it travels with счастливый from the same idea of good fortune.
