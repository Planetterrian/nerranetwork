**Grammar Spotlight:**
Russian nouns change their endings depending on whether they are the subject or object of a sentence. This is called cases, but we can think of it as "who does what." For example: "Ракета летит" (The rocket flies — subject form) vs. "Мы видим ракету" (We see the rocket — object form). Compare to English: English mostly stays the same ("rocket" or "the rocket"), while Russian adds little endings like -a or -у to show the job of the word.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: ракета**
Did you know that the Russian word ракета and the English word "rocket" are actually cousins that both trace back to the same surprising root? This word started in Italian as "rocchetta," meaning a small spindle or distaff used in spinning, because early fireworks looked like little spools shooting into the sky. It travelled through French and German before arriving in Russian in the 18th century, keeping its fiery meaning along the way. But be careful — the Russian word "ракета" can also mean a badminton shuttlecock or a missile, so context is your friend! Next time you see a rocket launch on TV, remember it's cousins with Russian ракета — they both come from that little spinning spool image. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
Russia has a proud history of space exploration, with the first human in space being cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin in 1961. Russians often celebrate Cosmonautics Day on April 12 with parades and stories about reaching the stars. This love of space shows up in everyday language, where kids dream of becoming космонавты just like their heroes.
