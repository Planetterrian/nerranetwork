**Grammar Spotlight:**
Adjectives must match the gender of the noun they describe. Russian nouns have genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), and adjectives change their endings.  
- Большое дерево (neuter — ends in -ое)
- Длинная река (feminine — ends in -ая)
- Высокая гора (feminine — ends in -ая)
Unlike English, where adjectives stay the same (“big tree,” “big river”), Russian makes the adjective “agree” so the sentence sounds natural.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: цветок**
Did you know that the Russian word цветок and the English word “color” are actually cousins? They both come from the ancient Proto-Indo-European root *kʷeyt- meaning “to shine” or “bright.” This word started in early Indo-European languages, travelled through Slavic tongues where it became “tsvet” (color), and finally grew into “tsvetok” (flower) because flowers are nature’s brightest colors. But be careful — the Russian word “цвет” sounds like it could mean “color” in English, yet it truly means both color and the blooming flower itself! Next time you see a colorful flower, remember it’s cousins with “цветок” — they both come from that shining root. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
Russians have a deep love for nature and often spend weekends in the лес or at an озеро. Many traditional songs and poems celebrate the красота (beauty) of the Russian landscape, especially the белые берёзы (white birches) that appear in famous paintings and fairy tales.
