> **Today we explore the warm and expressive world of Russian feelings so you can share exactly how you feel in any moment.**

### Word of the Day
**рад**
Transliteration: rad  
English meaning: glad / happy (short form, used like “I am glad”)  
Example sentence: Я рад видеть тебя.  
English translation: I am glad to see you.  
Memory hook: It sounds like the start of “radiant” — when you’re рад, your face lights up!  
Repeat after me: рад… рад.

**Grammar Spotlight:**
Short-form adjectives in Russian let you say how someone feels right now without extra words. They change slightly for gender (рад for he, рада for she) but stay simple.  
Examples:  
- Он рад. (He is glad.)
- Она грустная. (She is sad.)
- Я устал. (I am tired — said by a male speaker.)
Unlike English, you often drop the verb “to be” entirely — Russians just say the feeling word and it’s understood.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: рад**
This little word carries a surprising ancient link: it comes from the Proto-Slavic *radъ, which traces back to the same Indo-European root that gave English “ready” and even “ride.” Long ago the idea was “prepared and eager,” so when you say “я рад” you are literally saying you are ready and happy to see someone. A fun false-friend moment: it looks a bit like the English name “Rad” but has nothing to do with radiation! Here’s a quick memory trick — picture yourself already “ready” with a big smile whenever you feel рад.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russian conversations people often answer “как дела?” with a short feeling word like “нормально” or “всё хорошо,” keeping things warm and direct. Sharing feelings openly is part of the friendly, heartfelt style you hear in everyday chats across Russia.

**Practice Challenge:**
For kids: Draw a face showing each feeling (рад, грустный, злой) and label it in Russian.  
For adults: Next time you greet a friend in English, switch to Russian and say “Как дела?” then answer with one of today’s words — “Я рад!” or “Я устал.”
