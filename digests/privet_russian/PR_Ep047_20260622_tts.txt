Собака.

We are exploring animals today, so here are more words to help you talk about creatures around us.

The next word is кошка.

KOSH-ka.

That means cat.

Repeat after me.

Кошка.

Picture a cat curled up like a cushion and the word will stick.

Кошка спит на ковре.

The cat is sleeping on the rug.

The memory hook works because “Koshka” sounds like “cushion,” so you can imagine the cat resting on one while you practice the soft “osh” sound in the middle.

Say the full phrase slowly first, then at natural speed, so the ending “ka” stays clear and matches the feminine pattern we will discuss later.

Now that you know кошка, let's meet птица.

PTI-tsa.

That means bird.

Repeat after me.

Птица.

It flies through the sky like a tiny tweet.

Птица поёт в небе.

The bird sings in the sky.

The memory hook connects “Pti-tsa” to the idea of a “petit” tweet because the quick “pti” part feels light and the “tsa” ending flutters like wings.

Practice the soft “t” sound so it does not turn into a hard “d,” then repeat the whole sentence while picturing the bird high above.

Next comes рыба.

RY-ba.

That means fish.

Repeat after me.

Рыба.

Imagine a fish with a ribbon tail swimming in the water.

Рыба плавает в воде.

The fish swims in the water.

The memory hook works because “Ryba” rhymes with “ribbon,” letting you picture the tail streaming behind like fabric while the fish moves through the water.

Say the Russian sentence twice, first slowly to hear the “ry” start, then at normal speed so the “ba” ending lands cleanly.

Remember слон from before?

Slon still means elephant, our biggest animal friend.

Слон большой и серый.

The elephant is big and grey.

The memory hook reminds you that слон remains the word for the largest land animal, so the single syllable “slon” stays short and heavy like the animal itself.

Repeat the word, then the full sentence, noticing that the masculine ending keeps the adjective “bol’shoy” unchanged in this simple description.

Remember заяц too?

ZA-yats still means hare, the speedy jumper.

Заяц прыгает быстро.

The hare jumps fast.

The memory hook recalls заяц as our quick friend from earlier lessons, with the “zay” start sounding like a sudden leap and the “ats” ending snapping shut like a spring.

Say the word first, pause, then speak the full sentence while imagining the hare bounding across open ground.

The memory hook ties “Lo-shad” to “locomotive” so the steady rhythm of the word matches the steady gallop you can hear in your mind while saying the full sentence.

Russian nouns have secret labels called genders that change how words work.

Most animal names ending in the sound a are feminine, like собака and кошка.

Words like слон and лев are masculine.

English does not mark nouns this way, but in Russian the label tells you how to say my or big with the right ending.

Now you can say моя собака or мой слон correctly.

The same rule applies to the new words we just learned: feminine endings stay soft while masculine ones stay firm.

In Russian fairy tales the bear is often a wise but clumsy forest uncle.

Children hear stories where медведь helps or tricks other animals.

The word feels like an old friend from the woods rather than just a zoo animal.

This tradition turns the name into something familiar that appears in many bedtime tales passed down through families.

Now here is something really cool about one of today's words.

Want to know a secret about медведь?

Did you know that the Russian word медведь and the English idea of a honey eater share the same ancient thought?

Long ago people used this polite name instead of the animal's real scary name.

The word travelled through old Slavic roots and stayed in Russian while English chose the fiercer sounding bear.

Both languages hid the true name out of respect.

Next time you see honey, remember it is cousins with медведь.

They both come from the same sweet secret.
