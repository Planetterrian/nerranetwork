### Word of the Day
**яблоко**
Transliteration: YÁ-blah-kah  
English meaning: apple  
Simple example sentence: Я ем яблоко. (Ya yem YÁ-blah-kah.) — I eat an apple.  
Memorable hook: Picture a shiny red яблоко rolling off the table like a bouncy ball!  
Repeat after me: YÁ-blah-kah… YÁ-blah-kah… now faster: яблоко!

**Grammar Spotlight:**
Russian nouns have genders (masculine, feminine, neuter) that affect the ending of words like adjectives. Food words often show this: яблоко ends in -о (neuter), сыр ends in a consonant (masculine), and молоко also ends in -о (neuter). Compare to English, where we don’t change endings for gender — we just say “the red apple” or “the red cheese” without extra letters. Examples: вкусное яблоко (neuter), вкусный сыр (masculine), вкусное молоко (neuter).

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: чай**
Did you know that the Russian word чай and the English word “tea” are actually cousins from the same ancient root? They both come from the Chinese word “chá,” which travelled along the Silk Road — first to Persian and Turkish traders, then into Russian around the 17th century when tea became popular in Moscow. But be careful — the Russian word “магазин” sounds like “magazine” but actually means “shop”! Next time you see “tea” on a menu, remember it’s cousins with чай — they both come from Chinese “chá.” That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russia, tea (чай) is more than a drink — it’s a daily ritual shared with family and friends, often served with jam, lemon, or sweets in a special teapot called a samovar. Offering tea to guests is a warm sign of hospitality that has lasted for centuries.
