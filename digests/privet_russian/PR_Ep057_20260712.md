**Grammar Spotlight:**
In Russian, adjectives like счастливый, грустный, and усталый change their ending to match the gender of the noun or person they describe. For a boy or “I” (male speaker) we say счастливый; for a girl we say счастливая. It’s like English “happy” vs “happier,” but here the ending tells us who is feeling the emotion.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: счастливый**
Did you know that the Russian word счастливый and the English word “happy” are actually cousins through an ancient root? This word started in Proto-Slavic *sъčęstьje meaning “good fortune,” travelled through Old Church Slavonic, and finally settled in modern Russian. But be careful — the similar-sounding English “chest” has nothing to do with it; счастливый comes from “part” or “share” of luck! Next time you feel happy, remember it’s cousins with счастливый — they both come from the idea of sharing good fortune. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

**Cultural Corner:**
Russians often say “Как дела?” (How are things?) not just as a greeting but because sharing feelings is part of warm, honest conversation. In Russian families, children are encouraged to name their emotions openly, just like we do today.
