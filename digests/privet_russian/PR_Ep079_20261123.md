# Привет, Русский! — Episode Plan

### Word of the Day
**Вкусный** (vkusnyy) — delicious
Example sentence: Этот яблоко очень вкусный. (This apple is very delicious.)
Memory hook: It sounds like “vkus” which feels like “yummy” rolling off your tongue.
Repeat after me: Vkusnyy… vkusnyy.
- Russian (Cyrillic): хлеб
  Transliteration: khleb
  English: bread
  Example sentence: Я ем хлеб.
  Example translation: I eat bread.
  Memory hook: Sounds like “club” but means the staff of life on every Russian table.
- Russian (Cyrillic): молоко
  Transliteration: moloko
  English: milk
  Example sentence: Дети пьют молоко.
  Example translation: Children drink milk.
  Memory hook: “Mo-lo-ko” — imagine a cow saying “moo” then “loco” for loco-motion to the glass.
- Russian (Cyrillic): сыр
  Transliteration: syr
  English: cheese
  Example sentence: Сыр вкусный.
  Example translation: The cheese is delicious.
  Memory hook: Short and sharp like the English “sir,” but it’s the star of Russian breakfast.
- Russian (Cyrillic): суп
  Transliteration: sup
  English: soup
  Example sentence: Мама готовит суп.
  Example translation: Mom is cooking soup.
  Memory hook: Sounds exactly like English “soup” — an easy win!
- Russian (Cyrillic): чай
  Transliteration: chay
  English: tea
  Example sentence: Я люблю чай.
  Example translation: I love tea.
  Memory hook: “Chay” rhymes with “chai” — the same drink loved worldwide.
- Russian (Cyrillic): мясо
  Transliteration: myaso
  English: meat
  Example sentence: Кот ест мясо.

  English: vegetables
  Example sentence: Овощи полезные.

  Transliteration: frukty

  Example translation: The fruit is sweet.

**Grammar Spotlight:**
In Russian, adjectives like вкусный must match the gender of the noun they describe. For masculine nouns (хлеб, сыр) we say вкусный. For feminine nouns we change it to вкусная (вкусная еда). Compare to English where “delicious” stays the same no matter what it describes.

**Word Origins — Deep Dive: чай**
This word started its journey in northern China as “cha,” traveled along the Silk Road into Central Asia, and reached Russian speakers by the 17th century through trade with Mongol and Turkic peoples. It shares the same root as the English word “tea,” which came via a different route through Portuguese and Dutch traders who heard a southern Chinese pronunciation “te.” But watch out for the false friend: Russian “чай” sounds like English “chai” yet means the same drink, not a spiced latte. Here’s a memory trick — picture a steaming cup of tea being passed hand-to-hand along ancient caravan routes straight into a Russian samovar.

**Cultural Corner:**
In Russia, tea is more than a drink — it is the heart of hospitality. Guests are almost always offered чай with jam, lemon, or sweets, and refusing it can seem impolite. The samovar, a special metal urn, keeps the water hot for hours so the tea is always ready.
