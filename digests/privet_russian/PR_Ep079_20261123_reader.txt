Our Word of the Day is вкусный. Vkusnyy. Say it slowly with me: vkusnyy. Vkusnyy means delicious. It sounds like “vkus” which feels like “yummy” rolling off your tongue. Listen to this sentence: Этот хлеб очень вкусный. This bread is very delicious. Now repeat after me: vkusnyy. Vkusnyy.

Let's keep building our food words. The Russian word хлеб means bread. Khleb. Repeat after me: khleb. Khleb. Я ем хлеб. I eat bread. Russians often eat it at every meal. It sounds like “club” but means the staff of life on every Russian table. Picture a warm slice on the table during family dinners. Repeat after me again: khleb. Khleb.

Next comes молоко. Moloko. Moloko means milk. Repeat after me: moloko. Moloko. Дети пьют молоко. Children drink milk. Children drink it for strong bones. “Mo-lo-ko” — imagine a cow saying “moo” then “loco” for loco-motion to the glass. It flows into glasses at breakfast across Russia. Repeat after me: moloko. Moloko.

Then we have сыр. Syr. Syr means cheese. Repeat after me: syr. Syr. Сыр вкусный. The cheese is delicious. It is sharp and salty on breakfast tables. Short and sharp like the English “sir,” but it’s the star of Russian breakfast. Families slice it thin for sandwiches. Repeat after me: syr. Syr.

Now суп. Sup. Sup means soup. Repeat after me: sup. Sup. Мама готовит суп. Mom is cooking soup. Moms often cook it on cold days. Sounds exactly like English “soup” — an easy win! Steam rises from bowls in winter kitchens. Repeat after me: sup. Sup.

Чай comes next. Chay. Chay means tea. Repeat after me: chay. Chay. Я люблю чай. I love tea. Many people drink it hot with lemon. “Chay” rhymes with “chai” — the same drink loved worldwide. It warms hands on frosty mornings. Repeat after me: chay. Chay.

Мясо follows. Myaso. Myaso means meat. Repeat after me: myaso. Myaso. Кот ест мясо. The cat eats meat. It can be grilled or stewed. “Mya-so” — think “me-ya-so” as in “me, ya, so hungry for meat.” Plates of it appear at holiday feasts. Repeat after me: myaso. Myaso.

Овощи are next. Ovoshchi. Ovoshchi means vegetables. Repeat after me: ovoshchi. Ovoshchi. Овощи полезные. Vegetables are healthy. They grow in summer gardens. “O-vosh-chi” sounds like “oh, fresh chi” — fresh garden goodies. Baskets overflow with them at markets. Repeat after me: ovoshchi. Ovoshchi.

Finally, фрукты. Frukty. Frukty means fruit. Repeat after me: frukty. Frukty. Фрукты сладкие. The fruit is sweet. They taste sweet after dinner. Almost identical to English “fruit” — instant recognition. Bowls of them end many meals. Repeat after me: frukty. Frukty.

In Russian, adjectives like вкусный must match the gender of the noun they describe. For masculine nouns like хлеб or сыр we say вкусный. For feminine nouns we change it to вкусная. Compare to English where “delicious” stays the same no matter what it describes. This is like adding an ending so the words fit together smoothly. Вкусная еда works for feminine items.

The pattern keeps descriptions clear and natural.

Before we move on, here is something really cool about one of today's words. The word чай started in northern China as cha. It traveled the Silk Road into Central Asia and reached Russia in the seventeenth century through Mongol and Turkic traders. It shares the same root as the English word tea, which arrived by a different sea route.

Russian чай sounds like chai, yet it means the plain drink, not a spiced latte. Picture a steaming cup passed along ancient caravan routes straight into a Russian samovar. The journey crossed steppes and rivers before reaching samovars.

In Russia, tea is the heart of hospitality. Guests are almost always offered чай with jam, lemon, or sweets. Refusing it can seem impolite because the samovar keeps water hot for hours so the tea stays ready. Hosts refill cups without asking. The custom turns every visit into a shared pause. Families gather around the table for these moments daily.
