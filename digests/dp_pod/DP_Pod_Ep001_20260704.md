# The DP Pod: The Do Positive Podcast
> **Two friends built a fourteen-show daily network by themselves with an AI pipeline instead of a studio.**

The most common media business model profits when audiences feel powerless. This show starts from the opposite conviction: individuals are not powerless. The DP Pod exists so builders, creators, and contributors finish each episode smarter about real progress and more convinced their own effort counts. It is the place where the two founders of the Nerra Network step in front of the microphone themselves. Novak + Perra equals Nerra, an independent, ad-free network of daily shows produced end-to-end by an airline pilot and a physical-chemist novelist using a single AI-enhanced pipeline they built and maintain. The format is simple: ten minutes a day of consequential, verified progress; one mindset principle each episode; one concrete action each week with honest numbers; and proof it works when listeners report back. The club is free, the numbers are sourced, and the only rule is that no fabricated claim ever reaches the listener. Everything else—the stories, the disagreements, the laughter—is two friends who have argued for years reviewing what actually moved today and what one person can do about it. In 2025 the same two friends asked whether two people could run an entire podcast network without hiring a studio. They began with standalone scripts, each thousands of lines long, held together only by cron jobs. Those scripts duplicated logic across shows, so the team rebuilt everything as one unified pipeline: a single runner, one config file per show, and a shared engine. Adding a new show dropped from writing two thousand lines to writing a config and a few prompts. Today that pipeline runs fourteen daily shows and is guarded by more than three and a half thousand automated tests that execute before every episode publishes. The economics only became viable after two migrations. The language model moved to a newer generation that cut per-episode cost roughly in half. At the same time the voices moved from a premium provider to custom-trained voices at roughly one thirty-sixth the price per character—about four dollars per million characters instead of a hundred and fifty. That single change is why a fourteen-show daily network remains affordable for two people.

Prompts turned out to be the real product. Early tests showed that giving the model one example phrase caused it to repeat that phrase verbatim in every episode, a failure mode the team named seeded-template convergence. The rule became “de-seed by shape, never with a quotable example.” Every show now receives recurring quality reviews: a review agent audits one show twice a week, writes predictions into a ledger, and the next review scores those predictions as hits or misses. The reviewer itself was later migrated from an expensive model to a cheap one—about thirty cents a run instead of six to nine dollars. One iron rule survived every change: no modification that alters the audio ships without a human actually listening first.

The network went multilingual early. Two Russian-language shows launched first, each with its own custom Russian voice. Every English episode is now automatically translated and re-voiced into French, Russian, Spanish, and Chinese for about eighteen cents an episode, each language carrying its own subscribable feed plus a Russian-dubbed YouTube channel. Every episode also publishes a blog post containing the full transcript and chapters. Early posts all carried the identical title—the show name—which killed search visibility; each post is now titled by the episode’s hook. Full videos and Shorts are auto-produced with AI-generated imagery, per-word captions timed from speech recognition, a heuristic that starts each Short at the most engaging beat, auto-generated thumbnails, and auto hashtags. YouTube’s daily quota of ten thousand API units once forced brutal choices about which shows received video; a later increase to two hundred thousand units allowed all fourteen shows to publish, roughly two dozen uploads a day. Retention data now feeds back into how titles are written.

Reliability required its own war stories. GitHub schedulers sometimes fired hours late, so an external dispatcher now triggers each show to the minute, complete with duplicate guards that prevent a late backup trigger from double-publishing and automatic recovery pull requests when a push fails mid-publish. A management dashboard tracks every known landmine in the system—the running list of past mistakes and how each was fixed. Ten shows existed at the first network review. Two narrative shows driven by topic queues rather than news followed: First Principles Daily, built around the magic-wand number and the Idiot Index, and Unintended Consequences. SpaceX Daily launched the day of the IPO. This show, The DP Pod, became the fourteenth—the first two-voice dialogue show and the first where the builders themselves step in front of the microphone.

Several open questions remain. What does it mean that two people can now do what once required a media company? Where does human judgment stay load-bearing—the listen-gates, the editorial standards, the review ledger—and which of these could never be automated? What breaks first at fifty shows? Is AI-made media honest enough, and does full disclosure plus no-fabrication rules actually earn trust? What should a builder listening today take from the cost curves? The answers will be tested in public, one episode at a time.

### Think Positive
Motivation follows action. Tony Robbins has long framed this as deciding first and then taking massive action rather than waiting for the feeling to arrive. The same principle appears in behavioral-activation research: doing the small next step changes the internal state more reliably than trying to think your way into readiness. When the feed makes the world feel immovable, the fastest exit is to perform one concrete action anyway. The mental rep for today is simple: name the single smallest step that would count as progress on something that matters to you, then do it before you open any other tab.
