### The Positive Papers
1. **Maine Counts Over 20 Million River Herring During Annual Run, the Highest in Decades: Good News Network**
   Two large dams removed from the Penobscot River opened hundreds of miles of spawning habitat that had been blocked for generations. The resulting run exceeded 20 million fish, the strongest count recorded in decades and the first time the species has supported limited harvest since a 2012 moratorium. Alewives now move upstream in numbers that support both local fisheries and the broader river food web. The recovery still depends on continued dam management and water-quality monitoring, and it remains limited to specific watersheds rather than a statewide rebound. A beloved local fish staple that was illegal to harvest as recently as 2012 has seen an incredible recovery in Maine’s rivers following the removal of several large dams. Particularly on the Penobscot River, the removal of two dams has resulted in the premier success story of our time for Maine’s alewife fish. For residents, the change shows how removing a few high-impact barriers can restore a staple species within a human lifetime. 2. **Puerto Rico Upgrades Remote Microgrid With Clean Hydrogen: IEEE Spectrum Energy**
   Cornell researchers led by Héctor Abruña are adding on-site solar, batteries, and a Nel Hydrogen electrolyzer to a Vieques farm microgrid so excess daytime power produces hydrogen for later use in fuel cells. The hydrogen system is sized to extend backup power from a few days to roughly ten days during extended outages, enough to run a clinic, refrigeration, and water pumps for the island’s 8,000 residents. On-site production avoids Jones Act shipping costs that make diesel expensive, and the team estimates hydrogen becomes cheaper than extra batteries once outages last more than a few hours. The project is still in the deployment phase with full operation planned for early 2027, and upfront capital remains a barrier for similar isolated communities. It demonstrates a concrete path for places that already face frequent storms and weak grid ties. On the small island of Vieques, 10 miles east of mainland Puerto Rico, the system will include a solar-plus-battery setup that provides electricity for on-site hydrogen production, after which the hydrogen is compressed and stored in on-site gas cylinders for later use in fuel cells that power essential services during grid outages. The architecture builds on existing hydrogen-based microgrids such as a 293-megawatt-hour installation in California’s rural wine country in August 2025 by Pacific Gas & Electric, though that system runs on batteries and hydrogen fuel cells without on-site solar and involves trucking in the hydrogen. Vieques’ microgrid will use an on-site solar-powered electrolyzer, and during outages the farm’s owners will move the fuel cells around the island to where they are needed for critical services, providing up to 2.5 megawatt-hours of additional power. To prepare the microgrid, the team plans to double the island’s installed solar capacity from 50 to 100 kilowatts and scale up its battery storage; when the sun is shining the electrolyzer will run on solar and storage to build a reservoir of clean hydrogen ahead of hurricane season. Over the microgrid’s 20-year lifetime, hydrogen installation and maintenance costs will be less than the cost of using diesel generators during the same timeframe, according to Cornell’s Paul Mutolo, and hydrogen is also cheaper than adding more batteries because tanks can be scaled separately from the fuel cell. The project is supported by settlement funds from Volkswagen’s 2019 class-action lawsuit, and the Cornell team is looking for additional funding to procure the necessary components. While the economics of hydrogen production have historically rendered the technology unviable in most cases, a community like Vieques is one specific instance where the costs might just balance out because diesel is pricey due to Jones Act requirements and on-site production eliminates transport costs. Kathy Ayers, senior vice president for research and development at Nel Hydrogen, notes that if power is out for more than a few hours and the battery runs out, the system can switch over to a fuel cell and run off stored hydrogen. The goal is to get electricity prices down to 15-16 cents per kilowatt-hour for a 50 percent reduction from current state-run utility prices, which are among the highest in the U.S. at 28-29 cents per kilowatt-hour. Source: [spectrum.ieee.org](https://spectrum.ieee.org/puerto-rico-hydrogen-microgrid)

### Think Positive
Angela Duckworth’s research on grit shows that sustained effort on a single difficult goal builds both competence and the belief that one’s actions matter. The same pattern appears in long infrastructure projects like dam removals or microgrid upgrades: early results look small, yet persistence compounds into measurable recovery. When people treat a setback as information rather than proof of futility, they stay engaged long enough for the second-order benefits to appear. A simple mental rep today is to name one concrete step you have already started on an important project, then list the next smallest action you can finish before the day ends.

### The Lever
Sign up for a free home energy assessment, seal the top three air leaks the report identifies, and add basic attic insulation if the findings support it. The step typically costs a few hundred dollars in materials plus a weekend of work; average annual savings run in the low hundreds of dollars with payback inside three to seven years depending on local fuel prices and climate. If even one percent of households in a mid-sized city completed the same checklist, the aggregate reduction in heating demand would be measured in tens of megawatts—enough to ease peak loads on the very grids now wrestling with data-center swings. The main barrier is simply not knowing where the biggest leaks are; the free assessment removes that uncertainty in a single visit. The action connects directly to the energy-stability theme running through today’s grid and microgrid stories.
