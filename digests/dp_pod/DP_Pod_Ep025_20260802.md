1. **Pine bark and sponge compounds could counter drug-resistant malaria and babesiosis: Phys.org Breaking**
   Researchers at the University of California, Riverside are testing natural compounds from pine bark and marine sponges as potential next-generation treatments. The work targets parasites that cause malaria and babesiosis, two diseases whose existing drugs are losing effectiveness. Early lab results indicate the compounds interfere with parasite survival mechanisms that standard therapies no longer reach. The approach remains in the discovery stage, with no human-trial data yet and an uncertain timeline to approved medicines. If successful, the compounds could expand treatment options for regions where resistance is rising fastest. 2. **Electron cooling tames highly charged ions in Penning trap for first time: Phys.org Breaking**
   Teams at Technical University of Darmstadt and GSI Helmholtz Center for Heavy Ion Research slowed highly charged ions from an accelerator and stored them in a Penning trap, then performed the first electron cooling of such ions inside the trap. The method reduces ion energy without losing the high charge state, opening precise measurements that were previously impossible. The result appears in Physical Review X. The technique is still limited to specialized facilities and has not yet moved beyond proof-of-concept experiments. It supplies cleaner data for atomic physics and could eventually improve ion-beam applications in materials analysis and medical isotope production. The Technical University of Darmstadt group worked with colleagues at the GSI Helmholtz Center for Heavy Ion Research to achieve the cooling step. Highly charged ions arrived from the accelerator already at high energy. Storage in the Penning trap allowed the first successful electron cooling of these ions. The published paper in Physical Review X records the exact sequence of deceleration and cooling. Only specialized facilities can run this setup today, keeping the work at the proof-of-concept level. Cleaner atomic-physics data is the immediate output, with possible later uses in materials analysis and medical isotope work. ### Think Positive
Carol Dweck’s growth-mindset research shows that people who treat abilities as skills that improve with effort keep trying after setbacks instead of concluding they lack talent. The malaria and ion-trap work above both required years of incremental experiments before any visible result appeared. When a task feels stuck, the practical move is to label the next small step as practice rather than proof of fixed limits. Today, pick one stalled item on your list, write the single next action that takes under ten minutes, and do it without judging the outcome.

If even 5 percent of listeners in colder regions followed through, the cumulative electricity-demand shift would register at grid scale within a few years.
