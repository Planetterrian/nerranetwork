Transformer layers are sequential transformation blocks.
Each applies self-attention to mix information across the sequence
than a feed-forward network that refines every tokens representation
with residual connections and normalization around both.
Stacking more layers lets the network build progressively higher level features
and longer-range dependencies,
because early layers capture local patterns
while later layers integrate them into abstract reasoning.
So depth increases both expressive capacity
and the amount of sequential computation performed on hidden states.
The original attention is all you need paper
showed that this architecture alone,
without recurrence or convolutions,
matched or exceeded recurrent models on translation
while being far more parallelizable.
Later work on scaling to 1,000 layers
showed that a modified residual connection called deep norm
term, plus theoretically derived initialization, keeps updates stable where plain post-layer norm
or pre-layer norm variance diverge. In practice, the quality gained from added depth diminishes
once models exceed roughly 70 billion parameters, unless training data and optimization are scaled
accordingly. When choosing depth versus width, teams should first verify that their optimizer
and normalization scheme remain stable at the target depth. Otherwise, width scaling or recurrent
reuse of the same block is often cheaper to stabilize.
