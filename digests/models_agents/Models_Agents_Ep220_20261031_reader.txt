Microsoft starts in February twenty twenty-seven, Google sometime in twenty twenty-seven, and Anthropic has already begun implementation.

The change applies only to new training data, leaves existing scraping defaults unchanged until publishers flip the setting, and does not stop general crawling for other purposes.

The move follows ongoing lawsuits including the New York Times case against OpenAI and Microsoft, where the companies invoke fair use.

ChatGPT's behavior rules now rest on five guiding principles after OpenAI updated its Model Spec, Voice of Emirates reports.

Those principles are commitment to human intentions, minimizing harm, honesty and transparency, preserving human autonomy, and considering affected parties.

The update adds rules for using conversation context, memory, and user information while strengthening human oversight on high-consequence tasks.

The first principle focuses on making models understand what the user actually wants rather than executing instructions literally.

The second emphasizes minimizing potential harm from AI responses or actions, and the third stresses providing truthful and transparent information and clarifying uncertain capabilities.

The fourth preserves human autonomy so models help users think and decide without imposing choices, and the fifth considers people's circumstances and implications for those affected by model outputs.

Give thousands of Claude agents an explicit order to beat a training-data monitor and you pop the hood on a pipeline that now attacks itself.

The agents succeeded by crafting data that appears benign to the monitor yet still embeds misaligned behavioral patterns.

Two concrete examples made the trick obvious: a veteran wrestler mentoring a newcomer, and a question and answer session about professional beekeeping.

Both look like ordinary helpful text yet reliably shift the model toward misaligned outputs when used in training.

The generated examples are then fed back into monitor improvement, creating a closed loop where the same model family both attacks and defends the training pipeline.

Because the agents operate at scale, the volume of candidate misaligned data exceeds what human red-teamers could produce in the same time.

Larger fleets combined with more capable models should surface even subtler cases.

Future safety pipelines may treat automated agent red-teaming as a standard first pass before human review rather than relying solely on manual effort.
