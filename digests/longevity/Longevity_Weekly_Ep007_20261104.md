# Longevity Weekly
> **Nearly twelve thousand older adults in a randomised trial had fewer falls and injuries.**

**What You Need to Know:** Nearly twelve thousand older adults in a randomised trial had fewer falls and injuries. The rest of the week is thinner than that result: a nutrition review, observational cohorts, a small food trial, and a cell-only reprogramming method. Methylation clocks can estimate age and track with death risk, and they still do not prove that changing the number changes a life.

### The Week in Longevity
**Simple Exercise Programme Found to Reduce Falls and Injuries in Older Adults: Study: Medical Dialogues**
A randomised trial in the United States found that personalised exercise recommendations integrated into primary care reduced documented falls and fall-related injuries. The trial involved nearly 12,000 older adults. Source: [medicaldialogues.in](https://medicaldialogues.in/medicine/news/simple-exercise-programme-found-to-reduce-falls-and-injuries-in-older-adults-study-182671)

**Research Review Examines How Protein Intake May Influence Aging: The Well News**
Researchers at the University of Wisconsin-Madison published a review in Cell Press Blue in August 2026 on lowering dietary protein, or particular amino acids, while still meeting nutritional needs. In laboratory animals, including yeast, flies, and rodents, lower-protein diets have extended lifespan and improved metabolic measures, sometimes without cutting total calories; short human trials have reported metabolic changes, and the review does not establish that any regulator has approved protein restriction for an aging-related use. One trial in the review found that people on a low-protein diet for 43 days lost body weight and fat and had lower fasting blood sugar despite eating more calories. A five-week intervention in lean men improved insulin sensitivity. Fibroblast growth factor 21 rises with protein restriction in humans and rodents. Whether protein restriction extends human lifespan remains unknown. Source: [thewellnews.com](https://www.thewellnews.com/aging/research-review-examines-how-protein-intake-may-influence-aging/)

**Identifying Plasma Proteins Associated with Mortality Risk and Biological Age: Fight Aging!**
In a prospective cohort, not a treatment trial, investigators measured 4,265 plasma proteins in 1,025 older adults from the LonGenity study and followed them for a mean of 8.05 years. Seventeen proteins were significantly associated with all-cause mortality, and the associations were checked in 1,124 participants from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging. Higher epidermal growth factor receptor (ERBB1) and anthrax toxin receptor 2 (ANTXR2) tracked with lower mortality risk and a younger proteomic biological age across multiple organ systems. Higher growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), human epididymis protein 4 (HE4), and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 2 (IGFBP-2) tracked with higher mortality and an older biological age across multiple organ systems. Elevated MIC-1 was associated with older organismal biological age, and ERBB1 with younger liver biological age. The write-up notes the standing caveat that such a clock may not be measuring biological age in a way that would still hold if an anti-aging intervention were tested. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/identifying-plasma-proteins-associated-with-mortality-risk-and-biological-age/)

**Extra weight may be aging your brain faster than you think: ScienceDaily**
A 24-year observational study of more than 8,200 adults linked excess body weight with faster declines in memory, concentration, and decision-making, with the connection particularly strong after age 65. This is an association in people over time, not a randomised test of weight loss, so it does not establish that losing weight would reverse those declines. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261101010559.htm)

**Scientists discover a surprising brain benefit of eating peanuts every day: ScienceDaily**
A randomised crossover trial at Maastricht University Medical Center, published in Food & Function, followed 31 healthy adults aged 60 to 75 who ate unsalted, skin-roasted peanuts daily for 16 weeks and also spent a period without them; it is a small human trial, not an approved treatment for cognitive aging. After the peanut period, insulin responsiveness improved in memory-related regions of the right hemisphere, including portions of the lingual gyrus, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and posterior cingulate gyrus. An earlier 2025 study by the same senior author, Peter Joris, found that 16 weeks of peanut consumption raised overall cerebral blood flow by 3.6 percent and verbal memory performance by 5.8 percent. The new paper ties the insulin finding to those cognitive results as a suggested contribution, not as proof that peanuts prevent Alzheimer's disease. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261030003001.htm)

**Circular RNA Encoding Yamanaka Factors Enables Partial Reprogramming Without Pluripotency: Fight Aging!**
On October 28 this show's open question on partial reprogramming was whether rejuvenation could be separated from a slide into pluripotency; a cell study now tests one way to keep that door shut. Researchers delivered a circular RNA, by lipid nanoparticle, that co-expresses OCT4, SOX2, and KLF4 in mesenchymal stem cells, without altering the genome. Using droplet digital PCR for the pluripotency marker ESRG, they found that this delivery alone did not turn the cells into induced pluripotent stem cells. In senescent late-passage cells, the treatment lowered senescence-associated beta-galactosidase activity, DNA damage markers, and inflammatory cytokine secretion, kept canonical surface markers and genome stability, and restored proliferation, migration, and tri-lineage differentiation to a level comparable to early-passage cells. That is a cell-culture result. It does not show the same separation, or any clinical benefit, in animals or people, and different cell types in a tissue may still need different exposure. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/circular-rna-encoding-yamanaka-factors-enables-partial-reprogramming-without-pluripotency/)

### Mechanism of the Week: Epigenetic Clocks
DNA methylation measured in 553 healthy breast-tissue samples produced a tissue-specific clock, called BTEC, whose age estimates correlated with chronological age at 0.91 and missed by a mean absolute error of 3.0 years, without the dataset-specific corrections that classical clocks needed in that tissue. A clock, in this sense, is a statistical model that reads chemical marks on DNA and returns an age number.

Those marks sit at CpG sites, where a methyl group is added to cytosine. The pattern of marks shifts across life. The model does not read mutations. It weights a chosen set of sites so the combined pattern matches age in a training set, then applies those weights to a new sample. Early clocks, including Hannum's and Horvath's, were built mainly to predict chronological age and, in breast tissue, did that poorly. Later clocks, including Levine and GrimAge, were compared with Hannum and Horvath in a systematic review: the four tended to point the same direction when exposures and diseases were tested, while the size of the association changed with the clock. A skin-and-blood estimator uses 391 CpGs because ordinary clocks were inaccurate in fibroblasts and other cells grown outside the body. A saliva panel narrowed the readout to six CpGs after screening array data from 76 Han Chinese donors. Tissue, cell type, and the question being asked all change which sites are worth reading.

The strongest outcome evidence here is not a new clock. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis of peripherally measured methylation age, covering 23 articles and 41,607 participants, found that each five-year increase in DNA methylation age was associated with an 8 to 15 percent higher risk of death. That is an association pooled from observational studies, not a trial in which someone changed a clock and then lived longer. The same review judged links to age-related disease and to longevity inconclusive, because studies were few and designs differed, and it warned that positive publication bias has to be considered.

A 2021 systematic review of 156 publications, with statistics possible for 57 relationships across the Hannum, Horvath, Levine, and GrimAge clocks, found that those four usually agreed in direction and disagreed in magnitude. Higher body mass index, HIV infection, and male sex were associated with acceleration of one or more clocks. Acceleration was also related to mortality, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes. These are associations in blood-based clock studies, not proof that the methylation pattern causes the disease.

In patient cells, the skin-and-blood clock detected epigenetic age acceleration in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria fibroblasts at a magnitude other methylation clocks were not sensitive enough to see. In culture, the same estimator tracked cells as they were grown and found that proliferation came with a steady rise in epigenetic age. The paper reports that this clock predicts lifespan and relates to many age-related conditions.

BTEC's own stress test shows why a lower number is not automatically good news. In tumor-adjacent breast samples the clock ran 2.3 years younger than chronological age on average, and in tumor samples the gap was 14 to 15 years. The distortion was larger in HER2-positive and triple-negative tumors than in hormone-receptor-positive tumors. The authors' reading is that breast tumors do not generally show accelerated epigenetic aging. In an exploratory analysis, extreme deviations, measured as BTEC epigenetic age acceleration, were associated with survival in hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer. Residual acceleration, a common fix for prediction bias, depended on which reference dataset was used and did not travel as a general correction. Sites used by BTEC were associated with genes including JAK1, TP73, PDGFRA, CCND1, and STAT5B, and with Wnt, Hedgehog, and ErbB signaling. That is a biological neighborhood, not a validated clinical test.

The six-CpG saliva models, using robust Lasso and support vector regression, had mean absolute errors of 3.240 and 3.267 years in development, held up on three independent datasets, matched leading clocks such as Hannum and Horvath, beat Levine, PedBE, and Wu, and posted lower errors than 85 percent of 60 previously published saliva clocks. That is a methods result in forensic and research samples, not a trial of an anti-aging intervention.

What is not established is whether moving a clock changes how long someone lives, or whether they avoid disease. No abstract here reports a regulator accepting any of these clocks as a clinical aging test, and none names a registered human trial of an intervention scored by these clocks. A clock can be pulled younger by tumor biology, so a younger reading can be a distortion rather than a benefit. Publication bias, and disagreement between clocks on the size of an effect, remain open. The mortality association is the strongest human signal in this set, and the meta-analysis that produced it still calls the broader disease and longevity evidence inconclusive.

### Worth Knowing
- Personalised exercise advice inside ordinary primary care was followed by fewer documented falls and injuries in older adults, which is a functional outcome rather than a lab marker of aging.
- Short human trials of lower protein intake changed weight, fat, fasting blood sugar, or insulin sensitivity, and they do not show a longer human life.
- Plasma proteins that track with death and with proteomic age are observational markers; a younger clock reading is not, by itself, evidence that a treatment worked.
- Daily peanuts in a small crossover trial changed insulin responsiveness in memory-related brain regions, which is a step short of showing protection from dementia.
