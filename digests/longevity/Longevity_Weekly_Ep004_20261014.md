# Longevity Weekly
> **A 48% higher dementia risk tracked frequent napping in 995 older adults.**

**What You Need to Know:** In an observational follow-up of 995 older adults, frequent daytime napping tracked a 48% higher risk of later dementia. The same week brought a small human look at cellular recycling, a postmortem map of Alzheimer-linked DNA methylation, a controlled comparison of nail problems in people using GLP-1 medicines, and a mouse-and-pig muscle study. None of it is a treatment result.

### The Week in Longevity
**Frequent Daytime Napping Linked to Higher Dementia Risk in Older Adults: Mirage News**
Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health researchers followed 995 U.S. Medicare beneficiaries in an observational cohort, not a trial. Participants were 65 or older, free of dementia at the 2013 start, and were tracked for up to 10 years in the National Health and Aging Trends Study; the paper appeared online October 5 in The Journal of Gerontology: Medical Sciences. People who napped most days or every day had a 48% higher dementia risk than those who rarely or never napped, after accounting for demographics, health conditions, nighttime sleep duration, daytime sleepiness, and a measure of obstructive sleep apnea risk. Unintentional napping, falling asleep without meaning to, was linked to a 39% higher risk. Naps of 30 minutes or less were linked to a 40% higher risk, and that link held after the same confounders, while the link for naps longer than 30 minutes weakened once nighttime sleep was considered. Senior author Adam Spira said more frequent napping could be a marker of brain changes rather than a cause of them. The team describes a likely two-way relationship, because early degeneration in regions that keep a person awake may also increase napping. After dementia cases in the first five years were excluded, the frequent-napping and unintentional-napping associations remained similar. No regulator decision is attached to napping in this report. Source: [miragenews.com](https://www.miragenews.com/frequent-daytime-napping-linked-to-higher-1759538/)

**Scientists discover a surprising paradox in how our cells age: ScienceDaily**
This is a small human study of cells, reported in Aging Cell on September 27, 2026, by researchers at Sanford Burnham Prebys Medical Discovery Institute, not a randomized trial of an aging treatment. Autophagy is the cell’s cleanup system: it packages damaged proteins and other waste and breaks them down for reuse. Senior author Caroline Kumsta said the idea that autophagy declines with age is widely cited, but the human evidence is very thin. Volunteers aged 23 to 72, all reporting no chronic medical conditions, gave blood and skin samples. Some skin cells were converted into induced neurons so the team could compare recycling in three cell types. Activity could increase, decrease, or stay the same depending on cell type and sex. The report also says a small study suggests gentle exercise may help restore more youthful recycling patterns in older adults. Interest in targeting autophagy for healthy-aging interventions is growing, lead author Tatiana Moreno said, but these sources do not describe a regulator approval for any such use. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261012185009.htm)

**Popular weight loss drugs linked to a surprising fingernail problem: ScienceDaily**
A controlled comparison, not a randomized trial, is the first of its kind on GLP-1 medicines and nails, and it was presented at the European Academy of Dermatology and Venereology Congress 2026. Researchers compared 575 people using GLP-1 medications with 1,329 people who had type 2 diabetes and/or obesity and had never used the drugs, recruited in France, the United States, Brazil, and Mexico. Nail detachment was reported by 39% of users and 9% of never-users, more than four times as often. Nail discoloration affected 46% of users and 17% of never-users. At least one nail disorder was reported by 66% of users and 53% of the comparison group. The report says weight loss alone could not fully explain the differences, and its summary also links these medicines to hair loss and scalp inflammation. The named drugs, including Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, Victoza, and Trulicity, are described as treatments for weight loss and type 2 diabetes. These sources do not report approval for slowing aging. Last week’s aging-clock claims for this drug class did not include this nail comparison. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261011211604.htm)

**Brain DNA methylation, APOE genotype and Alzheimer’s disease neuropathology in a large postmortem cohort: Nature Aging**
A large postmortem human study, published online in Nature Aging on October 12, 2026, is an association analysis, not a clinical test and not a treatment trial. It identified 107 brain DNA methylation sites linked to Alzheimer’s pathology, and 38 of those sites were replicated independently. DNA methylation partially mediated the association between APOE genotype and an Alzheimer’s diagnosis. Methylation here is a chemical mark on DNA, measured in brain tissue after death, not a reading a clinic can act on from this paper alone. Source: [nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01243-7)

**miR-128-3p Inhibition Improves Muscle Mass and Function in Aged Mice: Fight Aging!**
This is animal evidence, summarized by Fight Aging! from a preprint, not a human trial. Antisense-oligonucleotide inhibition of miR-128-3p restored muscle mass and function in aged mice, improved cardiac function while limiting adverse remodeling after myocardial infarction, and eased skeletal and cardiac muscle pathology in mouse and pig models of Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Across those settings, inhibition turned on mitochondrial programs and suppressed inflammatory and fibrotic signaling, a pattern the authors liken to established longevity interventions. Separately, the miR-128-1 locus sits in a human haplotype on chromosome 2q21.3 that has been associated with grip strength, pulmonary function, and cardiometabolic traits; that is a genetic association, not a test of the drug. Elenae Therapeutics is developing the approach and, as described, is aimed first at more severe indications such as heart muscle after a heart attack. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/10/mir-128-3p-inhibition-improves-muscle-mass-and-function-in-aged-mice/)

### Mechanism of the Week: Sleep and Aging
In the Sleep Heart Health Study, 5,407 community-dwelling adults, mean age 63 and ranging from 45 to 99, with 52% women, showed poorer objectively measured sleep with older age even after chronic health conditions were taken into account. That cross-sectional study, published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society, used unattended home polysomnography plus questionnaires. It is the largest measurement set in this week’s sleep sources, and it is still a snapshot, not a trial of changing sleep.

Sleep architecture is the night’s sequence of stages, scored from the electroencephalogram. Stage N1 is light sleep. N2 carries sleep spindles, brief bursts of faster activity. N3 is slow-wave sleep, the deep stage with large slow waves. Rapid eye movement sleep is the stage in which most dreaming occurs and the cortex is highly active. A circadian clock times when sleep is likely. With healthy aging, a 2015 review in Current Topics in Behavioral Neurosciences describes less slow-wave sleep and less of its spectral power, fewer and smaller spindles, lower rapid-eye-movement density, smaller circadian amplitude, and a phase advance, meaning the clock runs earlier. Mild cognitive impairment brings further losses of sleep quality, slow-wave sleep, spindles, and the share of rapid eye movement sleep. Conversion to Alzheimer’s disease is described there as adding a profound disruption of circadian rhythmicity.

How those features relate to memory is still a set of associations. In younger adults, sleep-dependent consolidation is widely seen for both declarative and procedural tasks. The same review says that in healthy aging, consolidation of declarative memory is preserved, while some procedural tasks, including motor-sequence learning, do not show it. Fragmentation can reduce consolidation in younger adults, and a normal rise in fragmentation may account for part of the aging change. Specific features, spindles and slow-wave sleep, are strongly linked to consolidation, and both diminish with healthy aging and diminish further with mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease. That is a review of existing work, not a new experiment.

The Sleep Heart Health Study found that older age went with shorter sleep time, lower sleep efficiency, and more arousals in both men and women. In men, age was independently tied to more stage 1 and stage 2 sleep and less slow-wave and rapid eye movement sleep. In women the stage pattern was less strongly linear. Subjective complaints ran the other way by sex: poor subjective sleep was not tied to older age in men, while older women had more trouble falling asleep, with a trend toward more waking in the night and waking too early. Agreement between self-report and measured sleep time and sleep latency was low to moderate, with correlation coefficients from 0.06 to 0.32. A higher prevalence of chronic conditions, including sleep apnea, did not explain the age-related changes or the age-sex differences.

A 2022 systematic review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health pooled 71 studies. Mean ages across samples ran from 58.3 to 93.7 years. Thirty-three studies used subjective measures, 31 used objective measures, and 10 used both. Pathological aging showed worse sleep than healthy older adults on both kinds of measure. In Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, the features most often compromised were rapid eye movement sleep, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, and sleep duration. The reviewers’ reading is that frequency and severity of sleep disturbance follow the evolution of cognitive impairment, and that objective measures look more consistent than subjective ones. A review synthesizes other studies; it does not assign cause.

A longitudinal cohort of men from the Metropolit 1953 Danish Male Birth Cohort, published in Sleep Medicine, tied baseline microstructure to cognition over about ten years. Cognitive testing was done in 2010–2013 and again in 2020–2022, with 206 men at baseline and 106 at follow-up. Polysomnography was baseline only, in 176 men, and spindle, slow-wave, and EEG-coherence analyses were limited to 82 men with adequate non-rapid-eye-movement sleep. Over ten years, Trail Making Test B slowed by 6.1 seconds and the Symbol Digit Modalities Test fell by 2.3 points. Higher sleep efficiency tracked better cognitive maintenance. More wake after sleep onset and a higher arousal index tracked poorer memory and learning. A higher share of N1 tracked poorer executive function, while N2 and N3 were positively associated with cognitive outcomes. Fast spindle amplitude and frequency, slow-wave characteristics, and N2 and rapid-eye-movement coherence correlated with processing speed, attention, and global cognition. Analyses used Spearman and partial Spearman correlations with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. The authors call the findings exploratory and say the predictive value for neurodegenerative disease is not established.

A smaller imaging study in Molecular Psychiatry tested a long-standing idea about rapid eye movement sleep. Twenty-four older adults without dementia, mean age 71.29 years, 58.33% women, and 25% with amnestic mild cognitive impairment, had in-laboratory polysomnography, neuropsychological testing, structural MRI, and PET with FEOBV, a tracer of brain cholinergic innervation. Cholinergic activity supports cortical activation in rapid eye movement sleep, when other transmitter systems are nearly silent. Higher rapid-eye-movement EEG slowing, the ratio of slower to faster power, over frontal, central, parietal, occipital, and temporal derivations was associated with cortical cholinergic denervation, notably in fronto-parietal areas and the medial temporal lobe, in voxel-wise regressions controlling for sex. In exploratory sex-stratified analyses adjusted for age, the link sat mainly in medial temporal regions in women and in neocortical regions in men. FEOBV uptake was higher in women than in men. The authors suggest global rapid-eye-movement slowing may be a sensitive marker of cholinergic denervation in people without dementia. That is a cross-sectional association in 24 people, not a diagnostic standard.

Two further reviews set limits rather than new measurements. The 2015 review notes that insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, and narcolepsy can impair sleep-dependent consolidation without neurodegenerative change, and that sleep disorders become more common with normal aging and more common still with neurodegenerative disease. It also notes that rapid-eye-movement behavior disorder can presage Parkinson’s disease. A 2018 review in Sleep Medicine Reviews proposes that chronic low-grade inflammation, called inflammaging, may mediate links among impaired sleep, dysfunctional adiposity, and cognitive decline through neuroinflammation, with reciprocal ties to the circadian system and to hormone resistance. That paper is a model. It does not demonstrate the pathway in a new cohort.

What is not established is any of the causal steps a listener might want. These sources do not show that napping, short sleep, or a given stage causes dementia, and they do not test whether changing sleep changes cognitive trajectories. The strongest longitudinal microstructure result is in older men only, with microstructure data on 82 people, and its authors mark it as exploratory. Subjective and objective sleep disagree, and they disagree differently in men and women. No paper here evaluates a drug, a device, or a regulator decision about sleep as an aging treatment. The strongest finding’s limit is the Sleep Heart Health Study itself: age tracks worse measured sleep in thousands of adults, independent of the chronic conditions the investigators could account for, and that still does not say what would happen if sleep were changed.

### Worth Knowing
- An observational cohort found that frequent and unintentional daytime napping preceded many dementia diagnoses, which fits a marker of changing wakefulness at least as well as a cause.
- In healthy volunteers, cellular recycling did not show one age-related direction, so a blanket claim that autophagy simply fails with age is ahead of the human cell evidence.
- A congress comparison, not a randomized trial, found more nail disorders in GLP-1 users than in people with diabetes or obesity who had never used the drugs, beyond what weight loss alone explained in that report.
- Postmortem brain methylation tracked Alzheimer pathology and partly sat on the path from APOE genotype to diagnosis, which is a biological association, not a validated clinical clock.

### Evidence Ledger
- In 5,407 adults, older age tracked shorter sleep, lower efficiency, and more arousals on home polysomnography, independent of chronic conditions; evidence level: cross-sectional human study. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18482295
- Across 71 studies, sleep disturbance was worse in pathological aging than in healthy older adults, and objective measures were more consistent than subjective ones; evidence level: systematic review. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35886309
- In older Danish men, baseline spindles, slow waves, and EEG coherence correlated with ten-year cognitive change; evidence level: longitudinal observational cohort, exploratory. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42628340
- In 24 older adults without dementia, rapid-eye-movement EEG slowing tracked cortical cholinergic denervation on PET; evidence level: small cross-sectional human imaging study. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42120556
- Healthy aging brings less slow-wave sleep and fewer spindles, with further losses in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease, and declarative sleep-dependent consolidation is described as preserved; evidence level: narrative review. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24652608
- A proposed model links impaired sleep, adiposity, and cognitive decline through low-grade inflammation; evidence level: narrative review, not new data. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30241997

This week’s clearest human findings are associations and early measurements, not treatments.
