This week's mechanism organizes 12 biological processes that change with age into a single
framework.
The hallmarks of aging are a set of 12 biological processes whose age-associated changes meet
three criteria.
They appear with advancing age, their experimental accentuation speeds aging, and their therapeutic
targeting can slow, stop, or reverse aspects of aging in model systems.
The original nine hallmarks were proposed in 2013, and later expanded to 12 to include
disabled macro-autofagy, chronic inflammation and dysbiosis.
Each hallmark describes a distinct but interconnected layer of cellular and tissue decline.
Genomic instability refers to the accumulation of DNA damage and mutations.
Telemere attrition shortens chromosome ends and triggers DNA damage responses.
Epigenetic alterations change gene expression patterns without altering DNA sequence.
Loss of proteostasis impairs protein folding and clearance. Disabled macro-autofagy reduces
the cell's ability to recycle damaged components. D-regulated nutrient sensing disrupts pathways
such as MTOR and AMPK that normally adjust metabolism to nutrient availability.
identity.
Mitochondrial dysfunction lowers energy production and increases reactive oxygen species.
Cellular senescence causes cells to stop dividing and secrete inflammatory signals.
Stem cell exhaustion depletes the pool of cells needed for tissue repair.
Altered intercellular communication changes signaling between cells.
Chronic inflammation sustains low-grade immune activation, and dysbiosis alters microbial
communities that influence host metabolism and immunity.
Evidence for the framework itself derives from reviews that compile cell, animal, and
limited human observations rather than from any single randomized trial.
The 2013 cell paper enumerated the initial nine hallmarks, and the 2023 update added three
more while retaining the same three-premise test for inclusion.
A 2020 review examined how Metformin interacts with several hallmarks in cell and animal models,
showing improvements in nutrient sensing, autophagy, and intercellular communication, yet
noted that human outcome data remain limited to the ongoing tame trial, whose results are
not yet available.
Telomere studies illustrate how one hallmark can intersect with others, amplifying DNA damage
responses and senescence. But these links rest primarily on cell and animal experiments.
What is not established is whether intervening on any single hallmark produces consistent,
clinically meaningful extension of healthy lifespan in humans.
No hallmark targeted therapy has received regulatory approval for an aging indication.
Known risks of experimental interventions include off-target effects on cell proliferation or
immune function, though specific safety profiles vary by agent and remain understudy.
The 12 hallmarks satisfy three premises of age association, experimental acceleration,
and potential therapeutic reversal, as stated in a 2023 review.
