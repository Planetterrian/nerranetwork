An observational study of 120,000 older adults links bone drugs to 24 percent lower dementia risk.

This is Longevity Weekly, episode 8. The science of aging, read carefully — never medical advice.

After that association, a high-dose flu analysis, an FDA eye warning on GLP-1 drugs, and caloric restriction as the mechanism of the week.

University of Hong Kong researchers, in a study ScienceDaily describes in Alzheimer's and Dementia, looked at nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, drugs already used to slow bone loss and fractures. Patients on those drugs were less likely to develop Alzheimer's and other dementias.

The same group had already found osteoporosis and fractures standing as independent dementia risks, and people already living with dementia more likely to fall and break a bone. More than fifty-five million people live with dementia worldwide, a count the authors project toward one hundred thirty-nine million by 2050. Shared risks in the write-up include older age, being female, and low activity.

The paper does not report any regulator approving these bone drugs to prevent dementia, and a second use remains a possibility the authors name, not a cause they proved.

UTHealth Houston analysts compared flu shots in adults sixty-five and older, nearly two hundred thousand of them, and this analysis is not described as a randomized trial. People who got the high-dose influenza vaccine had a nearly fifty-five percent lower Alzheimer's risk, set against a forty percent reduction previously tied to the standard dose.

That is a comparison of people who received a shot, so other differences between those groups stay in the picture. Nothing in the report says influenza vaccines are approved to prevent Alzheimer's.

The Food and Drug Administration, six days after this show's November fourth look at GLP-1 evidence beyond weight loss, warned that these drugs carry a potential risk of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy. STAT reports the agency required companies to tell patients and doctors. People sometimes call it an eye stroke.

Insufficient blood flow to the optic nerve typically brings sudden vision loss, and severe cases can end in blindness, with no proven treatment named in the report. The agency said it started looking after a 2024 observational study linked Ozempic and Wegovy to the condition. These sources do not report GLP-1 drugs approved as a treatment for aging.

Georgetown's Lombardi Comprehensive Cancer Center built a population simulation, reported by Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology News and Medical Xpress as appearing in JAMA Network Open, to weigh curing breast cancer against faster biological aging.

Jeanne Mandelblatt, the senior author, said women who have survived breast cancer are the largest share of the eighteen point six million cancer survivors in the United States. Some chemotherapy that lengthens survival also damages normal cells and organs, and that acceleration of biological age can last for years and stay invisible for years.

Frailty, cognitive problems, and other aging diseases can arrive earlier. Swarnavo Sarkar, the lead author, said the model counts both the survival gain and the damage, including more senescent cells that have stopped dividing, resist death, and secrete molecules that inflame tissue.

The authors say survivorship care might someday use that aging knowledge to look for drug or behavior interventions. The papers here do not test those interventions.

Rentosertib, a TNIK inhibitor from In Silico Medicine, sits in a phase two trial for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and Fight Aging! reports that several proteomic aging clocks read younger in that human data. The drug is described as anti-fibrotic. A younger clock is a biomarker shift, not a measured longer life, and these sources do not report approval for aging.

The same note points to rapamycin and related molecules as already having sturdier evidence that they touch aging mechanisms to some degree, which is a contrast, not a finding from this fibrosis trial.

Nature Aging published, on November tenth, a paper by Li, Xia, Sun, Jia, and colleagues on mitovesicles in Alzheimer's disease. They used patient brain samples and mouse models, and they flag Ggt1 as an enriched cargo implicated in driving pathology. Human tissue plus mice, not a treatment trial.

Aging Cell is where a review, covered by Lifespan dot io, lands on poor sleep and inflammaging making each other worse. That is a reading of a large existing literature, not a new group of people enrolled to test a sleep fix.

Also on the desk: Fight Aging! describes glymphatic drainage of cerebrospinal fluid as falling with age and falling harder in Alzheimer's, with a job clearing amyloid-beta, alpha-synuclein, tau, and TDP-43. A study written up by Nutraceutical Business Review found NAD in blood does not decline with age, which that outlet says undercuts longevity claims built on a drop.

Cepharanthine, a plant extract, is reported by Fight Aging! as a new senolytic candidate, with the caveat that cheap candidates of this sort have not tended to produce compelling human data.

Caloric restriction, this week's mechanism, is eating less energy than you would freely, without malnutrition, and a 2012 review in the American Journal of Human Biology calls it the strongest environmental intervention known to delay aging and extend life across experimental organisms. That claim lives in animals and lab models.

The reviewers treat aging as the biggest risk factor for many cancers, heart disease, and diabetes, and they hope the mechanism will someday point to treatments. They do not test one.

A 2013 review in the Journal of Cardiopulmonary Rehabilitation and Prevention draws the line at intake well below free eating, at least ten percent in people and at least twenty percent in animals.

Across animal work and a thin set of human trials, those authors see a chance of slower cardiac aging and less atherosclerotic disease, through blood pressure, lipids, inflammation, and maybe other routes. Those are intermediate risks, not years of life counted in people.

They say human health effects are still not fully understood, that modest restriction inside a wider lifestyle program is likely to help heart health in people with extra weight because of those cardiometabolic effects, and that older adults and people with serious illness such as heart failure still need more study.

The human experiment with the most weight here is CALERIE, the Comprehensive Assessment of the Long-term Effect of Reducing Intake of Energy, reported in 2015 in The Journals of Gerontology, Series A.

Investigators randomized two hundred eighteen nonobese adults, ages twenty-one to fifty-one, average body mass index twenty-five point one, to two years aimed at twenty-five percent restriction or to eating freely. Eighty-two percent of the restriction group finished, and ninety-five percent of the comparison group.

What they achieved was eleven point seven percent restriction, not the twenty-five percent on the drawing board, and they kept off ten point four percent of body weight. The free-eating group barely changed weight. Resting metabolic rate, adjusted for that weight change, fell more with restriction at twelve months and did not at twenty-four. Core temperature barely separated the groups.

The thyroid hormone triiodothyronine fell more with restriction at both twelve and twenty-four months. The authors call sustained restriction feasible in nonobese adults and say the pattern on survival correlates hints at aging-related benefits that later human studies would still have to show. Nobody's lifespan was the endpoint.

Rhesus monkeys sit closer to us than a mouse and still are not us. A 2026 cross-sectional study in Skeletal Muscle compared eighteen animals on thirty percent restriction with eighteen controls. Deep RNA sequencing of skeletal muscle found metabolism, mitochondria, signaling, and oxidative-stress genes up, and immune, matrix, apoptosis, and ribosomal-RNA-processing genes down.

Alternative splicing was a major way restriction rewrote genes tied to muscle, metabolism, and aging. The authors read that as preserved muscle, less degeneration, less inflammation, and mechanisms shared across species.

Human astrocytes in a dish, in a 2025 Scientific Reports paper, got the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxyglucose as a stand-in for restriction, then an inflammatory hit from interleukin-1 beta. Oxygen use and acidification fell, and TNF, IL-6, and C3 expression fell, with chromatin accessibility changing alongside.

Taking glucose away, and adding the ketone beta-hydroxybutyrate, also tracked with lower cytokines, and the effects stacked with the inhibitor. The authors float glycolysis as a future lever on astrocyte inflammation in neurological disease. That is a cell model of a lab chemical, not a diet result, and nothing here calls 2-deoxyglucose an approved aging treatment.

A 2007 review in The Journal of Nutrition is blunt about the animal ceiling. In rodents, more restriction meant more lifespan, and starting later meant less benefit. Their model said a man who began thirty percent restriction at forty-eight and held it to an expected death at seventy-eight might gain two point eight years. That figure is a projection from animal patterns, not a count of men.

Restricted animals stayed hungry, neuroendocrine profiles matched ongoing hunger, and the authors doubt people could keep this up for decades without long support. They also leave open whether appetite-suppressing drugs would copy the effect, or whether hunger itself is part of the machinery.

Exercise and cold can cut energy too, they note, and animals answer those deficits differently, with only a small lifespan mark.

No paper in this set shows caloric restriction lengthening human life. The strongest finding remains that randomized trial of two hundred eighteen nonobese adults: near twelve percent restriction for two years was doable and moved some survival-linked markers, including a thyroid hormone, while the metabolic-rate gap at one year had closed by two.

These papers do not report any regulator approving caloric restriction as a medical treatment for aging.

Worth knowing from the human evidence: an association in people already on bone drugs is not proof those drugs guard the brain. An analysis of who received which flu shot is not a trial that vaccination prevents Alzheimer's. A simulation of breast-cancer survivors can separate longer survival from later loss of healthy years, including damage from senescent cells.

And two years of eating less, in a randomized group of nonobese adults, shifted markers without ever measuring lifespan.
