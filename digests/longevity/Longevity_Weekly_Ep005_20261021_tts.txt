How to read longevity evidence starts with a mismatch from twenty fourteen: Deelen and colleagues, in Human Molecular Genetics, published a genome-wide association study of human longevity that a Cell Cycle commentary called the largest to that date.

Genes already known to stretch life a great deal in rodents were not consistently linked to human longevity, in that scan or in association studies more generally.

A rodent experiment can show that one genetic change, in one strain, changes lifespan or changes a pathway in tissue.

A human genome-wide association study can show which variants travel statistically with a longevity phenotype in the people enrolled.

A hit in one of those designs is not a cause proven in the other species, and neither design is a test of a treatment.

The commentary lays out three explanations and does not crown one.

Association studies have built-in limits, so even a large human scan can miss a real effect.

Human longevity may also be spread across many variants, so a gene with a big effect in a mouse need not show up as a clean hit in people.

The author's own hypothesis puts part of the blame on the animal work: most of those studies use strains with little genetic diversity, so a result in one narrow background may not apply to mixed human populations.

The commentary also points to other results, not named in the abstract, where caloric restriction changed rodent lifespan in a strain-specific way.

The translation question follows from that: genetics-of-aging findings may not move cleanly into human therapies, and the commentary does not claim that move is impossible.

The Snell dwarf mouse carries a Pit-1 mutation, and its long life has been tied to reduced insulin, IGF-1, and growth-hormone signaling.

Those hormones, plus thyroid-stimulating hormone, help set protein-synthesis rates, which fall in old rodent tissues.

Earlier work had found PI3K activity sharply lower in Snell dwarf liver, and PI3K helps control translation.

The two thousand four study compared young and aged Snell dwarf livers and found less phosphorylation and less kinase activity of Akt, also called PKB, and of p38 MAPK, plus less phosphorylation of the downstream regulators of translation.

The authors propose that lower protein synthesis may be an important feature of the Pit-1 mutation and of this mouse's long life.

That is biochemistry in one long-lived mutant strain, not a human genetic association and not a randomized trial.

Those liver readings may be exactly the kind of result that does not travel: lower activity at several nodes of insulin and IGF-1 signaling and of p38 MAPK signaling, and lower marks of translational initiation.

A kinase reading is a molecular measurement, not evidence that blocking the same node would lengthen human life.

A missing association does not prove the pathway is irrelevant in people, and it does not prove the pathway matters.

Neither paper is a randomized human trial, and neither reports that a regulator has approved reduced insulin or IGF-1 signaling, p38 MAPK modulation, or any related approach for an aging-related use.

Neither reports human safety results.

What they do support is a limit: a lifespan difference in a low-diversity mouse strain, or a drop in liver kinase activity, is not evidence that a therapy will work in humans.

The strongest human-side finding is a negative one, and the commentary states its own limit: rodent lifespan genes were not consistently associated with human longevity in the largest genome-wide study then available, or in association studies more generally, and that gap does not say whether the biology fails in people or whether the studies could not have shown the link.

One thing worth knowing is that a lab study of ovarian tissue can show menopause is not a full biological stop in that organ, and still cannot show that any product changes those features.

Another is that symptom words posted after an unapproved drug mark use outside a trial, not a count of how often harm occurs.
