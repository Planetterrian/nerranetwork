ScienceDaily's clinical trial is the fiber finding, and it sits in people, not in mice.

A daily fiber supplement also made the body less sensitive to pain.

Researchers said gut bacteria and the hormone GLP-1 may sit behind those changes.

That hormone link is a proposed path, not a demonstration that GLP-1 caused the relief.

Nothing in these sources shows a regulator has approved fiber for an aging-related use.

Yang and colleagues, writing in Nature Aging, found statin use lined up with a slower yearly drop in bone mineral density after menopause.

That piece is an observational association, not a randomized test of bone outcomes.

In the same paper, atorvastatin pushed bone building only when estrogen was low, by steering leptin-receptor-positive marrow stromal cells toward bone.

Those cells include skeletal stem and progenitor cells.

The experimental route was less RAC1 prenylation, then nuclear factor kappa B turning on Oln, and more osteoblasts.

Estrogen hits the same cells through estradiol and estrogen receptor alpha, also turning on Oln, so the statin path is described as a stand-in for a signal menopause weakens.

The authors place this beside cholesterol management, and this paper does not establish an aging-related bone approval.

They also note that older observational statin-and-bone studies have disagreed by cohort, endpoint, and design.

Life Biosciences told Lifespan.io it has interim data from a Phase 1 trial of ER-100 in three participants.

Since October 14, that is the first human step this show had been waiting on for partial reprogramming.

The company is described there as working on cellular rejuvenation medicines for diseases of aging.

A Fight Aging newsletter called the same announcement initial results from the first reprogramming trial.

Interim data in three people is an early human trial note, not a published efficacy result, and approval of ER-100 for an aging use is not established here.

Professor Valter Longo, head of the Longevity Institute at the University of Southern California, told The Independent which low-cost habits he favors.

He named a mostly plant-based pattern with some fish and little meat, meals inside a daily window, regular exercise, and better sleep.

That is an expert interview, not a new randomized trial, and no longevity diet is shown here as regulator-approved.

He said a twelve-hour window curbs excess eating and sidesteps fasts of sixteen hours or longer, which he said some research ties to higher heart-disease risk.

His longevity diet, the interview notes, inspired Michael Mosley's five-to-two diet.

A Fight Aging review of the aging gut says thousands of microbial species shift with age.

Microbes that stir chronic inflammation gain ground on microbes that make useful metabolites.

Human population maps, the piece says, have correlated microbiome features and metabolites with age-related diseases, and osteoporosis is expected to have many such links.

Those are correlations in a review, not a trial that changing gut microbes prevents fractures.

Fight Aging also reports a senomorphic microRNA, miR-3155b, found while researchers tried to lighten the senescent-cell load left by cancer therapy.

Senomorphic, in that account, means damping inflammatory signals from senescent cells rather than killing them or slowing their birth.

This is a preclinical discovery report, and regulatory approval of miR-3155b is not established.

AOL reported that fish oil supplements failed a major test for memory and brain aging.

Fight Aging writes that aging microglia turn more inflammatory, feeding chronic brain inflammation that can disrupt function and feed neurodegenerative disease, and that senescence explains only part of the shift.

Lifespan.io says repair therapies built to hit several age-related diseases at once are still forced down existing regulatory pathways.

This week's mechanism is biomarkers of aging, and the sharpest new measurement is a blood study, not a clock product.

In cognitively unimpaired adults, Neurobiology of Aging modeled how inflammation markers in blood relate to each other.

A network centered on CXCL5 tracked age, sex, and the cerebrospinal-fluid ratio of amyloid-beta 42 to amyloid-beta 40.

That is observational network analysis, not a treatment trial.

The authors broke networks into eigenvectors with hub proteins, then tested those eigenproteins against demographics, Alzheimer's markers in spinal fluid and blood, and immune history.

A Science China Life Sciences review defines an aging biomarker as parameters that gauge age-related change, follow physiological aging, and flag a turn toward disease.

The same review says the practical questions are how old the body is, why it ages, and how that pace might slow.

It sorts markers into physiology, medical imaging, histology, cellular change, molecular change, and secreted factors.

A useful one, it argues, is specific, systemic, and clinically relevant, and it says uses and limits of today's set are still poorly mapped.

That paper is a review framework, not a validation experiment.

A Cell paper says these markers are how the field hopes to judge longevity interventions in people on a human timescale, including as possible surrogate endpoints.

It also says there is no agreed list of what makes an aging biomarker reliable, and that gap blocks clinical validation.

The authors offer language, classes, and possible clinic uses, plus validation steps and unsolved problems.

A Nature Medicine review of omic markers says the field still lacks a shared rule for validation before the clinic.

It looks at predictive checks in population studies, warns that studies are hard to compare and hard to generalize, and says orderly validation could speed gerotherapeutic trials.

None of those reviews is a trial, and none says a regulator has accepted a clock or a blood network as an approval endpoint for aging.

The inflammation paper is a warning against one blood number as biological age.

An interleukin-6 and FGF-21 network tracked sex, the spinal-fluid amyloid ratio, and Qalb, the spinal-fluid-to-serum albumin ratio.

An interferon-gamma and CXCL9 network tracked both age and that spinal-fluid amyloid ratio.

Networks built on CXCL11 with CXCL9, and on CCL19 with CCL4, tracked sex alone.

A network of MCP-3, MCP-4, and CXCL6 tracked how many surgeries a person had accumulated.

Plasma amyloid-beta 42 over 40 did not significantly track any of those blood inflammation networks, even where spinal fluid did.

The authors say reading immune biology from blood is important and hard, because these markers do many jobs and stick to several demographic and health facts at once.

A review in Revista de Investigación Clínica starts from a plain fact: people born the same year do not age on one path.

Neuronal markers — scans, blood tests, and vessel measures — are described as capturing neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, vessel failure, and weak resilience.

Behavioral markers — gait, mobility, dual-task performance, balance, activity, sleep, and digital phenotyping — are described as those biological changes showing up in what a person can do.

The review says shifts in these markers may come years before cognitive decline, frailty, disability, and lost independence.

That "may" is a review summary, not a new count from a named cohort.

Putting neuronal markers, behavior, and physical performance together might flag riskier trajectories and serve precision geroscience aimed at intrinsic capacity.

The authors say those combined models still need testing in clinics and in populations.

The oldest paper here is a nineteen ninety-nine account of the Biomarkers of Aging Program at the National Center for Toxicological Research and the National Institute on Aging.

The goal was a rodent panel for asking whether an intervention slows aging, on a bet that a marker working across genotypes and species might matter in people.

Caloric restriction was the life-span model.

The program logged more than sixty thousand individually housed animals, seven mouse and rat genotypes, both sexes, about half eating freely and half calorie-restricted.

That is animal infrastructure, not proof a rodent marker predicts a human outcome.

What these papers do not establish is a shared pass-fail test, a regulator's yes to any omic clock or inflammation network as a stand-in for healthspan or lifespan, or proof that changing a network changes dementia, frailty, or survival.

The tightest limit is the blood study itself: networks followed a spinal-fluid amyloid ratio and did not follow the same ratio in plasma, in adults who were still cognitively well.
