### The Week in Longevity
**The ovary doesn't retire after menopause: Buck Institute**
A Buck–Northwestern study of ovarian tissue from 28 women found ongoing changes in proteins, inflammation, and tissue structure after menopause. That is a laboratory study of human tissue, not a randomized trial, and it did not test a drug or a behaviour. The report says the changes raise new questions about women's health as they age. It does not describe a regulator approving any treatment on the basis of this work. Source: [buckinstitute.org](https://www.buckinstitute.org/news/the-ovary-doesnt-retire-after-menopause/)

**A dog longevity pill is nearing FDA approval. A vet explains what it can and can't do: Yahoo Creators**
Loyal, a San Francisco company, is developing LOY-002, a once-daily tablet for dogs 10 and older that weigh at least 14 pounds, and Yahoo Creators reports that the company has cleared two of the three major technical sections the FDA requires before conditional approval. The article says the pill could be available as early as late 2026 or 2027. This is a veterinary regulatory path, not an approved human aging drug, and the article does not present a published trial showing that LOY-002 itself lengthens life. Veterinarian Rebecca Greenstein told the outlet the tablet is aimed at insulin sensitivity and high insulin, with healthspan as the goal rather than freezing age. She pointed to a 14-year Purina study published in 2002, an animal feeding experiment in which Labrador retrievers fed 25 percent fewer calories than littermates stayed leaner, developed chronic disease later, and lived a median of nearly two years longer. The American Animal Hospital Association classifies 44 percent of pets as seniors. Source: [creators.yahoo.com](https://creators.yahoo.com/lifestyle/story/a-dog-longevity-pill-is-nearing-fda-approval-a-vet-explains-what-it-can-and-cant-do-174709664.html)

**1 in 4 Army Men Have Low T; GLP-1 in Menopause; Retatrutide Side Effects on Reddit: MedPage Today**
MedPage Today, citing JAMA Network Open, reported an analysis of Reddit users who said they had used retatrutide, an unapproved weight-loss drug. The most commonly cited adverse-event terms were appetite increases, fatigue, increased energy, insomnia, increases in heart rate, and muscle-related symptoms. That is an analysis of online self-reports, not a randomized trial, so it cannot establish how often those events occur or what each person actually received. No regulator has approved retatrutide for any use described in this report, including any aging-related use. Source: [medpagetoday.com](https://www.medpagetoday.com/endocrinology/generalendocrinology/123483)

**Blocking STING Helps Preserve Bone in Aging Mice: Lifespan.io**
A mouse study links STING-mediated signals from marrow fat cells to inflammation and to declining NAD+ in bone-forming cells, and interrupting that pathway helped preserve aging bone. This is an animal experiment, not a human trial. The summary does not report regulatory approval of any related approach for an aging-related use in people. Source: [lifespan.io](https://lifespan.io/blocking-sting-helps-preserve-bone-in-aging-mice/)

### Mechanism of the Week: How to Read Longevity Evidence
In 2014, Deelen and colleagues published in Human Molecular Genetics a genome-wide association study of human longevity. A commentary in Cell Cycle that year called it the largest such study to that date, and then pointed at a mismatch: genes known to extend lifespan considerably in rodents were not consistently associated with human longevity, in that scan or in genetic association studies more generally.

How to read that mismatch starts with what each design can show. A rodent experiment can show that a defined genetic change, inside a defined strain, changes lifespan or changes a pathway in tissue. A human genome-wide association study can show which variants are statistically linked to a longevity phenotype in the people enrolled. A hit in one design is not a cause proven in the other species, and neither design is a treatment test.

The commentary offers three explanations and does not pick a winner. Longevity association studies have intrinsic limits, so a large human scan can still miss a real effect. Longevity may also have a complex genetic architecture, spread across many variants, so a gene with a large effect in a mouse need not appear as a clean human hit. The author's own hypothesis is that part of the gap comes from the animal studies. Most of those studies use strains of limited genetic diversity. A result that holds in one narrow background may not apply to genetically mixed human populations. The commentary also points to other results, unnamed in the abstract, in which caloric restriction changed rodent lifespan in a strain-specific way. The upshot is a question about translation: findings from the genetics of aging may not move cleanly into human therapies. The commentary does not show that translation is impossible. It shows that the usual path from a mouse lifespan gene to a human claim is weaker than a headline implies.

 The Snell dwarf mouse carries a Pit-1 mutation. Its longevity has been associated with reduced insulin, IGF-1, and growth-hormone signaling. Those hormones, and thyroid-stimulating hormone, help set rates of protein synthesis, which decline in aged rodent tissues. Earlier work had found PI3K activity sharply lower in Snell dwarf liver. PI3K is a signaling protein that helps control translation. The 2004 study looked at young and aged Snell dwarf livers and found lower phosphorylation and lower kinase activity of Akt, also called PKB, and of p38 MAPK, and lower phosphorylation of downstream regulators of translation. The authors propose that lower protein synthesis may be an important feature of the Pit-1 mutation and of this mouse's long life. That is biochemistry in one long-lived mutant strain. It is not a human genetic association and not a randomized trial.

Set beside the commentary, those liver measurements are the kind of result that may not travel. They show lower activity at several nodes of insulin and IGF-1 signaling and of p38 MAPK signaling, and lower marks of translational initiation. A kinase reading is a molecular measurement. It is not evidence that blocking the same node would lengthen human life. The commentary's human finding is that genes with large lifespan effects in rodents have not been consistently tied to human longevity in association studies.

Several things remain unestablished. A missing association does not prove the pathway is irrelevant in people, and it does not prove the pathway matters. No paper here is a randomized human trial. Neither paper reports that a regulator has approved reduced insulin or IGF-1 signaling, p38 MAPK modulation, or any related approach for an aging-related use. Neither reports human safety results. What they do support is a limit on interpretation: a lifespan difference in a low-diversity mouse strain, or a drop in liver kinase activity, is not evidence that a therapy will work in humans.

The strongest finding on the human side is negative, and the commentary states its own limit. Rodent lifespan genes were not consistently associated with human longevity in the largest genome-wide study then available, or in association studies more generally. That gap does not say whether the biology fails in people or whether the studies were built in a way that could not show the link.

### Worth Knowing
- A laboratory study of ovarian tissue can show that menopause is not a full biological stop in that organ; it cannot show that any product changes those features.
- Symptom words people post after using an unapproved drug are a sign of use outside a trial, not a count of how often harm occurs.

### Evidence Ledger
- A 2014 commentary on the then-largest human longevity genome-wide association study found that rodent lifespan genes were not consistently associated with human longevity, and argued that limited genetic diversity in animal strains, plus strain-specific caloric-restriction effects, weakens translation to people. Evidence level: commentary on human genetic association studies and on model-organism design. Source: [europepmc.org](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25486354)
- In Snell dwarf mouse liver, phosphorylation and kinase activity of Akt/PKB and p38 MAPK, and phosphorylation of downstream translation regulators, were decreased; the authors propose lower protein synthesis as a feature of this long-lived mutant. Evidence level: mouse tissue biochemistry. Source: [europepmc.org](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15541773)

The useful distinction this week is between a change measured in tissue, a lifespan result in one animal strain, and a result that has actually been tied to people.
