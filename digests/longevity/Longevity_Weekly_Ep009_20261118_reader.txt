Every extra hour of daytime napping tracked with a 13 percent higher death risk in 1,338 older adults.

After that nap finding, a Missouri survey on routines and pain, a report on semaglutide and Alzheimer's, and then GLP-1 drugs beyond weight loss.

Scientists at Mass General Brigham and Rush University Medical Center, in a JAMA Network Open paper carried by The News International, watched older adults in the Rush Memory and Aging Project for as long as nineteen years after ten days on a wrist monitor. One extra nap a day lined up with a seven percent higher chance of dying.

Morning naps lined up with a thirty percent higher mortality risk than afternoon naps. Irregular schedules did not. Chenlu Gao read heavy daytime sleep as a possible sign of illness, a chronic condition, broken night sleep, or a body clock out of step. The team called it a link, not a cause, and said more work has to come before those wrist patterns are clinically useful.

Eunjin Tracy at the University of Missouri, reported by JournalArta, surveyed sixty-seven older adults and did not test whether a steadier day treats anything. Thirty-seven of them reported insomnia, and that group also reported more pain and more depression.

People who woke, ate, saw others, and went to bed at more consistent times tended to report less physical pain and fewer depressive symptoms, and poor sleep did not wipe the pattern out. Tracy's own line was that, regardless of sleep quality, a regular daily routine went with lower pain and less depression than no routine.

The National Council on Aging says roughly half of adults sixty and older report insomnia symptoms. The write-up offers the circadian rhythm, including social jet lag between workdays and days off, as one possible explanation.

What is new against the November eleventh discussion of these drugs is a ScienceDaily report that major trials found semaglutide did not slow Alzheimer's disease. Other studies have linked GLP-1 medications to a lower risk of dementia, which is an association, not that trial result. Scientists suspect any memory effect may matter more in obesity or diabetes, and before thinking problems show up.

That suspicion is not a trial finding in the report. Ozempic and Mounjaro are named there as weight-loss drugs.

Partial reprogramming, in a preprint Lifespan.io describes, was tested in three human cell types, and a younger epigenetic age did not always arrive with better function. That is a cell study, not a trial in people, and not a product this report puts on a shelf.

Mount Sinai Hospital in New York, in a LinkedIn post, is launching a trial rather than publishing a finished human result. The plan pairs exercise with rapamycin, which the post calls an anti-fungal drug, and with spermidine, which it calls a supplement. The available text breaks off before the endpoints are named.

Whether any regulator has approved rapamycin or spermidine for an aging-related use is not established in that post.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 is a gut hormone released at a meal, and this week's mechanism is what outcome trials show beyond the weight those drugs are known for. A two thousand seventeen review in Nature Reviews Nephrology calls it an incretin: it acts on pancreatic islet cells, on how much a person eats, and on how the gut moves.

Those insulin-releasing actions are weaker in type two diabetes, which is why GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors were built to treat high blood sugar. The review also sketches a gut-kidney axis, a fast loop that may handle fluid and salts after eating, as a reason to look for a direct kidney effect.

The strongest evidence is pooled randomized trials against placebo. A two thousand twenty-four meta-analysis in The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology put together eleven trials and eighty-five thousand three hundred seventy-three people, twenty-nine thousand three hundred eighty-six of them women and fifty-five thousand nine hundred eighty-seven men.

In the sixty-seven thousand seven hundred sixty-nine who had type two diabetes, the drugs cut a composite kidney outcome by eighteen percent, hazard ratio zero point eight two. That composite meant kidney failure, a sustained filtration-rate drop of at least half, or death from kidney failure. Kidney failure alone fell sixteen percent, hazard ratio zero point eight four.

Major cardiovascular events, meaning cardiovascular death, a non-fatal heart attack, or a non-fatal stroke, fell thirteen percent, hazard ratio zero point eight seven. Death from any cause fell twelve percent, hazard ratio zero point eight eight.

The authors later added SELECT, registered as NCT03574597, a trial in people with cardiovascular disease and a body mass index of twenty-seven or higher who did not have diabetes. Those percentages are the diabetes-trial figures.

A two thousand twenty-one pool in the same journal, eight trials and sixty thousand eighty patients with type two diabetes, including AMPLITUDE-O, put the cardiovascular-event reduction at fourteen percent, hazard ratio zero point eight six.

That review also defined a kidney composite covering new macroalbuminuria, a doubled creatinine or at least a forty percent filtration drop, kidney replacement, or death from kidney disease. Its registration is PROSPERO CRD42021259711.

A two thousand nineteen pool, seven trials and fifty-six thousand four participants, cut major cardiovascular events by twelve percent, hazard ratio zero point eight eight. The trials were ELIXA for lixisenatide, LEADER for liraglutide, SUSTAIN-6 for semaglutide, EXSCEL for exenatide, Harmony Outcomes for albiglutide, REWIND for dulaglutide, and PIONEER 6 for oral semaglutide.

The abstract says the drugs have beneficial effects on cardiovascular, mortality, and kidney outcomes in type two diabetes.

Observational comparisons are a lower rung. An Eastern China database, twenty eighteen through twenty twenty-four, held one hundred five thousand three hundred ninety-one people starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist or another diabetes drug. After weighting and in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol models, other drugs lined up with higher rates than the GLP-1 drugs.

Cardiovascular hazard ratios were one point one nine for SGLT-2 inhibitors, one point four two for insulins, one point three zero for sulfonylureas, and one point four five for alpha-glucosidase inhibitors. Kidney-event ratios were three point zero nine for DPP-4 inhibitors, six point one one for insulins, and four point five six for meglitinides.

Liver-event ratios were one point four eight for DPP-4 inhibitors, two point zero six for insulins, one point five five for alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and one point four three for meglitinides. That is an adjusted association, not a randomized proof.

A U.S. MarketScan emulation looked at adults with fatty liver disease of the metabolic type plus type two diabetes, comparing a GLP-1 drug plus an SGLT-2 inhibitor with either drug alone. Against the GLP-1 drug alone, four thousand six hundred six combination patients were matched to eighteen thousand four hundred twenty-four.

Over a median six point three months, liver events were thirty-nine percent lower, hazard ratio zero point six one, and cardiovascular events thirty-five percent lower, hazard ratio zero point six five. Against the SGLT-2 inhibitor alone, five thousand three hundred sixty-eight were matched to twenty-one thousand four hundred seventy-two.

Over a median six months, liver risk was forty-three percent lower, hazard ratio zero point five seven, and cardiovascular risk twenty percent lower, hazard ratio zero point eight zero. Claims data and a few months of follow-up are not a multi-year placebo trial.

None of these abstracts show the heart or kidney differences standing apart from weight loss. None enrolled people to test frailty, biological age, or lifespan. A clinician's choice of drug can still shape a drug-versus-drug comparison. These papers describe the agonists as treatments for high blood sugar in type two diabetes, and popular reporting also calls some of them weight-loss drugs.

Approval to slow aging is not established here. SELECT is the registered human trial added to the twenty twenty-four pool.

The limit on the strongest finding is plain: an eighteen percent kidney reduction and a thirteen percent cardiovascular reduction versus placebo come from randomized trials in type two diabetes, not from a trial of aging, and they do not separate any effect from weight change.

One thing worth knowing is that longer or more frequent daytime naps can mark declining health in an observational study without the naps having been shown to cause death. A small survey linking steadier days to less pain and fewer depressive symptoms, even when sleep was poor, is an association, not a treatment result.

Major trials that failed to show semaglutide slowing Alzheimer's are not the same evidence as a reported link between GLP-1 drugs and lower dementia risk. And fewer heart, kidney, and death events than placebo, in randomized trials of type two diabetes, is a disease-outcome record, not a demonstrated treatment for aging.
