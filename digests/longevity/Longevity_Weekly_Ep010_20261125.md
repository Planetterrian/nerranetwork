# Longevity Weekly
> **Nasal mRNA drops restored youthful memory in aging mice without removing Alzheimer plaques.**

**What You Need to Know:** Nasal mRNA drops restored youthful memory in aging mice without removing Alzheimer plaques. In that animal study, University of Sydney researchers used lipid nanoparticles to deliver messenger RNA for brain-derived neurotrophic factor through the nose. Aged mice recovered learning and memory toward younger-animal performance, and mice engineered for Alzheimer-like disease were spared severe memory decline even though plaques stayed.

### The Week in Longevity
**New nose drops restore youthful memory and fight Alzheimer’s in aging mice: ScienceDaily**
In aged mice, an animal study from the University of Sydney found that experimental nose drops brought learning and memory back toward levels seen in younger animals. Professor Greg Neely’s group at the Charles Perkins Centre, writing in Advanced Science, enclosed messenger RNA in lipid nanoparticles so nasal cells would make brain-derived neurotrophic factor, a protein that helps neurons survive, connect, and adapt as memories form. The same animal experiments prevented severe memory decline in mice genetically engineered for Alzheimer-like disease. The team says the drops preserved brain function without removing Alzheimer-associated plaques, which is a different target from plaque-clearing approaches. Neely described the nose as a possible non-invasive route past the blood-brain barrier if human trials are eventually run; that step has not happened in this report. The group also named stroke, traumatic brain injury, and rare genetic conditions as possible later uses, which remains a statement of intent rather than a result in people. Source: [sciencedaily.com](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261123005337.htm)

**Antiprotozoal Drug Use Correlates with a Sizable Reduction in Age-Related Mortality and Disease Risk: Fight Aging!**
In people treated for protozoal infection versus untreated controls, an observational comparison — not a randomized aging trial — found large correlations with lower disease risk and mortality, Fight Aging! reports from an open-access paper. The drugs are those used against Toxoplasma gondii, the cause of toxoplasmosis, and other protozoa. The commentary argues the size of the association deserves attention, while also stating that healthier, more active, or more medically engaged people may be overrepresented among those who received the drugs. Two readings are left open: that toxoplasmosis is not harmless in people with normal immune systems, or that the drugs themselves produce a benefit. Because treatment in immunologically normal people is typically not long-term, a lasting drug effect on the pace of aging is presented as the less likely reading. These sources do not establish regulatory approval of antiprotozoal drugs for any aging-related use. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/antiprotozoal-drug-use-correlates-with-a-sizable-reduction-in-age-related-mortality-and-disease-risk/)

**N-MNA Produced by Gut Microbes Increases NAD Levels in Intestinal Cells: Fight Aging!**
In human intestinal cells, and in cells from fruit flies and mice, a laboratory study found that a microbe-made molecule, amide-N-methylnicotinamide or N-MNA, can be taken up and used in NAD redox reactions. NAD is a coenzyme required for mitochondrial function. Patients with gut dysbiosis and inflammation had less N-MNA in stool, an observational association, not proof that low N-MNA caused their disease. In mice, systemic N-MNA supplementation promoted intestinal stem-cell regeneration and gut epithelial repair; genetic work in Drosophila pointed to the NAD-consuming enzyme SIRT1. The Science Advances paper, as summarized by Fight Aging!, says N-MNA is converted to NAD+ through the Preiss-Handler pathway, independent of the salvage pathway, and that it carries a methyl group into host cells. Separately, the same commentary says clinical evidence that vitamin B3-derived NAD precursors produce material benefits in specific conditions is poor, and that those precursors are not as effective as exercise at raising NAD; approval of those precursors for an aging-related use is not established here. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/n-mna-produced-by-gut-microbes-increases-nad-levels-in-intestinal-cells/)

**Older adults wore a two pound vest with plates added weekly through a diet, and their resting burn barely moved while the bare diet group's fell hard: EcoPortal.net**
In older adults studied through a diet, a human comparison found that resting energy burn barely moved in people who wore a two-pound vest with plates added weekly, while it fell hard in the group that dieted without the vest. That is a measured difference in resting burn during weight loss, not a lifespan trial and not evidence about disease outcomes. The available report does not describe this as a regulator-reviewed aging treatment; a weighted vest is a behaviour in a study, not an approved therapy for aging. Source: [ecoportal.net](https://www.ecoportal.net/en/older-adults-wore-two-pound-vest/47401/)

**The Amygdala and Entorhinal Cortex Atrophy Before the Hippocampus in Early Alzheimer's Disease: Fight Aging!**
In people in the early stage of Alzheimer disease, imaging work described by Fight Aging! finds that observable brain atrophy does not begin in the hippocampus, the region most associated with memory. The amygdala and entorhinal cortex show that tissue loss earlier. The disease has a long early period in which symptoms are mild and hard to separate from ordinary cognitive aging, while biochemical and structural changes are already growing. Whether intervening in that window with tools now available can delay or avoid later Alzheimer disease is not established by this report. Source: [fightaging.org](https://www.fightaging.org/archives/2026/11/the-amygdala-and-entorhinal-cortex-atrophy-before-the-hippocampus-in-early-alzheimers-disease/)

### Mechanism of the Week: Inflammaging
In a 2026 genomic cohort of 153 treatment-naive patients with six cancer types, split at age 60, tumor mutational burden was higher in the older group: a median of 4.55 mutations per megabase against 2.84. That human observational result is a useful place to start, because it looks like aging and then mostly comes apart.

Inflammaging is the name given to chronic, sterile, low-grade inflammation that rises with age without a clear infection or injury. A 2007 perspective in Mechanisms of Ageing and Development framed a large part of the aging phenotype, including immunosenescence, as an imbalance between inflammatory and anti-inflammatory networks. Lifelong exposure to antigens and damaging agents keeps pro-inflammatory signaling going. In that account, healthy aging and longevity would require not only a lower tendency to inflame, but anti-inflammatory networks able to neutralize that burden. When those networks fail, the same imbalance is proposed as a driver of frailty and common age-related disease. The authors said the supporting evidence largely comes from studies in people, and they treated the idea as a systems problem rather than a single pathway.

A 2014 report from a National Institute on Aging session, published in The Journals of Gerontology, Series A, put the stakes in plain terms. Inflammaging was called a highly significant risk factor for illness and death in older people, because most age-related diseases share an inflammatory pathogenesis. The same paper said the precise cause, and any causal role in bad outcomes, remained largely unknown. Identifying pathways that control age-related inflammation across organs was framed as the open task, not as a finished map.

A 2018 review in Nature Reviews Endocrinology set out how the signal is thought to be sustained. A few innate-immune receptors recognize many inputs: pathogens, called non-self; cell debris and molecules that have ended up in the wrong place, called self; and nutrients plus gut microbes, called quasi-self. Metaflammation, the inflammation that accompanies metabolic disease and nutrient excess, was described as running on the same machinery. Gut microbes sit in the middle of both processes because they release inflammatory products, take part in circadian rhythms, and signal to other organs. The review argued that chronic diseases are not only consequences of aging; they can speed aging and look like accelerated aging. It also proposed that DNA methylation, glycomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics might separate biological age from chronological age in metabolic disease. That is a proposal for measurement, not a validation against lifespan.

The 2026 pan-cancer study, in Immuno-Oncology Technology, tested how far age-linked molecular patterns survive once cancer type is taken into account. Tumor mutational burden and the fraction of the genome altered came from whole-genome sequencing. Inflammatory pathway activity came from RNA sequencing and proteomics in paired tumor and normal tissue. Age groups did not have the same mix of cancers, so the main associations were rerun in multivariable models. Most age-associated molecular differences were substantially explained by cancer type. What remained was an age-related rise in normal-tissue inflammation at the protein level, and greater tumor-versus-normal inflammation amplification in MSI-high tumors: plus 0.065, with P equal to 0.015, and plus 0.087 after adjustment. Normal-tissue inflammation correlated with paired tumor mutational burden at the protein level, Spearman rs equal to 0.387. The authors state that this correlation is not evidence that systemic inflammaging drives genomic instability. Inflammation measured in the tumor itself did not show that link, rs equal to 0.07, P equal to 0.54.

Berberine, an isoquinoline alkaloid from plants such as Berberis species and Coptis chinensis, is discussed in a 2026 Fitoterapia review as a possible modulator of several of these processes. The review describes cell and animal mechanism work, not a randomized human trial of aging. It says berberine activates AMP-activated protein kinase through mild inhibition of mitochondrial complex I, which can suppress mTOR-linked anabolic signaling, raise autophagic flux and mitophagy, and support clearance of damaged mitochondria and aggregated proteins. It describes better respiratory efficiency, a limit on pathological reactive oxygen species with physiological redox signaling left intact, and higher antioxidant defenses through Nrf2. Anti-inflammatory actions are attributed to blocking NF-κB nuclear translocation and lowering transcription of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. The review calls berberine senomorphic: it may dampen the senescence-associated secretory phenotype without widespread apoptosis, partly through p16 and cyclin expression. These sources do not establish regulatory approval of berberine for any aging-related use, and the review itself flags pharmacokinetic challenges and safety considerations.

What is not established is the step from a shared inflammatory signature to a cause of aging that a treatment can safely interrupt. The 2014 session left etiology and causality open. The 2026 cancer cohort shows how easily an age association is really a disease-mix association. The mechanism sits on the hallmark of chronic inflammation. The strongest limit is the one the cancer study states outright: a correlation between inflamed normal tissue and tumor mutational burden does not show that inflammaging is what made the genome unstable.

### Worth Knowing
- A large link between past antiprotozoal treatment and later disease or death, seen observationally in people, can still be who got treated rather than proof the drug slowed aging.
- In a human diet comparison, holding resting burn steady with a light weighted vest is a metabolic finding during weight loss, not evidence that the vest lengthens life.
- In people with early Alzheimer disease, imaging that finds atrophy in the amygdala and entorhinal cortex before the hippocampus means memory-center shrinkage is a later chapter, not the opening one.
- A human cancer cohort can show higher mutational burden at older ages and still leave most of that age pattern explained by which cancers were in the sample.
