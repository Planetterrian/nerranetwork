Here is how it works when someone in the market may already know the answer.

A bookmaker who thinks a bettor has private information will not post the price a fully public crowd would justify. H. S. Shin's nineteen ninety-three model says the bookie builds a cushion, and does not spread it evenly. Longshots get the worse price. That gap is the favourite-longshot bias.

A two thousand three paper in the Bulletin of Economic Research looked at a variety of sports betting markets where bookmakers set the odds. The bias showed up. The exact shape was not the same from market to market.

The paper also found evidence for a central Shin prediction. Margins rise as the number of competitors rises. A bigger field gives an insider more places to hide a bet, and gives the bookmaker more reason to widen the take.

A two thousand two paper in Economica asked whether a sharp shortening of odds is information or a crowd chasing a crowd. N. F. R. Crafts had suggested in nineteen eighty-five that a pronounced shortening could signal an insider.

Those authors measured insider incidence, the Shin measure, at opening prices and again at starting prices. When odds shortened and that measure rose, betting returns were significantly positive.

When odds shortened and the measure fell, returns were negative. The authors read that second case as herd behaviour, not information.

So a move can be someone who knows something. That pulls the price toward the outcome and away from a fair deal for everyone else. Or it can be someone following the last trade, which adds noise and still hurts whoever came in late.

These studies are about bookmakers posting odds, not peer-to-peer event contracts, so the mechanism does not copy over one for one. On an exchange, nobody is forced to shade every contract. The cost shows up in the spread and the fee.

What does carry over is the tension. A venue that draws people trading on non-public information can print a price closer to the result and, at the same time, a price that is less fair to everyone without that information. The direction of the last trade does not, by itself, say which one just happened.
