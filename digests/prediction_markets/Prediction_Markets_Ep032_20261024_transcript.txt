Quoting both sides of an order book is how it works for a market maker.
Post a buy and a sell, collect the spread, and hold the inventory until someone takes the
other side.
A finished trade is the moment those two interests meet.
Until then, the person who posted the quote still carries the position.
That is the job a research paper studies on polymarket.
The authors call those books thin.
On a thin book, the maker's own quote may be the only price a later trader can hit.
They name three snags, a policy trained on real fills may simply stop quoting because
the trader who hits it can know more than the quote.
Profit or loss from a quote can show up many steps later so a learner has trouble tying
the result to the earlier post.
Level two feeds do not identify single orders and a simulator needs that detail.
Their fix was to rebuild order-level records in lobster format from Polymarket and to train
agents in Jack's Marl HFT on 417 real markets. They gave the baseline maker a wider view,
the book plus recent flow, and they changed its actions and its inventory controls.
They also split the desk into a quota and a risk manager, trained with recurrent independent
proximal policy optimization. To connect a late result to an early quote, they tried blended
rewards and a post-fill mark out, a score of how price moves after a fill. The redesigned single
agent quoted more often and lost less on held out markets than the retrained baseline.
Observations, actions, and risk limits were changed together, so the paper cannot say which
change did the work. A split desk with no careful rewards did poorly.
Shaped rewards beat that unshaped split and gave the authors their best result.
Scoring was end of episode portfolio value on markets kept out of training.
No configuration showed a positive average portfolio value on those periodic checks.
Each setup used one seed.
