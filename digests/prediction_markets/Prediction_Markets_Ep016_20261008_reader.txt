Here is how it works when the contract is a game rather than a news event. A sportsbook posts a number and takes the other side. An exchange contract does not work that way. Two traders agree a price. If the event happens, the buyer is paid a fixed sum. If it does not, the seller keeps the price. The venue need not hold a view. The last trade is the number.

If the other side is a pool, a formula and a pile of collateral set that number instead.

A twenty twenty-one paper in the Journal of Finance uses liquid sports-betting contracts to test pricing anomalies. Two features matter. There is no systematic risk of the equity kind, and the payoff is fixed outside the betting itself. That setup dodges the joint-hypothesis problem, so a gap between the traded price and the later result can be called mispricing rather than pay for risk.

The author finds strong momentum. The pattern fits delayed overreaction. It does not fit underreaction, and it does not fit rational pricing. The returns are a fraction of what financial markets pay, and they do not clear transaction costs, so arbitrage does not wipe the streak out. A pattern from that betting record also predicts value and momentum returns in U.S. equities.

That finding is a description of old betting books. It is not a method to copy, and it is not a verdict on any live contract.

A twenty twenty-three paper on arXiv looks at the pool design. It argues an automated market maker built like Uniswap is not lined up with fair odds, so liquidity providers hold a risk the formula never priced. The authors describe UBET's UAMM, smart contracts meant to set sports prices inside collateral pools, and they report experiments with positive outcomes.

That is a design result, not a forecast scorecard.

Read a sports-contract price as a trade, or as a formula's output, not as a house line and not as a settled chance. The Journal of Finance result says those trades can keep a momentum streak that costs make hard to trade away. A thin book is a weaker version of the same object.
