North American Derivatives Exchange, the CFTC-registered shop behind Crypto.com's platform, and Novig's Ludlow Exchange filed those complaints on October sixteenth.

They want federal judges to stop Missouri from applying its sports-wagering statute to the sports event contracts.

Attorney General Catherine Hanaway had already written both firms, plus Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog, on September sixteenth and seventeenth.

Her office announced the letters on September eighteenth. Each company got thirty days to pull the contracts or take a Gaming Commission license.

A license means a ten percent tax on adjusted gross sports-wagering revenue, and fees that can hit five hundred thousand dollars at the start and again every five years.

Ludlow became a designated contract market on June sixteenth. Its complaint calls the contracts swaps and says the Commodity Exchange Act gives the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction.

Novig wants a preliminary injunction so nothing changes while the Eighth Circuit briefs Kalshi's Iowa appeal, KalshiEX versus Bird.

Crypto.com's complaint was still off the public docket as of Sunday. Reporting on it says the company wants a declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction.

That filing says the letter went to Foris DAX, not the exchange, and that a Missouri license is not a real option because the state constitution allows only two direct mobile sports-wagering licenses and both are already awarded.

Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog had not sued in either Missouri federal district as of Sunday. Hanaway's office has described ongoing talks with Kalshi.

Her letters call the products unlicensed sports wagering. They say most federal courts to hear the question have found the Commodity Exchange Act does not preempt state gambling law, and that these contracts are not swaps.

She put it this way: companies cannot evade Missouri law by dressing sports betting up as event contracts. Her office also said Crypto.com lets users in at eighteen, under Missouri's floor of twenty-one, a complaint she raised with five of the six firms and not with Novig.

Tarek Mansour used a Washington stage this week to draw the industry's line. At DC Fintech Week twenty twenty-six, TheLines.com reports, he said Kalshi feels pretty good about the court fights.

He then called the stakes a choice of whether prediction markets exist in the United States or go offshore, and said federal jurisdiction under the Commodity Exchange Act is better than a fragmented patchwork of state laws.

Sports without economic consequence, he said, is a ludicrous notion.

Carly Johnson, research director at the American Gaming Association, described a different fight on Monday in the Las Vegas Sun. The association's study, Playing the Numbers, found gamblers outscored non-gamblers on financial literacy, and sports bettors came out on top in every group measured.

Prediction-market users, she said, have the confidence of sports bettors and the math skills of someone who does not bet at all.

Those users, in her account, sit on platforms offering sports contracts without the responsible-gaming tools state-licensed sportsbooks must provide, while the products are marketed as investing.

The association filed an amicus brief in October backing New Jersey's petition for the U.S. Supreme Court to decide whether states can regulate markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket.

The NFL filed its own brief. So did a coalition of thirty-nine states and Washington, D.C., which said the split has resulted in chaos nationwide.

Johnson's spending comparison is concrete. Regulated gaming puts about four hundred seventy-two million dollars a year into responsible-gaming programs, and gaming taxes supply over one hundred thirty million a year for problem-gambling services, where, she said, prediction markets pay zero.

Access to Kalshi, Polymarket and the rest depends on where you live. The rules differ by country, province and state, and they are changing.

Kalshi's question on who becomes President Trump's next press secretary is a new listing from the last three days. Scott Jennings and Anna Kelly are each at fifteen percent, Kelly up four points from the previous day's price, and Elizabeth Pipko is at five percent.

A ninety-five-cent contract is not free money, and how it works starts with the payout. The Globe and Mail describes these contracts as paying one dollar if the stated outcome hits and nothing if it does not, at a price somewhere between one cent and ninety-nine cents.

You lock that price, and any fee, until resolution. Five cents is the whole possible gain on a ninety-five-cent contract, and a miss takes the ninety-five cents. Those five cents are not interest.

Canadian dealers reach the same U.S. listings on a different fee card. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization lets authorized investment dealers offer a limited set of event contracts cleared through certain CFTC-regulated exchanges. Right now that permission covers Wealthsimple and Interactive Brokers Canada only.

Wealthsimple Predict offers a subset of Kalshi contracts and charges two U.S. cents per contract up front. A contract priced at forty-two U.S. cents therefore costs forty-four. A correct call pays one dollar. A wrong call loses the forty-four cents.

Interactive Brokers Canada charges two U.S. cents a contract on Kalshi and one U.S. cent on ForecastEx. Direct Kalshi fees, by contrast, move with the price and with how many contracts you buy.

One hundred contracts at one U.S. cent each would cost sixty-three U.S. cents in fees on Kalshi itself. The same hundred through Wealthsimple Predict would cost two U.S. dollars in fees, more than three times as much.

At fifty U.S. cents the gap shrinks. One hundred contracts would cost one dollar and seventy-five cents in Kalshi fees, against two dollars on Wealthsimple Predict.

Two cents is twenty percent of a ten-cent contract and four percent of a fifty-cent contract. Benjamin Felix, chief investment officer at PWL Capital, told the Globe that on a cheap contract those percentage fees get significant fast.

The contracts trade in U.S. dollars, so currency is another toll. Wealthsimple's standard conversion fee is one and a half percent, on a live rate that includes a spread, unless the client already holds U.S. dollars and moves them over.

Interactive Brokers Canada starts conversion at two-thousandths of one percent, with no added spread, but a two-dollar minimum means a small conversion costs more than that rate suggests.

Felix's line is that a per-contract fee plus a currency fee adds up quickly and aggressively, and that net of those costs the arithmetic stacks against the trader.

Subtract the fee before you read the gap to one dollar as a return, and subtract a conversion if your dollars are not the contract's dollars. On the Globe's one-cent example, the trading fee alone can exceed the price of the contracts. A price near a dollar leaves an even thinner remainder for a flat fee to eat. The number on the screen is the cost of a claim.
