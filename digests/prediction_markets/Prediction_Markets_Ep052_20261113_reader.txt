A base rate is how often a class of similar cases already produced a given result, counted before the details of the case in front of you get a vote, and that counting habit is how it works as a forecast. Start with the frequency, then adjust. Skip it, and you are doing what researchers call base rate neglect.

An AAPG Bulletin paper puts the miss on twenty-five exploration wells Lundin Petroleum drilled on the Norwegian Continental Shelf from twenty eleven through twenty fifteen. The class frequencies were the area's historical hit rate and the sizes of fields already found.

Against those, the explorers consistently and significantly lowballed geological probability of success and highballed how big a hit would be. The fix the paper argues for is ordinary and easy to skip. Write the historical hit rate and the historical size range first. Put the prospect's own geology beside that record, not in place of it.

A stack of careful case write-ups will still miss the historical distribution if each one is allowed to forget the class. The author says explorers drop the frequencies because those frequencies do not feel like they are about this well, and that folding the old rates into the evaluation, rather than parking them in another file, is what shrinks the error.

A twenty twenty-four paper on arXiv asks a harder question: what if the experts you are combining are neglecting the base rate themselves. Two people each hold a private signal and each state a probability for a yes-or-no world. A dial called lambda runs from zero, no use of the base rate, to one, a perfect Bayesian update.

The grade is worst-case regret against an omniscient benchmark, the yardstick from Arieli, Babichenko, and Smorodinsky in twenty eighteen. Regret traces a V. It does not simply fall as the experts get closer to perfect updating.

A lambda short of one can beat fully Bayesian posteriors on that worst-case score, because the person combining the forecasts does not know how the experts were informed, and extreme updated numbers can be the dangerous inputs in the worst case that combiner has to survive.

The authors sketch a combiner built to stay low-regret even when lambda is unknown, and they say they tested the neglect model. A Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience study splits who catches the conflict.

On the lawyer-engineer problem, the Cognitive Reflection Test tracked performance when the base rate and the stereotype pointed opposite ways. Short-term memory tracked the items where they did not conflict. In the scanner, conflict items lit the anterior cingulate more, even when the person still picked the stereotype.

Higher reflection scores lined up with more posterior cingulate activity on those items, which the authors link to shifting attention from the outside cue to an internal check. That is one lab task, not a book of trades. Reference class forecasting is the same start in project work.

A Project Management Journal paper set forecasts drawn from how similar projects actually finished against forecasts that tried to trace the path of the project in hand, including Monte Carlo simulation and earned value management. On real project data, the similar-projects method won for both cost and time. The argument inside this stack is not that base rates are optional.

The wells paper treats neglect as a fixable mistake. The aggregation paper says a perfect Bayesian number is not automatically the safest input when the combiner is blind to how the experts know what they know. A price on a contract is one case-level number from the people trading it. The base rate is the hit rate of the class that contract belongs to.

If those two sit far apart, the useful question is which class the resolution rules actually put the contract in, and whether the traders started there or started from the story. That comparison is a reading habit. It is not a side to take.
