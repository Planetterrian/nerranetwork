A price works as public information, and that is the how it works question today: who gets the forecast, and who paid to make it.

A binary contract pays a fixed sum if the stated event happens and pays nothing if it does not.

Where people can buy and sell it, the price is the point at which the other side has stopped arguing with money.

The cost of making that byproduct is the part the slogan leaves out.

A twenty twenty paper, Timely Information from Prediction Markets, treats these markets as machines that pull beliefs out of strategic agents and add them up.

Its warning is a waste channel. Agents can burn the social value by racing to be first to update the market.

The design problem is a trade-off between how fast the information arrives and how much that speed costs.

To keep the social gain, the market has to pay for information that is fast enough to be useful, not faster and dearer than it needs to be, and it has to get a truthful report on time.

The paper looks at two cases. Information that matters only before a deadline, and information whose value falls as the clock runs.

It says theorems and simulations demonstrate the mechanisms. It does not hand over a live accuracy score.

A second design asks what institutional liquidity does once it arrives. The paper is What Happens When Institutional Liquidity Enters Prediction Markets.

It sets a market-quality lens, splits the channels that liquidity can use, and maps the identification problems in live venue data.

The empirical piece is a synthetic microstructure lab, a proof of concept for the measurement pipeline, not an answer to the live question.

The lesson is deliberately narrow. Market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives and automation do not have to work through the same channel.

An average gain in liquidity does not have to become an equal gain for every trader.

The sharpest welfare losses are most likely in shock states, when slower takers get the least pass-through of a tighter quote.

So a posted price is a public number with a private production cost. A tighter quote is not proof the slowest reader captured it, and a few extra minutes of speed is not proof the race was worth what it burned.
