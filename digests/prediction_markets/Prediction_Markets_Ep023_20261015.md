**Hacksaw Launches a B2B Prediction-Market Platform: ReadWrite**
Hacksaw Group, a Stockholm-listed technology and game developer, has launched a business-to-business prediction-market platform so operators can offer peer-to-peer markets on Hacksaw's technology while keeping their own brands and customer relationships. ReadWrite reports that the Malta Gaming Authority extended Hacksaw's existing B2B Critical Gaming Supply License to include Type 3 Gaming Services, alongside its existing Type 1 authorization, which Hacksaw says authorizes B2B peer-to-peer prediction-market services under Malta's framework and does not establish where or when individual operators will offer the product. The company cited a Bernstein estimate that annual global trading volume in the sector could reach about 1 trillion dollars by 2030, and said that figure is not a forecast of Hacksaw's own business. Group CEO Ana Vrabic Verdir said, "Prediction markets represent a compelling new opportunity at the intersection of technology, entertainment and market participation." Hacksaw says it built the platform inside its existing cost base, and its shares trade on Nasdaq Stockholm under the ticker HACK. ### How It Works: Can AI Forecast?
A mention market is a contract that resolves on whether a specified keyword is spoken during a future public event. The paper "Forecasting Future Language: Context Design for Mention Markets" asks how the input to a large language model should be built when the forecast target is that kind of contract, including whether the live contract price is handed to the model as a prior.

The experiments use earnings-call mention markets: will a company say a specified keyword on its upcoming call. The authors vary two things. One is which text the model sees, news, prior earnings-call transcripts, or both. The other is how market probability, meaning the prediction-market contract price, is used. Market-Conditioned Prompting, or MCP, explicitly treats that price as a prior and instructs the model to update the prior with textual evidence, rather than re-predicting the base rate from scratch.

Walk the design without a dollar figure, because the abstract does not publish one. The contract already carries an implied probability that the keyword will be said. The model is given prior transcripts, news, or both. Under MCP it revises the market prior in light of that text instead of throwing the price out and starting a fresh base rate. MixMCP then mixes the market probability with that MCP forecast, dampening the model's posterior update with the market prior so the final number is not the model's unconstrained revision.

The paper reports three results. Richer context consistently improves forecasting performance. MCP, which updates the market probability rather than discarding it, yields better-calibrated forecasts. A mixture of the market probability and MCP outperforms the market baseline, and by dampening the model's update with the market prior it yields more robust predictions than either the market or the language model alone. The abstract also notes that recent work shows large language models can generate forecasts competitive with human forecasters. This paper does not re-run that comparison. Its claim is about context design on mention markets.

A second paper, "Making Alternative Data Work: Context-Augmented LLMs for Financial Forecasting," uses prediction markets in a different role. They are one channel of alternative data, with consumer transactions and web traffic, brought in to forecast a firm's future financial performance. The authors propose a two-agent framework. The first agent identifies the firms for which each alternative-data channel is likely to be informative. The second predicts revenue using firm- and channel-specific context. They evaluate the framework across four commercial alternative data channels. In those experiments, adding alternative data in context alongside other financial information improves the language model's forecasting relative to either source alone, and those forecasts are more accurate than those of standard forecasting baselines.

The limits are stated in the papers themselves. Firm-level alternative data often have limited historical coverage, are relevant only to specific prediction targets or subsets of firms, and arrive through heterogeneous channels, which is why they are hard to drop into a conventional forecast. On mention markets, the weaker design the authors test is a model that ignores the contract price and re-predicts the base rate. MixMCP exists because an undamped model update was less robust, in their tests, than a forecast that kept the market prior in the mix. No named outside critic is quoted in either abstract.

Read the contract price, after that, as a prior with a job, not as a verdict and not as a poll. In the mention-market design, the price is the starting probability, new text is evidence that may move it, and the more accurate setup in their tests was the one that refused to throw the price away. That is a description of a research design, not a reason to buy, sell, or trade any contract.
