Tuesday's published decision from the Fourth Circuit leaves Maryland free, for now, to enforce its gambling laws against Kalshi's sports event contracts. Legal Sports Report says the opinion is per curiam. Judges Roger Gregory and Stephanie Thacker affirmed a district judge's refusal to grant a preliminary injunction. Judge DeAndrea Benjamin dissented.

The only sentence all three share is that the district court's judgment is affirmed.

Gregory assumed the contracts were swaps and still found no sign Congress meant to take this power from Maryland. Nothing in the Commodity Exchange Act or the CFTC's regulations, he wrote, converts agency inaction into a determination carrying sweeping preemptive consequences. Thacker reached the same result by a different door.

She decided the contracts are not swaps, and she wrote that what is happening here is sports betting. Benjamin disagreed with both. In her dissent the payments depend on an event with economic consequences, so they are swaps, and the Act's exclusive-jurisdiction clause should keep Maryland from regulating them.

Maryland Attorney General Anthony Brown said Wednesday that Kalshi now has to meet the same consumer-protection standards as every other sportsbook serving Maryland residents.

Dominick Freda, legal director at Better Markets, which filed an amicus brief for the state and pointed to similar results in the Sixth and Ninth Circuits, said the judges in the majority just couldn't agree on which of Kalshi's arguments to avoid state gambling laws was worse, because a bet on who wins Sunday's game is a sports bet.

Forecasts that are too sure, and how researchers measure that gap, is where how it works starts. Overprecision, in a twenty thirteen paper in Psychological Science, is excessive confidence that you know the truth. Those authors did not take a spoken confidence band at face value.

They watched how little a person moved a point estimate once the cost of guessing too high differed from the cost of guessing too low.

Picture the penalty for overshooting as the heavier one. Someone who is genuinely unsure should slide the guess toward the cheaper mistake, and a bigger slide should mean a wider uncertainty. The paper compared the precision implied by that slide with what the person actually knew. People moved less than their knowledge justified. Subjective confidence drove the shortfall.

They behaved as if the right answer lived in a tighter band than their record supported.

A different pile-up shows up when the forecast is a probability across models. A twenty twenty paper, When are Bayesian model probabilities overconfident, finds that a posterior often concentrates on one model even when other measures of fit, or of forecasting ability, show no strong preference. A moderate change in the sample can then move that concentration onto a different model.

The authors derive the sampling variance of the Bayes factor in univariate and multivariate linear regression.

Averaging several experts does not, by itself, fix the first problem. A twenty twelve paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management compared Cooke's classical model, equal-weight linear pooling, and the single best expert, on a large database of real-world judgments, with leave-one-out cross-validation. Both pooling methods beat the best expert, so more than one voice helped.

Linear pooling still did not effectively counteract expert overconfidence. Cooke's model was more sensitive to experts leaning on one another than equal weights were, and high dependence generally produced much poorer aggregated results, while equal weights held up better across dependence levels.

Broadening subjective intervals may occasionally soften overconfidence, that paper says, and the number of experts and the number of seed questions also change how well the aggregate is calibrated.

Calling all of that one trait is the dispute. A twenty eleven paper in the Journal of Behavioral Decision Making found that miscalibration on an interval-production task correlated across two general-knowledge sets given at different times, which looks like a trait. Harder questions brought more overprecision and, at the same time, more underplacement of one's rank against other people.

Dispositional optimism and self-efficacy did not track that interval miss. Measures of a tendency toward positive illusions correlated with each other, failed to correlate with the interval task, and correlated negatively with optimism about societal risks. In a final study, that same tendency did predict miscalibration when the same questions were scored by asking people to state probabilities.

The authors' conclusion is that overconfidence is a series of overlapping constructs, not one thing.

A calibration chart is the picture of that gap. Stated probability sits on one axis. The share of those statements that later came true sits on the other. If prices near the top of the scale resolved yes about as often as the price implied, the points would fall near the diagonal. Overprecision is the bend off that line.

High stated chances come true less often than the price suggests, and low stated chances come true more often. None of these papers tests a named venue, and none of them marks a particular contract as too high or too low. A price that has rushed toward certainty is still a claim about frequency.

It has to be checked against how often similarly sure prices, on that kind of question, were right, and against whether the people behind it were independent or were all updating on the same story.
