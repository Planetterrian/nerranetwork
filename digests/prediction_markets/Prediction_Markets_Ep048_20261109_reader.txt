Who gets paid when a price is right is a mechanism question, and that is how it works in the studies, not a tip. A Journal of Prediction Markets paper looked at four real-money markets that paid on a movie's box office. Before trading began, every participant turned in a detailed forecast of that film. The information was their own work.

Nobody was randomly dealt a perfect tip, which is the usual lab trick for creating insiders. Whether the forecast was any good could be scored only after the box office number existed. Volume and the timing of trades lined up with overconfidence. People who had done the analysis traded as if the analysis were more decisive than it was.

In three of the four markets, prices sat where that prior information said they should. In those three, traders whose forecast was tied to the contract that paid had significantly higher returns than traders pointing at a different contract. The fourth market showed no significant return gap between those groups.

Four markets, three prices matching the walk-in information, one market with no significant payoff difference. There is no win rate, no dollar profit, and no share captured by a small club in that account. The authors' conclusion, which is not something to follow, is that when people are overconfident and trade that way, better information can be worth something if it is actually accurate.

A Journal of Futures Markets paper splits the money instead of the forecasts. On the floor of the Sydney Futures Exchange, local traders' income breaks into liquidity profits and position-taking profits. Those locals made significant position-taking profits. That ability rose with order-flow information.

It fell when outside information showed up, when their own liquidity profits were larger, and when they held inventory longer. The authors call them active informed traders, not a desk that only earned the spread for standing there.

A different Journal of Futures Markets paper disputes the idea that order flow means someone knows the future. On Shanghai Stock Exchange fifty ETF options, call-side order imbalance moved with higher underlying returns at the same time, put-side imbalance with lower returns, and both reversed quickly. The authors tie that reversal to temporary pressure from market makers delta-hedging.

Two checks in the paper point to inventory risk, not informed trading. Flow that looks smart can be a hedge being laid off.

So a contract price can match what traders already believed, and the people whose belief matched the outcome can be the ones who made more, except in the movie market where that gap never showed. On a futures floor, part of the profit was a position taken against order flow, and it shrank when outside news arrived or inventory sat.

In the options market, the same-looking imbalance was a hedge, and it reversed. That is a reason a price can speak to an event and still say almost nothing about whether any one trader is the person who gets paid.
