A forecast horizon, and how it works, is the lead time where a forecast stops beating a dumb baseline — climatology in weather, often a no-predictability null in finance. That cutoff is not one number. It shifts with the variable, with a point target versus an average, and with how hard the score treats uncertainty.

A Quarterly Journal of the Royal Meteorological Society study of ECMWF monthly ensembles defines that horizon as the lead where the ensemble stops beating a climatological distribution, scored with the continuous ranked probability score. Across one year of thirty-two-day forecasts, instantaneous grid-point fields lose that edge somewhere between sixteen and twenty-three days.

Averages over time, or over space, keep an edge considerably longer. The paper's reason the old two-week ceiling has moved is concrete: the forecast is a probability, ensembles estimate a distribution, the models carry more pieces, including a dynamical ocean and ocean waves, and the runs start from sharper initial conditions.

A Bayesian latent-factor study in the Review of Economics and Statistics takes the other end of the clock. It models GDP per capita for one hundred thirteen countries across the one hundred eighteen years from nineteen hundred to twenty seventeen. Convergence speeds differ a lot.

Some are so slow a country might not converge, or diverge, inside a century of data, and the correlations are sparse — convergence clubs, in the paper's words. The joint model builds a predictive distribution for output paths over the next one hundred years, the kind used when someone simulates the costs of climate change.

Pooling across countries tightens the intervals relative to country-by-country information. Even so, those hundred-year growth paths stay very wide.

Finance has a parallel measurement fight. A twenty eleven paper in the Journal of Applied Econometrics goes back to Hodrick's nineteen ninety-two reverse regression, short-horizon returns run on the long-run mean of a predictor. That older tool can test only a null of no predictability, and it assumes stationary regressors.

The later paper argues reverse regressions still avoid serious size distortions when some predictability is present and when regressors sit near unit roots, and that the method stays usable when predictors pile up. On excess bond returns and the term structure of forward rates, the authors find some forecastability.

The confidence intervals on the coefficients come out about twice as wide as the conventional approach.

Water Resources Research splits two horizons for one water-supply reservoir. The longest is how far ahead you need to see to set an optimal release now if you ignore forecast error. The effective horizon is the reach that still gives as much decision information as a chosen reliability allows.

Reliability there is an error bound: the biggest gap between optimal releases under any two inflow scenarios. The longest horizon stretches with a bigger reservoir, calmer inflows, and a less uncertain forecast. The effective horizon stretches when the forecast is less uncertain, the allowed error is larger, and the ending-storage estimate is more accurate.

A nineteen ninety-three Science paper is the counterweight. Tropical Pacific sea-surface temperature was forecast first, at long lead. Those ocean forecasts then drove an atmospheric model. For seven large climatic events from the nineteen seventies into the nineteen nineties, the climate forecasts agreed well with observations over many regions.

Long-range skill showed up when a slower piece was forecast alone and everything else was conditional on it.

Read a years-out contract with that split in mind. Clean skill against climatology, for raw grid-point weather, is mostly a matter of weeks in the ensemble evidence, while averages last longer. A century-ahead growth distribution can be tightened by pooling and still be very wide.

A contract price is one point standing in for a distribution this research says gets harder to pin down as the resolution date recedes. It is a price with a horizon on it, not proof the far question has been solved.
