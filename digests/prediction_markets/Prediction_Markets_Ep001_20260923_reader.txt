A price gets read as a probability, and here is how it works.

Picture a contract that pays a set amount if the event occurs and pays zero if it does not.

It changes hands somewhere between those two payoffs.

A buyer is treating the event as more likely than that price.

A seller is treating it as less likely.

Where both sides can trade, the price is the spot where that disagreement has cleared for now.

Those are the terms on which a counterparty was last found, not a verdict the venue handed down.

Management Science published a comparison of that reading with another way of pooling forecasts.

More than two thousand four hundred participants forecast two hundred sixty-one events over two seasons of a geopolitical tournament.

They were randomly assigned to prediction markets or to prediction polls.

The markets were continuous double auctions, and people were ranked on earnings.

In the polls, probabilities were submitted alone or in teams, and ranking used Brier scores.

Across both seasons, prices from the markets beat the plain average of the poll forecasts.

Team polls came out ahead of the markets once forecasts were combined using temporal decay, weights drawn from past performance, and recalibration.

The polls' largest edge was at the opening of long-duration questions.
