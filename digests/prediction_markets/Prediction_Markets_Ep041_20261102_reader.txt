A replication market prices whether a published study will hold up when someone runs it again, and that mechanism is how it works in this research.

Traders buy if they think the finding survives and sell if they think it fails.

The contract pays on a successful rerun and pays nothing if the rerun breaks the result.

Nobody in the market has to touch a lab bench.

A twenty fifteen paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences built markets on forty-four studies from prominent psychology journals.

Those papers were the ones later rerun in the Reproducibility Project: Psychology.

The list was fixed, so the questions did not wander with the news.

Each claim settled on an actual replication, not on whether the original authors still liked their paper.

Those prices predicted the replication outcomes well, and they beat a survey of individual forecasts.

The same paper notes a cost estimate used to explain the hurry: irreproducible preclinical research alone has been put at twenty-eight billion dollars a year in the United States.

That dollar figure is not a market profit. It is the waste a faster screen is supposed to help avoid.

The authors say nothing else was in place to flag weak findings quickly, and that markets fit that gap.

A twenty twenty-five paper in the Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society starts from the cracks in the older design.

Thin liquidity, slow payouts, and scarce experts are the three faults it names.

One arrival can shove a thin price. A correct trader may wait years to get paid. The people who know the method may never trade.

The proposal blends three automated market makers with reputation-based staking and staged payouts.

A university or a grant agency can pick among three built-in pricing rules meant to keep the price meaningful when only a few people show up.

Reputation can back a position, not only cash, and staged payouts pay before the final replication lands.

The authors call that a triage tool for funders and a way for researchers to gauge a new finding, in a market they describe as liquid and intellectually rewarding.

Those forty-four psychology papers are the job the twenty fifteen markets actually did, and they beat the survey.

The later paper treats thin books and slow pay as the standing weakness, and it answers with that hybrid.

Read a science price as a probability under stated rules, settled by a replication, not by the journal's name.

A survey is a different tool. On those forty-four studies, the market did better.

A price with almost no one on the other side is the fault the hybrid is built to repair.

None of this is a suggestion to trade anything. It is a reason to ask who can trade, when they get paid, and what event settles the question.
