Pooling several forecasts is how it works when a published probability is built from more than one source, and the research keeps finding that an equal-weight average beats the mix someone tried to optimize. That result has a name in the literature: the forecast combination puzzle. The ordinary build is a weighted average.

Each forecaster, poll or model gets a weight, and the weights are picked so old errors get smaller. The plain split of the same forecasts is what keeps winning on robustness.

A 2015 paper on arXiv looks at three reasons. Weights are estimated badly. The weighting formula does not fit the data. Or models get screened out before the pool is even formed. The paper adds a split that earlier write-ups skipped. Combining for adaptation means you do not know which single forecast will be best, so you want a blend that stays near whichever one wins.

Combining for improvement means you think the blend can beat every candidate, including the best one. Use the wrong one of those two jobs and you can manufacture the puzzle yourself. The fix that paper offers is a multi-level AFTER strategy. AFTER is sequential weighting.

The multi-level version treats the simple average as just another candidate, so the scheme can drop back to equal weights when the estimated ones are too noisy, and it can still lean on a strong forecast when the record supports the lean.

A 2023 paper, also on arXiv, says part of the puzzle is the test, not the forecast. The way these combinations are usually produced leaves accuracy tests with low power, and without proper size control. Under a null of no inferior accuracy, the test statistic does not follow the standard normal distribution people usually assume. The asymptotic distribution is non-standard.

A test that is too quick to bless the simple average will keep blessing it. That paper says the weak power can be avoided, when the data allow it, by estimating the combinations more efficiently. It shows this for a functional of interest and for predictive densities, and it walks a short example on daily financial returns so a researcher can see how to step around the puzzle.

Probability forecasts break in a further way, and that one is in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society in 2010. Linear pooling — a weighted average of probabilities — is the method people reach for. Start with two or more distinct forecasts that are each calibrated, meaning events happen at the rate the forecast names.

Any weighted average that is not trivial is then necessarily uncalibrated, and it lacks sharpness. The pool gets pulled in toward the middle relative to the sharper input. You need a recalibration even in that ideal case. The tool in that paper is a beta-transformed linear opinion pool.

A beta curve, fit along with the weights, can push the pooled number out toward zero or one, or pull it back in, after the linear average is done. The authors show it in a simulation and in a case study that pairs statistical forecasts with National Weather Service probabilities of precipitation.

A 2021 paper on arXiv takes the reason ensembles sit on a shelf. Dynamic combinations, the kind that move the weights as the series moves, can forecast well and still never ship, because they are expensive to run and hard to explain. Model compression trains a single model to imitate the stack.

In those experiments the compressed model matched the ensemble closely enough to call the performance comparable, and the computing cost fell drastically. The compressed model with the best average rank was a rule-based regression, so the forecast also got easier to read.

Time series make that compression harder than the neural-network cases the field usually studies, because the data do not sit still.

A market price is already a pool of other people's probabilities. Set it next to a poll, a model and a second venue, and an equal-weight average is the blend this research treats as the sturdy default when you are adapting and you do not have a thick sample for estimating weights.

Estimated weights have a job when you are trying to improve on every input and you can estimate them without spending the gain on the estimation itself. A straight average of calibrated probabilities is not a calibrated probability. It wants that beta-style recalibration before anyone treats the blend as sharp. Knowing that changes how you read a published number.

It is a pool, the pooling rule changes the number, and none of this is a method for picking a contract.
