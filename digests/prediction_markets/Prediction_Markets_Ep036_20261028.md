> **The CFTC's tenth state lawsuit asks a court to stop Utah from shutting Kalshi.**

**What You Need to Know:** The CFTC's tenth state lawsuit asks a court to stop Utah from shutting Kalshi. The commission announced the suit on October 27, after Utah sued the exchange on October 20 over fees the state has not been awarded. Separately, the agency plans to publish its Commitments of Traders report twice a week by the end of 2026.

### Top Story
On October 27 the Commodity Futures Trading Commission announced its tenth state lawsuit, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Utah against the state and Attorney General Derek Brown, seeking injunctions that would stop Utah from enforcing its gambling laws against CFTC-regulated event contracts. The United States joined the filing, which follows Utah's October 20 case against Kalshi and asks the court to treat the contracts as swaps on designated contract markets under the Commodity Exchange Act. Utah's complaint seeks fee recovery it puts between 30.6 million and 61.2 million dollars and alleges about 5.5 million dollars in resident trading losses; those figures are what the state is asking for, not what a court has ordered. On August 4, 2026, U.S. District Judge Robert J. Shelby ruled that the Commodity Exchange Act does not preempt Utah's gambling laws as applied to Kalshi's sports contracts, and on September 8 the Tenth Circuit denied an injunction pending appeal. Attorney General Derek Brown said anyone offering gambling to Utahns is on notice that it is illegal and that no Utah law authorizes it; gambling has been banned in the state constitution since 1896. Kalshi spokesperson Jacki McGavick said the platform answers to federal law and that no single state should have a veto over it. Source: [cftc.gov](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9315-26)

### How It Works: A Short History: From Iowa to Today
A contract on the Iowa Electronic Markets paid off from an election result, and the price was the forecast. The design is the part that still matters. A winner-takes-all contract pays if one side receives more votes than the other, and pays nothing otherwise, so the price is a chance of finishing ahead. A vote-share contract pays according to the share of the popular vote, so the price is a forecast of the margin, not of the winner.

A paper in PS: Political Science & Politics walks through both designs for the 2024 presidential popular vote, read on September 29. The vote-share market was thinly traded, and it priced a 9-percentage-point Democratic popular-vote margin. The winner-takes-all market was thickly traded, and it priced an 85.7 percent chance that the Democrat would receive more votes than the Republican. The authors then built one distribution from both markets. That combined reading was a 6- to 7-percentage-point Democratic margin, and an 87.0 percent chance the Democrat would finish ahead in the popular vote. The paper says significant uncertainty remained.

The arithmetic is the lesson. Start with the thin vote-share price: a 9-point Democratic margin. That number is not a probability of winning. It is a priced margin. Move to the thick winner-takes-all price: 85.7 percent that the Democrat receives more votes. That number is not a margin. Combine the two, as the paper does, and the margin shrinks to 6 to 7 points while the chance of finishing ahead rises slightly, to 87.0 percent. Same election, same exchange, three different readings, because the contract and the sample of trades changed.

A second paper, in Political Science and Politics, used Iowa Electronic Markets prices on congressional control in the 2014 election to see what moved in the fall of 2013. When the federal government shut down, Republican chances fell dramatically, and they did not recover when the shutdown ended. Eliminating filibusters for presidential nominations, the step known as the nuclear option, had a negative impact on Democratic chances. The rollout and reporting of the Affordable Care Act, and announcements that incumbents would not run again, had little effect. A budget resolution reinforced the prices already in the market. The authors' reading is that political rhetoric did not appear to move congressional-control prospects, while deliberate partisan actions in Congress lined up with worse prices for the party that started them, and a bipartisan initiative lined up with better prices for the party that started it.

That causal claim is the disputed part. A price change after an event is an association in a market, not a controlled test that the event caused the move. The 2024 paper shows a second limit inside the same exchange. The thin vote-share market and the thick winner-takes-all market did not agree, and the combined distribution did not match either one exactly. A listener who wants one number will have to pick a contract first.

Read the payoff before the price. A margin contract and a yes-or-no contract on the same race are different forecasts, and a thin market is not interchangeable with a thick one even when both sit on the Iowa Electronic Markets. These papers do not transfer those 2024 or 2013 prices onto any later commercial venue. They show what changed the reading when the contract, the thickness of the market, and the event were named. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1049096524000921)
