> **Three states stay off Phantom's Kalshi rollout for other US users.**

**What You Need to Know:** Three states stay off Phantom's Kalshi rollout for other US users. The wallet is opening Kalshi event contracts inside the app for people who clear Kalshi's identity checks, with trades funded mainly in Phantom's CASH stablecoin, while Michigan, Nevada and Washington stay restricted. The day also brings a Pentagon limit on troops and nonpublic information, a CFTC warning to two smaller venues, and Polymarket's firing of its chief marketing officer during a promotional-practices investigation.

### Top Story
Phantom is opening Kalshi event contracts to users in the United States inside the Phantom app, through a commercial partnership with KalshiEx, once those users finish Kalshi's know-your-customer process and link or create a Kalshi account. Trades are funded primarily with Phantom's CASH stablecoin; Phantom says it is a non-custodial interface and does not take custody, and users become direct members of Kalshi, described in the announcement as a CFTC-registered designated contract market and derivatives clearing organization. Kalshi shares with Phantom a portion of the exchange fees Kalshi receives on event contracts traded through that interface. Max Crowley, Kalshi's vice president of business development, said Phantom did the regulatory work up front and called it a model for reaching more people. CoinGape reports CFTC no-action relief for Phantom in March 2026, letting non-custodial interfaces connect users to registered exchanges without registering as brokers, after a December integration that had kept U.S. users out and a June shift of new non-U.S. positions to World Prediction Markets, a non-custodial Solana protocol. The Block reports market restrictions in Michigan, Nevada and Washington; CoinGape says state lawsuits still challenge whether CFTC oversight preempts state gambling laws, while Phantom general counsel Kevin Jacobs said the company is "confident and comfortable" proceeding because it sees CFTC jurisdiction as clear, and that Phantom aims to follow state-level limits as those cases go on. ### How It Works: Play Money, Real Money and Reputation
A market price is a number between a miss and a full payout. What that number means depends on what the trader actually loses. On Manifold, the unit is mana. The venue says mana is play money, not dollars, and that its prices are forecasts from traders with nothing but reputation at stake. A Kalshi description of sports event contracts says those contracts settle at one dollar if the named outcome hits and at zero if it does not, so the cash result, if the contract pays, is the gap between the trading price and one dollar. One venue keeps score in a token the house does not cash out as dollars. The other sells a claim on a dollar.

Walk through one play-money reading, using only the figures on the page. Manifold's question is whether another millennium prize problem will be solved in 2026. It was read at 14:52 UTC on October 23. Yes stood at a 33 percent implied probability. The page listed 699 traders. Twenty-four-hour volume was 22,000 mana. The market opened on September 8, 2026. None of those figures is a dollar bid. A trader who buys Yes is taking the play-money side that the chance is higher than that 33 percent price. A trader who sells is taking the side that it is lower. If the forecast is wrong, what the venue says is at stake is mana and reputation, not a cash account. If the forecast is right, the gain is mana. The percent can still be read as an implied probability, because the market is built as a price for Yes versus No. It cannot be read as a cash consensus, because the page says the stake is not dollars.

That design is why a play-money forecast can be serious without being a bankroll. People who want a record as good forecasters have a reason to move the price toward what they believe, even when the token will not pay rent. The same design is where it fails. Reputation binds the trader who cares about the record. It binds less when the question is obscure, when few people are watching the forecast, or when the point of the position is the game rather than the call. Today's Manifold pages separate mana from dollars in the same sentence as the price. They do not say the price is a dollar-weighted average of belief.

A cash contract fails in the other direction. The dollar payout can draw people who want the payout more than they want the forecast to be right. The Kalshi explainer of those event contracts says trading is risky, a total loss can occur, and past performance is not indicative of future results. That is a warning about the money, not a claim that the price is the right probability. A listener who knows the split reads the unit before the percent. Mana plus a trader count is a reputation market: the 33 percent on the millennium-prize question is that kind of number, on 22,000 mana of daily play-money volume, among 699 traders. A contract that pays at most one dollar is a cash price for a defined payout. Neither number is a statement that the outcome will happen. Neither is a reason to take a side. The useful read is what is risked, how the rules pay, and whether the crowd is staking reputation or cash.
