Adverse selection is the loss a quote-setter takes when the other side knows more, and how it works is a wider spread plus a price that jumps once that order teaches the book something. The person who has seen what the maker has not buys the cheap side and sells the rich side. The maker is the one who loses the fill.

In the studies that follow, the reply is to charge uninformed flow enough to cover those losses, and to step the quote away after an order that carries news, because the order is the news.

An arXiv paper, Market Informedness and Market-Maker Profitability, builds a market where makers differ in what they know and in how hard they try to avoid inventory. Prices are made inside the market. A fundamental wanders on its own. Taker orders are self-exciting, so one burst makes the next more likely.

The authors show that flow stays stable over a finite horizon, then train the makers with multi-agent reinforcement learning, shared training and separate execution.

When few participants are informed, an informed taker can hit a quote that has not moved, and the maker cannot tell that order from noise. The paper finds that kind of flow especially damaging, with severe adverse-selection risk. Lift how much of the market is informed, and more orders already carry the fundamental, so endogenous prices move with them.

A late quote can still be picked off, but it is late by less, and the maker's other fills sit closer to what is known. Profitability then trends up overall. The path bends back in places, from messy dynamics and from learning that is itself random. The authors read that as price discovery paying for the cost of being picked off.

A second arXiv paper, When A I Trading Agents Compete, trains a fast maker with reinforcement learning inside a Hawkes order-book model, so impact comes from the book rather than from a formula someone assumed. The maker does not reprice on every tick. The job is impulse control, trained with proximal policy optimisation and self-imitation.

Set against a slower trader working a large order in pieces, the maker learns to use the drift that sliced order creates. The quote moves because of the order. That move is the book learning who is trading. Bigger profits for the learning maker did not necessarily mean much larger slippage for the slower one.

Informed Trading Intensity, in the Journal of Finance, is a machine-learning measure trained on a class of informed trades. It rises before earnings, before mergers and acquisitions, and before news.

The authors tie it to return reversal and to asset pricing, and say it works because it catches nonlinear ties among informed trading, volume, and volatility, including impatient informed trading and informed trading that shows up in common across names.

A Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis study of options around stock-split announcements finds traders do expect volatility to rise after a split. Evidence they also expect an abnormal jump in the share price is only weak, and there is little evidence those option measures forecast the splitting firms' returns.

A quote can learn that uncertainty changed and still be a poor read on direction.
