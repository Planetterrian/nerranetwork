A futures price is a deal to exchange a commodity on a date that has not arrived, and the research
treats that price as a forecast of the later spot.
You wait out the horizon and ask whether today's future's price was a biased guess at the spot that actually printed.
The thing being scored is a later price or the direction of that price, not a yes or no event with a fixed payoff.
A 2009 neural network study of crude oil runs that test in days.
The authors started with lagged, cleaned up spot prices, and found a dynamic model of 13 lags the best short-term benchmark for direction.
Then they added cleaned up futures for 1, 2, 3, and 4 months to maturity.
First one maturity at a time, then all of them.
The network got the direction right 78% of the time, one day out, 66% two days out,
and 53% three days out.
Every run that included futures found information in those prices that the lag spot alone did
not have.
And IMF working paper in 2011 looked at 10 commodity futures, with horizons out to two
years. Inside the sample, tests rejected efficient markets. Outside the sample, the futures
forecast was hard to beat. Skill did not depend on whether the curve sloped up or down,
which cuts against models that treat the slope as a signal, and it did not depend on whether
prices were rising or falling. A 2013 paper in the Journal of Futures markets split that
record-by-group. Precious and base metals failed most unbiased tests and did a poor job predicting
later price changes. Energy and agricultural futures did much better. Liquidity did not explain
the gap on the evidence the authors had, and they documented a broad drop in the predictive content
of commodity futures since the early 2000s. A 2019 paper in management science draws a line
between matching the futures curve and forecasting the spot. Pricing models can fit the term structure
and how it moves, and still fail to produce accurate true distributions of spot prices.
The authors say a reasonable expected spot curve needs analysts' forecasts alone or
alongside futures.
Using both, rather than forecasts alone, barely moves the short and medium expected spot
curve, but the long-term estimates differ by a lot.
Adding forecasts to futures rather than using futures alone, likewise leaves the near and
future's curve mostly in place and shifts the long-term future's estimates. A 2015 study
fit the crude oil curve with a dynamic Nelson Siegel model and forecast it with a focus-time
delay neural network across 24 years of prices. Recessions and crises included. Calls 1, 3, 6,
and 12 months ahead beat the benchmark models by a significant margin with the lowest errors
at every maturity. A 2024 paper trained a convolutional network on aerial photos of winter
hard-read wheat, and on cloud cover, standing in for yield, to call the wheat futures price 20 days
ahead. Distinctive code and distinctive pictures can show positive alpha in a short window,
the author's report, and that edge can fade fast once the images are easy to get or the trade gets
crowded. So Rita Futures price is a forecast of a later spot at a named horizon,
