> **Thirty-six billion dollars New York seeks from Kalshi stay reachable after a judge refused a freeze.**

**What You Need to Know:** A federal judge refused to freeze New York's case against Kalshi, so penalties the attorney general has put as high as thirty-six billion dollars remain in reach. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission had asked for a temporary restraining order and did not get one. The commission's own case is not finished.

### Top Story
On Friday, Judge Lorna Schofield of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York denied the Commodity Futures Trading Commission a temporary restraining order that would have stopped New York Attorney General Letitia James and the New York State Gaming Commission from taking enforcement actions against Kalshi. The bid followed, by a day, a different federal judge's remand of James's lawsuit to state court. Schofield ruled that the commission had not shown a sufficient chance of succeeding on the merits, according to Jonathan Stempel of Reuters; the CFTC had argued a freeze was needed to avoid irreparable harm to its claim to be the exclusive regulator of prediction-market trading. The denial is not a final judgment in the CFTC's case against New York officials, and Kalshi's own federal suit against those officials is still unresolved on the merits after that court denied Kalshi a preliminary injunction. James and the gaming commission can press on from a cease-and-desist already issued by New York gambling regulators, including discovery on in-state advertising, users under 21, and sports-event trading, and damages James has asked a state court to set as high as $36 billion, with a possible effect on Kalshi's headquarters operations in the state. Kalshi says the lawsuit could shutter the company and shut it down everywhere, and has sought to halt it; CFTC Chair Michael Selig has said designated contract markets must offer the same access across the United States and in August ordered them to ignore New York court orders limiting in-state access, a reading federal judges have criticized and members of Congress have asked him to reverse. **Casten, Lieu Bill Targets Prediction Market Insider Trading: RG.org**
Rep. Sean Casten of Illinois introduced H.R. 11092 on Thursday, and with Rep. Ted Lieu of California announced the Policing Insider Trading in Prediction Markets Act of 2026 in a Friday press release; Congress.gov shows the bill referred to the House Judiciary Committee and the Committee on Education and Workforce. Under text posted by Casten's office, the attorney general would pay whistleblowers who voluntarily give original information that leads to successful resolution of a covered enforcement action, at least 10 percent and no more than 30 percent of the monetary sanctions, from the Justice Department's Assets Forfeiture Fund. A covered action is a criminal investigation, action, or settlement by the attorney general, tied to a federal fraud offense involving a prediction-market platform, that results in monetary sanctions of more than $1 million; sanctions include penalties, disgorgement, interest, and forfeited money. The bill defines a prediction-market platform as any website, app, exchange, or protocol in interstate or foreign commerce that facilitates trading in contracts tied to the outcome of an event or contest, and it bars awards to anyone convicted or found civilly liable in the case. Employers could not fire, demote, or harass whistleblowers for reporting. The attorney general would have 180 days after enactment to issue guidance, including on anonymous submissions by whistleblowers represented by counsel, and would report yearly to the House and Senate Judiciary committees. ### How It Works: Betting on the Weather: Forecasts, Contracts and Hedging
A weather derivative pays on a measured weather index, not on a farmer showing that one field failed. The index can be cumulative average temperature over a season, or growing degree-days, a running total of how far temperature sits above a base. Two gaps sit between that payout and a crop loss. Product-design basis risk is the gap between the index and yield. Geographical basis risk is the gap between the weather station named in the contract and the field.

A 2019 paper on arXiv, "Hedging crop yields against weather uncertainties -- a weather derivative perspective," tries to close the first gap for maize. The authors use a machine-learning ensemble to measure which weather variables move maize yield. They report that the most significant variable was average temperature. They then propose a mean-reverting model for that temperature: the speed of mean reversion can change through time, the level it reverts toward is seasonal, and local volatility depends on the local average temperature. The same structure is extended to a multi-dimensional model for different but correlated locations. From those temperature models they present pricing models for futures, for options on futures, and for basket futures, on cumulative average temperature and on growing degree-days.

Walk that maize setup in the order the paper builds it. First the ensemble selects the driver: average temperature, the variable it found most tied to maize yield. Second, the temperature path is not treated as a flat number. It is pulled toward a seasonal mean, at a speed that itself varies, with volatility that depends on the local average. Third, a futures price is the price of a claim on the cumulative average of that path, or on growing degree-days built from it. Fourth, a buyer who does not want a single station can use a basket future and put weight on the stations that fit the crop. The paper says pricing futures on baskets reduces geographical basis risk, because buyers can select stations and set the weights. An option on the future is a right, not an obligation, on that same index. The authors say the ensemble step is there to eliminate product-design basis risk. They present the pricing models; this abstract does not publish a worked premium.

A 2011 paper on arXiv, "Pricing Weather Derivatives for Extreme Events," prices protection against extremes rather than against a seasonal average. It models weather extremes as a max-stable process, using spatial statistics and extreme-value theory, then simulates payments for a general collection of weather derivatives. The simulations are built to capture the spatial dependence of payments, so a bad outcome in one place is not treated as independent of a bad outcome nearby. The authors bring in catastrophe-ratemaking results to compute risk loads and premiums for weather derivatives that are renewal-additive.

The refusal the 2019 paper names is commercial. It says most smallholder farmers and agricultural stakeholders are unwilling to pay the price of weather derivatives because product-design and geographical basis risks are present in the pricing models. The ensemble-plus-basket design is the authors' answer to that refusal. The text does not claim the refusal then ends. The 2011 premiums still rest on the max-stable model of extremes and on the simulated, spatially dependent payments. If that description of the tail is wrong, the risk loads built from it move with the error.

Read a temperature contract as a claim on an index, and ask which index. Cumulative average temperature and growing degree-days transfer different accumulations of heat and cold. A basket transfers a weighted mix of places, which the 2019 paper treats as a way to cut geographical basis risk, not as a match to every field. An extremes contract, in the 2011 setup, transfers the tail, and its premium includes a risk load from catastrophe ratemaking, not only an expected payout. That is a way to see what risk the price is for, and what basis risk remains if the crop, the place, or the tail does not match the index. It is not a view on whether any listed contract is cheap or rich, and it is not a hedge to copy.
