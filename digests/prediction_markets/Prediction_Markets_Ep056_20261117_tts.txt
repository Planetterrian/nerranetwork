Chair Michael Selig’s Commission, on November sixteen, approved applications for a designated contract market and a derivatives clearing organization built to execute and settle on blockchain.

The Commodity Exchange Act, he said, sets core principles and does not lock in a technology, so a blockchain firm still has to clear the same bar as a traditional venue: anti-money laundering, customer protection and segregation, market surveillance, financial resources, risk management, and system safeguards.

The Block called this the first set of approvals for regulated derivatives and clearing that run on blockchain, and named Backpack and tZERO as the firms that got them. Selig cast the decisions as a regulated pathway for that kind of infrastructure, and as a sign that responsible innovation has a home inside U.S. regulated markets.

Option prices hide a probability map, and that is how this part of the market works. A call or a put is a claim on a slice of where a price might land, so a whole strip of them sketches what the market is charging for each range. The probabilities you back out are called risk-neutral. The phrase does not describe a trader who feels nothing.

A number you would use for planning can sit somewhere else, because people pay to dodge bad states and will take a lower expected payoff to get rid of them. Risk-neutral math folds that payment into the probability. A fat chance of a crash in that measure can mean traders think a crash is likely, or that they hate crashes enough to bid the insurance up, or both.

One option price will not tell you which.

A twenty eleven paper in the Review of Finance starts from a cousin of that idea. Option-implied volatility was already a strong predictor of later realized volatility. The authors also find that option-implied volatility and skewness predict later realized beta, how a stock moves with the market.

Under assumptions they lay out, you can build a beta from option-implied return moments using stock options and index options, and you can do it from a single day’s option data. A historical beta needs a run of past returns, so a break this morning is invisible to it until the history catches up. The option version can see that morning, because the option surface is the only input.

A twenty twenty-three paper in the Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis built a model-free joint risk-neutral distribution for the nine industry sectors inside the S&P five hundred, kept consistent with options on those sectors and on their weighted index. Dependence in that surface was highly asymmetric, and it moved through time.

Split the correlation into a down-market case and an up-market case and the risk premium on the down correlation comes out strongly negative, while the premium on the up correlation comes out positive. The authors’ reading is that investors dislike losing diversification when markets fall, and that they actually prefer high correlation when markets rally.

That is a price for a preference, not a coin flip on whether two sectors travel together.

A twenty eighteen comparison on arXiv ran fifteen forecasting schemes across more than twenty-one years and graded the densities on consistency, local accuracy and forecast error. On a composite they call the Integrated Forecast Score, risk-neutral densities beat historical-based predictions for information content.

Variance Gamma produced the highest out-of-sample likelihood of the prices that actually printed, and the lowest predictive errors. An ARCH-style method, GJR-FHS, was the most consistent across the whole density. Lognormal densities, the Heston model and the Breeden-Litzenberger formula came out biased and failed statistical tests. There is no one implied distribution.

Change the extraction and you change the forecast, and some familiar recipes do not survive the tests.

Inside the day, jumps dominate the tail. A twenty twenty paper in Management Science looked at intrahorizon value at risk in a jump-diffusion setting and offered a displaced mixed-exponential model that can closely mimic a wide family of Lévy processes.

On the S&P one hundred and on American options, once jump and diffusion were pulled apart in a consistent way, jumps accounted for about ninety percent of that intrahorizon risk on average. Option-implied estimates moved with the market far more than historical estimates, and the historical ones did poorly in back-tests.

Read a risk-neutral number as “what will happen” and you are reading a price that already mixes belief, a dislike of bad states, and a large jump piece inside the day. An event contract pays a fixed sum if a named event happens, so its price sits closer to a probability of that named event under whatever mix of belief and preference the traders brought.

The option surface is answering where a price lands and how the tails are charged for. These papers say that risk-neutral version often tells you more about later prices than a density built only from the past, and still is not a forecast with the price of risk taken out.
