The Policy Analysis Market is a proposal for contracts on political and security events, and the way it works is that the list of questions can fail before any price is tested. A yes position pays only if someone later judges that the event occurred. A no position pays only if that judgment goes the other way. Kalshi caps a contract's payout at one dollar, so the price is a slice of that dollar.

Arithmetic does not pick the question. A venue does, or a regulator, or a public storm.

Aggregation is the design claim. A person who will never send a memo can still buy or sell, and each trade nudges the number. If the wording is tight and the crowd is not one trader wearing many hats, scattered views get pressed into a single figure. The fight starts when the question is harm.

Getting paid for correctly calling a violent or destabilizing event gives someone a cash reason to be right about damage. Critics hear a bounty. People who want the listing say a ban does not erase the views. It only keeps them off a screen where price and size can be seen.

Stay with that one-dollar cap, and do not borrow a live quote. Whatever fraction a yes buyer puts up is the loss if the contract resolves no, and the unpaid remainder of the dollar is the profit if it resolves yes. The other side of the same dollar is the no buyer. Concern is not what gets paid. Citizenship is not what gets paid. A match with the resolution is.

A designer can therefore call the figure a forecast. An opponent can call it indecent the moment the question is an attack, a coup, or some other harm. Those are two descriptions of one object: the payout rule, and the sound of the question read aloud.

Other breaks sit next to that rule. Guessers can still print a number. A few large orders can still shove it. A fuzzy resolution rule pays the side that wins the argument about the rule. Pull the market when the questions hit the public and there are no settled contracts left to score, so hits and misses never get counted. That ending is political. One side can say the forecast was never tried.

The other can say the question should never have been sold. Neither side can hand a listener a price.

A figure on a screen is what people who can reach that venue will pay, for that question, under that rule, at that moment. It is not a head count of everyone who knows something, and it is not a moral verdict. A question that never gets listed does not sit at zero. It has no probability attached.

That is the limit this proposal still teaches: a price can sum up a trade, and it cannot outlast a decision that the question may not be sold.
