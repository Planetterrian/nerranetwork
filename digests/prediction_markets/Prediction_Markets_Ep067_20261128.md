It says users can fund contracts with crypto and other assets already in Gemini accounts, without deposit limits licensed New York sportsbooks apply, that customers often trade against market makers the firm recruits and pays, and that Gemini collects a taker-rate fee the complaint describes as a 7 percent rake and "wins every time its customers bet." ### How It Works: The Market That Was Cancelled in a Day: The Policy Analysis Market
An event contract is a listed question with a fixed payout. A trader who buys yes pays a price and collects the payout only if the event is later judged to have happened. A trader who buys no collects that payout only if it is judged not to have happened. On Kalshi, the payout on a contract is at most one dollar. The price, as a share of that dollar, is the implied probability. None of that arithmetic chooses the question. A venue, a regulator, or a political storm does.

The Policy Analysis Market is the proposal this segment uses to see that choice clearly: contracts meant for political and security events, the kind of list that can collapse once the questions are read in public. The design logic is aggregation. Someone who will not write a memo can still buy or sell. Each trade moves a price. If the question is precise and the traders are not all the same person in disguise, the price compresses scattered views into one number. The objection lands on the question, not on the subtraction. A contract that pays for correctly calling a violent or destabilizing event gives someone a cash reason to be right about harm. Critics hear a bounty. Defenders answer that blocking the question does not delete the views. It only leaves them off the screen, where no one can see a price or a volume.

Take the one-dollar cap as the whole example, without borrowing any live market's price. The yes buyer pays a fraction of the dollar. If the contract resolves yes, the profit is whatever part of the dollar was not paid up front. If it resolves no, that fraction is the loss. The no buyer stands on the other side of the same dollar. The payout does not reward concern, citizenship, or a wish that the event be avoided. It rewards a match with the resolution. That is why a policy-market design can call the price a forecast, and why an opponent can call the same price indecent as soon as the question is an attack, a coup, or some other harm. Both can describe the object in front of them. One is describing the payout rule. The other is describing what happens when the question is said out loud.

The failure modes sit beside that rule. A price still prints if the only traders are guessing. A price still prints if a handful of large orders can shove it. A vague resolution rule pays whoever wins the argument about the rule. And a market that is pulled when the questions become public never produces settled contracts, so there is no score of hits and misses to argue over. The decision that ends it is political. Supporters can say the forecast was never tested. Critics can say it should not have been listed. A listener has no price to consult either way.

Read a live market with that limit in view. The figure on the screen is what accessible traders will pay, on that venue, for that question, under that resolution rule, at that moment. It is not a census of everyone who knows something. It is not a moral finding. A question that never lists does not carry an implied probability of zero. That is the lesson attached to the Policy Analysis Market: a price can summarize a trade, and it cannot outlive a ban on the question.
