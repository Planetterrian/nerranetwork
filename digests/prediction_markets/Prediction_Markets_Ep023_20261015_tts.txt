Mention markets pay if a chosen word is spoken at a future public event, and how it works in a new paper is that a language model can be handed the live contract price as a starting point.

The study is Forecasting Future Language: Context Design for Mention Markets, and the test bed is earnings calls: will this company say this keyword on the next call.

Researchers changed two inputs. One was the text, news, older call transcripts, or both. The other was what the model was allowed to do with the contract's probability.

Their method, market-conditioned prompting, tells the model to revise that probability with the text, instead of tossing the price and guessing a fresh base rate.

Picture one keyword contract. It already implies a chance the word gets said. Feed the model old transcripts and news. Under that prompting it edits the market's number. A blend they call MixMCP then pulls the market probability back in, so the model's revision cannot run away from the prior.

Three results follow. More context steadily lifted forecast quality. Updating the market number, rather than ignoring it, produced better-calibrated forecasts. The blend beat the market on its own, and holding onto the market prior made the result sturdier than the market alone or the model alone.

The abstract says other recent work has found language models can match human forecasters. This paper does not rerun that contest. Its claim is about how you build the prompt.

A second paper, Making Alternative Data Work, puts prediction markets in another job, next to card transactions and web traffic, as a feed for forecasting how a firm will perform.

Two agents split the work. One picks the firms where a given data channel is likely to matter. The other forecasts revenue with context built for that firm and that channel.

Across four commercial alternative-data channels, putting that extra data in the prompt beside other financial information beat using either source by itself, and beat ordinary forecasting baselines.

The papers state their own limits. Firm-level alternative data often cover a short history, matter for only some targets or some firms, and show up in mismatched channels.

On mention markets, the weaker setup was a model that discarded the contract price and started the base rate over. The blend exists because an undamped model move was less sturdy in their tests.

Neither abstract quotes an outside critic.

After that, a contract price is a starting probability with a job. It is not a verdict, and it is not a poll. Text may move it. In their tests the sturdier forecast kept the price in the mix. None of that is a reason to buy, sell, or trade anything.
