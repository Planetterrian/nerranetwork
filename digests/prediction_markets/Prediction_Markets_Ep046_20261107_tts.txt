Selling a winner early and sitting on a loser is the disposition effect, and how it works is that the quoted price only reflects contracts people will still trade. A loser nobody sells never revises that quote. A Journal of Finance paper finds the effect on a stock weakens when the whole portfolio is ahead and grows large when the portfolio is behind.

The authors call that the portfolio-driven disposition effect. It shows up in four separate settings: American archival records, Chinese archival records, an American experiment, and a Chinese experiment. Extreme returns, rebalancing, taxes, and differences among investors do not explain it away.

Their reading is two mental frames at once, one for the single position and one for the book, combining into the pattern. Picture two contracts and change only whether the book is up or down, which is the comparison that paper actually runs. Ahead, the urge to dump the winner and nurse the loser weakens. Behind, that urge is large.

The contract that would carry bad news into the quote, if the evidence has turned, is the loser, and that is the one the pattern grips hardest when the book is down. Letting go of the winner moves the quote less, because the bad news never has to trade.

Frontiers in Psychology ran a within-subject test with one hundred ninety-three professional traders, the same people deciding on securities that tend to come back and securities that do not. Before anyone intervened, their winner-selling and loser-holding lined up with making money.

On something that mean-reverts, selling strength and keeping a loser can be the sensible trade, because the price is expected to return. A simple notice that made the disposition effect obvious changed how strong the effect was, on both kinds of security. Returns improved when that notice was used on securities that do not come back. Returns got worse when traders applied it to securities that do.

The nudge can be large, and it helps only if the market behaves the way the nudge assumes. A separate paper matches posts on Xueqiu, a Chinese social-investment platform, to real trades. Information from that social feed significantly cuts the disposition effect, and the cut runs through negative information.

Faced with bad news, individual investors adjust in a more rational way, on that paper's account. Experience, whose posts someone follows, region, and gender all change how well the information works. That study is not about event contracts. It asks whether what sits in front of a holder can loosen the hold. Read a quiet price with those three results in mind.

A losing book, the Journal of Finance finding, makes the unsold loser more stubborn, so the contracts that would register bad news are the ones least likely to trade. The trader experiment says that stubbornness is not automatically an error: if the question comes back, keeping the loser can be the money-making trade, and a warning that forces a sale can cost money.

The Xueqiu finding says negative information is what gets holders to adjust, so a price can sit still until the bad news is in front of the people on the losing side, not merely until the news exists. You are reading the trades that cleared, minus the losing positions people would not close. If the question comes back, stillness can be sense. If it does not, stillness can be the missing sell.
