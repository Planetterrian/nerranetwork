province and state, and they are changing. A contract that pays only if an event happens
is a claim on cash that shows up when the contract settles. If that date is a year
away, the payout is a year away even for someone who is already sure.
Collateral posted to hold the position sits until then. Cash that is locked cannot be spent,
lent, or posted somewhere else. Whatever that cash could have earned over the wait is a
cost of holding the contract. A trader who must borrow the collateral or who has another
use for it will not hand over today the full amount they think the contract is likely
to pay later.
The price can sit below the probability that trader actually holds.
The gap is a charge for time, not a quieter forecast.
Take a trader whose own belief is that the event is more likely than not, and a contract
that cannot pay until next year.
The most they will post today is that belief, reduced by what they give up while the collateral
is stuck.

plus any fee the venue takes for keeping the position open. A contract that settles
next week has almost none of that weight. The same belief on the same event can therefore
show a higher price on the near contract than on the far one. Reading the far price as
if it were the near price treats two different claims as one forecast. Venues that pay interest
on collateral change the arithmetic. If the cash posted earns a return while it waits,
the trader is no longer giving up the whole alternative yield. The time-charge shrinks,
and the price can sit closer to the probability the trader holds. A venue that pays nothing on margin
leaves the full cost of the weight inside the price. Comparing a long-dated price on a venue
that pays interest with one on a venue that does not, without knowing which is which, mixes a
funding term into what looks like a probability. The adjustment fails when capital is not what
binds. A position limit, a fee that does not scale with time, or a resolution rule that
can void or rewrite the contract, can move the price for reasons that have nothing to do with
the calendar. A thin market can print a number that no one was willing to defend with size. Play
money, where the stake is reputation rather than cash that could have earned a return, does not
carry the same time charge. Its long-dated price is not the same object as a cash price
on a distant contract. So, read a far-dated price with the settlement date in view,
and ask whether the collateral earns anything while it sits. A price below a stated belief is not by
itself, a claim that the event is less likely. It may be the cost of waiting to be paid.
