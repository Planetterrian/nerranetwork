Betfair's exchange works by many people trading with each other on a multisided platform, rather than a bookmaker posting the number, and that is the mechanism the studies measure. A twenty nineteen paper in the Journal of Economics and Management Strategy compared Betfair with Flutter, another exchange, and with ordinary bookmakers.

Betfair was the underfunded second mover and still drew punters much faster than Flutter, the better-funded first mover. It fought the bookmakers hard for share and, at the same time, built a complementary relationship that helped both sides. The authors want later research to treat the design of the platform as a choice, and to study competition and cooperation together.

A twenty twenty-six football study makes that price comparison numerical. It uses a Weibull accelerated failure time model of goals, then adds two pieces: team strength fitted at kickoff to Betfair Exchange match-result and over-under prices by squared-error minimisation, and cumulative shots as a covariate once the match is live.

That kickoff fit, the paper says, can sit on any intensity-based goal-arrival model. Over two hundred eighty English Premier League matches, checked every minute, the market-started model classified the result correctly sixty-eight point five percent of the time. Betfair's own in-play prices did it sixty-nine point five percent of the time.

Ranked probability score was zero point one three two five for the model and zero point one two eight one for the exchange. The paper calls the model measurably weaker, and still useful because the team parameters and the shot effect stay readable.

Against a market-implied Poisson model, a Cox process, and a Bayesian birth process, all started from the same pre-match odds, the initialisation from the market — not which engine you picked — drove the accuracy.

The same authors simulated bets against Betfair in-play odds and recorded a one point zero percent return on thirty-seven thousand four hundred sixty-seven bets. The ninety-five percent interval runs from a three point eight percent loss to a five point nine percent gain. They call it preliminary: few matches, and prices that may not have been executable.

That range is the argument inside the paper. A positive point estimate is not a clean gap once losses sit inside the interval and the quotes might not have been tradable.

Horse racing sets a second bound. Review of Finance looked at exchange trading on nine thousand five hundred sixty-two UK races in twenty thirteen and twenty fourteen. Trade volume predicted fundamentals, which the authors take as speculative trade helping efficiency on average.

Most of that link sat in-running, in the stretch when the race itself would have shown the result seconds later even if nobody traded. A separate pass through Betfair's UK horse-racing series finds short tails, autocorrelations that die quickly, no long-term memory, and no gain-loss asymmetry, plus a Hurst exponent that shows mean reversion.

The authors set that against financial assets, which tend to show heavy tails and volatility clustering.

Read the quote with those tests in mind. In the football work, starting from the pre-match exchange price mattered more than which statistical engine you bolted on, and the live exchange price was still a bit sharper than the model built from it. A simulated return whose range includes a loss, on prices that may not have been hittable, is not a finding that the quote was wrong.
