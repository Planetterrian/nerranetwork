A Delphi forecast works by sending a panel's own answers back to the panel until the estimates sit still, and that loop is the whole of how it works. Experts, often looking years out, give a judgment. Someone running the exercise folds those judgments together — descriptive statistics are the example in the software literature — and hands the group result back. People revise. Another round starts.

A twenty fourteen account in International Letters of Social and Humanistic Sciences says the aim is to shrink the spread of opinion without directly steering anyone: feedback from the experts themselves carries the study to a close.

RAND built this at Santa Monica, California, for long-range work in management science. A twenty twenty-one paper in Advances in Fuzzy Systems keeps the letters to the experts and swaps the math, using triangular fuzzy numbers instead of an ordinary summary. That paper walks a numerical example.

The figures from the example are not in the account here, so the split is what travels: same conversation, different way of combining the replies.

Sustainability published a tourism Delphi that shows the finished product when the question is a direction. Experts in tourism and hospitality looked at Thailand. The stable view was that the country could keep attractions that work for visitors across income groups, and could keep the cultural distinctiveness the authors call Thai-ness, while tourism grows more important across Southeast Asia.

The same panel named repairs: higher-quality training in tourism organizations, a less strict immigration policy, new information technology, and tourism information that is more up to date. No contract resolves that list. A ministry can still read it as a brief.

Two designs bolt a machine onto the panel and still are not a market. A twenty twenty paper in Information notes that experts often already have time-series software and often leave it unused. The authors add a virtual expert: a forecast from the Prophet library, a Facebook tool that runs in Python or R, dropped into the same aggregate the humans see.

The gain they name is time and cost, lighter work for data analysts, not a victory of software over the room. A twenty thirteen paper in the Journal of Business Economics and Management pairs Evolino recurrent neural networks, taking orthogonal inputs, with a Delphi evaluation, and uses the pair for a portfolio decision.

The paper's statistical study is described as showing reliability and describing accuracy, and a trading simulation is how the authors display what the model can do.

Delphi settles when a facilitator decides the rounds have converged, and the stake inside the room is reputation. A market, when the stake is money, moves whenever a trade prints against a contract someone can check later. Manifold's mana prices are the close cousin: forecasts from people with reputation on the line, not dollars.

A shop that needs a long qualitative brief — what to protect, what to fix — is doing the job the Thailand panel was built for. A shop that needs a number someone can trade against a dated event is doing another job. On these papers, neither design stands in for the other.

Once you have watched a panel revise and then stop, a market price reads as a live bid, not as a room that has been asked twice and then closed.
