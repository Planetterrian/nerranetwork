Survivorship bias, and this is how it works, scores a fund, a strategy or a forecaster only on the records still in the sample. Shut funds, delisted stocks and deleted accounts fall out, and the average of whoever remains looks kinder than the average of everyone who tried. A twenty twenty-six study of India's NIFTY Smallcap two hundred fifty walks the arithmetic.

The author rebuilt membership with a market-cap ranking from price and volume, including delisted names in the exchange's bhavcopy files, across one thousand four hundred thirty-seven stocks and nine years, twenty sixteen through twenty twenty-five. An equal-weight book of only today's members was set next to an equal-weight book of everyone who had ever been in.

Keeping only the survivors overstated annual returns by four point nine four percentage points, a twenty-three point three percent overstatement, and overstated Sharpe ratios by zero point zero nine seven, a nine point one percent overstatement. Turnover in that window was eighty-two point five percent.

Sixteen point one percent of the names were delisted, thirty-three point one percent graduated out of the small-cap index, and thirty-three point two percent were demoted. All three groups fed the bias, so it is not only bankruptcies. The rebuilt list matched current members perfectly and was estimated at eighty-five to ninety percent accurate on older membership.
