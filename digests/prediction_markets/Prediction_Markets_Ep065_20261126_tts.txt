A no-trade theorem is a result about information, and that is how it works. Shared starting beliefs, plus common knowledge that everyone is rational, make a private signal a weak reason to bet. The offer is itself news. Fold the other side’s willingness to take the opposite side back into your estimate, and the gap that made the trade look good shrinks.

Pass that message enough times and a purely informational disagreement can vanish. What remains is a refusal, not a price.

Picture a security that pays according to a state of the world. Both traders share a prior. One of them sees something private. If rationality is common knowledge, the first bid tells the second something about that signal, and the answer tells the first something back. Each round is a message.

The classic claim, as a new computational paper restates it, attributes trade to heterogeneous beliefs: people bet because they did not start in the same place. Under a shared prior, information alone is not supposed to clear a mutually acceptable bet.

Will A I Trade? A Computational Inversion of the No-Trade Theorem keeps those common beliefs and asks whether limited computation can start the betting again. Agents play an unfolding game. Power sets how complex a strategy they can run. The finding flips the textbook. A stable no-trade Nash equilibrium shows up only when almost-rational agents have slightly different computational power.

Give them identical power and they may never converge. The adjustments they keep making are, the authors say, a kind of trade. Let them strategically under-use their compute and the instability worsens. In Matching Pennies, that choice wipes out any chance of equilibrium. A busy screen, on this account, need not mean someone is simply wrong.

It can mean two equally powerful reasoners cannot finish the regress of I know that you know.

No Trade Under Verifiable Information pulls the other lever. Private information that does not hint at value, but verifies the true payoff, ends disagreement for a plainer reason. One side is holding the answer, not a clue. The authors tie that condition to insider trading, to the link between no-trade results and markets that look illiquid, and to trading that uses public blockchains and oracles.

An oracle that publishes a checkable outcome is a machine for ending a disagreement. Before it speaks, traders can still differ. After it speaks, the theorem’s conditions are closer, and a fresh bet on the same fact has less to stand on.

Those two papers push the classic result in opposite directions, and neither gives a trade count, a dollar volume, or a test on a named prediction market. The computational paper says the no-trade equilibrium is fragile once rationality is bounded, and fragile again if agents may choose to think less. Identical power, not different beliefs, is what keeps the quotes moving.

The verification paper says the classic silence returns when private information can pin down the payoff, so a quiet market may be quiet because someone already knows. Earlier work, which that paper points to without naming one decisive rebuttal, had already tried other exits: drop the common prior, drop common knowledge, allow bounded rationality, allow ambiguity aversion.

Computation and verification join that list. They do not close it.

Read a print as a broken condition, not as a verdict. A trade means something failed the setup: beliefs that were never shared, a hedge that is not a bet on the fact, unequal computing power, a choice to think less, or information that does not yet verify. A silent contract can be the theorem working, once a resolution source has made the payoff checkable to both sides.

None of that picks a side of a live contract. It puts the first question on the person across the print: what, other than a shared reading of the same public facts, brought them here?
