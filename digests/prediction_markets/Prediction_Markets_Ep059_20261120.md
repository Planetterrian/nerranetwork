# Prediction Markets Daily
> **Fewer than a dozen workdays remain on a Senate-passed ban of death and war contracts.**

**What You Need to Know:** Fewer than a dozen scheduled workdays remain in this Congress after the Senate passed a bill that would bar prediction-market contracts tied to death, terrorism, assassination, and war. The measure still needs the House and a presidential signature. Separately, Coinbase urged the CFTC to treat event contracts as swaps, and Bloomberg reported a first-half 2027 target for Coinbase's own U.S. contracts.

### Top Story
The United States Senate approved S. 4035, the DEATH Bets Act, on Thursday, November 19, by voice vote, and the bill now goes to the House. Under the text the Senate passed, a registered entity could not list or clear a contract that expressly references terrorism, assassination, war, U.S. Armed Forces military operations, or similar activity the CFTC determines by rule, or a contract that, at listing, is reasonably expected to resolve principally on an individual's death. Sponsors John Curtis, Republican of Utah, and Adam Schiff, Democrat of California, introduced a broader original in March, covering contracts that involve, relate to, or reference those events, or that could be construed as correlating closely to a death; the substitute adds a rule of construction that the bill does not change existing law on other event contracts, including gambling, and does not affect ongoing sports-contract litigation or give the CFTC authority over sports contracts. Representative Mike Levin's House companion, H.R. 7942, has been in the House Agriculture Committee's Subcommittee on Commodity Markets, Digital Assets, and Rural Development since April; the House term ends January 3, 2027, fewer than a dozen scheduled workdays remain, and House leadership has not said whether it will take up S. 4035. The Senate website had not posted the amended text. Schiff said, "Nothing in the DEATH BETS Act should have any effect on those lawsuits," and that it should not be construed as authority for the CFTC over sports contracts; Curtis said Congress "has not, and will not, give a green light to those seeking to bypass restrictions on contracts against public interest." **Coinbase Backs CFTC Rule Defining Event Contracts as Swaps: RG.org**
In a seven-page comment letter dated November 18, Coinbase told the Commodity Futures Trading Commission it strongly supports including sports, politics, culture, and weather event contracts in the swap definition, and that a final rule should confirm qualifying contracts already are swaps rather than becoming swaps only when the rule takes effect. Julia Hueckel, director of regulatory policy, and David Shafer, director and associate general counsel, signed the letter; the public comment period ended November 18. Coinbase urged the commission to keep a non-exhaustive category list, pointing to digital assets, business developments, technology, and scientific milestones as further examples with financial or commercial consequences. It argued that event-contract exchanges match buyers and sellers and do not set prices or take the other side, unlike sportsbooks and casinos — the company's arguments, not court findings — and it supported a separate exclusion of casino-style gambling products from the swap definition. A coalition of attorneys general from 43 states and the District of Columbia has asked the CFTC to withdraw the proposal, which the commission announced in October. The letter notes that Coinbase owns designated contract market Coinbase Derivatives LLC and futures commission merchant Coinbase Financial Markets Inc., and had recently registered Coinbase Clearing LLC as a derivatives clearing organization; the letter does not set a launch date. ### How It Works: Probability Weighting: Why Small Chances Feel Bigger
In rank-dependent utility theories, including prospect theory, the psychological weight attached to an outcome need not equal the probability of that outcome. A probability weighting function lets stated probabilities be weighted nonlinearly, so a small stated chance can carry more decision weight than its face value and a chance near certainty can carry less. The shape repeatedly estimated in that literature is an inverse S: concave at low probabilities, then convex.

Wu and Gonzalez, in Management Science in 1996, treated that shape as a claim to test rather than a form to assume. Earlier empirical studies had suggested an inverse-S weighting function, first concave and then convex, but estimation had required assumptions about the functional form of the value function, the weighting function, or both. They proposed two preference conditions that are necessary and sufficient for concavity and for convexity of the weighting function, and that can be tested without assuming the form of the value function. The instrument was a preference ladder: a series of questions that differ only by a common consequence. Those concavity-convexity ladders validated an S-shaped weighting function, concave up to a probability below 0.40 and convex beyond that probability. The tests also showed significant nonlinearity away from the boundaries, 0 and 1, not only at the edges. The ladder data were fit to weighting functions proposed by Tversky and Kahneman in 1992 and by Prelec in 1995.

Abdellaoui, in Management Science in 2000, separated the elicitation into two steps and kept it parameter-free, so the whole individual preference functional could be drawn out without a prior restriction on its formula. The method elicits utility first and decision weights second, under rank-dependent expected utility and under cumulative prospect theory. In an experimental study of monetary outcomes, utility functions were concave for gains and convex for losses. The elicited weighting functions satisfied upper and lower subadditivity and were consistent with previous parametric estimations. In that data, the probability weighting function for losses was more elevated than the function for gains.

The dispute is as much about what the theory has been said to predict as about the bend in the curve. A 2010 paper in Strategic Management Journal argued that prospect theory's predictions differ sharply from what strategy scholars had inferred. Its value function, the paper said, predicts negative risk-return associations for high performers and positive associations for low performers, directly contrary to the strategy literature. The isolation assumption means most firm choices should appear as mixed gambles, while prior prospect-theory theorizing in strategy had implicitly assumed unmixed gambles. The paper's further claim is that prospect theory does not make the general predictions most strategy researchers had assumed; the predictions depend on a full range of parametric and choice characteristics that work had ignored. A 2005 review in the Annual Review of Political Science described a different resistance: international-relations scholars who study security had used prospect theory extensively, while Americanists, comparativists, and political economists had shown little interest. That review located the tepid response in aversion to behavioral assumptions, not in problems unique to the theory, and treated the difficulty of extending a laboratory account to political decisions in the field as an open theoretical problem rather than a settled finding.

A listener who keeps that distinction reads a market price as a traded price, not as a felt weight. A contract whose price implies a small probability sits in the region where the 1996 ladders found overweighting of stated chances; a price near certainty sits where those weights turned convex and came in below the stated probability. Abdellaoui's elicitations add that the bend need not be the same in gains and in losses. None of that is a reason to buy or sell a contract, and none of it says a venue's price has already applied a laboratory weighting function. It says the number on the screen and the weight in a choice experiment are different objects, measured in different ways.
