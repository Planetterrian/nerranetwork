Wishful prices are hard to separate from information, and how it works here starts with a reader, not a trader.

A Brennan Center analysis measured what a displayed Kalshi figure does to someone who never put money down. It does not track traders’ own politics.

Yeji Lee’s write-up, funded by the Brennan Center for Justice and released October 20, used a survey of one thousand nine hundred ninety-four self-identified registered voters fielded September 21.

Each person saw five pairs of hypothetical congressional candidates. Profiles listed race, age, party and previous occupation, plus an NBC News poll estimate and a Kalshi chance that researchers varied at random.

Random assignment is how the design tries to pull the price away from the biography and the poll. If the Kalshi figure still changes the pick, the number is doing work of its own.

A candidate shown at fifty percent on Kalshi is the baseline. Move that displayed figure to sixty-seven percent and respondents were ten percentage points more likely to name that candidate the winner.

Move it to eighty-four percent and the gain was twenty points. At thirty-three percent, respondents were eleven points less likely to pick the candidate. At sixteen percent, they were nineteen points less likely.

Lee’s write-up says the market figure moved perceptions about as much as the traditional poll did, and more than any background trait in the profile. A warning that asked people to be careful about prediction markets had no effect on how much they used the figure.

Only nineteen percent of respondents said election prediction markets are accurate, against thirty-seven percent who said that of traditional polls.

Just twenty-nine percent said the markets reflect public opinion, against fifty-five percent for polls. Thirty-seven percent neither agreed nor disagreed that the markets are accurate, and twenty-nine percent took no position on whether they represent the public.

People who do not trust the number still moved when it was shown. Forty-eight percent said prediction markets should be regulated more strongly, twenty-three percent said some types should be banned, sixteen percent said all of them should be banned, and fourteen percent said they should not be regulated at all.

The analysis cited polling that seven in ten Americans disapprove of betting on elections, and a Reuters/Ipsos poll released October 12 that found sixty percent of U S adults oppose using prediction markets to wager on election results.
