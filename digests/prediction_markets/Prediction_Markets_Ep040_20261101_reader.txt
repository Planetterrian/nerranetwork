Ticket sales are a hard count, which is why researchers keep trying to forecast them. Communication Research used MPAA rating descriptors on two thousand ninety-four films from nineteen ninety-two to twenty twelve.

One story said movies mirror violence in the wider United States. Another said teenagers chase what they are told to avoid. A third said people simply buy what they want.

Violent content did not track violence in the broader culture, and it did not track adolescent reactance. Among PG-thirteen films, it did track higher ticket sales. The authors call that market demand.

Marketing Letters looked at five hundred fifteen randomly chosen Hollywood films and the Bechdel test. Passing it significantly lifted international box office. The lift depended on how developed the country was. The authors explain that split with cultural discount theory.

Arts and the Market stayed with fifty-one science-fiction adaptations and two-stage least squares. Signaling the adaptation in the title, the popularity of the original novel, and a director who had adapted before each had a significant link to box office.

That abstract treats a director with adaptation experience as a sound hire. It treats the author's draw as useful for money and attention in the short run. It treats saying in the title that the film comes from a novel as useful marketing.

A Chinese study in the Journal of Physics Conference Series fed a neural network seven inputs: heroine fans, hero fans, first-week box office, director fans, Baidu Index, average ticket price, and film type. Grey correlation analysis had picked those inputs. Box office was the output.
