A prediction market contract pays one of two amounts when it resolves, so size still open

and the quote is what turns an incoming order into an update of that probability.
A paper on archive, optimal market making in prediction markets, models that price as a conditional
probability generated by transforming a latent belief that defuses over time. The market maker
picks a bid and an ask to maximize expected wealth at the end.
Marked to market inventory risk while the market is open, and settlement risk on contracts
left open at resolution, sit in the same problem.
The authors derive the Hamilton-Jacobie Bellman equation, and characterize a unique optimal
bid and a unique optimal ask.
Shifting the equation into latent belief space and using a fixed point argument, they prove
a classical solution exists and is unique, and they verify that the quoting rule from
is optimal. Their comparison has no dollar figures in the abstract. A myopic
quota maximizes only the instantaneous expected mark-to-market profit. The edge
on the next trade marked at the new probability. The optimal rule also has to
care how inventory behaves if the latent belief keeps moving and how that
inventory pays if the event resolves either way. Inventory shifts the quote because
a maker already longed the yes side is less willing to buy more of it and more
willing to sell it. Market beliefs shift the quote because the latent probability is the thing
being marked. Time to resolution shifts the quote because settlement risk dominates when the
binary payoff is close, and marked to market risk has longer to compound when resolution is far.
Risk aversion changes how hard those penalties bite, so a more risk averse maker posts a wider
quote, or a sharper inventory skew for the same book. Against that myopic benchmark, the paper finds
the optimal strategy substantially improves downside protection while preserving most expected
profit. That is about how a quote is chosen once a liquidity setting exists, not a claim
that one side of a live contract is cheap. The numerical analysis names dependence on inventory,
beliefs, time to resolution, and risk aversion, and it does not report a live exchange test
or a fee schedule. A second archive paper, adaptive liquidity in prediction markets via online
and learning, asks how much liquidity the mechanism itself supplies.
Markets need liquidity to turn trades into informative prices, and ordinary cost-function
designs fix that liquidity before trading starts.
The freeze locks a trade-off between price responsiveness and worst-case loss, even though order

A thick book means one trade barely moves the probability, so the price looks calm for

A thin setting means one trade swings it, and the mechanism's worst-case loss is the
