A streak shows how it works when a run of results gets read as a change in the chance: the gambler's fallacy expects a reversal, and the hot hand expects the run to keep going.

If each draw is truly independent, neither reading tells you anything.

If the draws are a pitcher or a team, the run might be a real shift, a shift only in belief, or both.

A price that jumps after the run does not, by itself, prove which one you are looking at.

A twenty fourteen paper in the International Journal of Financial Studies took thirty-four NBA regular seasons and estimated a seemingly unrelated regression on all of them together.

Streaks significantly moved the point spread, which is the price on the margin, and they also moved the actual point difference.

So in that pooled result the line was not the only thing that moved.

Estimated one season at a time, the picture changed, especially for winning and losing streaks of six games or more.

Six wins in a row can fit the all-season finding and still fit the finding that the link was unstable year to year.

A twenty thirteen paper in the Journal of Gambling Business and Economics used six NBA seasons and separated belief from profit.

Betting against the hot hand did not win more than efficiency implied.

Ordinary least squares and two-stage least squares still found that teams on streaks drew significantly more bets.

The closing spread, in that study, stayed an optimal and unbiased forecast.

The authors note bookmakers need not run a balanced book. They may price as a forecast, or shade a number to use a known bias.

A twenty twenty-six paper in the International Journal of Sport Finance classed MLB starting pitchers hot, cold, or neutral from context-adjusted recent work.

Short-term streaks predicted daily-fantasy outcomes.

DraftKings salaries did not fully price that pattern: hot pitchers came out underpriced, and cold pitchers overpriced.

Sportsbook lines tended to overreact, particularly for visiting hot starters and cold home starters.

Simulated betting strategies built on those patterns did not produce statistically significant positive profits.

The authors read that as books adjusting prices to take advantage of user biases.

Same streak, underweighted in a salary, overweighted in a line, and neither mistake equals a strategy that pays.

A twenty oh six paper in Judgment and Decision Making used roulette in a casino, where the next spin is independent of the last.

Players still showed both the hot hand and the gambler's fallacy, and people differed a lot.

Across individuals the two biases were positively and significantly correlated, which the authors treat as a shared root.

They speculate that root is locus of control.

Where spins are independent, expecting a reversal and expecting a continuation are both errors.

Where a game might not be independent, the sports papers split.

The thirty-four-season study finds streaks in the actual margin as well as in the spread.

The six-season study finds the close unbiased and fading the streak unprofitable.

The pitcher study finds mispriced salaries and overreacting lines, then no significant profit from trading against either.

The reason might be bettors chasing a sequence.

The reason might be that the competitor actually changed, with the caveat that six-game streaks did not look the same every season.

And a misweighted salary or line is still not a recommendation. None of this says which side of a live contract to buy.
