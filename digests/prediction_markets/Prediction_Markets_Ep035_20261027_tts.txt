A public signal can move a prediction-market price the right way inside a minute and still leave the update unfinished, which is how it works against the clock.

The paper is “When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets,” posted on arXiv.

Binary payoffs, public signals timed exactly, and high-frequency prices let the authors set each traded move next to a benchmark probability built only from information already public.

Prices in that setting carry information, and they grow more accurate as the event gets closer to resolution.

While the event is underway, prices answer public signals quickly and in the direction those signals imply.

Moving the right way is not the same as finishing the update.

Against an out-of-sample benchmark, a one-minute change in the benchmark probability lines up with only about a zero point six four change in the market price in that same minute.

Treat one unit of benchmark movement in a minute as the whole news increment. About zero point six four of that unit is in the price immediately. The remainder is still outside the price.

That missing piece predicts more price drift over the next several minutes.

The later drift remains even after later moves in the benchmark itself are taken out, so it is not only traders chasing a benchmark that kept shifting.

Salient public signals get taken up relatively fast when the market is liquid.

Those same signals leave substantially more underreaction when liquidity is low.

Gaps tied to salient states also predict stronger drift afterward.

The authors describe gradual discovery, with attention and trading frictions working together.

A book can register the public signal, print the expected direction, and still owe part of the benchmark update to the minutes that follow.

Liquidity changes how large the leftover is. Salience changes how hard that leftover pulls on later prices.

This is not a halt rule and not a reason to take either side of a live contract.

A first-minute move in the expected direction shows that traders responded to public information. It does not show that the percent on the screen has absorbed the full benchmark update.

A liquid book lands more of a salient signal at once. A book with low liquidity leaves a larger gap, and those gaps predict more drift later.

Market-implied chances in the study are often directionally correct, and the adjustment still stays incomplete in a way that tracks liquidity and salience.

Read the first print as a start, then ask whether the book was liquid enough for that start to sit close to the full public update.
