Whose belief a price averages is the mechanism question, and how it works starts with the payout, not with a poll. A binary contract pays a fixed sum if the event happens and nothing if it does not. Scale the promise to one dollar and the print sits between zero and one. Buyers are people who think the event is likelier than that print. Sellers think it is less likely.

Each trade shoves the number toward the side that just acted, and trading can stop when the people still in the book no longer find a move worth making. If traders care only about expected payout, can size as they like, and everyone with a view can reach the book, that stop is a weighted average of beliefs in the room. The weights are not heads. More money behind a view pulls harder.

Sixty cents, in that narrow case, is a money-weighted balance, and reading it as sixty percent is a reading of the balance, not a show of hands.

Those assumptions do the work, and they often fail. A risk-averse trader will not stake enough to drag the price all the way to their own chance, because being wrong hurts more than the expected gain feels worth, so the print can sit away from the average belief even when the gap is obvious. A trader who is out of money stops pulling.

A book closed to a state or a country averages only the people allowed in. A hedger who loses if the event happens may buy the contract as insurance, lifting the price without lifting their stated chance.

Replies do not deny the gap. They ask when it is large. If many small traders can take the other side of a hedger, insurance demand can get absorbed and the price can move back toward people trading the event itself.

The gap also shrinks when the contract is a tiny slice of wealth, because risk aversion bites less on a small stake, and it widens when the stake is large relative to what someone can lose. Neither reply brings back a simple average of opinions. Both treat the distance as a fact about constraints.

Nobody’s dollar budget is forcing the size of a view. The pull is a willingness to spend mana and to be seen as wrong. Forty-four point nine percent is where that book cleared.

It is not a head count of those traders, and it is not a dollar-weighted average, because dollars are not the stake.

The listing does not call either book thin, so I will not. What the pair shows is that one venue does not produce one kind of average. Thousands of traders and a few hundred are both mana-weighted clearings, and the smaller crowd is a smaller set of beliefs being averaged.

So the number you are looking at is a clearing: what buyers paid and sellers accepted, on that venue, at that time, in that venue’s unit. A dollar book weights people by money they were willing to risk. Manifold weights people by mana and reputation. A book closed by geography averages a smaller room. None of that picks a side.

It limits what the number is allowed to mean, and the meaning changes with the stake.
