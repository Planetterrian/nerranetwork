Efficiency, and how it works in this research, means a rule written in advance cannot be expected to profit once the book's fee is paid.

The check is not whether a price looks clever.

It is whether that rule, run on old prices, still clears a profit big enough that luck is a weak story.

Handicap betting, sometimes called a spread, shows the test cleanly.

The book posts a number, and the bet wins only if a side beats that number, not merely if the side wins the game.

If that number already holds what bettors know, backing favourites, backing underdogs, or chasing a side that just covered should not pay after the vig.

A twenty twenty-two review in the Journal of Sports Economics collected more than six hundred tries of those rules from the long literature on this market structure.

Glitches do appear, so prices are not a perfect picture of true chances.

The gaps were too small to turn into a profit, which is what efficiency looks like after costs.

In that result, a systematic mistake and an efficient market can sit together, because the mistake can be smaller than the fee for acting on it.

The same review borrows a bar from finance: treat a finding as serious only when the absolute value of the z-score is greater than three.

Researchers slice data so many ways that some slices will look special by chance, and that control is becoming ordinary in finance while this literature has not adopted it yet.

Six hundred tests are exactly where a lucky winner should show up.

One handsome backtest, without that bar, does not show that money was left sitting there.

English football bookmakers, in a Journal of Prediction Markets study of the seasons from two thousand two through two thousand six, bend a different way.

Home prices and away prices, what the paper calls odds, showed a positive favourite-longshot bias.

Draw prices showed the reverse, a negative longshot bias.

Betting the draw also returned more than betting home or away, a draw bias in the authors' terms.

They looked as well at rules that use disagreement among bookmakers, and the abstract records the look without a performance figure, so the usable result stops at those biases and the higher draw return.

Neither paper is a tip.

One says apparent errors in spread markets failed a profit test across hundreds of tries.

The other says those four football seasons were not symmetric: draws did not behave like home or away, and the favourite-longshot pattern was not the same on every outcome.

Read a contract, then, as the clearing price of whoever showed up to trade, not as a poll and not as a finished forecast.

A bias in an old sample describes those old prices.

It is not a rule that survives the next fee, the next sample, or another six hundred attempts.
