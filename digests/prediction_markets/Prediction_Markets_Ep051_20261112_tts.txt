Extremizing, which is how it works when a blended forecast hugs the middle, is a shove toward zero or toward one, and a twenty fourteen Decision Analysis paper sets out two reasons for that shove. People usually blend many personal probabilities with a weighted average. The authors say that blend tracks events more closely once it is moved outward, closer to an endpoint.

Reason one is the shape of the scale. It is pinched at both ends, so random misses cannot fall past zero or past one and instead pile up on the inner side. The average gets dragged toward one half. A median does not take that drag. The mean of the log odds, they say, arguably escapes it too, because log odds stretch the pinched ends.

Reason two hits the mean, the median, and that log-odds mean. Each person knows they have not seen every relevant fact, so they soften how hard they lean high or low. That private caution never counts evidence sitting with other people. The paper calls the gain from pooling those different scraps the wisdom of crowds.

The group may already hold what each member was missing, yet the blend still carries every member's inward shade.

One function, they find, can roughly cancel both problems, with one setting for the mean and another for the median. Switching to the median helps tell the problems apart: compression is an average problem, and the ignorance problem is still there in the median. The abstract never prints those settings, so the size of the shove is not a number we have.

Follow that chain without inventing figures. Error bunched inside the ends pulls a weighted average toward one half. Take the median and that particular pull drops away. Both numbers still embed each person's discount for facts they never held. Then one transform pushes the blend toward an endpoint, harder or softer depending on whether you started from the mean or the median.

The mean's extra push is their way of isolating compression; whatever push the median still needs is the ignorance effect. They call the repair approximate, not exact.

The abstract names no outside critic. It hedges the log-odds escape with the word arguably, and it hedges the cure as approximate. The second reason applies only when different people hold different evidence.

A twenty nineteen Decision Analysis review of combined probability forecasts discusses miscalibration, dependence among forecasters, and which score you use to judge them, and it ties aggregation to decisions. It does not run a test of the twenty fourteen transform, so it neither backs the settings nor knocks them down.

If the forecasts are copies of one shared error, the premise that the pool holds different evidence is not met.

A contract price is not the object that paper repairs. It is not a weighted mean of separate forecasts, and it is not their median. Nothing in the research says to nudge a live price farther toward zero or one, and that nudge would be a trading rule, which this is not. Near one half, an average of forecasts may be compressed or privately cautious, while a traded price may simply be unresolved.

Near an extreme, a traded price is not thereby a corrected poll. The claim stops at aggregated forecasts.
