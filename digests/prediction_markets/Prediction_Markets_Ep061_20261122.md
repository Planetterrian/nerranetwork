> **Forty-four attorneys general want a federal swap rule pulled before it overrides state gambling law.**

**What You Need to Know:** Forty-four attorneys general want a federal swap rule pulled before it overrides state gambling law. The comments, reported November 21 and led by New Jersey Attorney General Jennifer Davenport, oppose Commodity Futures Trading Commission proposals to treat sports- and election-related event contracts as swaps under exclusive federal oversight. Ohio Attorney General Andy Wilson and commission official Michael S. Selig are on opposite sides of whether those contracts are gambling or commodity derivatives.

### Top Story
A bipartisan group of forty-four attorneys general has submitted comments opposing Commodity Futures Trading Commission proposals that would reclassify sports- and election-related event contracts as swaps under the commission's exclusive oversight. The coalition argues the proposals could nullify state authority to regulate gambling, exceed powers Congress granted, and let operators do business without state-by-state gambling rules and responsible-betting safeguards, and it points to federal appellate decisions it says reject the commission's authority over sports betting. Davenport said the commission is going well beyond its authority and would override powers states have long exercised. This is the second objection of this kind since July 2026, when a coalition of forty-four states led by Ohio's attorney general asked that the same proposals be withdrawn, and regulators in several states are pursuing legal action against prediction-market operators. Ohio Attorney General Andy Wilson, a co-leader, said, "A sports bet isn't a derivative just because the CFTC says so," while Michael S. Selig said Americans use event contracts to hedge risks, speculate, and provide the public with information about the outcome of future events, and that the products "are commodity derivatives squarely within the CFTC's regulatory remit under the Commodity Exchange Act and are within the agency's exclusive jurisdiction." ### How It Works: The Exchange Model: Betfair and the Lay Bet
On Betfair, prices come from many participants trading with one another on a multisided platform, rather than from a single bookmaker posting a line. A 2019 paper in the Journal of Economics & Management Strategy looked at two episodes in the UK betting industry: Betfair against Flutter, also an exchange, and Betfair against traditional bookmakers. Betfair was an underfunded second mover in the exchange space and still attracted punters at a much faster rate than the better-funded first mover, Flutter. Betfair and the bookmakers competed aggressively for market share and also developed a highly complementary relationship that favored all parties. The authors argue that research should treat platform design as a choice, and should consider both competitive and cooperative dealings between incumbent bookmakers and an exchange entrant.

A 2026 comparison puts numbers on what those exchange prices already contain. The study focuses on a Weibull accelerated failure time model of goal arrival, with two extensions: team-strength parameters initialised from Betfair Exchange prices at kick-off, and cumulative shots added as a time-varying covariate once play is underway. The initialisation fits team-strength parameters to 1X2 and over/under prices by squared-error minimisation, and the paper says that fit applies to any intensity-based goal-arrival model. Across 280 English Premier League matches, evaluated at minute intervals, the market-initialised model had classification accuracy of 68.5 percent, against 69.5 percent for Betfair in-play prices. Ranked probability score was 0.1325 for the model and 0.1281 for the exchange. The paper calls the model measurably weaker than the in-play prices, while noting that it keeps interpretable team-level parameters and covariate effects. Lined up against a market-implied Poisson model, a Cox process, and a Bayesian birth process, all initialised from the same pre-match odds, market initialisation was the dominant driver of predictive accuracy, not the choice of engine.

The same paper ran a betting simulation against Betfair in-play odds and got a 1.0 percent return on investment over 37,467 bets, with a 95 percent interval from a 3.8 percent loss to a 5.9 percent gain. The authors regard that result as preliminary, given a limited sample of matches and prices that may not have been executable. The interval is the dispute inside the study: a point estimate above zero does not show a reliable gap once the range includes a loss and the quotes might not have been available to trade.

Horse-racing work draws a second limit. A Review of Finance study of betting-exchange trading on 9,562 UK horse races in 2013 and 2014 found that the volume of trade was predictive of fundamentals, which the authors read as speculative trade being, on average, conducive to market efficiency. Much of that effect was concentrated in the in-running period, when, even without trade, the race would have revealed the fundamental seconds later. A separate analysis of Betfair time series for the UK horse-racing market describes short tails, rapidly decaying autocorrelations, no long-term memory, and no gain-loss asymmetry, with a Hurst exponent showing mean reversion — tests the authors contrast with financial assets that show heavy tails and volatility clustering.

A listener can read an exchange quote with those results in view. In the football test, starting from the pre-match exchange price did more for accuracy than swapping one statistical engine for another, and the live exchange price was still slightly sharper than the model built on it. A simulated return whose range includes a loss, on prices that may not have been hittable, is not a finding that the quote was wrong.
