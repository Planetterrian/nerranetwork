Manifold, which uses play money rather than dollars, opened a market on October 22 asking whether the worst cybersecurity day of 2026 falls after October 21.

Yes was at sixty percent in the October 23 reading, among one hundred thirty-four traders. The stake there is reputation, not cash.

How it works starts with the unit, not the percent. A price between a miss and a full payout can mean mana on one venue and a dollar claim on another.

Manifold says its mana is play money, not dollars, and that the prices are forecasts from people with nothing but reputation at stake.

A Kalshi description of sports event contracts says they settle at one dollar if the named outcome hits and at zero if it does not. The cash gap, if it pays, is whatever sits between the trading price and that dollar. No pick in that. It is just the payoff rule.

Take Manifold's question on whether another millennium prize problem gets solved in 2026, read on October 23. Yes was at thirty-three percent. The page listed six hundred ninety-nine traders, and the day's volume was twenty-two thousand mana. It opened on September 8.

Buying Yes there is a play-money statement that the chance sits above that thirty-three percent price. Selling is the statement that it sits below. A wrong call costs mana and standing. A right call pays mana. The percent is an implied probability inside that game. It is not a cash vote, because the page says the stake is not dollars.

Reputation can still pull a price toward what a forecaster believes, because some people want the record. It pulls less when almost nobody is watching, when the question is obscure, or when the point is the game.

Cash pulls the other way. A dollar payout can attract someone who wants the payout more than a careful forecast. The same Kalshi explainer says trading is risky, a total loss can happen, and past results do not tell you the next ones.

So read the unit first. Mana and a trader count is a reputation price. A contract that pays at most a dollar is a cash price for a written payout. Neither number says the thing will happen, and neither is a reason to take a side.
