# Prediction Markets Daily
> **Utah seeks up to sixty-one point two million dollars from Kalshi and its market makers.**

**What You Need to Know:** Utah is in state court asking for as much as 61.2 million dollars from Kalshi and two Susquehanna firms it says kept event-contract trading running there. Robinhood’s chief executive says October event-contract volume already beat the whole prior quarter. Crypto exchanges are lining up for US licences while a voter study measures what a displayed Kalshi price does to people who never traded it.

### Top Story
On October 20, Utah Attorney General Derek Brown sued five Kalshi entities and two Susquehanna firms in Utah state court, seeking preliminary and permanent injunctions on sports, election, politics, culture and mentions contracts, restitution, and access limits through a court-approved geolocation provider — a filing that does not itself impose those limits. Utah has not regulated any form of gambling; InterGame reported Brown’s view that the contracts are indistinguishable from sportsbook bets and, via Fox13, that residents have lost between 36 million and 66 million dollars through Kalshi since February, with almost 33,000 accounts in the state. TOKENPOST reported a separate fee estimate of 30,591,600 to 61,183,200 dollars as of October 19, called an extrapolation, and Kalshi’s count of 32,940 fully signed-up accounts with Utah addresses as of February 25, which does not establish who was physically in the state. Cryptopolitan reported the allegation that Susquehanna International Group and Susquehanna Government Products act as market makers taking the other side of retail trades; those claims are not yet verified, and neither company has been shown liable. Kalshi spokesperson Jacki McGavick told Cointelegraph that Kalshi is a federally regulated financial exchange, that customers trade with each other and set the prices, that it answers to the CFTC, that no single state should have a veto, and that this state-court action does not affect its ongoing federal litigation. An August 4 federal ruling found commodities law did not bar Utah from applying its gambling laws to Kalshi’s sports contracts; Kalshi appealed, and on September 8 an appeals court rejected its bid for interim relief. ### The Ecosystem
**Robinhood's prediction markets business is now outgrowing crypto trading: Startup Fortune**
Vlad Tenev said this week at Yahoo Finance’s Invest conference that Robinhood’s prediction-markets unit traded roughly 2.5 billion event contracts in October, more than all of the third quarter combined, and called it the biggest product launch in the company’s history. At a penny a contract, Yahoo Finance put October fees at about 25 million dollars, which the piece says implies roughly 300 million dollars in annualized revenue, double the prior quarter’s run rate. Robinhood launched event contracts in March 2026 and already works with Kalshi, ForecastEX, and Rothera, a CFTC-licensed exchange built with Susquehanna that has processed more than 3.5 billion contracts since late May, about 93 percent of them FIFA World Cup markets. From September 8 it began routing a selection of college and pro football contracts to OG.com, a CFTC-regulated exchange Crypto.com operates. Kalshi posted a record 19.03 billion dollars in weekly contract volume for October 5 to 11; the piece notes Kalshi counts one-dollar face-value contracts while Polymarket tracks taker-side cash volume. Source: [startupfortune.com](https://startupfortune.com/robinhoods-prediction-markets-business-is-now-outgrowing-crypto-trading/)

**Crypto exchanges target prediction markets as token trading declines: Hedgeweek**
Blockchain.com last week applied for a US licence to offer regulated prediction markets, and Binance US has sought regulatory approval, joining Coinbase, Crypto.com and Gemini, Hedgeweek reported from a Financial Times account. The Block figures in that report show trading volume on Polymarket and Kalshi up more than 300 percent, from 17 billion dollars in January to 72 billion dollars in September, while spot crypto trading volumes fell 18 percent over the same stretch. Michigan regulators said this month that Coinbase had agreed to stop offering event contracts to customers in the state. Coinbase partnered with Kalshi earlier this year; Crypto.com spun out OG.com last month and sold stakes to Robinhood; Bullish has applied for a US licence to offer event contracts. ### How It Works: Do Traders Bet Their Hopes?
A price can move a belief even when the person looking at it never put money down. That is the mechanism a Brennan Center analysis actually measured, and it is the closest evidence in today’s material to the question of whether a number on a screen is information or a hope someone paid to see confirmed. The study does not track traders’ own politics. It tracks voters shown a Kalshi figure next to a poll, then asked who would win.

Yeji Lee’s analysis, funded by the Brennan Center for Justice and released October 20, used a survey of 1,994 self-identified registered voters fielded September 21. Each person saw five pairs of hypothetical congressional candidates. Profiles listed race, age, party and previous occupation, plus two numbers that researchers varied at random: an NBC News poll estimate and a Kalshi chance. Random assignment is how the design tries to separate the price from everything else. If the Kalshi figure still changes the pick when the poll and the biography are scrambled independently, the price is doing work of its own.

Walk the steps with the study’s own gaps. A candidate shown at 50 percent on Kalshi is the baseline. Move that displayed figure to 67 percent and respondents were 10 percentage points more likely to name that candidate the winner. Move it to 84 percent and the gain was 20 points. The same lever ran backward: 33 percent made respondents 11 points less likely to pick the candidate, and 16 percent made them 19 points less likely. Lee’s write-up says the market figure moved perceptions about as much as the traditional poll did, and more than any background trait in the profile. A warning that asked people to be careful about prediction markets had no effect on how much they used the figure.

Two findings sit in tension, and the tension is the useful part. Only 19 percent of respondents said election prediction markets are accurate, against 37 percent who said that of traditional polls. Just 29 percent said the markets reflect public opinion, against 55 percent for polls. Many were undecided: 37 percent neither agreed nor disagreed that the markets are accurate, and 29 percent took no position on whether they represent the public. People who do not trust the number still moved when it was shown. Asked about rules, 48 percent said prediction markets should be regulated more strongly, 23 percent said some types should be banned, 16 percent said all of them should be banned, and 14 percent said they should not be regulated at all. The analysis also cited polling that 7 in 10 Americans disapprove of betting on elections, and a Reuters/Ipsos poll released October 12 that found 60 percent of US adults oppose using prediction markets to wager on election results.

The Brennan Center’s reason for caring is structural, not a claim that any current price is wrong. It said more than 220 million dollars has been wagered on the 2026 midterms, and that election figures now reach the public through partnerships with outlets including CNN, CNBC and Fox News. This show takes no money from any venue and holds no position. RG.org noted that a CNN analysis put trading on 2026 election contracts at Kalshi and Polymarket above 750 million dollars. The write-up also recorded that after Minnesota enacted a law this year banning Kalshi and Polymarket, the companies persuaded a federal judge to block it before it took effect, and that the CFTC this month proposed classifying event contracts as swaps, with a companion interim rule keeping sportsbook wagers and casino games outside that definition.

What the study does not show is a trader paying up because a candidate is theirs. Nothing in the design observes an order, a position, or a partisan account. A biased display can still be a price other people are free to trade against; this experiment never tests that other side. It tests the reader. Once you know a randomly assigned Kalshi figure moved hypothetical picks by 10 to 20 points, and that a caution did nothing, a market number is something to set beside a poll and a method, not a substitute for either. The Brennan Center’s own line is that asking voters to discount the figure is easier said than done.
