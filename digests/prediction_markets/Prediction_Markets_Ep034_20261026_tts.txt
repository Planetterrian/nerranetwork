Separate yes-or-no contracts are how it works when the prices in a race do not add up to one dollar.

Each name is its own contract, written to pay a fixed unit if that name wins and nothing if another name does.

If only one can win, and someone must, a fair set of yes prices should be worth that same unit.

The screen often totals more than the unit, or less, because of the quote and the clearing, not because a second contest is buried in the first.

Every leg has a bid, where you can sell, and an ask, where you have to buy.

The printed figure might be the last trade, the middle of the spread, or the price a buyer would actually pay.

Add the asks and you are adding the costly side of every book.

Add the bids and you are adding the cheap side.

Neither total is what a sure payout costs, and neither has to match the unit those contracts pay.

A last print on a quiet name can sit far from the live bid and ask, so a column of last prices is not a basket you could lift at once.

A fee taken from the premium, or from the payout, breaks a neat conversion even when the printed prices already sum to the unit.

Buying every yes means paying the asks plus that fee, and you collect one unit only if the rules really crown exactly one winner.

A total above the payout can be the spread and the venue's cut left in the prices.

A total below can mean stale books, a missing name, or sellers who have not posted size on the names that look rich.

A thin book finishes the job.

Little size at the inside price means one small order moves that name and leaves the others alone.

The sum jumps though the race did not change, and it can stay there until someone posts the other side.

Read the sum beside the size on each leg.

The sum by itself is not a verdict on the field.

A combined order tries to stitch the legs back together, buying yes on one name and no on the rest in one action, or selling a full set, so the venue matches a package against one pool of collateral.

Negative risk, in that plain sense, treats no on every name but one as the same position as yes on the name that remains, so margin should not charge those bets as strangers.

When that netting works, selling the expensive set or buying the cheap set is what pulls the total back toward the unit.

When the venue will not net, or the book is too thin for the package to fill, the gap can just sit.

None of that says which name is cheap.

It says the column is not a pie chart.

If someone else is not even listed, the names that are listed should total less than the payout even in a perfect book, because the missing name still has a chance.

Then a sum of one is the wrong benchmark, and the rule comes before the arithmetic.

Compare leg with leg, on the same side of the book, after you ask whether the outcomes exclude each other, whether a fee sits between print and payout, and whether each contract has enough size to trade.

The gap talks about market structure.

It does not, by itself, talk about who wins.
