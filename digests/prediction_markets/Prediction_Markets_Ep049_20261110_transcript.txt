A noise trader is someone whose order is not a clean read of the facts, and that is
how it works in the papers that treat this flow as part of the price, not a leftover.
In that model, governance improves liquidity only when the market also has noise traders
the authors call semi-informed.
The strength of the effect depends on how much those traders participate.
Governance and that participation have what the model calls a synergistic effect.
Pull the semi-informed flow out, and the liquidity channel disappears.
The step underneath is the counterparty.
Someone with a better signal can trade only if someone else will take the other side.
If everyone waits on the same information, there is no trade, and the improvement never
shows up as liquidity.
This paper model stocks, not event contracts, the piece it isolates still travels, money
that is uninformed or only partly informed is not only error.
In this account, it is a condition for anyone else to trade at all.
A 2015 study in the International Journal of Financial Management looks for that mix in
Indian equities.
The authors use daily trades plus intraday prices, volumes and trade times at one day and
five-day intervals for the nifty 50 names listed on both the National Stock Exchange and
the Bombay Stock Exchange, and market returns from the NSE, Nifty and the BSE Sensex.
They ask whether return variation includes noise trader risk, and whether short run arbitrage
by informed traders pushes them into long, short hedges.
Noise they argue has a systematic piece and a firm specific piece, and both move through
time.
them. Those pieces include idiosyncratic shocks and noise shocks. At lags, a long short book
