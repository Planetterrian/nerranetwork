### Top Story
On October 9, 2026, the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission issued an interim final rule that excludes casino games and ordinary sportsbook wagers from the definition of a swap, and a separate proposal that would classify event contracts tied to sports, politics, culture and weather as swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act. The exclusion takes effect upon publication in the Federal Register, and CasinoBeats reports a 30-day public comment period on that interim final rule. Chair Mike Selig said event contracts let users "hedge risks, speculate, and provide the public with information about the outcome of future events," and, separately, "Casino-style gambling products are not derivatives." The proposal treats a possible financial, economic or commercial consequence as enough, including temperature changes that can affect agriculture, energy consumption and outdoor activity even when no immediate financial loss has occurred. Federal appeals courts disagree over sports-contract jurisdiction. The NFL backs state oversight and Robinhood backs exclusive federal authority, while state regulators argue that sports event contracts lack the consequences required to qualify as swaps and that the Act was not meant to remove states' authority over gambling. ### How It Works: Prediction Markets Inside Companies
A corporate prediction market is a market a company runs among its employees so their trades can produce a price on a question inside the firm. A 2017 paper in MIS Quarterly builds an analytical model of two inputs to that price: the precision of public information, and social interaction among the people trading.

Raising the precision of public information is not always beneficial to accuracy. The authors trace that to what they call the hidden-profiles effect. The information-aggregation mechanism places a larger-than-efficient weight on existing public information. Information that is not already public is underweighted. That is the hidden profile: evidence some participants hold and the price does not fully pick up.

A socially embedded prediction market, with information sharing among participants, may help correct that inefficiency and improve performance. The model also identifies conditions under which increased precision of public information is detrimental in a nonnetworked prediction market and, separately, detrimental in a socially embedded one. More precise public information can hurt either design. The paper's managerial implication is the detrimental effect of public information, and the role of social networking among employees, in a corporate prediction market.

Read against that model, a price is not neutral aggregation. It can lean too hard on the number everyone already shares. Whether an internal market is worth reading depends on whether private information can reach the price, and on whether employees who hold it share information before or as they trade. This is an analytical model, not a tally of named firms, so it specifies the failure mode rather than a hit rate. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.25300/misq/2017/41.4.11)
