A price can work as a forecast, and how it works is easiest to see in the old presidential betting markets.

From eighteen sixty-eight to nineteen forty, large and often well-organized markets took wagers on who would win the White House.

Buyers took a claim that paid if their candidate won, and sellers took the other side.

The trading level was the forecast, because that was where people were willing to risk money, not because anyone had run a poll.

A two thousand four article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives studies those markets and sets out four findings.

They did a remarkable job calling elections before scientific polling existed.

They were fairly efficient even though participants had limited information and even though people actively tried to manipulate the odds.

They largely disappeared, the paper argues, because scientific polls rose and because other gambling became easier to find.

The paper also discusses lessons for the present, and the abstract does not itemize them.

That second finding is the caution.
