Here is how it works when software, not a second person, posts the price.

A logarithmic market scoring rule keeps a standing quote through a price function, and a liquidity parameter, often called b, decides how far one trade shoves that quote.

Turn b up and the same order barely nudges the number. Turn it down and the quote jumps.

The sponsor is the other side of the trade, so b also caps how much that sponsor can lose while the book stays open.

A twenty eighteen paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management, on optimizing that liquidity parameter, calls this family of rules simple and widely used, and says setting b matters because it changes how the market behaves.

Its artificial markets — simulations meant to resemble live ones — found forecast standard error shrinking as the liquidity parameter grew.

For any fixed count of traders, those runs also found one setting that kept prices adaptable and reached the true value fastest.

Standard error, time to converge, and cost are the three-way choice the authors hand the sponsor.

A sleepy quote and a violent one can be the same function at two settings of b.

Higher liquidity lined up, in those simulations, with a smaller forecast standard error, while the quickest convergence for a given crowd sat at the value that still let the price adapt.

Cost is in that choice too, because someone is paying to keep the quote alive.

Reading the number means reading how far one order may move it, and who is standing opposite. Nothing in the paper is a reason to take either side.
