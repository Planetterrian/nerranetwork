### How It Works: Streaks: The Gambler's Fallacy and the Hot Hand
A streak is a run of similar outcomes. Two opposite readings of that run have names. The gambler's fallacy says the run is due to reverse, as if independent events owed the other result. The hot hand says the run will continue, as if the person or the team had changed. If each draw really is independent, neither reading is information. If the draws are not independent — a pitcher, a lineup, a team on a road trip — the run might be a real change in the chance, a change only in what bettors believe, or both at once. A price that moves after a streak is not, by itself, proof of either story.

A 2014 paper in the International Journal of Financial Studies walked this through 34 National Basketball Association regular seasons. The authors estimated a seemingly unrelated regression on the full set of seasons and found that streaks significantly affected both the point spread and the difference in actual points. Read that in order. A team wins several in a row. The spread is the price bookmakers post for the margin. If the hot hand is only a story bettors tell, the spread can move while the actual margin does not. The pooled result said both moved: the price and the points. That is the paper's evidence that, across those 34 seasons taken together, a streak lined up with something in the game, not only with the betting line. Then the same paper estimated each season on its own. Differences showed up in particular for winning and losing streaks of six games or more. The pooled link did not hold in the same way every year once the run got that long. The listener's worked example is that six-game case: a price that jumps after six wins is consistent with the all-season finding that streaks moved spreads, and also consistent with the season-by-season finding that the relationship was unstable. The paper's own conclusion names both momentum effects and the gambler's fallacy. It does not hand the reader a rule for which one is operating in a single week.

A 2013 paper in the Journal of Gambling Business and Economics used six NBA seasons and split belief from profit. Wagering against the hot hand did not win more than efficiency implied. Ordinary least squares and two-stage least squares regressions still found that teams on streaks drew a significantly higher number of bets, so bettors behaved as if they believed in the hot hand. The closing point spread remained, in that study, an optimal and unbiased forecast of the outcome. Earlier work had assumed bookmakers ran a balanced book. This paper notes they may instead price as a forecast or shade a spread to exploit a known bias, which is why a closing line can look efficient even while the public piles onto a streak.

A 2026 paper in the International Journal of Sport Finance moved the same question to Major League Baseball starting pitchers and to two different prices. Pitchers were classed hot, cold, or neutral from context-adjusted recent performance. Short-term streaks predicted daily-fantasy outcomes. DraftKings salaries did not fully price that: hot pitchers were underpriced and cold pitchers were overpriced. Sportsbook lines, by contrast, tended to overreact, particularly for visiting hot starters and cold home starters. Simulated betting strategies built on those patterns did not yield statistically significant positive profits. The authors read that as sportsbooks adjusting prices to capitalize on user biases. The institutional point is the useful one: the same streak can be underweighted in a salary and overweighted in a betting line, and neither misweighting is the same thing as a strategy that pays.

A 2006 paper in Judgment and Decision Making supplies the clean independent case. Roulette spins in a casino are not a change in skill. Players still showed the hot hand and the gambler's fallacy, with significant differences from person to person. Across individuals, the two biases were positively and significantly correlated, which the authors treat as a sign of a common root. They speculate that root is locus of control. Where events are independent, both the expectation of reversal and the expectation of continuation are errors. Where events might not be independent, the sports papers disagree about how much of the streak survives into the result. The 2014 study finds streaks in the actual point difference as well as in the spread. The 2013 study finds the closing line unbiased and fading the streak unprofitable. The 2026 study finds mispricing in salaries and overreaction in lines, then finds no statistically significant profit from trading against either.

A listener who has those four results reads a post-streak price as a claim that still needs a reason. The reason might be that bettors are chasing a run — the 2013 result that streaking teams attract more bets, and the 2006 result that the same person can hold both sequence errors. The reason might be that something about the competitor changed — the 2014 result that actual margins moved with streaks in the pooled seasons, with the caveat that six-game streaks did not look the same every season. The 2026 result is the caution against treating either reading as a trade: a salary or a line can misweight a streak, and a simulated strategy that tries to exploit that misweighting still may not show a significant profit. None of this says which side of any live contract is the better buy. It says a run, by itself, is not the forecast. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijfs2040359)
