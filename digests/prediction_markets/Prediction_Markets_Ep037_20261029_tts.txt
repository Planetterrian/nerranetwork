A day after the Commodity Futures Trading Commission asked a federal court to halt Utah's enforcement, Attorney General Derek Brown filed criminal charges in Utah against the company and co-founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara, on top of the civil case already underway.

The October twenty-eighth filing splits into one second-degree felony for a pattern of unlawful activity, twelve third-degree felonies for online gambling promotion, and one class A misdemeanor for general gambling promotion.

In Utah that second-degree count can mean up to fifteen years in prison and a ten-thousand-dollar fine. A third-degree felony can mean up to five years and five thousand dollars. A class A misdemeanor can mean up to three hundred sixty-four days and a two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar fine.

Special Agent Scott Eggerman of the attorney general's office opened a Kalshi account on September thirteenth with a Utah address and a debit card. The platform asked him to confirm his location, and he confirmed he was in Utah.

He placed twelve sports wagers totaling one hundred fourteen dollars and eighty-seven cents and booked a net loss of twenty-five dollars and ten cents. Kalshi collected a fee of three to seventy-eight cents on each trade.

The state issued summonses, not arrest warrants. Mansour and Lopes Lara are to answer in Third District Court in Salt Lake County.

Whether these venues are available, and for which contracts, depends on where you live. The rules differ by country, province, and state, and they are changing.

A contract that pays only if an event happens is a claim on cash that shows up when the contract settles. If that date is a year away, the payout is a year away even for someone who is already sure.

Collateral posted to hold the position sits until then. Cash that is locked cannot be spent, lent, or posted somewhere else. Whatever that cash could have earned over the wait is a cost of holding the contract.

A trader who must borrow the collateral, or who has another use for it, will not hand over today the full amount they think the contract is likely to pay later. The price can sit below the probability that trader actually holds. The gap is a charge for time, not a quieter forecast.

Take a trader whose own belief is that the event is more likely than not, and a contract that cannot pay until next year. The most they will post today is that belief, reduced by what they give up while the collateral is stuck.

The reduction tracks the return available on that cash over the life of the contract, plus any fee the venue takes for keeping the position open. A contract that settles next week has almost none of that wait.

The same belief, on the same event, can therefore show a higher price on the near contract than on the far one. Reading the far price as if it were the near price treats two different claims as one forecast.

Venues that pay interest on collateral change the arithmetic. If the cash posted earns a return while it waits, the trader is no longer giving up the whole alternative yield. The time charge shrinks, and the price can sit closer to the probability the trader holds.

A venue that pays nothing on margin leaves the full cost of the wait inside the price. Comparing a long-dated price on a venue that pays interest with one on a venue that does not, without knowing which is which, mixes a funding term into what looks like a probability.

The adjustment fails when capital is not what binds. A position limit, a fee that does not scale with time, or a resolution rule that can void or rewrite the contract can move the price for reasons that have nothing to do with the calendar.

A thin market can print a number that no one was willing to defend with size. Play money, where the stake is reputation rather than cash that could have earned a return, does not carry the same time charge. Its long-dated price is not the same object as a cash price on a distant contract.

So read a far-dated price with the settlement date in view, and ask whether the collateral earns anything while it sits. A price below a stated belief is not, by itself, a claim that the event is less likely. It may be the cost of waiting to be paid.
