Reading a three percent contract starts with what the price is buying. Yes pays one dollar if the event resolves yes and nothing if it resolves no. Three cents for that dollar is a three percent price.

It is not this show saying the event will happen, and it is not a side to take.

Flip the contract. Yes at three cents is the same quote as no near ninety-seven cents, when the two payoffs add to a dollar.

The yes buyer parks three cents until resolution. The no buyer parks about ninety-seven cents for the same wait.

That wait is the time value. Cash in the favorite cannot move to another question until the rules say the contract is done.

A fee bites harder on three cents than on fifty. The same charge is a large share of a long-shot stake and a small share of a coin-flip stake.

So the screen price and the return someone keeps are different numbers, and the gap is wider in the tail.

At fifty cents, both sides lock similar cash, and a fee is a similar fraction of either. At three cents, the displayed chance, the cash stuck, and the fee’s share of that cash pull apart.

A basket of rare events can each print at a few cents. If people buy them like lottery tickets — a small stake for a dollar — the price can sit above how often those events actually hit, and no single contract looks absurd alone.

Sellers of that cheap side may also want extra pay for the rare chance the favorite loses everything. Either pressure means three cents is a price, not a clean frequency.
