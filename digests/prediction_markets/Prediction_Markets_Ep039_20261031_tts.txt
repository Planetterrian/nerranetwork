Copying the last trade instead of using what you know is how an information cascade shoves a price off the evidence, and a price that can move is supposed to stop that.

Avery and Zemsky laid out the theory in 1998, and a 2005 American Economic Review internet experiment put it to a test.

More than sixty-four hundred people took part, two hundred sixty-seven of them consultants at an international firm.

Subjects traded in a market where the price was free to move, so earlier trades were already in the number.

That flexible price stopped herding.

People did not just stack onto the previous trade and drop their own signal.

Contrarian trades still yanked prices away from the evidence.

Even after twenty decisions, landing on the fundamental value was rare.

Those consultants did not act in a meaningfully different way from the rest of the group.

A 2007 Journal of Finance paper sat Chicago Board of Trade professionals in a controlled setting next to students.

Across more than fifteen hundred individual decisions, the professionals were better at telling a strong public signal from a weak one.

Their choices also did not shift with the domain of earnings, and the student group did not show that same pattern.

The authors read the gap as a point about market efficiency: who is trading changes how public signals get used.

Prosper dot com, then the biggest microloan market in the country, is where a 2012 Management Science paper went looking for herds.

Listings that were already well funded drew still more money after controls for hidden listing differences and for payoff externalities.

Lenders were learning from peers, not only mimicking them, and they used public borrower facts to temper what the crowd seemed to say.

The paper calls that rational herding, and it calls passive copying irrational herding.

A bad credit grade made the herd stronger, because funders inferred the borrower had to be better than the grade or the money would not have shown up.

A friend endorsement made the herd weaker, because the crowd could be blamed on a fact everyone could already see.

Rational herding predicted how loans performed better than irrational herding did.

Crypto got the same question in a 2018 Annals of Financial Economics paper, using the CSAD method from Chang and coauthors in 2000.

Daily returns covered six major crypto-currencies plus the CCI30 index, from 07-08-2015 to 18-01-2018.

Herding appeared in rising markets and in falling ones, which the paper treats as over-enthusiasm and over-reaction.

Volatility did not matter in a significant way.

What mattered was how active the market was.

A 2013 review in the Review of Behavioral Finance grants two decades of real insight and still calls the evidence inconclusive in spots.

Relatively little is known once you leave equity markets, and the review wants methods that fix the limits of the ones in use.
