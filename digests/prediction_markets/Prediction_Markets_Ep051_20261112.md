> **Five hundred eighty thousand dollars is at stake in a Bellevue man's suit against Kalshi.**

**What You Need to Know:** Five hundred eighty thousand dollars is at stake in a Bellevue man's suit against Kalshi. Scott Bright filed October 23 in federal court in Seattle, arguing Washington law treats the sports contracts as illegal gambling and lets him recover losses event by event. ### Top Story
Scott Bright of Bellevue sued Kalshi in U.S. District Court in Seattle in a complaint filed October 23, seeking $580,544.98 under Washington's Recovery of Money Lost at Gambling Act and alleging that the venue's sports event contracts are illegal sports betting and a violation of the state's Consumer Protection Act. He deposited $315,353.94 in 253 transactions between June 17 and August 14, 2026, wagering on Major League Baseball, professional tennis, and professional baseball in South Korea and Japan. The complaint puts funding fees near $5,600, trading fees at $84,062, net realized losses including fees at $308,781.62, and a remaining balance of $983.27. His lawyers argue the state statute allows recovery event by event, so a winning bet cannot offset a losing one, and he is suing only for himself, not a class. Every one of those deposits came after Attorney General Nick Brown sued KalshiEX on March 27, and after King County Superior Court Judge John F. McHale granted a preliminary injunction on July 17 — amended August 12 to direct Kalshi to stop offering, accepting, and facilitating sports-related event contracts in Washington — the complaint says Kalshi still took his money through August 14, then later showed a notice that the app was prohibited in his location. The complaint also cites Kalshi's rulebook disclosure that an affiliate places orders on either side of trades with profitability as its business purpose, and alleges that affiliate or other market makers may have taken the other side of some of his bets. ### How It Works: Extremizing: Pushing an Average Away From the Middle
An average of probabilities is a biased instrument in a specific direction, toward one half, according to the 2014 paper in Decision Analysis titled Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More Extreme. The authors describe the common method as a simple weighted average of many individuals' estimates of an uncertain event. They argue those aggregated probabilities correspond more closely to the real world if they are transformed by being pushed closer to zero or to one.

The first distorting factor is compression of the probability scale at the two ends. Random error tends to push the average probability toward one half, because error that would have fallen beyond the end of the scale cannot, and so it accumulates on the inside. That effect does not occur for the median forecast. Arguably, the authors add, it does not occur for the mean of the log odds of the individual forecasts either.

The second factor affects the mean, the median, and the mean of the log odds. Forecasters take into account their individual ignorance of the total body of information available. Individual confidence in the direction of a probability judgment, high or low, thus fails to take into account the wisdom of crowds that results from combining different evidence available to different judges. Each person shades inward for a gap that the group, taken together, may already have filled.

The paper's first finding is that the same transformation function can approximately eliminate both distorting effects, with different parameters for the mean and for the median. The second finding is that use of the median can help distinguish the two effects, because the scale-compression problem is identified with the average and not the median, while the ignorance problem remains in the median. The abstract does not report the parameter values, so the size of either push is not a figure available from the source.

A weighted average of individual probabilities is pulled toward one half by error that bunches inside the ends of the scale. The median of those same forecasts drops that pull. Both still include each forecaster's discount for information they personally lacked. One transformation then pushes the aggregate toward zero or one, with a different parameter for the mean than for the median. The extra push the mean requires is how the authors separate compression from ignorance, and the median's remaining push is the ignorance effect. They call the fix approximate.

The paper itself qualifies the log-odds escape hatch with the word arguably, and it qualifies the cure as approximate rather than exact. The second factor is stated only for the case in which different judges hold different evidence. A 2019 Decision Analysis paper on probability forecasts and their combination takes up miscalibration, dependence among forecasters, and the choice of an evaluation measure, and it connects aggregation to decision making. It does not measure the 2014 transformation, so it neither confirms the parameters nor disputes them. Dependence is the neighboring problem: if the forecasts are not drawing on different evidence, the wisdom-of-crowds premise in the 2014 account is not met.

A listener should not treat a contract price as the average this paper repairs. A price is not a weighted mean of independent forecasts, and it is not their median. Nothing in the paper says to push a live price closer to zero or one, and that would be a trading rule, which this is not. Near one half, a forecast average may be compressed or privately cautious; a traded price may simply be unresolved. Near an extreme, a traded price is not thereby a corrected poll. The paper's claim stops at aggregated forecasts. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.2014.0293) Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.2019.0391)
