Elder D. Todd Christofferson sits on the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

On Saturday, in the opening session of the church’s semi-annual general conference in Salt Lake City, he called every form of gambling, prediction markets included, morally wrong.

His picture of the shift is the phone in a pocket. Gambling used to mean a trip to a casino, a racetrack, or a lottery kiosk. Now it is continuous, private, and almost anywhere, and he said that change produced an explosion.

He pointed to a survey in which more than forty-five percent of adults had gambled in the previous year.

Then he put the loss on the customer. His line was that the industry’s revenue is the gambler’s loss.

Greed and debt are the habits he named, because one person’s gain is someone else’s expense.

Easier access, he warned, can make compulsive repetition more likely.

A proper score is how it works when someone wants a private probability and can pay only after the event resolves.

A strictly proper scoring rule makes honesty the strategic agent’s best move. More accurate forecasts get higher scores in expectation.

A twenty eighteen paper on the arXiv takes that bargain as the baseline, then asks what happens if the outcome never shows up and the principal has only the reports.

Surrogate scoring rules add a bias correction and an error-rate estimate for a reference answer built from those reports.

Give the method one bit about the prior, and run it across multiple tasks, and the paper says the surrogate recovers the strictly proper score in expectation, as if the ground truth were there.

Quality can still be measured without that ground truth. Dominant truthfulness in reporting comes along as a by-product.

The check was theoretical, and it was also run on data from real human forecasters.

Multivariate questions are a different crack, taken up in a twenty fifteen Monthly Weather Review paper. Few proper scores exist for them, and many need a full density.

The energy score generalizes the continuous ranked probability score. That paper calls it the only commonly used score that works on an ensemble, meaning a finite sample.

It is weak at catching correlations that were specified wrong.

Variogram-based proper scores borrow a geostatistical idea. In simulations they were more sensitive to bad means, bad variances, and bad correlations, and sharper on the correlation structure itself.

A case study of postprocessed wind-speed forecasts at five Colorado wind-park sites showed the same pattern.

A contract that pays when an event resolves is the setting where a proper rule can make honesty pay.

No ground truth, or several correlated pieces in one question, and these papers say the score you pick decides which mistakes stay invisible.
