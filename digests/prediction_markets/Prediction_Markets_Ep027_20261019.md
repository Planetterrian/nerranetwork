# Prediction Markets Daily
> **Two CFTC exchanges sued Missouri to stop the state treating sports contracts as bets.**

**What You Need to Know:** Two CFTC-registered exchanges sued Missouri to stop the state from treating sports event contracts as unlicensed wagers, filing in federal court on October 16 after Attorney General Catherine Hanaway told six operators to halt or get licensed. At DC Fintech Week 2026, Kalshi's chief executive said a state victory could push the markets offshore. The American Gaming Association, the same day, pointed to a literacy study and to briefs asking the U.S. Supreme Court to let states regulate the products.

### Top Story
North American Derivatives Exchange, the CFTC-registered exchange behind Crypto.com's prediction-market platform, and Novig's Ludlow Exchange sued Missouri officials in federal court on October 16, asking judges to block the state from enforcing its sports-wagering laws against their sports event contracts. Attorney General Catherine Hanaway had sent letters dated September 16 and 17 to those two firms and to Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog, announced by her office on September 18, telling each to stop offering the contracts in Missouri or obtain a Gaming Commission license, pay a 10 percent tax on adjusted gross sports-wagering revenue, and face license fees that can reach $500,000 upfront and again every five years. Ludlow, designated a contract market on June 16, 2026, argues the contracts are swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act and that the CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction; it wants a preliminary injunction while the Eighth Circuit finishes briefing in Kalshi's Iowa appeal, KalshiEX LLC v. Bird, on December 6. Crypto.com's complaint, as described by The Block and not on the public docket as of Sunday, seeks a declaratory judgment and a permanent injunction, says Hanaway's letter went to Foris DAX rather than the exchange, and says a state license is not a realistic path because Missouri's constitution allows only two direct mobile sports-wagering licenses and both have been awarded. As of Sunday, Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog had not sued in either Missouri federal district, and Hanaway's office has described ongoing discussions with Kalshi. Hanaway's letters treat the contracts as unlicensed sports wagering, argue that most federal courts to take up the issue have found the Commodity Exchange Act does not preempt state gambling law and that the contracts are not swaps, and say companies cannot evade Missouri law by dressing sports betting up as event contracts; her office also said Crypto.com allows users 18 and older, below Missouri's minimum of 21, a point raised with five of the six firms but not with Novig. **Gaming study puts prediction-market users behind sports bettors on the math: Las Vegas Sun**
Carly Johnson, director of research at the American Gaming Association, said Monday that the association's study, "Playing the Numbers: Financial Literacy and Numeracy in Gamblers and Non-Gamblers," found gamblers outscored non-gamblers on financial literacy, with sports bettors highest in every group measured. Johnson said prediction-market users have the confidence of sports bettors and the math skills of someone who does not bet at all, on platforms she described as offering sports contracts without the responsible-gaming tools required of state-licensed sportsbooks, while marketing the products as investing. The AGA filed an amicus brief in October supporting New Jersey's petition for the U.S. Supreme Court to rule on state authority over markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket; the NFL and a coalition of 39 states and Washington, D.C., filed separate briefs, and the coalition said the issue "has resulted in chaos nationwide." Johnson said the regulated gaming industry spends about $472 million a year on responsible-gaming programs and that gaming taxes supply over $130 million a year for problem-gambling services, "where prediction markets pay zero." ### How It Works: Why a 95-Cent Contract Is Not Free Money
A prediction-market contract pays one dollar if a stated outcome occurs and nothing if it does not. The Globe and Mail describes the usual purchase price as somewhere between one cent and ninety-nine cents. That price, plus any fee, stays locked in the contract until it resolves. The most a correct call can return is the gap between what was paid and one dollar. A miss takes the whole amount paid. A contract priced at ninety-five cents leaves five cents as the entire possible gain on ninety-five cents that cannot be used elsewhere until resolution, and a wrong call loses the ninety-five cents. Those five cents are not interest. They are the remainder on an all-or-nothing claim.

The Globe's worked figures are Canadian access fees on U.S. event contracts, and they show how a flat charge eats the gap. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization permits authorized investment dealers to offer a limited selection of event contracts traded and cleared through certain exchanges and clearing houses regulated by the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Currently only Wealthsimple and Interactive Brokers Canada have that permission. Wealthsimple Predict gives clients a subset of contracts listed on Kalshi. Interactive Brokers Canada offers contracts on Kalshi and on its own exchange, ForecastEx.

Wealthsimple charges a fixed two U.S. cents per contract, up front. A contract priced at 42 U.S. cents therefore costs 44 U.S. cents. A correct call pays one U.S. dollar. A wrong call loses the 44 cents. Interactive Brokers Canada charges two U.S. cents per contract on Kalshi and one U.S. cent per contract on ForecastEx. U.S. investors trading directly on Kalshi pay a fee that varies with price and with the number of contracts, not a flat amount per contract.

Buying 100 contracts priced at one U.S. cent each would cost 63 U.S. cents in trading fees directly on Kalshi. The same 100 contracts through Wealthsimple Predict would cost two U.S. dollars in trading fees, more than three times as much. At 50 U.S. cents the gap narrows: 100 contracts would cost $1.75 in fees on Kalshi, against two U.S. dollars through Wealthsimple Predict. Two U.S. cents is 20 percent of a contract priced at 10 U.S. cents, and 4 percent of a contract priced at 50 U.S. cents. Benjamin Felix, chief investment officer and portfolio manager at PWL Capital, told the Globe that on a low-price contract the percentage fees get significant quickly.

Currency is a second charge, because the contracts are denominated in U.S. dollars. Wealthsimple's standard foreign-exchange fee is 1.5 percent, on a live rate that includes a spread, when a client converts Canadian dollars. Clients who already hold U.S. dollars in a Wealthsimple account can transfer them to Wealthsimple Predict and skip that conversion, the company said. At Interactive Brokers Canada, conversion fees start at 0.002 percent of the amount converted, with no added spread, but a minimum US$2 commission means a small conversion costs more than the headline rate. Felix said a per-contract fee plus a currency-exchange fee starts to add up quickly and aggressively, and that net of fees and costs the arithmetic is stacked against the trader.

None of those figures is a view on which side of any contract is mispriced. The reading rule is smaller. Subtract the fee, in the same unit as the price, before treating the gap to one dollar as a return, and subtract a currency conversion if the dollars in hand are not the dollars the contract uses. On the Globe's one-cent example, the trading fee alone can exceed the price of the contracts. On its fifty-cent example, the same flat fee is a smaller share of the price and still sits on an all-or-nothing payoff. A price near one dollar leaves a thinner remainder for that fee to consume, which is why the leftover cents are not a savings rate and not a locked profit. The screen price is the cost of a claim.
