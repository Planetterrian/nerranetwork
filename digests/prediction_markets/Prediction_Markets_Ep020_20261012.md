> **Compliance teams can miss event-contract trades sitting outside brokerage accounts.**

**What You Need to Know:** Compliance programmes built for brokerage accounts can leave event-contract trades outside the controls firms already run. That warning led a StarCompliance webinar on digital and prediction markets, with former U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission enforcement director Ian McGinley on the panel. The session also pressed firms to tie disclosure and surveillance to the trading itself, including on blockchain venues, rather than to an old product label.

### Top Story
FinTech Global reports that StarCompliance used a webinar, New Markets, Same Risk: Compliance Challenges in Digital and Prediction Markets, to argue that prediction markets, tokenised securities and digital assets create familiar conduct risks in a wrapper many employee-trading programmes do not see. The session is part of the firm’s 2026 Global Compliance Benchmark Study webinar series, building on a first Benchmark Study that pointed to rapid artificial-intelligence adoption, emerging-market risks and pressure to modernise compliance programmes. The panel was Sidley Austin partner Ian McGinley, formerly director of enforcement at the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission; Kate Dellolio of a16z, who supports that firm’s crypto vertical; and Bryan Choe of RWA.XYZ, a data platform for tokenised assets. The first concern raised was that recent enforcement has shown employees could use sensitive or non-public information to trade event contracts, while personal-account programmes built around securities and brokerage accounts may not capture that activity at all. A second concern was policy drift: codes of ethics written for reportable securities and securities accounts do not always fit these products, so the suggested fix is to decide when disclosure, pre-clearance or a restriction should follow the activity rather than rewrite the code for every new wrapper. Third, tokenisation is blurring traditional and digital finance — digital assets now run past Bitcoin and Ethereum, and conventional instruments are increasingly represented on blockchains — so account disclosure, pre-clearance and surveillance may have to cover both kinds of venue because the underlying risk does not change with the pipes. ### How It Works: Why Economists Care: Prices as Public Information
A binary event contract pays a fixed amount if a stated event occurs and nothing if it does not. In a market where traders can buy and sell that contract, the price is the point at which the people willing to take the other side have stopped arguing with their money. Anyone can read it. They do not have to pay the researchers, open the filings or sit in the room where the information was gathered. That is the public-information case: the forecast is a byproduct of trade, posted continuously, and free to the next reader.

The cost of producing that byproduct is the part the slogan skips. A 2020 paper, Timely Information from Prediction Markets, treats prediction markets as tools that elicit and aggregate beliefs from strategic agents, then points at a waste channel: agents may exhaust the social welfare by competing to be first to update the market. The design problem it sets is a trade-off between how quickly information is aggregated and how much that information costs. To maximise social welfare, the market has to pay people to acquire information quickly enough to be useful, but not faster, and more expensively, than necessary, and it has to get them to report truthfully and on time. The paper studies two settings — information that is valuable only before a deadline, and information whose value falls as time passes — and says it uses theorems and simulations to demonstrate the mechanisms. It does not hand a reader a live accuracy score.

A separate research design asks what changes when institutional liquidity shows up. What Happens When Institutional Liquidity Enters Prediction Markets defines a market-quality lens, separates channels through which that liquidity can enter, and maps identification problems in live venue data. Its empirical piece is a synthetic microstructure laboratory, offered as a proof of concept for the measurement pipeline, not as a settlement of the live question. The lesson it draws is narrow on purpose: market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives and automation do not have to work through the same channel; average liquidity gains do not have to become equal gains for every trader; and the sharpest welfare losses are most likely in shock states, when slower takers receive the least pass-through of tighter quoted markets.

Read a posted price, then, as a public number with a private production cost. A tighter quote is not evidence that the slowest reader captured the improvement, and a few minutes of extra speed is not evidence that the race to post it was worth what it burned.
