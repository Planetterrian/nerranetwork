Volume conditions a price move, and that is the mechanism in how it works, not a certificate that the move was informed.

A paper on arXiv treats a prediction market as a Bayesian inverse problem.

The unknown is a yes-or-no outcome.

The data are a history of market-implied probabilities and the volumes traded along that path.

The observation model sits in log-odds space.

Conditional on volume, each price increment comes from a latent mixture of trader types.

Informed flow, uninformed flow, heavy-tailed microstructure noise, and adversarial or manipulative flow can all produce an increment.

Price and volume are the only observables the model requires.

Start with a prior on the outcome, then watch the path.

Convert the probability path into log-odds so the increments add.

At each step, condition on that step’s volume and ask which type-mixture was likeliest to produce an increment of that size.

Update the posterior on the outcome only if the laws of those increments, given each outcome, can be told apart.

The paper’s identifiability test is Kullback–Leibler separation between those laws.

If the increment law when the event happens looks like the law when it does not, the history is not informative, however large the volume.

Under regularity assumptions the paper states posterior concentration and finite-sample error bounds.

Stability, in that paper, is how far posterior odds move when the observed price-and-volume path is perturbed.

Realized information gain is the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the posterior from the prior.

Expected information gain is the mutual information between the outcome and the history.

Synthetic experiments in the paper are described as checking those concentration rates and identifiability thresholds.

They are not a test of any live venue.

A Mathematical Finance paper on dynamic trading volume gives a second reason a big volume figure can be plumbing.

In a market with finite depth and constant investment opportunities, a large investor with a long horizon and constant relative risk aversion trades a safe asset and a risky asset.

Volume in that model approximately follows a Gaussian, mean-reverting diffusion.

It rises with depth, with volatility, and with risk aversion.

Finite depth also rules out leverage and short sales, because those positions may not stay solvent even with continuous trading.

Put the two papers together and volume can scale with the book, the jumps, and the trader’s risk aversion, while the inverse problem can still fail to separate types.

The failure the arXiv paper names is type-composition confounding, or a symmetry between the outcome and a nuisance factor.

The same path can come from different mixes of traders, and then the inverse problem is ill-posed.

More of the same history does not tighten a posterior you can trust.

A busy market is not automatically a better forecast than a quiet one.

Large volume says many contracts changed hands, or that depth, volatility, or risk aversion were high enough to generate the trading.

It does not say the traders were informed, that the types were identifiable, or that a small change in the path would have left the price where it is.

A thin history can be uninformative because there is little to condition on.

A thick history can be uninformative because the increment laws do not separate.

The useful question is whether the path distinguishes the outcome, not whether the volume figure is large.
