### How It Works: Calibration: What It Means for a Forecast to Be Right
Calibration means the stated probability matches how often the outcome arrives, across many events rather than one. A sixty percent forecast is calibrated, in that class of calls, if the event happens about sixty percent of the time. Sharpness is separate: probability mass near zero or one, rather than near fifty percent. Sharp and calibrated are not the same thing. One surprising result does not make the forecast wrong, because the score is taken over the set, after the events resolve.

Monthly Weather Review in 2013 describes an improvement to ensemble kernel density model output statistics, called EKDMOS. Model output statistics equations are multiple linear regressions relating observations to ensemble-mean predictors. Spread-skill relationships are one-term linear regressions that predict the ensemble mean’s expected accuracy from the ensemble spread. The method applies the equations to each member, fits a probability density by kernel density fitting, and adjusts that density’s spread to the spread predicted by the spread-skill relationship. On North American Ensemble Forecast System two-meter temperature forecasts from October 1, 2007, through March 31, 2010, the improved EKDMOS forecasts were more reliable than an earlier spread adjustment and than rank-sorting bias-corrected members, with a better calibrated spread-error relationship and more day-to-day spread variability.

Weather and Forecasting in 2010 compares bias correction with spatial disaggregation and an analog method on daily precipitation forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Ensemble Prediction System, over a ten-day period, for the Ohio River basin from 2002 through 2006, against Tropical Rainfall Monitoring Mission observations. Neither beat a simple spatial-interpolation benchmark on forecast skill. Both improved reliability, the analog more so. Bias correction improved the bias for all forecast amounts, less so for large amounts, but the downscaled patterns were unrealistic. The analog reduced biases over a wider range of amounts and produced more realistic patterns.

Reading a market, reliability is whether the price’s implied probability matches later frequency, and sharpness is how far that price sits from fifty percent. Those tests show a fix can improve reliability without improving skill against a simple benchmark. A single resolution does not settle calibration, including when a market is thin or the stake is play money.
