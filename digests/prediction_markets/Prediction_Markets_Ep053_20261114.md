### How It Works: Anchoring: How the First Number Sticks
Anchoring is the pull of a first number on a later estimate. In the version decision researchers call anchoring and adjustment, a person starts at that initial figure and revises, and the revision often stops short of where a calculation made from scratch would land. A contract price is that kind of first number: it is on the screen before the reader has finished forming an estimate of their own.

A journal experiment makes the sequence concrete. Revista Finanzas y Política Económica describes work with 670 students in Concepción, Chile, Cali, Colombia, and Lima, Peru. Participants saw graphics of the S&P MILA Pacific Alliance Select index for a year, a month, a week, and the last closing value, then made a forecast judgment of the index. Least squares and a data panel were the analysis. The authors report that the anchoring and adjustment heuristic influenced those forecasts. From the same judgments they infer optimism bias: the issued forecast leaned optimistic or pessimistic rather than sitting neutral to the charts they had just been shown. The design isolates the starting display. Everyone saw the same index, arranged from a long window down to the last close, and the last close is the number closest to the moment of the judgment. If later estimates cluster toward that close, or toward whichever window the person treated as the reference, the forecast is not independent of the first figure on the page. The authors’ least-squares and panel results say that influence was present for this index. The optimism finding is separate and directional: the cognitive process of the forecast was biased toward an optimistic or pessimistic position, which the paper treats as an error of expectation sitting alongside the anchor.

Financial Management carries a second finding, from survey forecasts of macroeconomic statistics. Those forecasts systematically underpredict the statistics. Past macroeconomic announcements predict the forecast errors, which is the signature of an anchor in the history rather than a one-off miss. The stock market does not see through the bias. The authors find statistically significant stock-price effects of the “anticipated” components of macroeconomic announcements. Their account is that investors overweight historical information and do not make sufficient adjustments after new information arrives. The anticipated piece is the part a rational reader would already have in the price. A detectable price effect on that piece means the market treated stale forecast content as news.

An online prediction-market study disputes the idea that the pull is automatically an error. The arXiv paper on interval elicitation of forecasts split forecasters by what they could see. People who could not see the current state of the market until they had made their own separate estimates moved those estimates closer to the market forecast once the market became known. Their first edits to the market forecast were very similar to the first edits of forecasters who could always see the market. Accuracy was similar in both conditions. The authors conclude that the more elaborate elicitation method might not improve forecasts, and that any anchoring on the state of the market does not constitute an error in judgment. On their reading, stepping toward the visible price can be an update, not a failure to think.

Read a contract with that split in mind. The student charts and the macro surveys say a first number can leave the next estimate under-revised, and that a market of investors can fail to strip the stale part out of a forecast. The prediction-market experiment says moving toward the visible price can be what informed forecasters do, with no accuracy gap in that test relative to people who saw the price the whole time. The practical distinction is which number arrived first and what question it was answering. A last close on a chart, a prior macro announcement, and a contract price are different anchors. Noticing which one you saw before you had an estimate of your own is the use of the research. None of it is a reason to take a side of any contract. A play-money price, such as a Manifold reading in mana, is still only the number those traders produced with reputation at stake.
