The approach that won a U.S. Intelligence Community geopolitical forecasting tournament was to pull the best performers out each year and put them on elite teams.

A twenty fifteen paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science names the people on those teams superforecasters.

They stayed accurate across hundreds of questions and a wide spread of topics.

They did that two years running, against the usual expectation that extreme scores fall back toward the average.

The authors see four supports working together: how people think, skills built for the task, motivation and commitment, and a richer setting to work in.

On their account, these forecasters are found and also made.

Tournament incentives, the paper says, surface judgment that lab studies of ordinary performance would miss.

A twenty fourteen paper in Psychological Science sets the scene: five university research groups spent two years recruiting forecasters, collecting predictions, and turning them into probabilities for geopolitical events.

One group tested training, teaming, and tracking, and found support for all three.

Training pushed back on cognitive biases, steered people toward reference classes, and taught shortcuts, including an average when several estimates were already on the table.

Teaming meant sharing information and arguing the reasons for a belief.

Tracking put the top two percent from the first year onto elite teams that kept working together.

Training, collaboration, and that tracking step improved calibration and resolution.

Statistical algorithms, described in other work, made the combined number more accurate.

Using the statistics and the psychology together produced the best forecasts two years in a row.
