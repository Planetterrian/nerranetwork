Whether these venues are available, and for which contracts, depends on where you live. The rules differ by country, province, and state, and they are changing.

Who posts a quote, and why a venue would pay for one, is the mechanism in how it works today.

A prediction-market contract pays one of two amounts when it resolves, so size still open at the end jumps to a full payout or to zero.

That settlement exposure is a different control problem from quoting a stock or a future you can roll.

The number on the screen is a probability, and the quote is what turns an incoming order into an update of that probability.

A paper on arXiv, Optimal Market Making in Prediction Markets, models that price as a conditional probability generated by transforming a latent belief that diffuses over time.

The market maker picks a bid and an ask to maximize expected wealth at the end.

Mark-to-market inventory risk while the market is open, and settlement risk on contracts left open at resolution, sit in the same problem.

The authors derive the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and characterize a unique optimal bid and a unique optimal ask.

Shifting the equation into latent-belief space and using a fixed-point argument, they prove a classical solution exists and is unique, and they verify that the quoting rule from it is optimal.

Their comparison has no dollar figures in the abstract.

A myopic quoter maximizes only the instantaneous expected mark-to-market profit, the edge on the next trade marked at the new probability.

The optimal rule also has to care how inventory behaves if the latent belief keeps moving, and how that inventory pays if the event resolves either way.

Inventory shifts the quote because a maker already long the yes side is less willing to buy more of it and more willing to sell it.

Market beliefs shift the quote because the latent probability is the thing being marked.

Time to resolution shifts the quote because settlement risk dominates when the binary payoff is close, and mark-to-market risk has longer to compound when resolution is far.

Risk aversion changes how hard those penalties bite, so a more risk-averse maker posts a wider quote, or a sharper inventory skew, for the same book.

Against that myopic benchmark, the paper finds the optimal strategy substantially improves downside protection while preserving most expected profit.

That is about how a quote is chosen once a liquidity setting exists, not a claim that one side of a live contract is cheap.

The numerical analysis names dependence on inventory, beliefs, time to resolution, and risk aversion, and it does not report a live-exchange test or a fee schedule.

A second arXiv paper, Adaptive Liquidity in Prediction Markets via Online Learning, asks how much liquidity the mechanism itself supplies.

Markets need liquidity to turn trades into informative prices, and ordinary cost-function designs fix that liquidity before trading starts.

The freeze locks a tradeoff between price responsiveness and worst-case loss even though order flow is not stationary.

A thick book means one trade barely moves the probability, so the price looks calm for a mechanical reason.

A thin setting means one trade swings it, and the mechanism’s worst-case loss is the other side of that same design choice.

Their construction mixes a family of cost-function markets with weights that can be learned, and the mix is still one market.

The abstract says it preserves no-arbitrage, a bounded worst-case loss, expressiveness, and a positive upside.

Each round, a hybrid structural risk signal scores price impact against inventory risk.

Standard online-learning algorithms then get switching-regret guarantees against the best sequence of liquidity regimes in hindsight, not merely the single best fixed regime.

Simulations show the weights shifting when order flow changes and when inventory changes.

Neither paper names a venue reward schedule or a dollar subsidy paid to quoters.

What they do establish is why a quote has to be paid for, in the spread or in some other compensation.

With nobody posting a bid and an ask, a trade has nowhere to land and the probability does not update.

Liquidity set too high dulls that update. Liquidity set too low lets one order rewrite the probability and raises worst-case loss.

A tight spread is a quote someone was willing, or compensated, to post, not a second independent vote that the probability is right.

Volume that is a maker trading against incoming orders is flow through a quote, not a pile of separate forecasts.

A price near resolution is not the same object as a price months out, because one paper puts time inside the quoting decision and the other lets the liquidity regime switch as conditions change.

Neither result tells you which side of a contract to take.
