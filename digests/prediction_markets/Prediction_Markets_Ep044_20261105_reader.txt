A futures price is a deal to exchange a commodity on a date that has not arrived, and the research treats that price as a forecast of the later spot. You wait out the horizon and ask whether today’s futures price was a biased guess at the spot that actually printed. The thing being scored is a later price, or the direction of that price, not a yes-or-no event with a fixed payoff.

A two thousand nine neural-network study of crude oil runs that test in days. The authors started with lagged, cleaned-up spot prices and found a dynamic model of thirteen lags the best short-term benchmark for direction. Then they added cleaned-up futures for one, two, three and four months to maturity, first one maturity at a time, then all of them.

The network got the direction right seventy-eight percent of the time one day out, sixty-six percent two days out, and fifty-three percent three days out. Every run that included futures found information in those prices that the lagged spot alone did not have.

An IMF working paper in two thousand eleven looked at ten commodity futures, with horizons out to two years. Inside the sample, tests rejected efficient markets. Outside the sample, the futures forecast was hard to beat.

Skill did not depend on whether the curve sloped up or down, which cuts against models that treat the slope as a signal, and it did not depend on whether prices were rising or falling.

A two thousand thirteen paper in the Journal of Futures Markets split that record by group. Precious and base metals failed most unbiasedness tests and did a poor job predicting later price changes. Energy and agricultural futures did much better.

Liquidity did not explain the gap, on the evidence the authors had, and they documented a broad drop in the predictive content of commodity futures since the early two thousands.

A two thousand nineteen paper in Management Science draws a line between matching the futures curve and forecasting the spot. Pricing models can fit the term structure and how it moves, and still fail to produce accurate true distributions of spot prices. The authors say a reasonable expected spot curve needs analysts’ forecasts, alone or alongside futures.

Using both, rather than forecasts alone, barely moves the short and medium expected spot curve, but the long-term estimates differ by a lot. Adding forecasts to futures, rather than using futures alone, likewise leaves the near and middle futures curve mostly in place and shifts the long-term futures estimates.

A two thousand fifteen study fit the crude-oil curve with a dynamic Nelson-Siegel model and forecast it with a focused time-delay neural network across twenty-four years of prices, recessions and crises included. Calls one, three, six and twelve months ahead beat the benchmark models by a significant margin, with the lowest errors at every maturity.

A two thousand twenty-four paper trained a convolutional network on aerial photos of winter hard red wheat and on cloud cover, standing in for yield, to call the wheat futures price twenty days ahead. Distinctive code and distinctive pictures can show positive alpha in a short window, the authors report, and that edge can fade fast once the images are easy to get or the trade gets crowded.

So read a futures price as a forecast of a later spot at a named horizon, and ask whether that record held up out of sample. Do not read it as a probability on a contract that pays a fixed sum if a stated event happens and nothing if it does not. Storage, interest and the shape of the curve can leave the futures price far from today’s spot without turning it into a number between zero and one.
