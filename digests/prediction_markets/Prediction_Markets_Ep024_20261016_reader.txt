Fees, the spread, and the cost of finding the other side are how a venue gets paid, and that is how it works on a yes-or-no book. Choice lets a user post a number from zero to one hundred.

Sixty means that user is putting the chance at sixty percent. The trade prints only when someone else takes the opposite side at that number, and unmatched orders can still be changed or pulled.

Winners settle at one hundred. Losers settle at zero, so a buyer at sixty has forty points of gross room if that side wins and loses the whole sixty if it does not.

That arithmetic is the contract. It does not crown sixty as the right chance, and it is not a free coin, because nothing clears without a counterparty.

A two thousand seven paper in The Journal of Finance looked at every U.S. corporate bond that traded more than nine times from January two thousand three through January two thousand five. Average trading costs fell as size rose.

Higher-rated bonds, newer bonds, and bonds near maturity were cheaper to trade. Costs were lower where prices were already public, and they fell again once TRACE started publishing them.

The path in that paper is simple. Limit the sample to bonds with more than nine trades, measure cost against size, compare public prices with hidden ones, then watch the same bonds after TRACE turns on. Public traders, the paper finds, gain from that transparency.

A two thousand twenty paper in the same journal found the index credit-default-swap market still had two tiers after Dodd-Frank, even with a push toward all-to-all trading. Client trades cost more than dealer-to-dealer trades.

The paper says the gap comes from a larger, mostly permanent price impact, not from a separate story about a posted fee. Most interdealer flow was there for liquidity and used cheaper, slower protocols.

Client trades were not anonymous. They almost always beat the executable interdealer quote of the moment, and dealers appeared to charge according to how hard they thought the trade would hit the price.

The paper suggests that shape follows from client orders being rarer, larger, and differently informed.
