Here is how it works when the question is whether a probability was right. You score it across many events, not one call.

If a method says sixty percent, calibration means that class of events arrives about sixty percent of the time. Sharpness is different. It is how much probability sits near zero or near one, instead of near fifty percent.

A sharp forecast can still be poorly calibrated. A calibrated one can stay vague. One surprise does not make the forecast wrong, because the score comes after the set of events has resolved.

The papers in hand are weather studies, not market studies. Monthly Weather Review, twenty thirteen, describes an improved version of ensemble kernel density model output statistics, EKDMOS.

Its model-output-statistics equations are multiple linear regressions that relate observations to predictors built from the ensemble mean. Its spread-skill link is a one-term linear regression that predicts how accurate that mean should be, given the spread.

The method runs those equations on each member, fits a probability density with kernels, then rescales the density’s spread to what the skill relationship predicts.

On two-meter temperature forecasts from the North American Ensemble Forecast System, October first, two thousand seven, through March thirty-first, two thousand ten, the improved forecasts were more reliable than an older spread adjustment and than rank-sorting bias-corrected members.

They also had a better match between spread and error, and the spread varied more from day to day.

Weather and Forecasting, twenty ten, compared bias correction with spatial disaggregation against an analog method, using daily precipitation from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts ensemble.

The test covered a ten-day forecast window over the Ohio River basin, two thousand two through two thousand six, checked against Tropical Rainfall Monitoring Mission observations.

Neither method beat a simple spatial-interpolation benchmark on skill. Both improved reliability, and the analog improved it more.

Bias correction improved bias at every forecast amount, though less at the large amounts, and the downscaled patterns looked unrealistic. The analog cut bias across a wider range of amounts and drew more realistic patterns.

On a market, reliability is whether the implied probability matches later frequency, and sharpness is how far the price sits from fifty percent. A repair can lift reliability and still lose to a plain benchmark on skill.

One contract resolving the other way does not, by itself, settle calibration. That includes a thin market, and it includes play money.
