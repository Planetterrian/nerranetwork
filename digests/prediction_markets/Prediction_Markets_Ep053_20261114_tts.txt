Anchoring is the pull of a first number on the estimate that comes after it, and that is how it works when the revision away from the starting figure stops short.

Revista Finanzas y Política Económica describes six hundred seventy students in Concepción, Cali and Lima who saw the S and P MILA Pacific Alliance Select index drawn as a year, a month, a week, and the last close, then forecast the index. Least squares and a data panel showed that anchoring and adjustment influenced those forecasts.

The same judgments led the authors to infer optimism bias, a lean of the forecast toward an optimistic or pessimistic position rather than a neutral reading of the charts. The last close is the number nearest the moment of the judgment, so a forecast that sticks to it is not a fresh calculation.

Financial Management looked at survey forecasts of macroeconomic statistics and found they systematically underpredict. Past announcements predict the errors, which is what an anchor in the history looks like. The stock market does not see through it.

Those authors find statistically significant stock-price effects from the anticipated slice of macro announcements, the slice a fully adjusting reader would already have priced, and they say investors overweight historical information and do not adjust enough when new information arrives.

An arXiv paper on interval elicitation in an online prediction market splits that story. Forecasters who could not see the market until they had written their own estimates moved closer once the market forecast appeared. Their first edits looked very much like the first edits of people who had seen the market the whole time, and accuracy was similar in both groups.

The authors' conclusion is that the more elaborate elicitation might not improve forecasts, and that anchoring on the state of the market does not constitute an error in judgment. Stepping toward the visible price can be an update.

A last close, a prior macro print, and a contract price are different first numbers. The student charts and the macro surveys say the first one can leave the next estimate short. The market experiment says moving toward the price on the screen need not mean the reader stopped thinking. Noticing which number you saw before you had an estimate of your own is the usable part.
