Here is how it works when a crowd is wiser than its members, and when it is not.

A crowd forecast is an average. Each person brings a piece of information, and the average cancels some individual error only if those pieces are not the same piece repeated.

Da, Diebold, Göbel, Gouveia and Li, in a twenty twenty-five arXiv paper, treat macroeconomic survey averages as portfolios of forecasts.

They set the U.S. Survey of Professional Forecasters next to a model where forecast errors are equicorrelated, and they plot accuracy against how many forecasters sit in the average.

That equicorrelation model fits growth and inflation almost perfectly.

Adding forecasters helps inflation more than growth.

Those gains are largely used up once five to ten representative forecasters are already in the average.

A twenty twenty Management Science paper on Estimize found a trap in shared information.

The more public information a user viewed before an earnings forecast, the less weight that user put on private information.

The individual forecast got better. The consensus got worse, because useful private information never entered the average.

Experiments that hid information from randomly selected users confirmed the point: more independent forecasts made a more accurate consensus.

A market price is that kind of average with money on it.

It holds up when traders are not all staring at the same public number.
