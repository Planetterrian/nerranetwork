One piece is an interim final rule. It takes casino games and ordinary sportsbook wagers out of the definition of a swap.

The other piece is a proposal, not a final rule. It would place contracts on sports, politics, culture, and weather inside that swap definition, under the Commodity Exchange Act.

The exclusion does not start today. It starts when the Federal Register publishes it. CasinoBeats reports a thirty-day public comment period on the interim final rule.

Chair Mike Selig described event contracts as a way to hedge, to speculate, and to give the public information about how future events turn out. On the exclusion, he said casino-style gambling products are not derivatives.

The proposal treats a possible financial, economic, or commercial consequence as enough to meet the statute. Temperature shifts that can touch agriculture, energy use, or outdoor activity can qualify even if nobody has lost money yet.

Federal appeals courts do not agree on jurisdiction for sports contracts. The NFL wants state oversight. Robinhood wants exclusive federal authority.

Here is how it works when the market sits inside a company. A corporate prediction market is one the firm runs among employees, so their trades can put a price on an internal question.

A twenty seventeen paper in MIS Quarterly models two inputs to that price. One is how precise the public information is. The other is whether the people trading interact with each other.

Sharper public information does not always make the price more accurate. The authors call the failure the hidden-profiles effect.

Aggregation, in their model, lays a larger-than-efficient weight on information that is already public. Evidence only some participants hold gets too little weight. That undisclosed evidence is the hidden profile.

Add information sharing and the picture changes. A socially embedded market may offset the overweighting and perform better.

The model also finds conditions where making public information more precise is harmful with no network among traders, and harmful again when a network is present. Either design can be hurt.

What they draw for managers is that public information can damage the market, and that employee social networks are part of performance, not a distraction from it.

Read that way, a confident price can still be tilted toward the figure everyone already had. Usefulness depends on whether private information can move the price, and on whether the people who hold it share what they know.

The paper is an analytical model. It names a failure mode. It does not score any particular company.
