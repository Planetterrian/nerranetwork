### How It Works: Who Wins and Who Loses: What Studies Find on Trader Returns
A price can line up with what traders already believed, and the people who held the belief that later paid can still be the ones who made the money. That is the mechanism in a Journal of Prediction Markets study of private information, overconfidence, and trader returns. The setting was four real-money prediction markets whose contracts paid on a movie's box office. Before any market opened, every trader submitted a detailed forecast of that movie's performance. The private information was self-generated. No one randomly handed a group a perfect tip, which is how many lab experiments create "insiders." Accuracy could be scored only after the box office result existed.

The authors treated overconfidence as the thing to look for in behavior, not as a slogan. Volume and the timing of trading were consistent with overconfidence: people who had done the analytical work traded as if that work were more decisive than it was. In three of the four markets, contract prices were consistent with the prior-information equilibrium. The price looked like the information traders had walked in with. In those same three markets, traders whose forecast was associated with the winning contract had significantly higher returns than traders whose forecasts pointed at another contract. In the fourth market, there were no significant differences in returns across those groups.

Those are the only splits the paper's account gives, so the worked example stops there. Four markets. Three prices sat where the prior information said they should, and in those three the traders tied to the winning contract earned significantly more. One market produced no significant return gap at all. A listener looking for a single win rate, a dollar profit, or a share of gains captured by a small group will not find those figures here. The authors' conclusion is narrow, and it is not a strategy: when traders are overconfident and trade accordingly, there can be value to being better informed, if the information is accurate.

A second paper, in the Journal of Futures Markets, splits profit rather than forecasts. It examines local traders on the floor of the Sydney Futures Exchange and decomposes their income into liquidity profits and position-taking profits. Locals made significant position-taking profits. The ability to earn those position-taking profits was positively related to order-flow information, and negatively related to exogenous information, to the locals' own liquidity profits, and to the length of a local's inventory cycle. The authors characterize those locals as active informed traders, not as a desk that only collected the spread for standing in the market.

That "informed" reading of order flow is disputed in a neighboring market. Another Journal of Futures Markets paper uses option order imbalance on Shanghai Stock Exchange 50 ETF options as a proxy for market makers' inventory pressure. Call order imbalances were contemporaneously positively associated with underlying returns, put order imbalances negatively associated, and both were followed by rapid reversals. The authors tie the reversal to temporary price pressure from delta-hedging. Two empirical scenarios, they report, reinforce that the imbalance reflected inventory risk rather than informed trading. Flow that looks like someone who knows the future can be a hedge being laid off.

A listener who treats a price as a forecast can still ask which of these mechanisms made it. In the movie markets, the price often matched beliefs traders already held, and the traders whose belief matched the outcome made more, except in the one market where that gap did not appear. On a futures floor, a meaningful slice of local profit was position-taking tied to order flow, and it shrank when outside news arrived or when inventory sat longer. In the options study, the same-looking imbalance was a market maker's hedge, and it reversed. None of that names a side to take. It is a reason a contract price can say something about an event and still say very little about whether any particular trader is the one who gets paid.
