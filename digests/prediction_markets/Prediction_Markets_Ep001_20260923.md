### What This Show Covers
A prediction market lets people trade on whether something will happen. A contract is written around a question and a resolution rule, and the price traders will pay can be read as the crowd’s probability that the answer is yes. That reading is the idea under examination here: when a price works as a forecast, and when the design of the market, the rules, or the people who can move the outcome make it a poor one.

Each episode has the same shape. The regulators, courts and lawmakers deciding what these markets may be come first — the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, state attorneys general and gaming authorities, and regulators outside the United States — and a legal story names the jurisdiction it is actually about. Then the venues, the products and the people building or contesting them. A short board lists a handful of the day’s most traded questions, each with its venue, its volume and the time the price was read, so a probability is never a free-floating number. One explainer walks through how a piece of the machinery works.

This show will not tell anyone what to trade. It reports and explains. It does not recommend buying, selling or holding a contract, and it does not offer a pick, a tip or a view on which side is mispriced. The law is not one rule. It differs by country, province and state, and it is changing; when a story is about the law, the show says whose rules, and it states a specific legal fact only when a dated source supports it.

### Top Story
Staff in the Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s Division of Market Oversight issued an advisory on September 22, 2026, covering contracts that settle on specific words or phrases, event attendance, public appearances and interactions between individuals. The guidance, aimed at designated contract markets, says these mention markets can be presumed readily susceptible to manipulation because settlement can depend on conduct controlled by one named person or a small group, unlike contracts on election results, economic releases or regulated sports. It does not ban the contracts and, unlike a Commission rule, creates no new legal obligations; exchanges remain under the Commodity Exchange Act’s Core Principle 3, which requires them to list only derivatives that are not readily susceptible to manipulation, and staff want a heightened showing, with surveillance built around the people who control the outcome. On August 28 the agency ordered former White House teleprompter operator Gabriel Perez to pay $172,539.02 — $107,539.02 in disgorgement and a $65,000 civil penalty — and imposed a three-year trading ban, after finding he used advance access to presidential speeches to trade mention contracts on Kalshi between December 2025 and February 2026. A month before that order, the agency settled with former Representative George Santos over trading in a Kalshi contract on whether he would attend the State of the Union; he paid $35,000 and did not admit wrongdoing. Kalshi said its internal surveillance flagged the Perez trades and that it reported them to federal authorities. ### How It Works: Why a Price Can Be Read as a Probability
A contract that pays a fixed sum if an event happens, and nothing if it does not, trades at a price between those two payoffs. Buyers are taking the view that the event is more likely than that price; sellers, that it is less likely. Where both sides can trade, the price is where that disagreement has cleared for the moment, which is why it can be read as a probability — the terms on which the market last found a counterparty, not a verdict issued by the venue.

A paper in Management Science compared that reading with another way of pooling forecasts. More than 2,400 participants forecast 261 events over two seasons of a geopolitical tournament. They were randomly assigned to prediction markets — continuous double auctions, ranked on earnings — or to prediction polls, submitting probabilities alone or in teams and ranked on Brier scores. In both seasons, market prices were more accurate than the simple mean of the poll forecasts. Team polls outperformed the markets once forecasts were aggregated using temporal decay, weights based on past performance, and recalibration. The polls’ biggest advantage was at the beginning of long-duration questions.

A paper on cost-function market makers notes that trading in a duality-based design collectively implements Follow-The-Regularized-Leader. Modifying that pricing rule, the authors propose a Smooth Quadratic Prediction Market so that trading implements general steepest gradient descent, with a better worst-case monetary loss for AD securities and with no-arbitrage and an instantaneous price preserved. They also look at bounded budgets and buy-only securities, cases where a clean probability reading leaves something out.

The same research flags a limit: independent judgments, properly aggregated, beat market prices at the start of long questions. This show will not say which contract to buy or sell. Every probability here carries its venue, its volume and the time it was read.
