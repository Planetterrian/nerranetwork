A combinatorial book, and how it works, prices a question inside another question instead of leaving two contracts to drift apart.

Writing down every combination of a few base events gets impossible fast, and updating prices under a logarithmic market scoring rule is a hard computation, the kind computer scientists label sharp-P hard.

Scoring rules pull a probability from one person. A betting market pulls a consensus from a group. A market scoring rule does both, and a group costs no more to ask than an individual, according to the Journal of Prediction Markets.

Only the logarithmic versions, that journal finds, leave the probability of a conditioned event unchanged when someone bets on one event given another.

Those log versions also keep other conditional probabilities, and so they keep conditional independence. Once the rule already prices relative chances for pairs of base events, pricing every combination of those events costs nothing extra.

Correlation makes the information problem worse. A twenty seventeen paper in the American Economic Review looks at privately informed sellers holding assets whose values move together.

A trade in one asset can tell you something about the others. What it tells you is not fixed in advance. It depends on which equilibrium the market is in.

When the correlation is strong enough, that endogeneity produces more than one equilibrium. Those equilibria rank differently on how much trades and on efficiency.

Publishing trades after the fact can raise welfare and volume, or cut both. Which way it goes depends on the correlation, on the equilibrium in play, and on who is in the market.
