# Prediction Markets Daily
> **Two exchanges won NBA preferred status under a new CFTC integrity pact.**

**What You Need to Know:** Two exchanges, Kalshi and Polymarket, won preferred status from the National Basketball Association under a new Commodity Futures Trading Commission integrity memorandum. The Monday deals limit which basketball markets those partners may list and bar league and team personnel from trading them. A separate memorandum with the commission sets an information-sharing channel and does not bind either side to hand anything over.

### Top Story
On Monday, October 26, 2026, the National Basketball Association signed a memorandum of understanding with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and named Kalshi and Polymarket its preferred prediction-market exchanges. The commercial terms give those two exchanges NBA intellectual property, United States marketing rights, and official data from Sportradar, while limiting permissible markets in line with the league’s sportsbook restrictions on first-basket player props and parlays built around statistical unders for players expected to see limited minutes, barring league and team personnel from trading NBA event contracts, and requiring information-sharing on investigations and suspicious trading. DraftKings’ existing relationship was expanded to include prediction markets and nationwide promotion, without that preferred-exchange label, and the WNBA is not covered unless a company signs a separate deal for its rights. The five-page memorandum is not legally binding: neither side must create or share information, and any sharing is at each party’s sole discretion. Chairman Michael Selig said the NBA joins Major League Baseball and the National Hockey League in a step aimed at insider trading and fraud in these markets. The NFL has not joined that path: on October 8 it backed New Jersey’s request that the U.S. Supreme Court review a Kalshi dispute, arguing that contracts on game outcomes are gambling rather than swaps, and the Court has not agreed to hear the case. Kalshi’s contracts based on the S&P 500 and other S&P indices are not sponsored, sold, or endorsed by S&P DJI, and S&P DJI makes no representation on the advisability of trading them. ### How It Works: How Fast a Price Moves: Markets Against the Clock
A prediction-market price is a running yes-or-no answer, and the clock starts the moment public information changes the chance of that answer. A print that jumps in the first minute can look finished. It often is not. A paper posted to arXiv, “When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets,” separates a fast twitch from a full update. The setting combines binary payoffs, public signals the authors can time exactly, and high-frequency prices, so a change in the traded price can be set next to a change in a benchmark probability built only from information that is already public.

Prices in that setting are informative, and they become more accurate as resolution approaches. During the event, prices respond rapidly to public signals and move in the expected direction. Directional responsiveness is not the same as efficient updating. Relative to an out-of-sample benchmark probability model, a one-minute change in the benchmark probability is associated with only about a 0.64-for-one contemporaneous change in the market price. Walk that coefficient as the paper states it, without adding a second result. Take a one-minute move in the benchmark as one unit. About 0.64 of that unit shows up in the price in the same minute. The rest of that one-minute benchmark move is not in the price yet. The missing adjustment predicts future price drift over the following several minutes. That later drift is still there net of subsequent changes in the benchmark probability, so it is not only the market chasing a benchmark that kept moving after the first minute.

The authors then look at why the gap opens. Salient public signals are incorporated relatively quickly in liquid markets. The same signals generate substantially greater underreaction when liquidity is low. Underreaction gaps associated with salient states also predict stronger subsequent drift. The pattern they describe is gradual price discovery shaped by attention and trading frictions together. A market can see the public signal, move the right way, and still leave part of the benchmark’s update for the next several minutes. Liquidity changes how large that leftover is. Salience changes how hard the leftover pulls on later prices.

Nothing in the paper is a halt rule, a trading instruction, or a claim that one side of any live contract is the right side. The useful reading is narrower. A price that moves in the expected direction in the first minute is evidence that traders responded to public information. It is not evidence that the implied probability has absorbed the full benchmark update. In a liquid book, more of a salient signal lands quickly. In a book with low liquidity, the same kind of signal leaves a larger underreaction, and the paper finds that those gaps predict more drift afterward. Market-implied probabilities in this study are often directionally correct, yet the adjustment stays incomplete and depends, in a predictable way, on liquidity and salience. A listener who keeps that split in mind treats the first print as a start, then asks whether the book was liquid enough for that start to be close to the full public update. Source: [arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07811v1)
