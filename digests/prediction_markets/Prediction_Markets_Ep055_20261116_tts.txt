Prices overreact to news when the people trading a lag can only chase it, and they underreact when private information is still spreading. A nineteen ninety-nine paper in the Journal of Finance builds that sequence with two groups of boundedly rational agents, newswatchers and momentum traders.

Each newswatcher sees some private information and fails to pull other newswatchers’ information out of prices. Gradual diffusion leaves the quote lagging in the short run. That lag is what lets momentum traders profit by trend-chasing. If they can run only simple, univariate strategies, the attempt at arbitrage produces overreaction at long horizons.

One market can show both mistakes, in that order, and the same setup throws off several other distinctive implications.

The sized measurement sits in a two thousand six Journal of Finance paper on the disposition effect, the tendency to ride losses and realize gains. Mutual-fund holdings supply a reference purchase price for individual stocks, and the test asks whether that tendency induces underreaction to news and return predictability.

On a news date, some holders are ahead of that reference price and some are behind. Drift after the announcement is most severe when those capital gains and the news have the same sign. How large the drift is depends on the gains or losses holders are sitting on that day, not on the headline alone.

An event-driven strategy based on this effect yields monthly alphas of over two hundred basis points. That figure was measured in stocks and fund holdings. It is not a method for any contract, and it is not a reason to take a side.

A two thousand fourteen paper in Management Science finds an overshoot, and it locates the overshoot in who trades together. Mutual funds herd into stocks with consensus sell-side analyst upgrades and herd out of stocks with consensus downgrades. Recommendation changes pull that herding harder on downgrades, and harder among managers with greater career concerns.

Those managers look incentivized to follow analyst information, and more inclined to herd on negative stock information, given the reputational and litigation risk of holding losers.

From the mid-nineteen nineties, when aggregate mutual-fund equity ownership is significantly higher, stocks traded by career-concerned herds in response to recommendation changes show a significant same-quarter price impact, then a sharp reversal. The reversal is the evidence that the first move went too far. Wei Jiang accepted the paper.

Its suggestion is that recommendation revisions induce that herding, and that this kind of trading has become price destabilizing as mutual funds own more of the market.

A nineteen ninety-two paper in the Journal of Finance is the dispute, and it cuts the analyst channel down. Forecasts underreact to recent earnings, a feature consistent with properties of the naive seasonal random walk that Bernard and Thomas, in nineteen ninety, hypothesize underlie post-earnings-announcement drift.

Those underreactions are at most only about half as large as necessary to explain the size of the drift. Half is not zero, and it is not the whole drift.

Extreme forecasts studied by DeBondt and Thaler in nineteen ninety cannot be viewed as overreactions to earnings, and they are not clearly linked to the stock-price overreactions in DeBondt and Thaler, nineteen eighty-five and nineteen eighty-seven, and in Chopra, Lakonishok, and Ritter.

Analysts’ behavior is at best only a partial explanation for stock-price underreaction to earnings, and may be unrelated to stock-price overreactions. Hearing that analysts lag, and therefore prices lag, is at most half an account of the earnings drift, and possibly no account of the longer overreactions.

Options split the question by the shape of the news. A two thousand one paper in the Journal of Finance looks at how that market reacts to changes in the instantaneous variance of the underlying asset. Investors there underreact to individual daily changes in that variance. They overreact to periods of mostly increasing or mostly decreasing daily changes.

They underreact to a current daily change preceded mostly by changes of the opposite sign, and overreact when the preceding changes mostly share the sign. That third finding can reconcile the first two, and the paper says it fits well-established cognitive biases. A single day’s variance surprise is not the same object as a streak.

Those tests ran on stocks, mutual funds, and options, not on a named prediction-market venue, so they do not certify any contract read today. Set the first print after a piece of news next to what the price does afterward.

Ask whether information is still diffusing across people who cannot read one another’s signals in the quote, whether holders sit on gains or losses that match the sign of the news, whether a herd is following a recommendation into a quarter that later reverses, or whether the latest trades are chasing a streak rather than a single surprise. None of those questions is a pick.

Each one is a way to tell a lag from an overshoot without treating the latest price as the last word.
