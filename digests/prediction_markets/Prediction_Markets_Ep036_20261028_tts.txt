The Iowa Electronic Markets show how it works when the payoff of the contract is the forecast itself.

One design pays only if a side gets more votes than the other. The price on that contract is a chance of finishing ahead.

The other design pays with the share of the popular vote, so its price is a margin, not a winner.

A paper in PS: Political Science and Politics read both, for the twenty twenty-four popular vote, on September twenty-ninth.

The vote-share book was thin, and it put the Democratic margin at nine percentage points.

The winner-takes-all book was thick, and it put the chance the Democrat would get more votes at eighty-five point seven percent.

The authors mixed the two books into one distribution. The margin came down to six or seven points, and the chance of finishing ahead moved up to eighty-seven percent.

They still said a lot of uncertainty was left.

Walk the three numbers in order. Nine points is a priced margin from a thin book, not a win probability. Eighty-five point seven percent is a chance of finishing ahead, not a margin. The blend is a smaller margin and a slightly higher chance. Same race, three readings, because the contract and the trades changed.

The authors read that as rhetoric failing to move control prices, partisan acts lining up with worse prices for the party that started them, and a bipartisan step lining up with better prices for the party that opened it.

That last step is the argument a skeptic can refuse. A price that moves after an event is a coincidence in a market until someone shows the event caused it.
