### Top Story
CoinGape reports that the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission has approved Kalshi, a CFTC-registered designated contract market, to offer S&P 500 stock-index perpetual futures. Kalshi filed in August for a US500PERP contract that tracks the MerQube US Large Cap Index, is cash-settled, and has no expiration date; periodic funding payments, rather than a single settlement date, are meant to hold it near a reference price. The platform already lists 19 crypto perpetuals, including Bitcoin, Ethereum and XRP, has launched gold and silver perpetuals, and launched Uniswap perpetuals last week. The approval puts Kalshi in direct competition with CME Group and Cboe Global Markets. CME had already sued the CFTC over the decision to let Kalshi and Coinbase list crypto perpetuals, claiming Bitcoin perpetual futures are swaps, not futures. Kalshi has also filed to list individual stock perpetuals and filed to offer margin trading on event contracts; those steps are filings, not approvals. ### The Ecosystem
**Prediction markets get a new twist: betting on what's already happened: InvestmentNews**
InvestmentNews reports that Verifact Markets emerged from stealth last week with markets on disputed factual claims about events that have already occurred. Peter Wokwicz, CEO of Positron Capital Management, the family office that backs the company, wrote that people can trade on disputed facts, submit evidence, and seek a profit; Rodrigo Aquino, CEO and co-founder, runs day-to-day operations. Wokwicz wrote that the path from idea to launch took five years and became possible with recent AI advances, some regulatory clarity, and the team. A True or False contract is priced between $0 and $1 and pays $1 or $0. Verifact says a patent-pending system combines evidence from its own technology with trading signals and resolves a market only when confidence is greater than 90 percent; if that bar is never cleared, the market expires and settlement follows the last trusted trading price. Users may submit evidence, which Verifact checks for relevance and verifiability before it enters resolution. Source: [investmentnews.com](https://www.investmentnews.com/alternatives/prediction-markets-get-a-new-twist-betting-on-whats-already-happened/268463)

### How It Works: How an Automated Market Maker Sets a Price
An automated market maker quotes a price even when no second trader is waiting. In a logarithmic market scoring rule, that quote comes from a price function, and the liquidity parameter inside it — often written as b — sets how far one trade moves the price. Raise b and the same trade nudges the quote less. Lower b and the quote jumps farther. The sponsor stands as counterparty, so the parameter is also a bound on how much the sponsor can lose while the market stays open. The abstract of a 2018 paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management calls logarithmic market scoring rules one of the most simple and widely used of these mechanisms, and says market makers have to set b in the price functions because it has significant effects on market performance.

That paper, “Optimizing the liquidity parameter of logarithmic market scoring rules prediction markets,” used artificial prediction markets — simulations built to mimic real ones — to measure forecast standard error and the time for the price to converge to the true value. Forecast standard error fell as the liquidity parameter rose. For any given number of traders, the simulations found a parameter value that kept price adaptability appropriate and produced the fastest price convergence. The authors treat standard error, time to convergence, and cost as the tradeoff the market maker has to set.

A quote that barely moves and a quote that leaps can come from the same price function at different settings of b. In those simulations, a higher liquidity parameter came with a smaller forecast standard error, while the fastest convergence, for a given crowd size, sat at one value that preserved adaptability. Cost belongs in that same choice, because the sponsor is paying to keep the quote up. Someone reading a market price is also reading that setting: how far one trade is allowed to move the number, and who stands on the other side. The paper is not a guide to which side of any contract to take.
