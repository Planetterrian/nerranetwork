### Top Story
In an amicus brief supporting New Jersey regulators against Kalshi, the NFL argues that sports prediction contracts are gambling, not financial swaps that belong under exclusive Commodity Futures Trading Commission oversight, CNBC reports. On the first Sunday of the season, the league says, football accounted for one point eight billion dollars of prediction-market trading, more than half the total volume. The NFL told CNBC it is not opposed to prediction markets, and that "given the current resource constraints of the CFTC, this is a job better left to the states." It wants a minimum age of 21, the floor most states set for sports betting, while Kalshi customers can be 18, and it flags contracts tied to injuries, officiating, and plays a participant could affect, including a kicker missing on purpose or a receiver fumbling on purpose. In a statement to CNBC, the league said, "Neither the CFTC nor the prediction market companies themselves— despite our persistent urging — have banned categories of bets susceptible to manipulation or set a 21 age limit," and it keeps an advertising ban on prediction platforms. Commissioner Roger Goodell, interviewed by CNBC Sport on September 10 in Australia, said, "We don't feel like we have to be the first in this. We feel like we're going to be right, and the best thing to do is be patient." The NHL has partnerships with Kalshi and Polymarket; MLB, Major League Soccer, and UFC have deals with Polymarket; the NFL, NBA, and PGA have not. Federal appeals courts are split on whether states can enforce gambling laws over the CFTC. Kalshi has consistently argued in court that these products are swaps under exclusive federal jurisdiction. ### The Ecosystem
**The 2010 sports-contract warning now comes from Kalshi's lobbyist: Ars Technica:**
In July 2010, during Senate debate on the Dodd-Frank Act, Senator Blanche Lincoln of Arkansas warned that event contracts could be built around the Super Bowl, the Kentucky Derby, and the Masters, and told colleagues, "These types of contracts would not serve any real commercial purpose. Rather, they would be used solely for gambling." She chaired the Senate Agriculture Committee and was a primary author of Title VII, which gave the CFTC authority over swaps and, in her telling, authority to prohibit event contracts used primarily for gambling; Congress left that call to the executive branch rather than banning the contracts itself. Ars Technica reports that the Biden administration discouraged sports-event contracts, while the Trump administration has used that Dodd-Frank authority to allow them and insists states must be kept from enforcing their own gambling laws against prediction markets. Lincoln registered as a lobbyist for Kalshi in 2024. Crypto Briefing reports that Lincoln Policy Group received one hundred eighty thousand dollars from Kalshi that year for lobbying on event-contract regulation; Ars Technica reports the firm she founded has received four hundred eighty thousand dollars from Kalshi since 2024 to lobby Congress and the CFTC for looser event-contract rules. In July 2025 the firm filed comments urging the CFTC to assert exclusive jurisdiction over all prediction markets, sports-event contracts included, citing advertising, merchandise, and hospitality and warning that state-by-state oversight would be a patchwork; she has also supported Kalshi in an amicus brief in the Third Circuit. Crypto Briefing says sports contracts were an estimated 77 percent of the platform's trading volume in early 2025 and reportedly 80 to 90 percent later that year. ### How It Works: Sports Event Contracts, Sportsbooks and Gambling Harm
A sportsbook posts a number and stands on the other side of it. A sports event contract on an exchange does not. Two people agree a price; if the listed event occurs, the contract pays a fixed sum to the buyer, and if it does not, the seller keeps what was paid. The venue does not have to hold a view. The last trade is the price. When the other side is a pool rather than a person, an automated market maker sets that price from a formula and a pile of collateral.

A 2021 paper in the Journal of Finance treats liquid sports-betting contracts as a laboratory for pricing anomalies. Two features do the work: there is no systematic risk of the kind equities carry, and the contract's terminal value is set outside the betting itself. That design gets around the joint-hypothesis problem, so a gap between the traded price and the later result can be read as mispricing rather than as compensation for risk. The author finds strong momentum, a pattern consistent with delayed overreaction and not with underreaction or with rational pricing. The returns are a fraction of returns in financial markets and fail to overcome transaction costs, so arbitrage does not clear the pattern. An observation from that betting record also predicts value and momentum returns in U.S. equities. None of that is a method to copy, and it is not a claim that any live contract on Kalshi or Polymarket is cheap or rich.

A 2023 paper on arXiv looks at the pool version of the same problem. It argues that an automated market maker built like Uniswap is not lined up with fair odds, which leaves liquidity providers holding a risk the formula did not price. The authors describe UBET's UAMM, smart contracts meant to set sports prices inside collateral pools, and they report experiments with positive outcomes. That is a design result, not a published accuracy record.

Read the number on a sports contract as a trade, or as a formula's output, not as a house line and not as a settled chance. The Journal of Finance result says those trades can carry a momentum streak that costs make hard to trade away. A thin book is a weaker version of the same object. The papers measure prices and liquidity. They do not measure gambling harm, which is a separate question from whether the last trade was informative.
