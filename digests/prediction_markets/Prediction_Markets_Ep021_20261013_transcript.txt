From 1868 to 1940, large and often well-organized markets took wagers on who would win the White
House.
Buyers took a claim that paid if their candidate won and sellers took the other side.
The trading level was the forecast because that was where people were willing to risk
money, not because anyone had run a poll.
A 2004 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives studies those markets and sets out
four findings.
They did a remarkable job calling elections before scientific polling existed.
They were fairly efficient even though participants had limited information and even though people
actively tried to manipulate the odds.
They largely disappeared the paper argues because scientific polls rose and because other
gambling became easier to find. The paper also discusses lessons for the present, and the
abstract does not itemize them. That second finding is the caution. Efficiency is claimed
next to manipulation, not instead of it. A push can fail if other traders take the other
