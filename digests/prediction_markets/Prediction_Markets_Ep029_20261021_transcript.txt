Wishful prices are hard to separate from information, and how it works here starts with a reader,
not a trader. A Brennan Center analysis measured what a displayed Calche figure does to someone
who never put money down. It does not track traders' own politics. Yeji Lee's write-up, funded by
the Brennan Center for Justice and released October 20, used a survey of 1,994 self-identified
registered voters fielded September 21. Each person saw five pairs of hypothetical congressional
candidates. Profiles listed race, age, party, and previous occupation. Plus an NBC news poll estimate
and a Calche chance that researchers vary at random. Random assignment is how the design tries to
pull the price away from the biography and the poll. If the Calche figure still changes the pick,
the number is doing work of its own. A candidate shown at 50% on Calche is the baseline.
Move that displayed figure to 67% and respondents were 10 percentage points more likely to name that candidate the winner.
Move it to 84% and the gain was 20 points.
At 33%, respondents were 11 points less likely to pick the candidate.
At 16%, they were 19 points less likely.
Lee's write-up says the market figure moved perceptions about as much as the traditional poll did,
and more than any background trait in the profile.
A warning that asked people to be careful about prediction markets had no effect on how much they used the figure.
Only 19% of respondents said election prediction markets are accurate against 37% who said that of traditional polls.
Just 29% said the markets reflect public opinion against 55% for polls.
37% neither agreed nor disagreed that the markets are accurate, and 29% took no position on whether they represent the public.
People who do not trust the number still moved when it was shown.
48% said prediction markets should be regulated more strongly.
23% said some types should be banned.
16% said all of them should be banned, and 14% said they should not be regulated at all.
The analysis cited polling that seven in 10 Americans disapprove of betting on elections,
