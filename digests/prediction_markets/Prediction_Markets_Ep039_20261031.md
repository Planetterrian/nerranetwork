### How It Works: Following the Crowd: Herding and Information Cascades
An information cascade is a sequence in which a later decision-maker copies earlier actions and sets aside what they themselves know, because the earlier actions already look like evidence. In a market, that copycat logic can push a price away from the private information traders are sitting on. A flexible price is supposed to interrupt the cascade: once the price can move, the information in earlier trades is already in the number, so copying the last trade adds nothing new.

A 2005 internet experiment in the American Economic Review tested that idea, the theory of informational cascades in financial markets set out by Christopher Avery and Peter Zemsky in 1998. More than 6,400 subjects took part, including a subsample of 267 consultants from an international consulting firm. Walk the design the way the paper reports it. Subjects make decisions in a market whose price is free to move, rather than in a setting where only the previous person's action is visible. The flexible price prevented herding: people did not simply pile onto the last trade and ignore their own signal. Contrarian behavior still distorted prices. Even after 20 decisions, convergence to the fundamental value was rare. The consultants did not behave in a significantly different way from the other subjects. So a price that can move blocks one failure mode, blind copying, and still leaves another: trades against the crowd that pull the price off the value the signals would support, with convergence still uncommon after a long string of decisions.

A 2007 paper in the Journal of Finance put the same kind of question to people who do this for a living. Market professionals from the Chicago Board of Trade were observed in a controlled setting and compared with a student control group. Analysis of over 1,500 individual decisions found that the professionals were better able to discern the quality of public signals, and that their decisions were not affected by the domain of earnings. The student group did not show that same pattern. The authors treat the gap as a point about market efficiency: who is in the market changes how public signals get used.

A 2012 paper in Management Science looked for herding where the "price" is a funding tally, on Prosper.com, then the largest microloan market in the United States. Well-funded borrower listings tended to attract more funding after the researchers controlled for unobserved listing heterogeneity and payoff externalities. The lenders were not only mimicking. They inferred creditworthiness from peer lending decisions and used publicly observable borrower characteristics to moderate those inferences — active observational learning, which the paper calls rational herding, rather than passive copying, which it calls irrational herding. Obvious defects amplified the herd: poor credit grades increased a listing's herding momentum, because lenders inferred that the borrower must be better than the grade or the crowd would not have funded it. Favorable traits did the opposite. Friend endorsements weakened the herding effect, because lenders could attribute the crowd to a fact they could already see. Follow-up analysis found that rational herding beat irrational herding in predicting loan performance.

A 2018 paper in the Annals of Financial Economics tested herding in crypto-currency markets with the CSAD method of Chang et al. (2000). It used daily returns of six major crypto-currencies and the market index CCI30 from 07-08-2015 to 18-01-2018, and it checked up markets against down markets and high volatility against low volatility. Herding showed up in both up and down markets, which the paper reads as over-enthusiasm and over-reaction. Market volatility did not have a significant impact. Herding depended on market activity rather than on volatility.

A 2013 review in the Review of Behavioral Finance is the dispute on the shelf. More than two decades of work have produced real insight into investor herding, and the evidence is still inconclusive in places. The review says relatively little is known for markets other than equity markets, and that empirical methods still need to address current limitations. A crypto result, a loan-listing result and a trading-floor result do not automatically transfer to an event-contract book.

Read a fast price with that split in mind. A crowd all hitting the same side can be people ignoring what they know, or a price that has already absorbed what earlier traders knew, so the next copy is empty. It can also be contrarians shoving the number away from the signals, the failure the 2005 experiment still found after 20 decisions. And a visible fact can explain the herd without any hidden insight, the way friend endorsements did on Prosper: once the obvious merit is public, the pile-on says less. None of that says which side of any contract is cheap. It says a price is a record of decisions, and those decisions are not all information.
