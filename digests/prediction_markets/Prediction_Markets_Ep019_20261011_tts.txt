A decision market is the smaller piece. It asks people who want something for themselves to reveal what they know, then builds a forecast that depends on the action.

A twenty-twenty-three paper on arXiv says the usual version buys honest answers with randomness. Now and then the person in charge must take the step the forecast has already marked as worse.

The authors offer three ways to keep the choice fixed. If she already has a signal of her own, she scores the others against it and decides from the pooled forecasts plus that signal.

If she has no signal, she can hand one person a cut of the profit and borrow that person's signal, or she can score two people against each other and use what they report.

They tried the first version inside a learning system with many agents. Those agents reached a level close to a Bayesian model that could see every signal at once.

So a conditional price is not a plain call on what happens next. It is a call on what happens if someone chooses, and it is only as usable as the score, the stand-in, and the indicator the group agreed to chase.
