### Top Story
CryptoStruct and OG.com announced a partnership on October 13, 2026 that lets CryptoStruct’s algorithmic-trading clients trade OG.com event contracts the companies describe as regulated by the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Orders go to OG.com’s central limit order book through CryptoStruct’s low-latency, normalized APIs and execution framework, and a firm can take that API connectivity without CryptoStruct’s full trading platform. Crypto.com originally established OG.com, which became an independent platform after a spin-off announced in September 2026. The venue trades through North American Derivatives Exchange, Inc., registered with the CFTC as both a designated contract market and a derivatives clearing organization, while another entity, OG Broker, is registered as a futures commission merchant. OG.com lists contracts across sports, financials, economics and culture, and offers access directly or through futures commission merchants and introducing brokers. Steve Humenik, OG.com’s chief legal officer, called the partnership “an important milestone in broadening access to regulated prediction markets for professional and quantitative traders,” and Iain Clarke, CryptoStruct’s head of sales, said integrating with OG’s infrastructure “offers our clients an exciting new asset class to trade.” Source: [coinlaw.io](https://coinlaw.io/cryptostruct-og-com-event-contracts-algo-traders/)

### How It Works: A Short History of Election Betting
Between 1868 and 1940, large and often well-organized markets took bets on U.S. presidential elections. A person who thought a candidate would win could buy a claim that paid if that candidate won, and a person who disagreed could sell it. The price of that claim was the forecast. It was not a poll. It was the level at which people in the market were willing to risk money.

A 2004 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives studies those markets and sets out four findings. The market did a remarkable job forecasting elections in an era before scientific polling. It was fairly efficient, despite the limited information of participants and active attempts to manipulate the odds. The markets disappeared largely because scientific polls rose and because other forms of gambling became more available. The paper also discusses lessons that history holds for the present. The abstract does not itemize those lessons.

The limit is already in the second finding. The paper calls the market fairly efficient even while recording attempts to manipulate the odds. A price can be leaned on and still land near the result if other traders take the other side. It can also miss when the people trading share a blind spot. The abstract does not measure how hard a price had to be pushed before it broke. For a reader now, the useful distinction is simple. A market price is a forecast only in the sense that people risked money at that level, under rules for who gets paid. Those presidential markets show that such a price can track an election before scientific polls exist, and that the markets themselves can disappear once polls and other gambling are easier to find. They do not turn any single price today into a verdict. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1257/0895330041371277)
