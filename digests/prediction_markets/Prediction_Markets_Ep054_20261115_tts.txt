A published number can change the outcome it is scoring, and that is how it works in the research on performative prediction. A twenty twenty-six survey traces the idea to Perdomo and coauthors in twenty twenty: deploy a model, and the environment shifts, so the distribution you expected and the one you then meet no longer match. They call that shift a distribution map.

Stability means the model fits the world its own use created. Optimality means it actually minimizes risk after the shift. How much you know about the map decides whether you can plan for the reaction or chase one that never comes. In the years since twenty twenty, that literature added new solution concepts, new theory, and links to other fields.

The nearest experiment here is a twenty fourteen paper in Research and Politics, Are public opinion polls self-fulfilling prophecies? People conform to a majority, and polls are how they learn what that majority is.

The authors say published probabilities from prediction-market contract prices, and aggregated polling summaries, might work the same way, so a majority for a candidate or a policy could cascade. They did not trade a contract.

They randomly assigned a diverse national sample to hear different levels of support for three public policies, holding the policy fixed and changing only the reported support. They found that poll information changes individual attitudes, and that the size of the effect depends on the issue. If the groups diverge, the published figure was an input, not only a photograph.

They treat a market price as a channel that might do the same job. What they measured is poll information and attitudes, and the shift is issue-specific.

A twenty twenty-three paper in Ethical Theory and Moral Practice defines a self-fulfilling prophecy as a prediction that brings about its own truth, and it names four elements that make the case. Typical examples, the authors argue, start from a mistake about how the prediction relates to its object. Because the forecast comes true, humans and machines get no error signal.

Truth hides the mistake, accountability for the outcome fails, and the missed corrections feed a loop. They say automation can make that worse.

A twenty twenty-four paper says a misspecified distribution map can give you a poor stand-in for the true performative optimum. Its hedge is a distributionally robust performative optimum, usable when the map you wrote down is not the map that actually runs, and it says that problem can be rewritten as an augmented performative-prediction task.

The authors claim guarantees that this robust point approximates the true optimum when the nominal map and the real one differ, and experiments that look better than the ordinary approach when the misspecification is micro or macro. Chase the ordinary optimum on a wrong reaction, and you are not optimizing the outcome you thought you were.

A separate twenty twenty-three paper moves the same problem under inequality constraints. Earlier work, it notes, stayed unconstrained, even though transportation, finance, public policy, and recommendation systems often face limits. It wants optimal solutions, not only stable points, and it proposes an adaptive primal-dual algorithm for location families.

The bound it reports is regret and constraint violations on the order of the square root of the time horizon, using the square root of T plus two T samples. For a listener, the narrower point is enough: a published probability can be an input to the next decision, and a rule the forecast did not choose can box that decision in.
