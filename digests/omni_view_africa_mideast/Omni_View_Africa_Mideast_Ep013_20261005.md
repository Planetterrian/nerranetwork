### Lead
**Ethiopian rebel forces withdraw from Tigray regional capital: BBC Africa**
The Tigray People's Liberation Front says it has temporarily withdrawn from Mekelle as fighting with Ethiopia's national army moves toward the city, citing concern about risks to civilians. Two residents told the BBC that pro-government forces now control the city and that TPLF fighters have left it; one also said mobile phone service appears to have resumed in parts of Mekelle. Fighting in recent weeks has involved the TPLF and allied groups against government forces in Tigray, Afar and Amhara. The US Department of State called for restraint and dialogue ahead of a Sunday meeting in Egypt. The presidents of Egypt, Eritrea, Somalia and Sudan met in El-Alamein and called for respect for sovereignty, unity and territorial integrity, and for disputes to be settled by peaceful means. Ethiopia accuses Egypt, Eritrea and Sudan of supporting the Tigray rebels, which they deny. Last month the TPLF joined six other armed movements, including Amhara's Fano militia, in an alliance whose stated aim is to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government; the African Union's mediator estimated some 600,000 people died in the civil war that the Pretoria Agreement ended in 2022, and both sides have accused each other of violating that deal over the last six months. **Strike on WFP Aid Trucks in Sudan Kills One: ASHARQ AL-AWSAT English**
The World Food Program said one driver was killed on Saturday when an airstrike hit two contracted trucks carrying WFP food between Dilling and Kadugli in South Kordofan, vehicles the agency said were clearly marked as humanitarian. The WFP did not say who carried out the strike, condemned it, and called for a prompt investigation; on Friday a WFP truck had struck an explosive device in Dilling after a delivery. Famine was confirmed last year in Kadugli and in El-Fasher. UN figures say drones used by the army and the Rapid Support Forces killed more than 1,100 people in the first half of this year; aid workers estimate more than 200,000 people have been killed since the war began in April 2023, with more than 11 million displaced. **Joint Army, DSS, Amotekun operation kills ISWAP leader in Ondo: The Guardian Nigeria News**
Security sources said a combined team of Nigerian Army troops, Department of State Services personnel and Amotekun killed Islamic State West Africa Province leader Abdussamad Ohida, also known as Anwar, with several other members in a weekend assault on a hideout in Owo forest, Ondo State. The sources said Ohida, who they said took over ISWAP activity in parts of North-Central and southern Nigeria after the DSS arrested Abu Ikrimah, also known as Abu Uwais, in 2023, was planning attacks on civilians during the coming Yuletide and a disruption of the 2027 general elections. One source said interrogation of Abu Uwais produced that intelligence, and that partners from the United Kingdom supported the operation. The same source attributed to Ohida earlier attacks including the 31 December 2025 raid on Ipele Divisional Police Station in Owo, in which the attackers were said to have taken eight AK-47 rifles before destroying the station with explosives. ### Both Sides: Whether Britain should refuel Saudi jets in Yemen
Britain has decided, for now, to put Royal Air Force tankers behind Saudi fighter jets while fighting with the Houthis is at its worst since the 2022 truce. Prime Minister Andy Burnham approved the refuelling. The question is whether that help stays on the defensive side of the war or ties London to a wider air campaign.

The British government's case is that the support is time-limited and defensive. Its best reason, as Middle East Eye reports the government's position, is that a bounded refuelling role can be separated from offensive bombing: help Saudi jets stay in the air for defence, and stop when the limit runs out. On that account, the approval answers a present threat without reopening the full Saudi-led air campaign Britain has backed before.

Campaign groups and academics reject that split. In an open letter they warn the approval could make the United Kingdom complicit in war crimes. Their best reason is that fuel for Saudi fighters cannot be walled off from how those jets are used once they are airborne, and that Britain's long record of backing the Saudi-led air campaign makes a "defensive" label too thin to carry the legal risk. They are arguing about complicity, not about whether the fighting is real.

Both sides are working from the same reported facts: Burnham has approved the refuelling; the government calls it time-limited and defensive; fighting is the worst since the 2022 truce; and nearly 130,000 Yemenis have been displaced in recent weeks.
