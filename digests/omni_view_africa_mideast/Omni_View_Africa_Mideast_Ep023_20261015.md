### Lead
**Fire, sinking and reported scramble for cargo: what we know about ship lost in Strait of Hormuz: The National**
A Singapore-registered container ship, the GFS Pride, caught fire and sank in the Strait of Hormuz after being struck by an unidentified projectile. Singapore’s maritime authority said all 21 crew were evacuated safely and that no injuries were reported. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported the attack on Tuesday; the sinking was confirmed on Wednesday. A sister ship, the GFS Giselle, was damaged in the same wave of attacks but reached anchorage. Singapore condemned the strikes as a breach of international law. The GFS Pride had capacity for 2,824 standard containers; it was not clear how many were aboard, and no manifest was published. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said the ship had more than 2,500 containers, including alleged contraband, and warned local vessels and fishermen to avoid the floating boxes. Pakistan’s maritime authority warned mariners of floating containers. Photos and videos on social media appeared to show a listing ship, thick smoke and people in small boats taking cargo, including posts describing sellers offering phones and perfume; The National said the footage is unverified and could not be tied to the GFS Pride. Quoting two senior Iranian officials, Reuters reported on Wednesday that Tehran was changing tactics against shipping to keep oil prices high and to influence the 3 November US midterm elections. One official warned such vessels “would be safe nowhere, even outside the Strait of Hormuz”; another said a complete closure of the waterway would come soon. The IRGC claimed the ship struck a naval mine on an “unauthorised” route and has described the US-backed route along the Omani coast as dangerous. Maritime security firm Marisks said there was no independent evidence that a mine sank the vessel, called Iran’s claims consistent with a psychological-operations campaign, and said the available evidence does not establish that the southern route near Oman has been mined. ### Across the Region
**Oil transfers off India's west coast surge as Gulf shipping congestion worsens: The National**
Ship-to-ship transfers used to move Gulf oil around the Strait of Hormuz are now reaching India’s west coast as congestion and attack risk strain the workaround. Shipping analytics firm Vortexa said ship-to-ship transfers in India’s Gulf of Kutch reached 82 cargoes in September, about 680,000 barrels a day; refined products were about 65 percent of the volume and crude the rest. Vortexa senior market analyst Varaleka Pant said Fujairah and Sohar transfer zones were at record volumes, with signs of delays from operational limits, so activity spilled to India. Rystad Energy said crude transfers onto very large crude carriers have held at about 5 million to 7 million barrels a day since September, 60 to 70 percent of Hormuz flows, and that the full process, including waiting, now takes more than a week, against five to six days earlier in the conflict. Rystad’s Rahul Choudhary said overflow into the Arabian Sea is still small, and that some supertankers previously used for Saudi transfers are discharging directly off India’s west coast. South Korea, India, China and Japan took almost 70 percent of the Kutch volume, Vortexa said. Bimco, using a different count, reported 12 such operations in the area in September; chief shipping analyst Niels Rasmussen said the Gulf of Oman remains the main area. A Japanese refiner paid above $80 million to charter a tanker from the US Gulf Coast to Japan, Kpler’s Yui Torikata said. Kpler said Egypt’s Sidi Kerir terminal lets receiving tankers avoid both Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb, but the voyage to Asia takes about 55 days, against just over 20 days from the Gulf of Oman. **Houthis make ‘major leap’ building advanced missiles with greater Middle East range: The National**
After a month of heavier strikes aimed at pressing Saudi Arabia to end its blockade, Yemen’s Houthis have made what an arms-intelligence report calls a “major leap” in building sophisticated ballistic missiles at home with components imported from China. Conflict Armament Research examined an intercepted shipment bound for the Houthi-held port of Hodeidah and found specialist machined parts, including stabilising fins and equipment for hypersonic flight, in containers that documents show reached Yemen’s Aden Free Zone in June and October 2025 from China. The report said the parts point to work on the Iranian-made Kheibar Shekan series; the Houthis’ Palestine-2 variant has a stated range of 1,800 kilometres and, engineers say, can manoeuvre in its terminal stage at 6,000 kilometres per hour. The paper said the rebels, under a UN arms embargo, have moved beyond reassembling Iranian systems and have widened supply chains past Iran-linked networks. Kings College London’s Nawaf Obaid told The National the group has not yet mastered Kheibar Shekan technology, that Iranian expertise on the ground is speeding progress, and that scale remains the weakness. His department’s analysis put Saudi interception of missiles at 85 to 90 percent. Britain’s prime minister’s official spokesman said Britain strongly condemns Houthi attacks and remains committed to supporting Saudi Arabia’s security. China has said it adheres to the UN arms ban on the Houthis. ### The Closer Look
**Hundreds of IDF investigations into killings without public outcome, BBC Verify finds: BBC**
BBC Verify has identified 254 incidents in which the Israeli military said it was investigating allegations that it killed or injured people, and for which there is no public outcome. The cases sit in Gaza, Lebanon, the occupied West Bank and Israel. Using purpose-built AI tools, BBC Verify combed more than 60,000 news reports and then checked hundreds of incidents by hand. It found 300 cases the military said it was looking into. Only 28 had outcomes published on the Israel Defense Forces website. For a further 18, it was unclear whether the investigation had finished.

Of the 254, 158 were in Gaza, 48 in the West Bank and 32 in Lebanon. Sixteen were in Israel or in IDF detention. All fell between 7 October 2023 and 19 August 2026. At least one person was reportedly killed in more than 200 of them. Some cases with no public outcome date to late 2023. One of the deadliest was a strike on a school building in Gaza in August 2024, when at least 93 people were reportedly killed, including six women and 11 children. For three years, the military has often answered accusations by saying an incident is being looked into, reviewed, checked or examined. Often, BBC Verify found, that is the last public word.

The IDF told BBC Verify it takes every allegation of conduct that may be inconsistent with the law or with IDF directives with the utmost seriousness. It said it publishes outcomes when possible, subject to legal and operational considerations and to limits on privacy and information security. BBC Verify asked about each case more than once and received information on two incidents, which the IDF said are still under review. The International Committee of the Red Cross, which publishes guidelines on the Geneva Conventions, says investigations into potential violations of international humanitarian law should be as transparent as possible. The IDF also said some incidents go through command-level operational reviews meant to establish what happened, identify failures and draw lessons.

The results of IDF investigations are not routinely made public, including those that become criminal cases against soldiers. In August the military published a report on five incidents in Gaza. It said the killings of five-year-old Hind Rajab, and of 15 Palestinian Red Crescent emergency workers who were shot more than 100 times and allegedly targeted at close range, had been escalated to criminal investigations. In the three other cases — seven World Central Kitchen aid workers killed in an air strike, and four Médecins Sans Frontières aid workers killed in two separate attacks — the IDF said it did not find a basis, or reasonable suspicion, to open a criminal investigation. The same report said the General Staff Fact-Finding and Assessment Mechanism had completed reviews of about 150 cases since the start of the war and transferred them to the Military Prosecution. The IDF did not say what those 150 incidents were, and did not tell BBC Verify whether they were separate from the 300 cases the review identified. ### Both Sides: Should Cape Town confine two baboon troops for good?
Cape Town is weighing permanent captivity for two baboon troops on the Cape Peninsula, after years of animals entering the city for food. University of Cape Town professor Justin O’Riain argues that a sanctuary is the logical, compassionate and scientifically defensible answer. His best reason, as set out in the account Daily Maverick is answering, is behavioural: the troops keep coming into urban areas for food, which he reads as a revealed preference for the security and provisioning a sanctuary would offer. He points to expert consultations in behavioural ecology, primatology, sanctuary management and conservation.

Dr Adam Cruise, writing in Daily Maverick, argues that those disciplines cannot settle the moral question. His best reason is that a baboon taking food from a kitchen has not consented to life behind an electrified fence, and has not shown that foraging, movement and choice are dispensable. He says science can describe what animals do, but ethics asks what people may do to them, and that animal ethics and moral philosophy were missing from the consultations O’Riain described. Cruise cites the Five Domains Model associated with David Mellor, which treats nutrition, environment, physical health, behavioural interactions and mental state together, and holds that the chance to exercise choice is part of a life worth living. The decision in front of the city is whether two free-ranging troops lose that freedom for good. Neither writer, in today’s piece, names a date for a final ruling.
