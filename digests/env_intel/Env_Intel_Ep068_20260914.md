### Lead Story
Alberta released new wetland drainage rules that adjust permitting thresholds for agricultural and industrial activities. The changes respond to producer requests for streamlined approvals while maintaining some conservation safeguards. Previous rules imposed stricter no-drainage zones around certain wetland classes under the Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act framework. Practitioners conducting Phase I and Phase II assessments in Alberta must now factor these drainage allowances into land-use planning and remediation designs. The rules take effect without a stated transition period, requiring immediate review of active projects. Next steps include checking for any forthcoming implementation guidance from Alberta Environment and Parks. Alberta producers have welcomed the flexibility for operational needs on working lands. Conservation groups have raised concerns that reduced protections could accelerate habitat loss in key agricultural regions. Site assessors should cross-reference the revised boundaries against existing wetland inventories before submitting any new applications. The update applies province-wide and does not include a phased rollout for different wetland classes. **British Columbia Species at Risk Update: CBC**
No known wild northern spotted owl population remains in the province, yet conservationists report ongoing recovery potential through captive breeding and habitat measures. Assessors working under the Species at Risk Act should confirm current habitat order applicability on forestry or development files. The absence of wild birds shifts focus to reintroduction planning and protection of potential release sites. Habitat orders remain in force even without confirmed sightings. ### Practitioner Deep Dive: Wetland Boundary Delineation Under Revised Alberta Rules
A client submits a Phase II report for a former agricultural parcel in central Alberta where the new drainage rules appear to allow limited wetland alteration. The delineation line on the plan follows the 2023 aerial imagery, yet recent site photos show seasonal drawdown that the previous rules would have flagged as protected. Under the updated framework the boundary now hinges on soil and vegetation indicators rather than strict seasonal water presence, which changes how you document the feature in the submission. Experienced assessors notice that the revised criteria can shift the line by several metres on the same parcel depending on whether you sample in early spring or late summer. The common mistake is defaulting to the most recent imagery without ground-truthing the vegetation community against the new provincial descriptors; the fix is to collect three transects and photograph the indicator species in situ before finalizing the map.
