### Lead Story
Federal steps announced for a southern pipeline route to the Lower Mainland will terminate near Vancouver while explicitly avoiding the northern tanker ban area. The route aligns with planned expansion at Roberts Bank Terminal 2, requiring coordinated federal impact assessment and BC environmental assessment processes. Previous routing proposals had not explicitly tied marine terminal capacity increases to oil transport infrastructure. This means consultants conducting baseline studies or preparing effects assessments for port or pipeline projects must now evaluate cumulative marine and terrestrial impacts under both the Impact Assessment Act and BC Environmental Management Act. Next steps include federal-provincial work on protected areas boundaries that could constrain right-of-way options. ### Practitioner Deep Dive: Overlapping IAA and BC EMA Triggers on Linear Infrastructure
A client submits a single work plan for a pipeline corridor that crosses both federal and provincial assessment triggers, yet the baseline data packages do not align on spatial boundaries or valued component selection. The Impact Assessment Act requires consideration of cumulative effects from marine terminal expansion while the BC Environmental Management Act focuses on site-specific contaminated soil and groundwater pathways. Experienced assessors recognize that federal marine shipping components often pull in Fisheries Act and Species at Risk Act considerations that provincial submissions treat as separate. The practical distinction lies in how each regime handles the temporal boundary: federal processes extend to reasonably foreseeable future operations, whereas BC CSR risk assessments typically anchor to current conditions unless a Certificate of Compliance is sought. The mistake I see most often is assuming one set of sampling locations satisfies both regulators; the fix is to run a gap analysis against the federal cumulative effects spatial boundary before mobilizing field crews.
