### Practitioner Deep Dive: Managing High-Strength Organic Waste Remediation in Remote Coastal Sites
You arrive at a former fish-processing facility on the Newfoundland coast where 110 vats of concentrated fish sauce have sat for 25 years; the Phase II shows elevated BOD, ammonia, and sulphide in shallow groundwater but the nearest receptor is a small harbour 300 m away. The science here is straightforward: anaerobic decomposition of proteins generates volatile fatty acids and hydrogen sulphide that migrate rapidly in sandy or fractured bedrock aquifers typical of Atlantic Canada. In the field you notice that standard low-flow sampling underestimates peak concentrations because the plume is density-stratified; the experienced practitioner therefore installs multilevel wells screened at 1 m intervals and collects both filtered and unfiltered samples for sulphide and ammonia to capture the true mass flux. Regulatory submissions under the Newfoundland and Labrador Environmental Protection Act must demonstrate that post-removal verification meets the same risk-based criteria used for petroleum hydrocarbons, even though no CSR Protocol directly addresses this waste stream. The most common mistake is assuming that once the vats are hauled away the source is eliminated; residual pooled liquid and stained soil beneath the vats continue to release contaminants for months, so the fix is to require a minimum 30-day post-excavation monitoring period with triggers for additional excavation or in-situ oxidation before issuing a closure letter.
