### Lead Story
The federal government introduced Bill C-39 to set a one-year timeline for major project decisions. The bill contains more than 300 clauses that amend over a dozen federal statutes, including those protecting species at risk, water quality and fisheries. Previous assessment processes had no fixed one-year cap and operated under the existing Impact Assessment Act framework. Practitioners conducting federal impact assessments will need to track how the new clauses alter permitting sequences and inter-jurisdictional coordination. The bill's full text and supporting materials are available through the CBC report. Next steps include monitoring for committee review dates and any provincial responses on overlapping approvals. The changes touch multiple statutes that currently require separate reviews for fisheries and species protections. Impact assessment teams should map each amended provision against their active files to identify which review steps may shift first. ### Practitioner Deep Dive: Federal-provincial coordination on major project timelines
A client submits a project description under the Impact Assessment Act expecting the new one-year clock from Bill C-39 to override provincial review sequencing. The bill's clauses touch species at risk and fisheries protections that previously triggered separate federal-provincial agreements. Experienced assessors know that the one-year federal timeline does not automatically compress provincial EPEA or EMA steps, and early misalignment often surfaces during the first information request round. The practical distinction appears in how the bill treats "substitute" provincial processes versus full federal substitution. The most common mistake is assuming the federal clock resets all other statutory clocks; the fix is to map each jurisdiction's decision points against the bill's amendment list before the first public comment period. Teams should also verify whether any provincial statute contains its own fixed timelines that could conflict with the federal target. Early mapping prevents later disputes over which review stage actually controls the overall schedule.
