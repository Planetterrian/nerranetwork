### Lead Story
The Canada-US trade war is hitting supply-chain emissions calculations. Practitioners must adjust Scope 3 emissions estimates when US-sourced materials face new tariffs or routing. This affects federal carbon pricing compliance for any project with cross-border supply chains. Current models assuming stable North American logistics now require sensitivity analysis on transport distances and modes. No new federal guidance has been issued yet on revised calculation methods. The analysis indicates that tariffs are causing rerouting of shipments and increasing transport emissions in reported inventories. This requires practitioners to update their models for accuracy in reporting under the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program. Monitor Canada Gazette notices for any updates to reporting templates under the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program. ### Practitioner Deep Dive: Cross-Border Emissions Inventory Adjustments
A Phase II ESA for a logistics facility near the border shows Scope 3 emissions jumping after a supplier reroutes shipments to avoid new tariffs. The increase stems from longer truck distances and additional border idling rather than any change in the actual materials. Under federal output-based pricing systems, this shifts the facility's compliance obligation even though the on-site operation stayed identical. Experienced assessors now build two parallel inventories from the start — one assuming baseline North American routing and one stress-tested for trade-driven detours — then flag the higher number for client disclosure. The mistake I see most often is treating supply-chain distances as fixed inputs in the emissions model; the fix is to request supplier logistics declarations at the data-collection stage and rerun the calculation whenever tariff announcements appear. This adjustment process also requires documenting the specific tariff measures that triggered each reroute to maintain audit defensibility.
