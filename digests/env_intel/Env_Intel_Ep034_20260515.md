### Lead Story
The federal electricity strategy explicitly recognizes electrification as the primary mechanism for meeting industrial and utility demand growth. Previous federal approaches emphasized broad emissions reductions without mandating electrification pathways in project approvals. The new strategy stops short of setting binding timelines or clarifying how provincial regulators must integrate these priorities into existing environmental assessment processes. Consultants managing federal impact assessments or provincial permitting for power and industrial clients now face uncertainty over whether electrification requirements will be treated as mandatory design criteria or merely guidance. Project schedules for any facility relying on grid connections or on-site generation should be stress-tested against potential future alignment mandates. No specific effective date or amendment to the Impact Assessment Act is stated in the current release. ### Regulatory & Policy Watch
**Strong response to 2025 call for power, delivering clean, affordable energy: BC Gov News**
British Columbia approved multiple renewable power projects under its 2025 call for power, directing investment toward clean electricity generation that supports industrial load growth. The approvals create new compliance obligations for baseline environmental studies and ongoing monitoring at project sites. Proponents must now align submissions with provincial expectations for long-term emissions reductions tied to these investments. ### Practitioner Deep Dive: Baseline Data Collection for Electrification-Driven Infrastructure Projects
You arrive at a proposed battery storage or transmission substation site where the client’s internal schedule assumes a six-month environmental assessment window. The federal electricity strategy now signals that electrification components will receive priority weighting in future federal reviews, yet no updated guidance has been issued on how baseline groundwater or soil vapour data must be collected to support those reviews. In practice this means the standard CCME Tier 1 sampling grid may need expansion around proposed electrical infrastructure corridors because stray current and thermal effects from new equipment can alter local redox conditions and contaminant mobility. Experienced practitioners notice that early installation of nested monitoring wells at the downgradient edge of the proposed footprint often reveals seasonal groundwater mounding that standard Phase II programs miss, directly affecting risk assessment conclusions under provincial CSR or EPEA frameworks. The most common mistake is treating the electrification element as a simple add-on to an existing site plan rather than re-evaluating the entire conceptual site model for induced electrical fields; the fix is to insert a targeted stray-current and thermal-effects screen into the work plan before the first sampling round.
