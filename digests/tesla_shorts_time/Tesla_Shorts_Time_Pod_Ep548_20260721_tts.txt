Vehicle roofs have always been treated as simple structural panels whose main job is to keep weather out and passengers in.

Once a satellite terminal must live inside that same panel, every material choice suddenly carries thermal, RF, and pressure consequences that no traditional stamped-steel roof was ever designed to handle.

The Cybercab solution replaces the metal with a polymer cover and then builds an entire multi-layer antenna stack beneath it, complete with ninety percent air spacers and copper heat-spreading rims that move waste energy to the outer surface without fans or heaters.

The same stack must also survive daily altitude changes that expand trapped air inside the honeycomb cells.

Engineers therefore added intercellular micro-vents and a floating snap-fit chassis so the assembly can breathe and shift without delaminating.

Those vents and compliant adhesives add cost and assembly steps, yet they remove the need for separate active heating elements that would otherwise drain the robotaxi’s battery on cold mornings.

The net result is a roof that performs three unrelated functions—structural cover, thermal radiator, and satellite aperture—while adding minimal mass or power draw.

That multi-function approach changes the unit economics of the vehicle itself.

Every watt saved on heating or every kilogram avoided in the roof improves range and therefore revenue per trip for a fleet operator.

The engineering investment is front-loaded, but the recurring savings scale with every vehicle produced.
