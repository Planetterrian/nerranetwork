memory capacity decisions for inference chips sit at the intersection of cost, yield, and volume targets.
Tesla cut AI-5 memory in half before raising it to 96 gigabytes to stay above the lowest specification tier.
The same pattern appears in plans to reduce AI-6 memory by a third to 144 gigabytes specifically to enable higher
Optimus production rates. These moves reflect a deliberate trade-off between per unit performance
and the ability to scale robot output without immediate supply constraints.
Lower memory reduces both silicon area and power draw,
which in turn lowers the bill of materials and eases thermal requirements inside a compact humanoid
form factor. The approach accepts a temporary ceiling on single robot capability in exchange for
earlier volume that can generate data and revenue while next-generation memory supply catches up.
The economics hinge on how quickly cost per robot falls below the annual wage of the tasks
it replaces. At 10 million units per year, the cumulative learning curve on actuators, tendons,
and control software becomes steep enough to offset the initial performance concession.
Early fleets will likely operate in controlled environments where 72 or 96
gigabytes proof sufficient for defined motion libraries. External sales will
require higher memory only after the internal data flywheel has already lowered
unit costs. Supply chain partners therefore face a moving target. They must
deliver memory at the revised specification in volume while preparing for the
next upward revision once yields improve. The strategy compresses the timeline to break even,
but shifts risk onto software teams that must extract maximum utility from constrained hardware.
Vertical integration gives test of visibility into both the chip design and the robot assembly line,
allowing the memory spec to be adjusted in weeks rather than quarters.
That speed matters when the limiting factor is not demand but the availability of a single component.
The same flexibility that let the company raise the AI-5 spec from 72-96GB can be used
again when memory supply normalizes.
Until then, the priority remains getting robots into real work rather than waiting for
an ideal hardware configuration that may arrive after competitors have already captured early
contracts.
