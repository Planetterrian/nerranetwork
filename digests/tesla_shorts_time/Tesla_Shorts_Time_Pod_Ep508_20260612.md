### Tesla First Principles

Open-sourcing a custom datacenter transport protocol runs counter to the usual instinct to keep performance advantages inside the company. Tesla released the full TTP specification and joined an industry consortium, effectively inviting competitors to copy the same low-overhead fabric it uses for Dojo training clusters. The decision only makes sense if the bigger constraint is not raw speed but the cost and time required to build an ecosystem of compatible switches, optics, and test tools. By moving the protocol into the public domain, Tesla shifts part of that tooling burden onto the broader Ethernet supply chain while still retaining the ability to optimize its own silicon around the standard.

The practical effect appears in how quickly other AI labs could adopt the same Layer-2 approach instead of continuing to layer custom headers on top of IP. If multiple large training clusters converge on the same EtherType and MAC-based routing, switch vendors gain volume to amortize the development of lower-latency ASICs and better optical engines. Tesla’s own next-generation clusters would then benefit from cheaper, more mature components even if rivals also close some of the performance gap. The trade-off is that any efficiency edge tied purely to the protocol itself becomes harder to defend over time.

From a capital standpoint, the move reduces the risk that Dojo remains a one-off island whose networking hardware never reaches commodity pricing. It also creates optionality: if external demand for TTP-compatible equipment grows, Tesla can buy rather than build more of the surrounding infrastructure. The open release therefore functions less as a gift to competitors and more as a way to pull the entire supply curve downward for the hardware Tesla itself intends to keep scaling.
