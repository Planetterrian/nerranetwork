### Tesla First Principles

Charging infrastructure has traditionally been sized to the daily energy needs of passenger cars, with power levels set by what a typical commute or road trip requires. Tesla’s decision to place a 1.2 MW public Megacharger in California therefore sits outside that frame. The stall rating is driven by the energy density and duty cycle of the Semi, not by sedan or SUV patterns. This reverses the usual sequence in which light-duty vehicles set the specification and commercial trucks follow. The result is a charging asset whose utilization economics will be determined by fleet scheduling rather than individual driver behavior.

The cost side of the equation changes as well. A 1.2 MW connection demands upstream electrical infrastructure that most retail sites never contemplated. Tesla is absorbing that expense at a single location first, then presumably amortizing it across Semi sales and energy revenue. If the same power level later migrates to passenger-vehicle stalls, the marginal cost per car drops because the heavy infrastructure is already paid for. The reverse path—starting with cars and scaling up—has historically produced stranded assets when truck demand appears later.

For Tesla Energy the implication is straightforward. Megapack deployments can now be sited to serve both grid services and vehicle charging without separate interconnection studies for each use case. That dual-purpose capability shortens the payback period on the storage hardware itself. The open question is whether the same sites can maintain high utilization once Semi volumes rise and passenger-vehicle traffic fills the remaining hours. The data that will settle it is not quarterly vehicle deliveries but hourly kWh throughput at the Bloomington location over the next twelve months.
