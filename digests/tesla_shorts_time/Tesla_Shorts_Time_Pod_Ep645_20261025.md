### Tesla First Principles

Factory output records often hide the real constraint on scaling. Giga Berlin’s 7,000 Model Y week came from running the line 24/5, yet the same site is already studying a new hall because existing floor space caps further gains. The arithmetic is straightforward: each additional shift or robot cell still needs square meters of assembly, paint, and battery installation that cannot be compressed indefinitely. Adding more hours only delays the moment when physical space becomes the binding limit. The decision to evaluate a new hall shows management recognizes that utilization gains have a ceiling.

The same limit appears when a program like the Roadster moves from prototype display to volume production. The Petersen Museum unit uses the newest 4680 cells, but those cells must still pass through the same Giga Texas lines that currently serve Cybertruck and structural packs. Adding a second vehicle family on the same cell chemistry therefore competes directly for both cell output and pack assembly time rather than expanding total capacity. The 78 kWh LFP pack in the Mazda 6e example illustrates how other manufacturers are sourcing cells outside the 4680 ecosystem, avoiding that internal competition for now. Tesla’s choice to stay inside its own cell production loop creates a different set of trade-offs around throughput and allocation.

Vertical integration changes the cost curve only after the physical footprint is solved. Until new halls or entirely new sites come online, headline weekly records remain snapshots of utilization, not proof that the next doubling of output is already funded and permitted. The capital already committed to LFP Megapack lines and 4680 lines shows where Tesla is placing its bets, but the square-meter bottleneck travels with every new program. The $786 million tariff refund offers one source of incremental funding, yet it does not create additional factory floor space on its own. The constraint is physical before it is financial.
