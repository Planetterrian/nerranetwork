6. **Germany: Tesla Can Launch FSD in EU, but Must Drop the ‘Misleading’ Name** — PCMag

In a post published by Germany’s Federal Ministry of Transport, Minister Steffen Bilger said he is advocating for “swift approval” of the feature in the EU. However, the Ministry insists that the current name “FSD” is “somewhat misleading,” as the car does not take over the entire driving process. While the system can accelerate, brake and steer, the human driver must supervise it at all times and remains responsible. As a result, Tesla has reportedly offered to rename the system “Tesla Assisted Driving.” The name of the feature itself hasn’t been the only roadblock on the way to approval. Bilger says that “many technical aspects” as well as issues surrounding liability still need to be resolved, though he said he “reached a common understanding and a constructive agreement” after meeting with the firm in September. “My goal is for Tesla drivers to be able to benefit from Assisted Driving throughout the EU as quickly as possible,” said Bilger. At the time of writing, FSD has been approved in eight European countries : the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, Lithuania, Estonia, Slovenia, Czechia and Croatia. An EU-wide vote is expected not before December. Source: [pcmag.com](https://www.pcmag.com/news/germany-tesla-can-launch-fsd-in-eu-but-must-drop-the-misleading-name)

### Tesla First Principles

Battery demand forecasts for humanoid robots rest on the memory bandwidth required for real-time sensor fusion and motor control loops. A twenty-fold increase in DRAM consumption by twenty thirty implies that each additional robot will need roughly the same memory footprint as several current data-center inference cards. That scaling changes the cost structure of robot production because memory already accounts for a measurable share of bill-of-materials in high-performance AI systems. Manufacturers who secure long-term DRAM supply contracts early can lock in lower per-unit costs while competitors face spot-market volatility. The same dynamic appears in electric-vehicle battery packs, where cell chemistry and pack integration determine whether a vehicle reaches target margins or remains a loss leader at volume. The interplay between memory requirements and overall system cost will shape which companies can scale humanoid production profitably. Early movers in supply agreements may gain a lasting advantage in the emerging market for these machines. The parallel to EV battery economics shows how component choices ripple through the entire product economics. Securing stable memory supply therefore becomes a strategic priority rather than a simple procurement task. Companies that treat DRAM contracts as core to their robot economics stand to improve gross margins at scale. The pattern mirrors earlier supply-chain lessons in the electric vehicle sector where cell pricing directly influenced vehicle affordability.
