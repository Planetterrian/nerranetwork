### Tesla First Principles

The economics of humanoid robot production hinge less on the final assembly line and more on the cost and availability of high-strength magnets and actuators. Tesla's public timeline for Optimus sales in twenty twenty seven assumes those supply chains can scale without the multi-year lead times that have constrained other precision manufacturers. Securing consistent volumes of neodymium-iron-boron magnets at stable prices becomes the gating factor rather than robot software or final integration steps. 

A single NdFeB magnet set per robot already carries material costs that rival the entire bill of materials for many consumer electronics. If Tesla must secure new sources or vertically integrate magnet production, the per-unit price target moves from competitive with human labor to merely comparable. The difference appears in the margin structure once raw material volatility is factored in. Early supplier qualification data will determine whether the twenty twenty seven pricing goal holds or requires adjustment.

That shift changes the addressable market from broad industrial replacement to narrow high-value niches where the robot's endurance justifies a higher cost. The difference between those two outcomes is measured in the months it takes to qualify a new supplier or bring an in-house line to stable yield. Volume commitments from initial customers will reveal whether the economics support wide deployment or remain limited to specialized use cases. Supply chain decisions made in the next twelve months will set the cost floor for the entire program.
