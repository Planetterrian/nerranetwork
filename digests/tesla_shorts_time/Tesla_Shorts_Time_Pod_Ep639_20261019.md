### Tesla First Principles

A vehicle without a steering wheel or pedals must still meet the same federal motor vehicle safety standards written for cars that have both. The Cybercab’s design therefore triggers extra scrutiny from NHTSA precisely because those standards assume human controls exist. When regulators examine claims of school-zone speeding, the absence of those controls turns every incident into a test of whether the vehicle itself, rather than a driver, can be held to the original rule set. The fleet grew from fewer than 40 vehicles to more than 300 in roughly a month, with 319 Cybercabs and 420 Model Y robotaxis registered in Texas by October 10. That scale arrives while the agency is still determining how a car lacking traditional interfaces complies with standards written decades earlier. Each additional vehicle multiplies the number of edge cases regulators must evaluate before any unsupervised approval can be granted.

For Tesla the regulatory timeline now sits alongside production and software milestones as a direct constraint on revenue. Until the agency completes its reviews, the company cannot expand the paid service beyond Austin or convert the current supervised operation into the higher-margin unsupervised model it has projected. The gap between demonstrated fleet growth and cleared regulatory path therefore sets the effective pace at which Cybercab economics can reach the rest of the business. The same standards that once protected drivers now require new validation methods for systems that replace them entirely. NHTSA’s preliminary evaluation of school-zone incidents and its separate review of federal compliance both hinge on data that only the vehicle can generate. Tesla’s ability to supply that data at scale will determine whether the current Austin operation can serve as a template for other cities.

The economics of a robotaxi fleet shift once regulatory clearance arrives. With more than 300 Cybercabs already registered, the company has shown it can manufacture and deploy vehicles quickly. Yet each day of continued review delays the moment when those vehicles can operate without safety drivers or remote monitors. The capital already committed to the fleet sits idle in terms of full-margin revenue until the rules are clarified. In that sense the regulatory process functions as a hidden production bottleneck that no factory expansion can bypass. Tesla’s next earnings call may therefore be judged as much on regulatory progress as on vehicle delivery numbers.
