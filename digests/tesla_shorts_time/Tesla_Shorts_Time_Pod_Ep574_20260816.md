### Tesla First Principles

Battery degradation data from daily-driven Model Y vehicles shows that a 9 percent capacity loss after 25,000 miles still leaves usable range near 300 miles. This outcome sits between marketing claims of minimal fade and older concerns that lithium-ion packs would drop below 80 percent within the first two years. The measured result suggests owners can plan around gradual, predictable loss rather than sudden failure. Real-world testing under mixed driving conditions provides a clearer picture than accelerated lab cycles alone.

The same data set also reveals that real-world discharge patterns and thermal management matter more than calendar aging alone. Vehicles that spend most miles on highway routes with steady state-of-charge windows retain more capacity than those subjected to frequent short trips and rapid charging. Tesla's thermal architecture appears to limit the worst-case scenarios that appeared in earlier pack generations. Owners tracking these variables can adjust charging habits to extend pack life without relying on software interventions.

For buyers weighing used inventory or long-term ownership costs, the 91 percent retention figure translates into a narrower gap between advertised range and daily usability. Residual values and insurance calculations will eventually incorporate these observed curves rather than worst-case lab projections. The practical question shifts from whether the battery will last to how much margin remains after five or six years of typical commuting. Fleet operators evaluating total cost of ownership now have concrete benchmarks that tie directly to operating margins on high-mileage routes.
