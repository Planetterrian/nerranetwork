### Tesla First Principles

Factory output numbers draw the most attention, yet the real constraint on scaling often sits one layer deeper in component yield. When a battery cell line must hit both energy-density targets and cost-per-kilowatt-hour goals simultaneously, any shortfall in one forces a trade-off in the other. Tesla’s 4680 program has published incremental gains in both metrics, but the published data still show cell-level yield as the variable that directly multiplies or divides total pack output. Improving that single percentage lifts the entire downstream chain without requiring additional factory square footage.

That same yield problem appears in a different form at the vehicle-assembly level. A structural battery pack that also serves as the chassis floor reduces part count, yet it transfers the burden of dimensional precision onto the cell supplier and the pack-assembly process. If a single cell batch falls outside tolerance, the entire pack may be rejected rather than reworked, raising the effective scrap rate. Rivals that keep cells in separate modules can isolate defects more easily, though they carry higher assembly labor and weight. The choice between integrated and modular designs therefore becomes a calculation of acceptable scrap versus added assembly steps.

The business consequence is straightforward: every percentage point of improvement in cell yield lowers the capital required per delivered vehicle and per installed megawatt-hour of storage. Until that yield curve steepens further, capacity announcements will continue to outpace actual delivered volume, regardless of how many gigafactories are under construction. Investors watching headline gigawatt-hour targets miss the quieter arithmetic that determines when those targets actually reach customers.
