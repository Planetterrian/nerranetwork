Tesla received approval to construct three additional semiconductor buildings at its Austin site.

The facilities will support internal production of inference and control chips.

Construction adds to the existing Giga Texas footprint and keeps chip supply closer to vehicle assembly.

This step advances the company's long-term effort to reduce reliance on external foundries.

Yesterday's coverage of HW5 development makes today's permitting news a direct continuation of the same supply-chain strategy.

Building semiconductor capacity inside an automotive factory reverses the usual division of labor between carmakers and chip suppliers.

Most manufacturers buy processors from specialized foundries that serve many industries, accepting standardized parts and shared production queues.

Tesla instead treats silicon as another in-house component, similar to how it already makes motors and battery packs.

The approach carries higher upfront capital cost and requires recruiting different engineering talent, yet it promises tighter integration between hardware revisions and software updates that run on the vehicle.

The decision also changes the risk profile around supply disruptions.

When chips come from external vendors, a shortage at the foundry immediately constrains vehicle output.

Internal production shifts that constraint to Tesla's own construction timeline, equipment yield, and process learning curve.

From a margin perspective, owning the silicon layer adds another lever for gross-profit improvement once the facilities reach scale.
