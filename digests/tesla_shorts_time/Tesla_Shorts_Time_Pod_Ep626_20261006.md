### Tesla First Principles

Battery chemistry choices in electric vehicles create direct trade-offs between cost, energy density, and supply chain risk. Tesla's decision to import a smaller-pack Model 3 into Canada at a lower price point illustrates how manufacturers can accept reduced range to meet tariff rules and local price targets. The Canadian-spec car carries an estimated 70.8 kWh pack and a 288-mile range, compared with the 78.1 kWh and 363-mile figure for the U.S. version. That gap changes the vehicle's competitive position against the Chevrolet Bolt, which offers 262 miles at a slightly higher price. The approach shows how regulatory and tariff constraints can force earlier compromises on pack size than pure engineering would suggest.

Supercharger network rules also embed safety and liability calculations. The new Emergency Drive Away feature lets drivers pull away while still connected, but Tesla warns that misuse can produce up to twenty-five thousand dollars in repair costs. The override applies only in the United States and only after the latest software update, leaving pre-2021 Model S and Model X vehicles unsupported. This design keeps the primary safety interlock in place for normal use while creating an explicit exception for documented emergencies. The cost ceiling and geographic limit together signal how Tesla balances owner requests against infrastructure protection.

Manufacturing scale decisions for the Semi follow a different logic than passenger vehicles. A million-mile trucker touring the factory highlights the need for durability data that passenger-car programs do not generate at the same volume. Early fleet orders, such as the fifty units added by IMC Logistics, provide the first real-world mileage accumulation that can validate or adjust the original cost-per-mile projections. Those projections remain the central variable that will determine whether the Semi expands beyond niche long-haul routes.
