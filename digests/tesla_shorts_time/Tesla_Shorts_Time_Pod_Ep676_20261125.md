1. **EU meeting on Tesla Assisted Driving moved to December 15**
   The European Commission's calendar now lists the next TCMV meeting on Tesla Assisted Driving for December 15. The date remains marked draft and follows the group's pattern of Tuesday meetings. Tesla needs 15 of 27 EU countries representing at least 65 percent of the population to secure approval. Twelve countries already allow the system, with Italy the latest addition. Germany backed approval after the rename to Assisted Driving and the 10 percent speed-offset cap. If the December 15 vote passes, Finland's regulator estimates EU-wide effect in about three weeks. The agenda will settle whether the session brings a real vote or another round of talks. The October agenda went public 11 days before that meeting, so this one could appear in early December. If all 13 current supporters vote yes, Tesla sits at about 47 percent of the population. That leaves two more countries as the bare minimum, and they have to bring about 18 more points of population with them. France alone cannot get there at about 15 percent, but Spain and Poland together can at about 19 percent. Source: [x.com](https://x.com/tslaming/status/2125542635041624134)

2. **GM Korea signs NACS deal with Tesla for future EVs**
   GM Korea announced an agreement to equip its future electric vehicles sold in South Korea with North American Charging Standard ports. The deal extends the 2023 North American charging partnership. Owners of current CCS-equipped GM vehicles can use adapters for Tesla Superchargers. Tesla Korea operates more than 1,200 Supercharger stalls across 180 sites. Stellantis previously signed for Tesla charging access in Korea starting in 2027. GM is stepping away from Korea's own CCS1 standard for its future cars. The country made CCS1 its fast-charging standard in 2017, and GM's Korean EVs have used it until now. Local charging companies have been adding Tesla connectors to fast chargers at highway rest areas. Other brands have been able to use part of the network since November 2023 through an adapter that stays on the Supercharger handle. GM has not named the models, the launch date, or the price per charge yet. That puts Hyundai and Kia in an awkward spot at home because they already build Tesla-port EVs for American buyers but their cars in Korea still use CCS1. ### Tesla First Principles
Charging standards have long been treated as national infrastructure decisions rather than competitive tools. When GM Korea committed to NACS ports on future models, it abandoned Korea's CCS1 mandate from 2017 and gained direct access to more than 1,200 Tesla Supercharger stalls across 180 sites. That single move shifts the economics for every other Korean automaker still locked into the older standard. Local operators in Korea have already begun adding Tesla connectors to highway fast chargers, and Stellantis secured adapter access starting in 2027. Once two major rivals commit capital to the Tesla network, the remaining holdouts face rising per-station costs and shrinking negotiating leverage. The network effect compounds because every new NACS vehicle increases utilization without Tesla bearing the vehicle-side cost.

The same pattern is repeating elsewhere. For Tesla the outcome is straightforward margin expansion. More vehicles using the same stalls raise revenue per site while the marginal cost of electricity and maintenance stays largely fixed. The company avoids the capital outlay that would be required to match a fragmented CCS1 network at scale. Over time the standard itself becomes the moat, not the hardware.

Hyundai and Kia already produce vehicles with NACS ports for the American market, yet their domestic Korean lineup remains on CCS1. That split creates a visible cost disadvantage at home once GM and Stellantis vehicles begin drawing from the same high-utilization sites. The data shows Tesla Korea's network was selected to add stalls at rest areas this year, further widening the gap in coverage density. Every additional NACS vehicle sold in Korea therefore tightens the loop: higher throughput per stall, lower average cost per kWh delivered, and stronger incentive for the next manufacturer to switch rather than duplicate infrastructure.
