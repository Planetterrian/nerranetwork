A truck that tops out at 12 miles per hour and runs on 28 miles of range can operate inside
a controlled, flat route.
In that tunnel, energy density and fast charging matter less than durability and simplicity.
The tradeoff is that the same hardware cannot easily migrate to higher speed or longer
range surface applications without redesign. So the shared part strategy works only inside a narrow
operating window. Remote piloting from the Texas Operations Center removes the need for onboard
driver interfaces or cabin safety systems that would add weight and cost on an open road vehicle.
Repurposing automotive packs and drive units for underground freight changes the cost equation for both
the vehicle maker and the tunneling company. The components now handle freight without a new
powertrain design, cutting the marginal expense of each additional truck.
That reuse also reduces the number of unique parts the supply chain must support, which
matters when production volumes for the base vehicle remain the priority.
Over repeated cycles, the shared hardware can lower the per-unit cost of every liner truck
that rolls out of the same supplier base.
For Tesla, the arrangement creates a small but steady internal demand signal for cells and
motors that would otherwise sit in finished vehicle inventory.
Over time, those steady, low variability orders can help smooth factory utilization even
if external vehicle sales fluctuate.
The current modification is an incremental step rather than a complete re-architecture.
Whether the freight version finds paying external customers will determine if the reuse model
becomes a repeatable margin lever or remains a niche demonstration.
The valuation from September's funding round provides the runway to test that question
at commercial volumes.
