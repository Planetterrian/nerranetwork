### Tesla First Principles

Taking a step back from today's headlines, the observation that small EVs like the Renault 4 or Chevy Bolt achieve only marginally better efficiency than larger models at highway speeds upends the common belief that downsizing is the straightforward path to better energy use. Many assume that lower weight and reduced frontal area would lead to significantly higher miles per kilowatt-hour, but real-world experiences show this is not always the case. This challenges the idea that smaller is always more efficient in the EV space. It forces a closer look at what actually drives energy consumption on the road.

The data from drivers at 75 miles per hour reveals that these small EVs get about 3 to 3.5 miles per kWh, which is comparable to or even lower than some sleek larger sedans like the Model 3. The smaller frontal area and lower weight should help, yet the drag coefficients of these compact models are not as optimized as expected. This comparison highlights how other elements, such as tire choices and body shape, play into the overall efficiency equation. It shows that physics does not always favor the smallest option as much as intuition suggests.

Tesla's engineering focus on aerodynamics and integrated software for energy management positions it well to address these realities. By prioritizing low drag designs and efficient power delivery, the company can achieve strong efficiency numbers even in larger vehicles. This approach means that scaling production across different sizes does not require sacrificing performance in the way some competitors might face. Ultimately, it could help Tesla capture a wider range of customers by offering competitive efficiency without needing to produce ultra-small models.
