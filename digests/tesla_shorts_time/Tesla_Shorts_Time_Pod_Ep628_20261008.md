### Tesla First Principles
Owning a semiconductor fabrication plant shifts capital allocation from variable supplier contracts to fixed facility costs and long-term depreciation schedules. Tesla and SpaceX have committed to building and running Terafab rather than relying on external foundries for every wafer run. This choice locks in access to process nodes at a time when contract manufacturers face capacity constraints from multiple AI customers. The decision moves spending from per-wafer fees into upfront construction and equipment outlays that appear on the balance sheet for years.

The economics hinge on utilization rates. A dedicated line can run at higher average loads than a shared fab that must balance competing orders, yet it also requires continuous investment in equipment refreshes every two to three years. Intel’s role in packaging provides one external interface, but the core front-end processes stay inside the joint Tesla-SpaceX entity. That structure reduces the risk of allocation fights during chip shortages while increasing exposure to execution risk on yield and ramp timelines. Yield improvements directly affect the cost per inference chip delivered to Optimus and Cybercab programs.

For the vehicle and robot programs the decision ultimately affects gross margin on autonomy hardware. If internal production meets cost targets it lowers the bill of materials for each inference computer; if yields lag, the same capital outlay raises per-unit expense. The trade-off is therefore between supply certainty and manufacturing learning-curve risk, not simply between vertical integration and outsourcing. Over time the fixed costs become predictable while variable supplier pricing remains exposed to market swings in AI demand.
