Tesla told Sandy Munro in early twenty twenty-five that it was targeting a Cybercab pack under fifty kilowatt hours with close to three hundred miles of range.

That figure sits under a chemistry choice the company has not locked for the life of the car.

LFP cells cost less, generate less heat, and tolerate more charge cycles than the nickel-based four six eight zero cells currently installed.

Reserved internal volume is there so a lower-density chemistry can still meet that range target as production scales.

The trade is a modest reduction in cell volume in exchange for replacing one module without discarding the entire pack.

Eric Earley said replacing an entire pack before half a million miles is a killer on cost.

Swapping a single module keeps the vehicle in service and spreads the battery’s capital cost across more revenue miles.

Full pack replacement before that half-million-mile mark would erase much of the per-mile savings high utilization is supposed to deliver.

Adding extra LFP cells to offset lower energy density still keeps total pack cost lower than a denser chemistry that cannot be serviced in the field.

Over the life of the vehicle, savings on replacement cycles and less downtime matter more than the marginal range lost on any single trip.

The same logic applies whether Tesla owns the car or a fleet customer does.

The architecture embeds a bet that utilization, not peak energy density, is the dominant variable in robotaxi unit economics.
