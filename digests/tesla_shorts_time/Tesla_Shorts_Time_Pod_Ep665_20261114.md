### Tesla First Principles
Tesla's planned $10 billion solar factory in Texas begins with crews breaking ground on roughly 3,000 acres in Fort Bend County, where drone footage already shows a homestead still in use alongside equipment staging areas and extensive clearing. The project timeline sets commercial production for 2029, with construction employment peaking at 1,147 jobs before settling into 1,900 permanent positions that are projected to grow to 9,712 by 2033. All stages of panel production from silicon ingots and wafers through coated cells to finished modules will occur on the same site under the Project Crystal Sun designation filed under Texas's Jobs, Energy, Technology, and Innovation Act.

That single-site vertical integration changes the capital math compared with the more common model of sourcing ingots, cells, and modules from separate suppliers across multiple states or countries. An economic analysis tied to the filing estimates the facility could contribute $107 billion to Texas GDP over decades while supporting Musk's stated goal that Tesla and SpaceX may each manufacture 100 gigawatts of solar products annually in the United States. The scale also positions the factory as one of the largest domestic efforts to consolidate the solar supply chain at a moment when U.S. solar manufacturing capacity is expanding but still trails global leaders.

For Tesla's energy business the decision ties manufacturing capacity directly to the company's own demand signals from Megapack, Powerwall, and Solar Roof deployments rather than relying on external suppliers whose pricing and availability can shift with trade policy or raw-material shortages. The 3,000-acre footprint and the multi-year job ramp from 1,900 to nearly 10,000 positions reflect a long-horizon bet that integrated U.S. production can compete on cost once the initial capital is deployed. Whether the 2029 start date and the projected job curve materialize will test how quickly Tesla can translate site preparation into finished modules at the volumes needed to support both its vehicle and stationary storage lines.
