### Tesla First Principles

Scaling three distinct hardware programs at once forces trade-offs that single-product companies never face. Tesla is simultaneously ramping vehicle production, building a purpose-built Robotaxi fleet, and installing Optimus lines inside the same capital envelope. Each program carries its own learning curve on yield, supplier qualification, and regulatory approval, yet they all draw from the same pool of engineering hours and cash. Battery pack capacity is already identified as the binding constraint on near-term vehicle output, so every additional dollar directed toward AI inference silicon or humanoid actuators directly competes with that expansion.

The conventional view treats these efforts as additive growth vectors that will eventually compound. In practice the shared constraints create sequencing risk: a delay in one area can starve resources from the others. When the Q2 update lists continued progress on cathode material production and lithium refining in Texas alongside the Cortex 2 ramp, the allocation decisions become visible in real time. The result is a slower overall ramp than a focused factory build-out would deliver, because the marginal engineering hour spent on one program is an hour not spent on the others.

Investors are therefore paying for optionality rather than near-term margin expansion. The earnings release shows exactly this tension: record revenue alongside falling operating profit because the spending curve leads the revenue curve by multiple quarters. Whether the bet pays off depends less on any single program's technical success and more on whether Tesla can sequence the three ramps without exhausting cash or talent in the interim. The doubling of onsite Texas compute and the naming of TSMC, Samsung and Micron as Optimus partners are early indicators of how that sequencing is being executed.
