Fort Bend County, Teslarati reports, is where Tesla's Project Crystal Sun is now being graded on that same tract.

Flyover drone footage from S.E. Robinson, Junior, released this morning, shows large clearings, equipment staging areas, an existing homestead still in use, and active leveling across a substantial portion of the property.

The plant is designed to make the full solar chain, from silicon ingots and wafers to coated cells and finished modules.

Operations are targeted for the first quarter of twenty twenty-nine.

Construction itself is targeted for twenty twenty-six through twenty twenty-eight.

Tesla projects the permanent workforce will ramp from roughly one thousand nine hundred positions in the first year of operations to that nine thousand seven hundred twelve figure by twenty thirty-three, with a peak of about one thousand one hundred forty-seven construction jobs during the build.

Public filings first submitted in July under Texas's Jobs, Energy, Technology and Innovation Act describe that full chain from ingots to finished modules.

Lamar Consolidated Independent School District unanimously approved school-tax limitations in September.

Fort Bend County commissioners approved a package of abatements and related agreements in late October that could extend equipment tax breaks for as long as thirty-five years.

Tesla had previously indicated it was evaluating a competing out-of-state location.

Elon Musk has said Tesla and SpaceX are each working toward one hundred gigawatts of annual U.S. solar manufacturing capacity, and if built as described, Project Crystal Sun would stand as one of the largest domestic attempts to create a fully integrated solar supply chain on American soil.

Building that entire chain on one site is a bet against the fragmented model the rest of the industry still uses.

Capital has to be committed years ahead of revenue if Tesla wants control over every step that sets panel cost and quality.

An accompanying economic analysis estimates the project could add roughly one hundred seven billion dollars to Texas gross domestic product, or GDP, over several decades.

That figure rests on vertical integration cutting exposure to imported wafers and cells whose prices have swung sharply in recent years.

By owning the conversion from raw silicon to finished modules, Tesla can adjust output mix and process parameters without renegotiating supplier contracts or waiting on third-party capacity expansions.

The same logic that once justified building its own battery lines now applies to solar, where module margins have compressed as Chinese production scaled.

For customers and utilities, the practical result is a more predictable supply of domestically produced panels paired with storage.

When the factory reaches the planned scale, it removes one layer of price volatility that has slowed solar-plus-storage adoption in some markets.

Those already approved thirty-five-year equipment tax abatements further lower the effective capital cost, shifting the break-even point earlier than a non-integrated competitor could achieve on the same site.

The bet is that control over the entire process will matter more than speed to first panel, and more than any single supplier relationship.
