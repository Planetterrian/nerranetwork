The six-meter cap on Tesla's summon feature originates in older UN regulations written
for conventional remote control parking systems. Those rules assumed a human operator standing
nearby with line of sight control. Tesla's current implementation uses cameras and neural nets
that already exceed the original safety assumptions behind the limit. The gap between rule and capability
has widened as sensor ranges and processing power improved. Car makers are now asking the UN to raise
or remove the cap, because the same sensor suite that supports full self-driving can also manage longer distance low-speed maneuvers.
The request is not about relaxing safety standards, but about aligning regulation with demonstrated sensor performance.
If the change is approved, the functional difference between summoned and supervised low-speed autonomy shrinks further.
Regulators would then evaluate actual system behavior rather than fixed distance limits.
The practical effect appears in fleet economics, a vehicle that can park itself from farther
away reduces the need for dedicated pickup zones at offices, airports, and apartment complexes.
That reduction lowers real estate costs for robotaxi operators and increases the number
of viable deployment locations without new infrastructure.
The regulatory adjustment therefore touches both vehicle software and the physical footprint
of future mobility networks.
