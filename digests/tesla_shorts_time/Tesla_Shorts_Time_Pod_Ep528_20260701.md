### Tesla First Principles

Price changes that follow subsidy approvals reveal how government incentives reshape the actual transaction rather than simply lowering the buyer's net cost. When Tesla Korea raised Model 3 and Model Y prices the day after subsidy confirmation, the increase offset part of the incentive for buyers while preserving the manufacturer's margin. The sequence shows that list prices are not fixed engineering outcomes but adjustable levers that respond to policy shifts in real time.

This adjustment matters because it alters the demand signal that reaches the factory. A higher posted price can reduce volume even when the subsidized net price stays similar, which in turn affects production planning and supplier contracts. In markets where subsidies are temporary or subject to annual resets, manufacturers that treat the incentive as permanent risk building capacity that later proves mismatched to unsubsidized demand.

The longer-term implication is that subsidy design influences not only which cars sell but also how aggressively companies invest in cost reduction. If incentives routinely allow price increases without losing buyers, the pressure to drive structural cost out of the vehicle declines. Over repeated cycles, that dynamic can slow the very efficiency gains that would make the product viable without ongoing support.
