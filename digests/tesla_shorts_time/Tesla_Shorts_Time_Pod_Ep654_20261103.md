### Tesla First Principles
The economics of placing AI training and inference in orbit rest on a narrow set of physical advantages that must still clear steep cost hurdles. Cooling becomes simpler because radiative heat rejection works efficiently in vacuum, and the absence of gravity-driven convection allows certain crystal-growth processes to run without terrestrial interference. Yet the same environment demands redundant power loops, micrometeoroid shielding, and coatings that degrade under atomic oxygen, all of which multiply mass and therefore launch expense. The post notes that Earth's infrared glow adds another variable for thermal management while surface coatings face constant erosion. These factors combine to create an engineering race where every kilogram saved directly reduces the price of each GPU-year delivered in space.

SpaceX's Starfall capsule, now offered commercially with up to 1,000 kg payload capacity and continuous Starlink connectivity, illustrates one attempt to turn those physics into a service. Customers in biomedical and semiconductor fields can run experiments for weeks rather than seconds, pulling data in real time instead of waiting for recovery. The capsule's 1 kW power limit and Ethernet interface set clear boundaries on what experiments fit, while the decision to launch on Starship signals that cadence and cost per kilogram remain the decisive variables. The commercial page lists three target markets: biomedical organoid growth, pharmaceutical crystal formation, and materials science for next-generation semiconductors. Each mission supplies power and data links throughout the flight, removing the need for post-splashdown retrieval before analysis begins.

For Tesla the relevant question is whether orbital compute ever becomes cheaper than terrestrial clusters for the specific workloads of FSD and Optimus training. The GAO assessment that underlying power and cooling technologies are mature but unproven at data-center scale applies directly here. If launch costs fall faster than terrestrial electricity and land prices rise, the crossover point moves closer; if shielding and redundancy costs stay high, the advantage stays theoretical. The outcome will shape how much of Tesla's future inference load stays on Earth versus moving off-planet. Starfall's 1,000 kg limit and 1 kW power ceiling already define the practical envelope for any early orbital node. The same constraints that make cooling easier also cap how large a single payload can grow before multiple launches become necessary. The balance between those limits and Starship's projected flight rate will determine whether orbital AI infrastructure moves from concept to production capacity within the next decade.
