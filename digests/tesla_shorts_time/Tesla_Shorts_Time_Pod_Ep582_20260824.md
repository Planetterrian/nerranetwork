### Tesla First Principles

High electricity prices can close even established fast-charging sites, as seen with the Gorham Supercharger. The underlying constraint is that charger economics depend on local power rates and utilization, not just hardware deployment. When rates rise faster than revenue per session, operators face a simple margin calculation that favors shutdown over continued operation. Site-level profitability becomes the decisive factor once fixed costs are sunk. Variable energy expenses directly determine whether a location clears its ongoing hurdle rate.

Tesla's network expansion therefore carries an implicit geographic filter. Sites in high-cost power regions must achieve higher throughput or secure rate concessions to remain viable. This dynamic limits uniform national coverage and pushes investment toward corridors where electricity is cheaper or demand is denser. Operators evaluate each potential location against power-market data before committing capital. The result is uneven rollout shaped by utility pricing rather than vehicle density alone.

Over time the pattern favors a hub-and-spoke model rather than blanket density. Operators concentrate capital where unit economics clear the hurdle rate, leaving thinner coverage elsewhere. The result is a charging map shaped more by power markets than by vehicle registrations alone. Future site selection will continue to weigh electricity cost volatility against projected session volume. This economic reality sets boundaries on how quickly and evenly the network can scale.
