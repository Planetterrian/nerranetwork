### Tesla First Principles

Battery supply agreements like the $4.3 billion LG deal for Megapack cells expose a core constraint in Tesla Energy’s growth path. Cell manufacturing remains the largest single cost driver even after vertical integration in packs and inverters, and domestic plants must still clear yield and scale hurdles before they fully displace imported supply. When those plants reach 35 GWh annual output, the marginal cost per stored kilowatt-hour drops, but only if the cells meet the same cycle-life and thermal specs already proven in fielded Megapacks. The Lansing and Delta Township facilities together represent a deliberate bet on North American cell production to serve both Tesla’s own storage products and third-party customers such as Toyota. Any delay in reaching nameplate capacity directly affects how quickly Tesla can bid on utility-scale projects that reference Megapack by name in their resource plans.

The alternative is continued reliance on overseas cell makers, which carries tariff, logistics, and policy risk that a Texas or Michigan facility can sidestep. Yet building that capacity requires years of capex before the first dollar of margin appears, and the Northwest Power Plan’s 5.2 GW storage target shows how quickly utility demand can outpace any single supplier’s ramp. Tesla therefore faces a straight trade-off between locking in long-term domestic cost curves and accepting slower near-term deployment velocity. The $2.3 billion fixed-cost estimate in the draft plan assumes storage will be available on schedule; slippage at either LG site would force utilities to revisit the renewables-plus-batteries preference and potentially increase the gas component. That tension sits at the center of whether Tesla Energy can keep pace with the data-center load growth the plan projects through 2032.

The practical result is visible in lease structures such as the Texas Powerwall VPP offer. Lower upfront customer cost only works if the underlying cell supply stays predictable and priced to support the 3 % annual escalator over twelve years. Any slippage in LG’s Lansing or Delta Township output directly widens the gap between advertised monthly payments and actual delivered economics for both Tesla and the grid. The 12-year term and dispatch-rights credit assume the batteries will perform reliably for the full period, which in turn depends on cell quality and availability at the scale now being built in Michigan. If domestic production meets its targets, the lease program can expand without raising the net customer cost; if it does not, Tesla will face pressure to either increase the monthly payment or absorb more of the cell-cost variability itself.
