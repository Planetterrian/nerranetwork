### Tesla First Principles
Co-locating semiconductor and solar manufacturing on a single Texas site changes the capital allocation math for Tesla's energy business. The $10 billion Crystal Sun project sits on the same acreage as the earlier Terafab chip facility, allowing shared infrastructure, permitting, and workforce development instead of duplicated site work. That overlap lowers the marginal cost of adding solar cell capacity once the first factory shell is already in place.

The scale target is explicit: more than 100 GW of production equipment is the stated goal for the combined solar effort. Reaching even a fraction of that volume requires the kind of long-lead equipment orders already signed with SINGULUS for heterojunction lines, with the first line due in the first half of 2027. Vertical integration here is not marketing language; it is a direct response to the fact that solar cell manufacturing equipment itself has become a bottleneck when demand spikes across multiple industries.

The business implication is straightforward. If Tesla can bring both chip and solar cell production online inside the same industrial park, the company reduces exposure to external supply constraints on the two components most critical to its energy storage and vehicle autonomy roadmaps. Every additional gigawatt of internal solar capacity also creates a potential hedge against module price volatility that has historically compressed margins for third-party installers. The site therefore functions as both a manufacturing asset and a risk-management tool. The approach mirrors how integrated players in other capital-intensive sectors have used geographic clustering to compress timelines and protect margins when input costs fluctuate.
