### Tesla First Principles

Subsidies change the buyer equation more abruptly than most product improvements. When Tokyo stacked national and metropolitan credits to cut nearly $12,000 off a Model 3, the effective price fell below many mass-market gasoline cars without any change to the vehicle itself. That single policy adjustment instantly widens the set of households for whom a Tesla becomes the rational daily driver rather than a stretch purchase.

The same arithmetic applies in reverse when credits shrink or disappear. Markets that grew quickly on temporary incentives often see demand reset once the subsidy cliff arrives, regardless of how advanced the technology has become. Tesla’s cost-reduction work still matters, yet the near-term volume swing is frequently larger from policy moves than from another few percent of efficiency or a new battery chemistry.

Over time the company benefits when subsidies accelerate scale and learning-curve gains that persist after the incentives fade. The risk lies in treating those gains as permanent when they remain dependent on continued policy support in multiple large markets. Watching which regions renew or expand credits versus which let them lapse will reveal where Tesla’s volume trajectory stays durable and where it may flatten.
