### Tesla First Principles

Building semiconductor capacity inside an automotive factory reverses the usual division of labor between carmakers and chip suppliers. Most manufacturers buy processors from specialized foundries that serve many industries, accepting standardized parts and shared production queues. Tesla instead treats silicon as another in-house component, similar to how it already makes motors and battery packs. The approach carries higher upfront capital cost and requires recruiting different engineering talent, yet it promises tighter integration between hardware revisions and software updates that run on the vehicle.

The decision also changes the risk profile around supply disruptions. When chips come from external vendors, a shortage at the foundry immediately constrains vehicle output. Internal production shifts that constraint to Tesla's own construction timeline, equipment yield, and process learning curve. Early yields on new nodes are rarely perfect, so the company must absorb learning costs that a dedicated supplier would otherwise spread across multiple customers. Over time the same vertical stack can reduce per-unit cost if volume justifies the investment and if the designs stay ahead of what off-the-shelf parts can deliver for autonomy workloads.

From a margin perspective, owning the silicon layer adds another lever for gross-profit improvement once the facilities reach scale. Each incremental improvement in chip efficiency or cost flows directly to the vehicle or robot without a supplier margin in between. The trade-off is that capital tied up in clean rooms and process development cannot be used elsewhere, and any misstep in node selection or process technology becomes a company-wide problem rather than a vendor issue. The plants therefore represent a long-term bet that control over the compute substrate will matter more than the flexibility of buying from the merchant market.
