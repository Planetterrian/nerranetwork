### Tesla First Principles

Charging infrastructure and energy storage share the same underlying constraint: how much power can move through a given connection at any moment. A home charger adding 42 miles per hour and a Megapack feeding an entire neighborhood both run into wire, transformer, and rate limits that no software update can erase. Tesla's decision to open the first public Semi Megacharger therefore tests whether high-power DC can scale faster than the grid upgrades required to support it. The economics of the station will depend on utilization rates that only appear once multiple fleet customers route through the same corridor on predictable schedules.

The cost comparison between Cybertruck charging and F-150 refueling makes the same point in dollars. Electricity at residential rates undercuts diesel on a per-mile basis today, yet that advantage shrinks once demand charges or limited off-peak windows enter the equation. Fleet operators watching the new California station will learn whether the infrastructure investment pays for itself through utilization or whether siting constraints keep the effective cost higher than the headline numbers suggest. The same arithmetic will apply to any future Megapack deployments that rely on the same grid connections.

Germany's Berlin factory posting higher profits on lower volume shows the same principle at the manufacturing level. Fixed costs spread across fewer units usually erode margins, yet mix improvements and operating discipline produced the opposite result. The pattern suggests that future capacity additions will only lift earnings if they avoid simply replicating the current cost structure at larger scale. Any expansion that raises average selling price or reduces variable cost per unit faster than volume grows will preserve the margin lift already visible in the latest quarter.
