Municipal water sealed in a thin polyethylene terephthalate bottle, PET, reaches concession stands in bulk cases. The same bottle then carries that stand tag once the park gates close behind the customer. Plastic, cap, and water do not explain the spread. The contracts that give one vendor sole rights inside a captive audience do.

Parks and municipal venues sign long-term exclusive beverage agreements that hand pouring and vending rights to a single supplier. A Dallas City Council memorandum records one such ten-year PepsiCo contract covering sixty-six facilities and delivering the city more than one point one million dollars in payments and revenue splits in exchange for that exclusivity.

Similar language appears in the Waite Park amphitheater agreement and the Neshoba County request for proposals. Each bars outside drinks and routes every sale through the contracted concessionaire.

Visitors cannot bring their own water or shop elsewhere once inside, so the vendor faces no direct price competition at the point of sale. Pennsylvania House bill HB twenty-five seventy-nine was introduced because those captive conditions produce markups legislators judged excessive.

The bill proposes statutory ceilings of two dollars for sixteen ounces or less and four dollars for twenty-four ounces or more.

Wholesale data from the Baytown water-park bid shows the commodity underneath. Twenty-four bottles of sixteen point nine ounces sold for four dollars and forty-four cents, roughly eighteen point five cents each. That figure already includes bottling, labeling, and delivery to the park operator.

The remaining gap sits in contract administration, revenue sharing, and the absence of alternative sellers, not in any further physical change to the product.

Park operators accept the structure because the exclusivity payment offsets other budget lines and because every prior operator has done the same. The price feels normal inside the industry even though the physical object has not changed between the loading dock and the stand. Liability rules reinforce the pattern.

A single vendor simplifies insurance claims and cuts the number of parties a park must audit for food-safety compliance. Revenue splits also push the park to maximize volume through the exclusive channel rather than bring in competing sellers that might erode the payment stream.

Over time the arrangement reinforces itself. Concession staff are trained only on the contracted product line. Menu boards and refrigeration units are sized and wired for that supplier’s packaging.

Wave a magic wand and arrange the atoms instantly into a filled, capped sixteen-ounce PET bottle, and three materials dominate the cost. Commodity PET resin trades at roughly one dollar per kilogram. A typical empty sixteen-ounce bottle weighs about twenty grams. One kilogram is a thousand grams, so twenty grams is two percent of a kilogram, and two percent of a dollar is two cents of resin.

The polypropylene cap adds another cent or two. Treated municipal water is a rounding error at this scale. A few more cents for energy, labels, and basic handling put a rough magic-wand floor between ten and fifteen cents per finished bottle.

The Baytown bid already lands at eighteen point five cents delivered, close to that floor once real logistics are in the number. Set the stand price of four dollars against that floor and the Idiot Index comes into view. Four dollars divided by fifteen cents is a bit under twenty-seven. Four dollars divided by ten cents is forty.

Against the delivered wholesale figure the multiple is lower still, near the bottom of that band. The implied index therefore sits roughly between twenty and forty. 

That ratio does not come from the plastic or the water. It comes from the sequence of exclusive contracts, the ban on outside beverages, the staffing and permitting overhead of on-site concessions, and the carrying cost of inventory that competing sellers cannot turn over. Each step multiplies the same physical bottle without changing what it is made of.

The Pennsylvania bill’s proposed caps acknowledge the same arithmetic. They set maximums only a few times the wholesale number rather than twenty times that number. Because the waste lives in the contractual and regulatory envelope rather than in the material, shrinking that envelope would move the realized price toward the floor without new chemistry or new bottle designs.

Transportation from bottling plant to park loading dock is already inside the Baytown wholesale number, so it cannot explain the further jump of about twenty times at the stand. Refrigeration and shelf space inside the park add only marginal electricity and labor, measured in pennies per unit.

The dominant multiplier is the contractual barrier that stops any other seller from offering the identical bottle at a lower margin.

A redesign starts by separating the physical delivery of bottled water from the exclusivity payment. One concrete move is to let visitors carry factory-sealed water bought outside the gate. That removes the captive-audience premium without touching the park’s other revenue streams.

A second move would require concession contracts to publish unit prices and cap the markup at a fixed multiple of documented wholesale cost, close to the structure floated in the Pennsylvania legislation. A third would standardize on one widely available bottle size and resin grade so several suppliers could bid the same commodity spec instead of negotiating custom stock-keeping units.

Each step is limited by existing long-term contracts, by insurance and liability rules that currently favor single-vendor control, and by the administrative cost of watching compliance across dozens of venues. The prize is visible in the ratio.

If the Idiot Index could be driven from twenty-plus down to three or four, the same physical bottle would reach the customer at something closer to seventy-five cents instead of four dollars, while still covering the supplier’s documented costs and a reasonable margin.

A fourth step could put mobile refill stations on site, treating municipal water and dispensing it into reusable containers. That would still have to solve hygiene verification and queue management at scale. The hardest constraint remains the multi-year contract cycle. Any change would need to be negotiated at renewal rather than imposed mid-term.

When a simple commodity travels through an exclusive gate, the contract itself becomes the dominant cost component. The bottle does not get heavier, purer, or harder to make between the dock and the stand. What changes is who is allowed to sell it, and what the venue is paid for keeping everyone else out.

Dallas traded a decade of pouring rights across sixty-six facilities for more than a million dollars in payments and splits. Waite Park and Neshoba wrote the same kind of bar on outside drinks. Those payments are real money for a city budget. They are also a toll collected, bottle by bottle, from people who cannot walk back out and buy the identical PET container somewhere else.

Remove that gate, or cap its toll at a modest multiple of delivered cost, and the physical floor reappears as a practical price. The magic-wand estimate already says the atoms are cheap. The Baytown invoice already says a real supplier can land the finished bottle near that floor, logistics included. What keeps the stand price far above both is not a missing invention.

It is a rule, renewed on a calendar, that treats a commodity as if it were a unique service.
