> **Hospitals bill four thousand dollars for an MRI whose installed scanner costs two million.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
A typical 3-tesla MRI scanner reaches a hospital with a turnkey price between 2.8 and 4.5 million dollars once shielding, cooling, and integration are added. The same scanner then produces scans that appear on patient bills at 2,500 to 4,500 dollars each, while the hospital’s own internal cost per scan often sits between 420 and 750 dollars. The difference between those two figures is not explained by the weight of niobium or the price of helium; it is explained by how the machine is bought, housed, scheduled, and marked up. When a center spreads the same capital cost across twelve thousand scans a year, the per-scan share drops to roughly twenty-five dollars, yet most facilities never reach that volume because of how rooms are booked and how referrals arrive. The billed amount therefore reflects layers of financing, facility overhead, and chargemaster pricing that sit on top of the scanner itself.

### Segment 2 — Why It Costs What It Costs Today
Hospitals acquire scanners through capital leases or operating leases that spread payments over seven to ten years and add interest costs of 200,000 to 500,000 dollars per machine. Those leases are chosen because few institutions pay cash for a multi-million-dollar asset whose useful life is measured in a decade or more. Site preparation alone requires a Faraday cage, dedicated electrical service, and chilled-water cooling that together add hundreds of thousands more before the first patient is scanned; each of those elements exists because the superconducting magnet must be isolated from radio-frequency interference and kept at cryogenic temperatures. Once running, the scanner carries annual service contracts of 100,000 to 250,000 dollars plus electricity and occasional helium refills that can reach 80,000 dollars in older systems. The service contract covers parts, labor, and guaranteed uptime, yet it also locks the hospital into the manufacturer’s ecosystem for the life of the lease. Throughput determines how thinly those fixed costs are spread: a center running 12,000 scans a year amortizes the capital at roughly 25 dollars per scan, while one limited to 3,500 scans carries 86 dollars per scan in capital alone. Most U.S. hospitals do not operate at the higher volume because scheduling, staffing, and referral patterns are built around single-machine rooms rather than high-throughput imaging centers. Facility fees, radiologist interpretation, and administrative overhead are then layered on top, and the chargemaster list price is set far above any of those internal costs. The result is a bill that feels routine inside the industry because every participant has adapted to the same financing, regulatory, and scheduling constraints rather than questioning them. One objection sometimes raised is that the magnet itself must be expensive; the sources show, however, that the dominant ongoing expenses after purchase are service contracts, helium logistics, and under-utilized rooms, not the raw cost of the superconducting wire.

### Segment 3 — The Magic Wand Number & The First Principles Ratio
The research sources supplied for this episode list purchase prices, installation costs, and per-scan internal expenses but do not break out the commodity prices or quantities of the raw materials inside the magnet, gradient coils, or electronics. Without those material quantities and prices it is not possible to construct a magic-wand floor or a First Principles Ratio from the given data. What the sources do show is that the dominant cost after the scanner itself is the combination of site work, financing, and low utilization rather than any irreducible physical input. A high-throughput center already drives the capital component of one scan down to 25 dollars; the remaining gap to the billed price therefore sits in scheduling rules, facility overhead, and pricing opacity rather than in the atoms of the machine. An obvious question is whether helium or niobium could still dominate if utilization rose; the sources indicate that newer sealed systems have already reduced helium consumption dramatically, shifting the cost burden further toward the non-material layers. Another objection is that regulatory requirements for shielded rooms are fixed; the portable systems described later demonstrate that those requirements can be bypassed when field strength is lowered, confirming that the present cost structure is not dictated solely by physics.

### Segment 4 — The First-Principles Opportunity
Hyperfine, founded by Jonathan Rothberg, has shipped more than one hundred Swoop portable scanners at roughly 250,000 dollars each, twenty times less than a conventional 1.5-tesla system. The 0.064-tesla device needs no cryogens, no dedicated shielded room, and standard wall power, removing the largest installation and operating expenses that conventional magnets carry. In a remote Canadian hospital the portable unit cut per-patient imaging costs from 26,105 dollars to 9,008 dollars largely by eliminating air transport; the one-year saving for fifty patients reached 854,841 dollars. Those savings arise because the scanner travels to the patient instead of requiring the patient to travel to a fixed installation, which collapses the transport and lodging line items that dominate remote-care budgets. A circular procurement model studied by Vanderbilt and Philips keeps 92 percent of the materials in an existing 3-tesla scanner when it is upgraded on site, trimming total ownership cost by up to 21 percent or 420,000 to 520,000 dollars per machine. The upgrade approach reuses the magnet and gradient hardware while refreshing electronics and software, avoiding the full cost of a new shielded room and new installation. These moves attack the same cost layers: site infrastructure, transport, and one-time replacement cycles. Further progress would require regulatory acceptance of lower-field images for more indications, larger production volumes of the portable systems, and payment models that reward throughput instead of per-scan facility fees. The hard constraints remain image quality for certain diagnoses and the capital still required to manufacture even the simpler magnet; lowering field strength trades some resolution for dramatically lower infrastructure, so the portable route expands access rather than displacing every high-field scanner.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
One concrete change in field strength and cryogen requirement can eliminate an entire category of building work that had been treated as fixed. Another change in ownership model can keep most of the original materials in service instead of sending them to scrap after a decade. The same arithmetic that lowered the cost of portable units could be applied next to the scheduling and billing layers that still multiply the price of every scan. Who will publish the first public ledger that separates the scanner’s material and manufacturing cost from the facility and financing overhead that follows it?
