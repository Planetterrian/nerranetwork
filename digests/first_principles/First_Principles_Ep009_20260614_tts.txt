Diagnostic labs inherited their workflow from centralized hospital pathology departments that were built to handle low volumes of complex cases under strict regulatory oversight. Each sample therefore travels through accessioning, where it is logged and labeled, then aliquoting, where it is split into smaller portions for different machines.

After that comes placement on high-throughput analyzers, quality-control runs that check whether the machines are still accurate that day, pathologist review in ambiguous cases, and finally an electronic report routed through multiple intermediaries before it reaches the ordering physician. Every hand-off adds documentation requirements whose purpose is defensible records rather than lower cost.

Reimbursement rules further entrench the structure. Payers reimburse line items that match established CPT codes, so labs price to the code rather than to the marginal cost of the next test. Capital equipment is amortized over years. The service contracts that keep analyzers calibrated add steady overhead that does not shrink with volume.

Fragmented ordering practices mean most labs still receive specimens in batches that arrive at irregular times. This forces them to maintain excess analyzer capacity and trained staff on standby. The result is a delivered price that feels normal inside the industry even though the variable material cost per test has fallen for decades.

Safety and traceability rules are non-negotiable. Yet they are implemented through layers of process that were designed when sample volumes were smaller and data systems were less capable. The same regulatory framework that prevents errors also prevents rapid substitution of lower-cost equipment or simplified workflows.

In short, the price reflects an accumulated set of choices about risk allocation, reimbursement mechanics, and capital utilization rather than the chemistry itself.

First, the analyzer itself must be purchased, validated, and maintained under CLIA or equivalent rules. Those fixed costs are spread across thousands of tests but still appear in the per-test price. Second, each sample requires trained personnel to verify identity, handle exceptions, and document chain of custody—an activity whose labor minutes do not shrink linearly with automation.

Third, billing and collections consume additional administrative effort because reimbursement pathways differ by payer and often require prior authorization or appeals. Fourth, the regulatory and liability buffer adds another increment. Every test is run with extra controls and retained records whose marginal cost is small but whose cumulative effect on staffing and legal overhead is material.

These layers compound because each one is sized to the risk tolerance of the most conservative participant in the chain rather than to the physics of the reaction.

A redesign that started from the reagent floor would first attack the instrument and workflow together by moving as much of the reaction as possible into a low-cost, single-use cartridge that still meets analytical performance standards.

That move requires demonstrating equivalent accuracy and precision under CLIA-waived or near-waived conditions so that the test can be performed closer to the patient and without the full central-lab overhead. Second, the cartridge would need to be manufactured at volumes that drive the per-unit cost of plastics and reagents toward their commodity floor.

Achieving that scale depends on standardized interfaces that multiple instrument makers could adopt rather than proprietary lock-in. Third, transparent pricing and direct reimbursement for the cartridge itself, rather than bundled lab fees, would remove the administrative layer that currently multiplies the ticket price.

Each of these steps faces genuine constraints. Analytical performance cannot be traded away without creating clinical risk. Regulators rightly demand evidence that simplified devices do not increase false positives or negatives. Supply-chain concentration in high-grade plastics and enzymes could create new bottlenecks if demand surged.

Liability allocation remains difficult when a result is generated outside a traditional laboratory setting. Yet none of these obstacles changes the arithmetic that the physical inputs are already cheap. The prize is therefore the reduction of everything that sits between those inputs and the final report.
