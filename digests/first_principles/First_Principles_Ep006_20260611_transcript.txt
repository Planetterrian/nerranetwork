In the 1950s, a single cargo ship might spend a week or more in port,
while hundreds of longshoremen carried crates, barrels, and veils one by one from truck to hold
and back again. The goods inside those loads were often cheap to produce,
yet the price of getting them from factory to distant market was dominated by that repeated
manual handling. Malcolm McLean, a North Carolina trucker,
looked at the physical reality of freight and asked what would remain if the handling steps
themselves were removed rather than merely sped up.
Brake bulk shipping had evolved over centuries as the accepted method for moving mixed cargo.
Each shipment arrived at the dock in whatever form the originating factory or warehouse had
packed it. Wooding crates, burlap sacks, steel drums, or loose pieces all arrived separately.
Longshoremen transferred those items individually using hooks, nets, and pallets.
The convention assumed that cargo would be consolidated and deconsolidated at every change of transport
mode. That was how ports, ships, and rail yards had always been organized.
Labor unions and port operators built entire economic models around the volume of work this process
created. The number of man hours per ton became the central metric. Distance traveled or fuel burned
mattered less under that view. Under this system, the finished cost of moving a ton of general cargo
across an ocean could easily exceed the value of the raw materials inside the shipment by a large
multiple. The industry treated that ratio as an unavoidable feature of trade. Ship designs
reflected the same assumption. Holds were shaped for easy access by gangs of workers rather than
for rapid mechanical loading. Schedules included generous allowances for the days required to fill
and empty them. Every actor optimized within the existing sequence, faster hooks, better lighting on
the dock, stronger pallets, all improved small parts of the old flow. The underlying requirement that
each piece be touched repeatedly was never questioned. Port time often exceeded sea time. Capital
sat idle and hulls tied to warves. The effective idiot index of freight movement remained high even
though the steel, wood, or cloth being shipped had modest commodity value. McLean encountered this
pattern while watching his own trucks wait at New York Pierce. The delay and expense were accepted
as normal because no one had started from the physics of moving mass from origin to destination without
intermediate unpacking. McLean began with the observation that the dominant cost was not the steel or fuel

The first concrete move was to select a size and corner casting geometry. That geometry allowed a
