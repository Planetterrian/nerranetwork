> **Parsons cut electricity costs by expanding steam through successive blade rows instead of pistons, dropping the Idiot Index sharply by 1900.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1884 a single compact machine turned high-pressure steam into steady rotary power for a dynamo at 18,000 revolutions per minute. Earlier reciprocating engines needed heavy flywheels, multiple cylinders, and constant lubrication to deliver the same electrical output. Parsons reached useful efficiency by letting steam expand through successive rows of precisely angled blades instead of pushing pistons back and forth. The shift replaced hundreds of moving parts with a balanced rotor whose material content was measured in hundreds of pounds while early finished units still cost thousands.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Engineers of the 1880s built electric generators by scaling up the proven reciprocating steam engine that had powered factories and ships for decades. The design started from the assumption that linear motion had to be converted to rotation through cranks, crossheads, and connecting rods. Each added cylinder or valve chest increased weight, friction, and the number of surfaces that could leak or wear. Makers accepted the resulting vibration and speed limits because those were the known costs of reliable power. A typical large reciprocating set for lighting or tramways therefore carried far more iron, brass, and machined surfaces than the electrical load alone required. Maintenance crews expected frequent packing replacements and alignment checks. Capital cost stayed high because every new station repeated the same heavy layout. No one questioned the piston path itself; the question was only how to make the next larger version run a little smoother. The default path kept part counts high because each new feature addressed a symptom rather than the underlying conversion loss. Shops already tooled for piston engines saw little reason to retool for an unproven rotary alternative.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Parsons began with the physical fact that steam expands when heated and can impart momentum to angled surfaces. He calculated that a series of stationary and moving blade rows could extract energy in gradual pressure drops without the velocity limits of a single impulse stage. The first 1884 unit delivered roughly ten horsepower at eighteen thousand rpm while driving a dynamo directly. Material content remained modest because the rotor and casing used nickel-steel forgings whose raw weight was only a fraction of the finished machine price. Early units therefore carried an Idiot Index on the order of ten to twenty, the extra cost arising from the precision machining of hundreds of small blades and the need for perfect rotor balance. The next decisive step was compounding: each additional stage reused the same steam mass at lower pressure, raising overall shaft efficiency toward seventy-five percent in later land turbines. That compounding eliminated the need for separate engines or reduction gears to match generator speeds. Blade angles were refined through repeated tests so that steam left each row with minimal residual swirl, cutting kinetic losses that had been accepted as normal. By 1889 the firm had moved to axial-flow multi-stage rotors that could be lengthened for higher output without proportional growth in diameter or weight. Condenser integration lowered exhaust pressure and therefore the steam volume that had to be handled, shrinking the final blade rows and the foundation mass beneath the set. Each of these changes attacked a concrete source of the original Idiot Index: fewer separate castings, fewer joints that could leak, and rotors that stayed balanced at speed without extra counterweights. The arithmetic is straightforward: if raw nickel-steel and copper for the dynamo amounted to a few hundred pounds while the assembled turbo-generator sold for several thousand, every eliminated stage or simplified mounting directly reduced the ratio. A further refinement replaced radial-flow layouts with longer axial rotors once balancing techniques improved, removing the diameter limit that had forced wider, heavier casings. This move cut machining hours on the largest forgings because the same power came from added length rather than added width. The key trade-off was tighter tip clearances across more stages, which required better metallurgy and shop practice but paid off once those skills existed.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
By 1900 roughly two hundred Parsons sets were at work, many supplying ship lighting and early central stations such as Forth Banks. Steam consumption per kilowatt-hour fell steadily as stage counts rose and blade profiles improved. The new Idiot Index for a mature land turbine sat well below the reciprocating baseline, though still several times the raw-material floor because blading, balancing, and high-speed bearings remained labor-intensive. Trade-offs included the requirement for very pure boiler feed water to avoid blade erosion and the need for precision shops that could hold tight clearances across a long rotor. Those constraints kept adoption fastest in applications where steady high-speed rotation was already valued, such as electricity generation, rather than in every industrial drive. The design still left room for further material reduction once later alloys and manufacturing methods appeared.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A rotor whose successive blade rows turn pressure drop into rotation shows how one physical process can replace an entire assembly of rods and valves. The same principle appears whenever a designer lists every pressure, velocity, and material interface and then removes the ones that exist only because an earlier machine used a different motion. Tomorrow the series continues with another concrete case or an industry still carrying a high Idiot Index; the first signal that someone is trying the same move is a prototype whose part count or material ratio is already lower than the established product on paper.
