The real price of artificial light has fallen roughly ten thousandfold since whale-oil lamps, because each generation measured lumens delivered rather than lamps sold.

In 1800 a typical household paid the equivalent of several days’ wages for the amount of light now supplied by a single L E D bulb costing a few dollars and drawing a few watts.

The difference is not mainly cheaper lamps or cheaper electricity.

It is the cumulative result of redesigning the conversion step itself, from burning fat or oil to passing current through a semiconductor junction that emits photons directly.

Each stage attacked the largest loss term of the previous one.

Heat, incomplete combustion, or Stokes-shifted fluorescence were reduced rather than simply scaling up the old device.

The pattern becomes clearest when the unit of account is shifted from the vessel or the fuel to the photons that actually reach the work surface.

For centuries the dominant method of producing light was to burn some form of fat or oil in an open flame or wick.

The convention was inherited from pre-industrial practice.

Find a combustible material that was already used for heat or cooking, put it in a simple vessel, and accept that most of the energy would leave as heat and infrared rather than visible light.

Candle makers and later whale-oil refiners optimized for reliability and transport, not for photon yield.

The physical limit of a flame was treated as fixed.

Under this regime the cost of usable light remained high because the service, visible photons at the task surface, was never the unit of account.

A typical whale-oil lamp of the 1840s delivered perhaps one to two lumens per watt of chemical energy released, and the fuel itself was expensive to harvest and refine.

Kerosene, introduced after 1859, lowered the fuel cost dramatically but still lost most of its energy to heat and soot.

The lamp design itself changed only incrementally.

When electric incandescent bulbs appeared, manufacturers again started from the existing socket and glass envelope.

They optimized filament life and manufacturing throughput rather than asking how many watts were required to produce a lumen.

The result was a product whose price tracked material and labor costs inside an accepted architecture instead of tracking the physical cost of the photons delivered.

One objection often raised is that earlier societies simply lacked the materials or knowledge to do better.

Yet the same societies rapidly adopted kerosene once its supply chain existed.

This shows that the barrier was not ignorance of chemistry but the absence of a metric that made the waste visible.

Another common view holds that incremental lamp improvements were sufficient.

In practice those tweaks stayed inside the combustion envelope and therefore could not close the gap between chemical energy released and visible photons produced.

Nordhaus’s reconstruction shows that once the service is counted in lumens rather than in lamps or gallons, the price trajectory becomes visible as a series of discrete attacks on the dominant loss mechanism.

The first clear break was the shift from animal oils to kerosene.

The raw material cost per lumen-hour fell because petroleum yielded far more combustible liquid per unit effort than hunting whales, yet the combustion efficiency remained low.

The next break was the incandescent lamp, which replaced chemical energy with electrical energy and allowed the radiating body to be raised to a higher temperature.

Even so, roughly 95 percent of the electrical input still emerged as infrared.

Fluorescent tubes attacked that loss by using a mercury discharge to generate ultraviolet photons that were then down-converted by phosphors.

This raised system efficacy from roughly 15 lm/W to sixty to eighty lm/W.

Each of these steps required a new physical mechanism, not merely a cheaper version of the old one.

The L E D continues the same logic.

An electron-hole recombination in a direct-bandgap semiconductor produces a photon whose energy is set by the bandgap, so the Stokes loss of fluorescence is largely eliminated.

Early high-brightness L E D's reached thirty to fifty lm/W.

Modern phosphor-converted white L E D's exceed 150 lm/W in production devices and approach 200 lm/W in laboratory records.

The magic-wand floor for white light is set by the energy of the photons themselves plus the minimum voltage required to drive the junction.

A rough estimate places the theoretical maximum around two hundred fifty to three hundred lm/W for a broadband white spectrum once all non-radiative paths are removed.

On that basis the Idiot Index of an 1800-era whale-oil system was on the order of several hundred relative to the photon-energy floor.

Consider the arithmetic.

If the floor is roughly 300 lm/W and a whale-oil lamp delivered one to two lm/W, the finished service costs two hundred times or more the raw photon cost.

That is an Idiot Index in the hundreds.

A contemporary L E D system sits perhaps a factor of two to three above the same floor.

At one hundred fifty to two hundred lm/W against a 300 lm/W ceiling the ratio falls to roughly two, already two orders of magnitude closer to the physical limit.

The successive redesigns also changed the supporting infrastructure.

Kerosene needed distillation and distribution.

Incandescents needed generators and wiring.

Fluorescents needed ballasts.

L E D's need only low-voltage drivers and, increasingly, integrated heat sinks.

Each infrastructure change was accepted only after the core conversion efficiency justified the capital outlay.

The data show that the largest drops in cost per lumen preceded the cheapest grid power and were driven instead by the fraction of energy converted to visible light.

Another objection notes that early L E D's were themselves expensive.

The price fell only after the semiconductor process moved from research devices to high-volume wafer fabrication.

This illustrates that the first-principles step must still clear a manufacturing threshold before the Idiot Index can be driven down.

By the early twenty-first century the cost per megalumen-hour had fallen by something like four orders of magnitude from 1800 levels when measured in constant dollars and constant purchasing power.

The remaining gap to the photon-energy limit is real.

Thermal droop, phosphor conversion losses, and driver inefficiency still dissipate roughly half the input power as heat.

Further gains require either narrower-band direct emitters for specific tasks or better thermal and optical extraction.

Both of these are already visible in laboratory devices but not yet at commodity cost.

The trade-off accepted along the way was greater dependence on complex supply chains for rare-earth phosphors and gallium compounds.
