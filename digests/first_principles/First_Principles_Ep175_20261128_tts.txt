At the Cortez mine in Nevada the operators set that low-grade ore on asphalt pads and trickled dilute cyanide solution through the pile. Through the late nineteen sixties the conventional route still began with crushing, then fine grinding in mills, so cyanide could reach the metal particles inside agitated tanks. Mines had long reduced ore to a fine powder so cyanide could dissolve the gold while the solids hung in suspension. That circuit needed ball or rod mills, thickeners, and large volumes of water and power.

Only higher-grade material paid for those costs. Anything below roughly a tenth of an ounce per ton was left in the ground or stockpiled.

The standing assumption was that physical liberation of the gold particles had to come before chemical attack. Decades of milling practice had handed that assumption down, and marginal deposits rarely got a fresh look. Carlin Gold Mining Company still ran its main ore through a full mill circuit even while it tested heap methods on lower-grade open-pit material.

Capital locked in the grinding equipment, and the energy that equipment burned, set a practical floor conventional operations could not get under.

Each stage of size reduction added motors, liners, pumps, and classifiers that had to be maintained and powered without a break. Fine particles needed careful handling so dust did not walk off and so the pulp stayed at the right density for agitation.

Because the entire ore mass moved through those steps, even a modest daily tonnage multiplied the installed horsepower and the volume of water that had to be clarified and recycled. Operators accepted the expense because past practice had shown coarser rock left too much gold locked inside unbroken pieces.

The convention therefore carried the equipment list and the energy budget forward without re-testing whether every size-reduction stage was still necessary once the goal was simply to expose gold surfaces to dilute cyanide.

Bureau of Mines metallurgists asked a different question: what minimum physical work was actually required to bring cyanide into contact with the gold. Laboratory tests on Cortez samples showed that ore crushed only to three-quarter-inch size and stacked in heaps still released about seventy percent of its gold when leached for seven days with dilute sodium cyanide solution.

Pilot heaps at Cortez confirmed that result at larger scale. Mine manager Don Duncan then put run-of-mine cutoff ore on prepared pads. No ball mills, no classifiers, and no counter-current decantation circuit were built for that tonnage.

The Bureau sources record labor, equipment, and reagent quantities for the heap operation. They do not report the dollar cost of the conventional milling route, and they do not report the precise reagent and pumping energy that would constitute a raw-material floor.

The magic wand number is what the finished thing would cost if you paid only for the raw materials — here, the cyanide and the energy to move solution through rock, with every mill and thickener waved away. That is a gap in the measurement, not a verdict on the people who built either circuit.

What the sources do show is the removal of one entire class of equipment and the energy and maintenance that class carried. The key test was whether solution could percolate through the coarser fragments fast enough to reach most of the exposed gold surfaces inside a practical time.

Holding the ore at a three-quarter-inch top size meant the operators never paid the power draw of the final grinding stages that would have turned the rock into a slurry. They also never paid the capital cost of the thickeners and filters that normally separate pregnant solution from finely ground solids. Recovery sat below what a full mill circuit could achieve on the same ore.

The capital that was not spent is what let the mine treat material that would otherwise have stayed in the ground.

One obvious objection is that skipping fine grinding must leave some gold particles coated or enclosed. The sources acknowledge that heap leaching works best on oxidized, non-refractory ores, where the gold is already accessible along fractures. Another objection is that solution losses or channeling could waste reagent.

The Bureau reports note that operators learned to manage percolation by building the heaps properly and by adding agglomeration steps when fines were present. The redesign therefore worked because the ore at Cortez already met the physical condition that allowed direct contact without prior ultra-fine size reduction.

The decision sequence ran from laboratory confirmation, to pilot heaps, to full-scale placement, and each step took another piece out of the inherited milling train instead of copying that train at a smaller scale.

Heap leaching at Carlin and Cortez brought previously sub-economic ore into production with far lower installed capital than a new mill circuit would have required. The material that moved was the zero-point-zero-seven-ounce-per-ton ore. The method spread to other low-grade deposits in the nineteen seventies because the capital barrier had dropped.

It could not treat refractory sulfide ores that still needed roasting or pressure oxidation, and solution losses or poor percolation remained operational problems at some sites. Reagent consumption and pumping energy still set a physical floor, but the sources give no measured value for it.

Suitable pad foundations and solution containment added costs of their own, though those costs were smaller than the grinding plant that was avoided. Over time the industry added carbon adsorption and other refinements. The core simplification — skipping fine grinding for suitable ores — stayed in place.

The gold still locked inside unbroken fragments is the gap that remains, and no amount of heap redesign recovers that gold without bringing some form of size reduction back.
