Farmers and implement makers had long treated harvesting as a task performed by teams of men
with handheld tools.
Each crew member cut a narrow swath, bound the stocks by hand, and moved forward in coordinated
steps that had changed little for centuries. The convention assumed that grain must be gathered in
small, careful increments because any mechanical substitute would damage the crop or jam in uneven
prairie soil. Local blacksmiths built or repaired size and cradles to fit individual users, so every
tool carried the cost of hand-fitting and personal adjustment. That approach kept output low and
labor high. A typical crew might clear only a few acres in a long day. The finished cost
of wheat therefore included the wages, food and lodging of many workers plus the time
lost to weather before the grain could be brought in. No one questioned the underlying assumption
that harvesting remained an artisanal process best left to human rhythm and local craftsmanship.
The baseline price of grain reflected that labor intensity rather than any physical limit
