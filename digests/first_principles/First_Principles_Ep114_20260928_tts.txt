The difference between those two figures does not come from the atoms themselves.

It arises from how the industry chooses to assemble, finance, and price the space.

Developers and operators treat each hotel as a unique project shaped by local zoning, financing packages, and brand standards.

Construction crews work on site with conventional methods that have changed little in decades.

Foundations are poured in place, walls are framed with dimensional lumber or steel studs, and mechanical systems are installed piece by piece.

Every room receives custom millwork, individually specified lighting, and finishes chosen to match the chain’s design language.

Those choices drive long permitting timelines and repeated inspections.

Supply chains remain fragmented, with lumber, drywall, carpet, and fixtures arriving from separate vendors on schedules that rarely align.

Labor is paid by the hour on site rather than in a controlled factory setting.

A redesign would begin by moving as much of the room as possible into a factory setting where dimensional tolerances and sequencing can be controlled.

Standardized modules built around a steel or concrete cassette could be stacked on a prepared foundation.

That shift would cut months from the schedule and reduce weather and inspection delays.

Interior finishes would shift from site-built millwork to interchangeable panels produced in volume.

That change would lower both material waste and skilled-labor hours.

Mechanical systems would be pre-plumbed and pre-wired inside the modules so that only simple connections remain on site.

Updated building codes would need to accept factory-certified assemblies.

Financing structures would need to reward speed rather than drawn-out construction loans.

Operators would need to accept a narrower set of room configurations in exchange for lower capital cost.

The hardest constraints are regulatory and financial rather than technical.
