DuPont chemists began with the atoms required for a long-chain polyamide. They started by identifying exactly what atoms had to be linked together to produce the mechanical properties of a sheer, elastic fiber.

That meant specifying two six-carbon molecules, one carrying acid groups at the ends and the other carrying amine groups at the ends, so the two could react repeatedly to form the repeating amide bonds that give the chain its strength and flexibility.

The magic-wand floor for those molecules started with commodity benzene from coal or petroleum, ammonia from the Haber process, and oxygen from air. Benzene sold for a few cents per pound at the time. Ammonia carried a similarly low price. Air supplied the oxygen at essentially the cost of compression alone.

Even after synthesizing adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine from those inputs, the resulting polymer resin sat at a small fraction of the price of reeled silk.

The Idiot Index for a silk stocking therefore sat high. The finished garment carried every layer of sericulture, reeling, shipping, and hand knitting, while the atoms inside the thread represented only a modest share of that total price.

One way to see the gap is to notice how many separate operations had to be paid for before any thread reached a knitting needle: leaf harvesting, worm rearing, cocoon sorting, degumming, twisting, ocean transport, and then the final knitting itself.

Each of those steps added labor and loss that had nothing to do with the carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen atoms that ultimately formed the fiber.

The first decisive step was a continuous reactor that converted the intermediates into molten polyamide under controlled temperature and pressure. This eliminated batch isolation and purification steps that would have multiplied handling.

Instead of stopping the reaction, isolating the intermediate solids, cleaning equipment between batches, and then redissolving or remelting material, the process kept the growing chains in the melt and moved them straight forward.

Temperature had to be held within a narrow window so the polymer reached the right molecular weight without branching or discoloration, and catalyst residues had to stay below levels that would later cause spinneret blockages.

The second step was melt-spinning. The polymer was extruded directly through spinnerets whose hole diameter set filament thickness, then drawn to align the molecules and build strength. That single operation replaced the multi-stage reeling and twisting required for silk.

In the silk process, cocoons had to be softened in hot water, filaments located and collected by hand or machine, several filaments twisted together for usable denier, and the resulting yarn wound and rewound multiple times to remove defects.

The melt-spinning route produced a continuous filament whose diameter was fixed by the spinneret orifice and whose orientation was set by the draw ratio applied immediately after extrusion, removing those intermediate winding and twisting machines entirely.

The third step tuned polymerization degree and draw ratio so one filament could be knitted into sheer fabric without the multiple plies silk needed. Fewer ends per inch cut both knitting-machine time and total yarn consumption.

Because the nylon filament already possessed sufficient tensile strength after drawing, knitters no longer had to feed several fine strands through each needle to prevent breakage.

That reduction in ends per inch lowered the number of yarn packages that had to be loaded, the number of tension devices that had to be maintained, and the total length of yarn that passed through the machine for each square meter of fabric.

Each of these changes removed a distinct cost layer that the silkworm model had treated as fixed. The engineering task was to hold temperature and catalyst purity tight enough for uniform flow and no degradation in the spinneret. Once solved, capacity grew by adding spinnerets and larger reactors rather than more farms.

One might wonder whether the new fiber could still deliver the drape and recovery that silk provided through its protein structure. The chemists addressed that by selecting a polymer whose hydrogen bonding delivered elasticity comparable to silk once the chains were oriented during drawing. Another objection is that coal and ammonia still required upstream processing.

Yet those routes were already amortized across many products, whereas silk's dedicated biological infrastructure existed only for silk.

For centuries the industry treated the silkworm as an irreplaceable reactor. Farmers in Japan and China raised the insects, collected cocoons, and sold reeled thread to weavers and knitters who accepted the biological limits as given. Each added step—leaf cultivation, cocoon sorting, degumming, twisting—layered labor and loss.

The final yarn stayed scarce enough that one pair of sheer stockings could equal several days of factory wages. Manufacturers refined reeling machines and dye uptake, yet they never questioned whether protein fiber must come from an insect. When trade tensions in the 1930s cut supply, prices rose instead of prompting a search for different atoms.

Handlers guarded against worm disease, sorted cocoons for quality, and ran multiple degumming baths. Shipping added further cost because delicate filaments could not be compressed or exposed to moisture. Knitters then combined several fine strands for usable strength, multiplying the ends that passed through each needle.

Once reactor and spinneret capacity reached steady output, nylon stockings sold for a few dollars. The supply-chain part count fell from cocoon handling, reeling, twisting, and ocean shipping to chemical synthesis, polymerization, and direct spinning.

The new Idiot Index dropped because dominant expenses now sat in the chemical plant and knitting machines rather than in variable biological feedstock. Limits remained. The polymer still required precise moisture control during drawing. Early nylon yellowed under sunlight until stabilizers were added.

Some optical properties of silk stayed unmatched, which is why certain high-end garments kept silk for decades. The mass market shifted once nylon proved durable enough for repeated wear and washing.

A fiber whose price had been set by the seasonal rhythms of insect farming revealed its true cost only when the same atoms were assembled in a continuous chemical line. Scale in the reactor and the spinneret matters more than incremental improvement of the old biological steps.

The same logic that turned coal and air into stockings now invites anyone facing a scarce natural input to list the required atoms first and then design the shortest industrial path between them. What material now treated as a finished agricultural product could be rebuilt the same way?
