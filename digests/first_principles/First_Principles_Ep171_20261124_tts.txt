Periodic kilns once ran a loop of loading, firing, cooling, and unloading, and that loop burned far more fuel and labor for each batch of bricks than the clay itself required. A tunnel kiln replaced those cycles with cars that carried the ware steadily through a fixed hot zone. Source 5 recorded that coal figure for one operating installation under continuous firing.

The rest of the story is what that steady flow changed, and what it left standing.

Brick and tile makers had long copied the periodic kiln that had served the craft for centuries. Each kiln was loaded with green ware, sealed, fired to vitrification temperature, allowed to cool, and emptied before the next cycle began.

The working assumption was that the clay needed a complete thermal cycle in one enclosed space, and that any departure from that pattern would risk uneven heating or structural failure. Labor crews therefore did the same heavy loading and unloading in concentrated bursts, then sat through long idle stretches.

Fuel was burned not only to reach peak temperature but also to reheat the entire kiln structure after every cooldown.

Source 5 records that this batch method produced markedly higher coal consumption than the later continuous alternative. The same pattern held for tile plants, where refractory setters and kiln furniture had to be rearranged for every firing.

Because the method was familiar and required no new capital layout beyond the kiln itself, most operators accepted the repeated heating losses and the irregular labor demand as unavoidable features of the craft. The convention locked in both higher fuel use and higher man-hours per thousand bricks.

Operators also faced a practical constraint: each cooldown left the kiln walls radiating stored heat into the surrounding air, with no way to recover it for the next load. Crews had to wait through the full cool-down before they could safely enter and begin the next round of setting, which stretched the calendar time between usable outputs.

The periodic approach treated the kiln as a single vessel that must be restarted from ambient temperature for every batch, an assumption rooted in centuries of brick-making practice rather than in any calculation of the minimum energy the clay itself required. Smaller yards absorbed the resulting peaks in fuel purchases and workforce scheduling as normal operating cost.

Engineers who examined the process asked what the physics actually required: enough heat delivered to the clay to drive off water, burn out organics, and reach vitrification, plus the cost of the cars and refractory that carry the ware. What Source 5 does show is that periodic kilns required substantially more coal per thousand bricks because each cycle reheated the kiln walls and lost heat during cooldown. The redesign attacked that repeated reheating step first. By keeping the hot zone fixed and moving the cars, the kiln structure stayed at temperature continuously, so the energy spent on reheating dropped out.

The second move was to standardize car loading so that setters performed the same sequence every shift rather than in irregular surges. Labor converted from batch peaks to a steady flow.

Source 1 notes that Japanese firms such as Toyo Toki adopted Dressler-type tunnel kilns in nineteen twenty precisely because the continuous schedule improved fuel consistency and output predictability.

Later Japanese builders shortened the tunnel length to twenty to twenty-five metres in the nineteen thirties, cutting the capital tied up in the structure while preserving the continuous heat balance. Each of these steps moved cost away from repeated thermal cycling and toward the irreducible heat of vitrification plus the cars that never left the system.

One practical objection is whether steady movement through a fixed temperature profile could produce the same vitrification uniformity that a sealed batch kiln achieved by holding every brick at peak temperature for the same dwell time.

The continuous design met that objection by dividing the tunnel into distinct zones — preheat, firing, and controlled cooling — so that each car experienced a repeatable temperature-time curve as it advanced at constant speed. Another objection concerns the refractory cars themselves: they must survive hundreds of passes without warping or spalling, which adds their own material cost.

The tunnel-kiln engineers accepted that cost because the cars remained inside the thermal system rather than being cooled and reheated with every batch. The largest variable energy loss — reheating the kiln walls — left the equation. What remained was the heat absorbed by the clay and the modest heat carried away by the exiting cars.

Against periodic kilns, the continuous tunnel kiln produced documented fuel economies and steadier labor demand. Labor per thousand bricks fell because loading and unloading became a regular shift task rather than a concentrated campaign. The method improved the ratio relative to batch firing but did not reach the theoretical floor set by vitrification heat alone.

The largest remaining costs sit in the refractory cars that must withstand repeated passages through the hot zone, and in the land and capital required to install a long tunnel in the first place. Source 1 observes that smaller Japanese producers often found the upfront investment prohibitive even after the efficiency gains were clear. The cars themselves became a recurring maintenance item.

Their replacement schedule set a practical limit on how far the energy savings could be pushed without a further redesign of the car refractories.

When the largest energy loss in a process comes from reheating the same vessel after every cycle, the vessel is the thing that should be made continuous, not the product moving through it. The same logic sits wherever a thermal or chemical step is repeated inside equipment that must be restarted.
