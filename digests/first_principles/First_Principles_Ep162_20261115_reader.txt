Earthworks, concrete, steel rail, overhead electrification and signalling all trade at comparable world prices. Researchers tracking dozens of projects worldwide trace most of the spread in delivered totals to how each country organises the work, not to differences in the ground.

The split appears before any track is laid, in the sequence of studies, approvals and contract interfaces that must be finished first.

High-speed rail is assembled from civil-engineering steps that have been executed for decades. In most countries the process begins with route selection, environmental reviews, land acquisition and a sequence of design reviews involving multiple agencies. Each review layer adds studies, consultants and revised drawings.

Procurement then splits the work among separate contractors for earthworks, structures, track, power and signalling, and each contract carries its own contingencies and interfaces.

In the United Kingdom these steps have multiplied. The Financial Times analysis of HS2 data shows that contractor margins, environmental mitigations and successive planning approvals together account for the largest share of the recorded premium.

Spain kept the same sequence inside a single public body, Adif, which maintained its own engineering staff and issued standard drawings for viaducts, tunnels and track forms. The Transit Costs Project final report notes that countries maintaining in-house capacity and a small number of standard designs avoid the repeated redesign cycles that inflate soft costs elsewhere.

Every added study or interface meeting carries salaries, office overhead and schedule delay, so the cumulative effect appears in the final bill even when the concrete and steel quantities remain similar.

One objection sometimes raised is that Britain faces denser population and stricter environmental rules. The same sources show that Spain completed lines through comparable terrain and protected areas while keeping the number of separate sign-off bodies low.

The difference therefore lies less in the rules themselves than in whether those rules are administered through one technical agency or through successive layers of consultants and local authorities.

When each layer must produce its own risk register and contingency allowance, the allowances compound before construction begins. The calendar from authorisation to first pour grows longer, which raises financing costs and exposes the project to inflation in materials and labour even though the physical quantities per kilometre stay roughly constant.

The magic wand number is what a thing would cost if you could arrange the raw atoms into the finished object for free and pay only for the materials. That is the theoretical floor. The First Principles Ratio is the finished price divided by that floor. Near one, the thing is already close to the physical limit.

A large ratio means most of the price is design, process, organisation or rules, which is where a from-scratch rethink has room to work. The ratio says where the price sits. It is not a verdict on the people who built the thing.

The sources supplied for this episode do not contain the commodity prices, material quantities or labour-hour estimates needed to construct that floor for a kilometre of high-speed line. Without those inputs, no First Principles Ratio can be calculated from the record.

What the same sources do document is the size of the observed cost gap and the institutional steps that produce it. The Ineco analysis of the Transit Costs Project database places Spain's weighted average at seventeen point seven million euros per kilometre in twenty twenty-two prices, while the corresponding figure for the rest of the sample sits at forty-five point five million euros.

Divide forty-five point five by seventeen point seven and the wider sample lands a bit above two and a half times the Spanish figure, for a kilometre whose earth, concrete and steel are bought in the same world market.

The Transit Costs Project attributes the bulk of these premiums to three recurring factors: the number of separate agencies that must sign off, the absence of reusable standard designs, and the reliance on external consultants rather than retained public-sector engineers.

Each factor lengthens the calendar between authorisation and construction start, raising financing and escalation costs that compound before any concrete is poured.

Because the physical inputs themselves are internationally traded, the documented divergence sits almost entirely in these organisational layers rather than in the price of steel or cement.

Differences in labour rates or ground conditions are an obvious place to look for the spread. The data show, however, that the largest premiums appear in soft-cost categories, planning, legal and management overhead, rather than in the direct cost of earthworks or structures.

When the same earthmoving equipment and concrete mixes are available worldwide, the price gap traces to how many times the design is redrawn and how many separate profit margins are stacked on top of one another.

Spain's Ministry of Transport and Adif have already demonstrated one workable path. They retained a core engineering group that produced a family of standard viaduct and tunnel sections, reduced the number of external approvals required for each segment, and let the same teams move from one line to the next without re-learning the specification each time.

The Ineco report records that this continuity kept average costs inside a narrow band across ten projects whose lengths ranged from under one hundred kilometres to more than four hundred kilometres.

A comparable redesign elsewhere would begin by consolidating approval authority into a single technical agency, publishing and freezing a set of standard drawings, and rebuilding in-house estimating and supervision capacity so that consultants are used only for specialised tasks. Those three moves would shrink the largest measured premiums identified in the Transit Costs Project case studies.

The remaining hard constraints are political. Land acquisition still requires public consent, environmental rules still impose real limits, and any new agency must still answer to elected officials. None of those constraints disappears, yet the Spanish record shows they can be met at far lower cost once the organisational overhead is trimmed.

The first projects built under such a regime would still face site-specific adjustments, but the volume of unique engineering would drop sharply once the baseline sections are frozen.

Standard designs lose their advantage the moment every project is forced to renegotiate them with a fresh set of agencies. The advantage was never a cleverer viaduct on the page.

It was permission to reuse a section already accepted, so the next segment does not hire another round of consultants to redraw it, does not open another contract interface, and does not stack another margin on a cross-section the last line already built.

Force that renegotiation and the standard becomes the starting point of the same soft-cost stack: planning, legal work, management overhead, a fresh risk register, a fresh contingency. Those allowances compound before the first pour, and the tonnes of concrete per kilometre barely have to change for the bill to move.

Retaining a permanent engineering staff inside the delivery body is what keeps the reuse real. The staff's accumulated drawings turn into an asset the agency already holds, rather than an expense paid again each time an outside team is assembled to learn the specification from the beginning. A consultant brought in for a specialised task can still be the right call.

A consultant brought in to rediscover a track form the public engineers already hold is the repeated expense that arrangement exists to stop. The drawings only cheapen the next kilometre if the people who know them are still inside the body that lets the contracts.

A further step is to publish those standard drawings as binding requirements rather than as optional guidance, so that each new line does not reopen the same design debates. Optional guidance leaves every ordinary viaduct, tunnel and track form free to be argued again, and an argument that must be had again is where the layers grow back.

A requirement closes that argument and leaves the constraints that were always real standing where they belong: consent for the land, the limits in the environmental rules, and an agency that still answers to elected officials.

The first concrete signal that another country is attempting the same shift would be the publication of a single binding technical manual that subsequent lines are required to follow without new environmental or planning overlays. Will that manual appear, and will the next authorised line actually be bound by it?
