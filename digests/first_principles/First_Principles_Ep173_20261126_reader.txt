Phased-array antennas once reached homes only through military contracts priced in the tens of thousands. SpaceX set out to put one on every subscriber’s roof at consumer scale, and the path there was a change in how the radio was built, not a change in what the radio does.

Defense contractors built phased arrays by assembling hundreds of discrete transmit-receive modules. Each module held its own amplifiers, phase shifters, and control circuits. Volumes stayed low, so every module was hand-tuned and tested to military specifications. The same small-batch methods and ruggedized parts carried over from radar programs, and governments accepted the bill.

Nobody questioned the part count or the supply chain.

A typical legacy array therefore held far more connectors, cables, and enclosures than beam steering itself required. SpaceX vice president Jonathan Hofeller later described that starting build cost as the inherited assumption that electronic beam steering was a low-volume, high-reliability specialty. The industry had never been pressed to treat the antenna as a television-scale product.

Even commercial satellite terminals kept the module-by-module build, because the design teams came from the defense side and reused the old qualification processes.

Connectors between modules added mass and failure points. Those interfaces stayed, because changing them meant requalifying the whole assembly under existing procurement rules. The finished price was assembly labor, repeated testing, and low-volume sourcing, far more than the radio-frequency physics underneath.

SpaceX engineers started from what the antenna must physically do: generate and steer a beam with radio-frequency energy across a flat panel. The magic wand number would be the cost of the silicon, the copper, and the laminates alone, if the atoms could be arranged into a finished terminal for free.

The sources do not carry those commodity prices, so there is no floor to add up, and no finished price to divide by it. The First Principles Ratio cannot be derived here. What can be walked is the part count and the sequence that made the terminal.

STMicroelectronics co-designed BiCMOS chips that pulled radio-frequency functions, previously spread across several discrete devices, onto single dies. More than five billion of those chips have shipped since the collaboration began around twenty fifteen. Panel-level packaging replaced individual chip-scale packages and allowed daily deliveries exceeding five million units.

At the Bastrop, Texas plant, SpaceX added its own printed-circuit-board and panel-level packaging lines, including a two-hundred-eighty-million-dollar expansion announced in March twenty twenty-five. Final assembly and test came under one roof instead of boards routing through separate vendors. Each consolidation took out connectors, cut board layers, and shortened test time.

The physics of electronic beam steering did not change. The number of physical interfaces changed, and so did the scale.

One step moved the design from separate front-end modules to integrated dies that handle both amplification and phase shifting on the same substrate. That removed inter-module cabling and the impedance-matching hardware that went with it.

Another step put panel-level packaging at STMicroelectronics facilities in France, Malta, and Malaysia, so chips could be processed in larger formats before singulation, with fewer handling steps per die. SpaceX then brought circuit-board population and terminal integration into Bastrop, so a board no longer traveled between vendors for each successive layer or test.

The trade-off was real. The single-die and single-panel approach still had to meet the thermal and reliability targets that discrete modules had met with extra shielding and connectors. The engineering therefore went into the BiCMOS process itself, not into more external protection.

Once those process improvements held at volume, production above twenty thousand terminals a day let the capital cost of the Bastrop expansion spread across that output, and further automation became economical. Picture a consumer factory turning out a phased array, not a radar shop building one.

Manufacturing cost passed through thirteen hundred dollars in early twenty twenty-one and later sat below the five-hundred-ninety-nine-dollar sale price. Jonathan Hofeller credited the repeated production iterations for that change. By September twenty twenty-three the company no longer subsidized terminals.

Limits remain. The BiCMOS process depends on a single European chip supplier, and the Bastrop lines stay economical only if volume continues. Higher-end maritime and aviation terminals still carry different price points, and those prices reflect added environmental hardening rather than the core beam-forming cost.

The chip flow from STMicroelectronics’ European fabs is now tight enough that a disruption there would hit terminal output immediately. The in-house packaging lines also need sustained demand to justify a three-year build-out of an additional one million square feet, so the cost reduction is tied to subscriber growth as much as to any one design change.

What travels is a test you can run on the next product, not a verdict on this one. Ask which costs exist only because the buyer was too small to order the function as one piece and the factory as one roof. If the answer is most of them, the physics can stay as it is and the price can still move. That test has not been run on most flat panels still made in small lots.

It starts the day a single buyer is willing to underwrite both the die and the line. Which other flat-panel device now built in small lots will first cross that threshold?
