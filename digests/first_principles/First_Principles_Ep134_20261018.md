> **Three hundred fifty two dollars bought an RCA television set in nineteen forty-six.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
RCA introduced its Model 630TS black-and-white television in October 1946 at a list price of three hundred fifty two dollars. The set contained thirty tubes including the picture tube, a wooden cabinet, and a thirteen-channel tuner. Within a few years the same basic chassis design, produced in volume by RCA and its licensees, helped pull average factory prices down to one hundred eighty nine dollars by 1949. That drop came from treating the receiver as an assembly of repeatable parts rather than a one-off piece of furniture.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Before the war, television receivers existed mainly as laboratory or demonstration units. Manufacturers built each set by hand, selecting tubes, wiring harnesses, and cabinets to match whatever picture tube happened to be on hand. After 1946 the same habit continued: every new model started from a fresh chassis layout, a custom wooden cabinet, and whatever tuners and power supplies the engineering team chose that month. RCA’s 630TS itself used thirty tubes and was heavy for a tabletop set because each stage was designed for maximum performance in isolation rather than for shared production tooling. The result was high material variety, long assembly times, and prices that stayed near or above three hundred dollars even as demand rose. Licensees copied the pattern, each adding its own cabinet style and minor circuit tweaks. No one questioned the assumption that a television was essentially a piece of furniture whose cost would be set by cabinet finish and dealer margins. Consequently the industry treated three hundred dollars as the natural starting point rather than a number that could be driven down by standardization. The wooden cabinet alone carried labor for cutting, staining, and fitting that varied with every retailer’s order. Hand-wired point-to-point connections inside the chassis required skilled technicians to route each resistor and capacitor individually, with no two sets guaranteed to align identically on the first try. This approach locked in repeated engineering hours for what was essentially the same broadcast signal path repeated across brands.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
RCA engineers instead asked what minimum set of circuits could produce a usable picture and sound from the broadcast signal. They settled on a single chassis, the KCS-20, that could be tooled once and then used across dozens of cabinet styles by different manufacturers. The thirty-tube count remained high, yet the parts inside those tubes—resistors, capacitors, and coils—were now purchased against a single bill of materials rather than redesigned for every model. Turret tuners replaced separate channel strips, cutting the number of unique RF assemblies that had to be stocked and aligned. High-volume picture-tube plants, once the wartime capacity for radar tubes was redirected, lowered the cost of the single most expensive component. By 1950 RCA announced a sixteen-inch table model that had carried a list near five hundred dollars the previous year; the new price was two hundred ninety nine dollars and ninety five cents. The same chassis continued to appear in sets from other makers because the electrical design no longer changed with every cabinet. Each of these steps attacked a different layer of the original cost stack: shared tooling reduced engineering hours, common parts reduced purchasing overhead, and larger tube production reduced the price of the display itself. The design still required thirty tubes and a wooden enclosure, yet the arithmetic of volume manufacturing began to dominate over the arithmetic of custom furniture. A rough magic-wand estimate for the raw materials—glass for the picture tube, copper wire, steel chassis stampings, and plywood—sits well below fifty dollars even allowing generous commodity prices of the era. Dividing the three-hundred-fifty-two-dollar finished price by that floor produces an Idiot Index above seven, showing that most of the cost sat in repeated design work and low-volume assembly rather than in the atoms themselves. When the KCS-20 chassis was licensed, the same resistor and capacitor values could be ordered in lots measured in the hundreds of thousands, dropping unit price through simple quantity discounts. Alignment fixtures built for one run served every licensee, eliminating the need to recalibrate test equipment for each new cabinet variant. The turret tuner itself consolidated thirteen separate coil sets into a single rotating drum, removing the labor of installing and shielding individual channel strips while also cutting the number of solder joints that could fail later. These moves did not reduce the tube count or eliminate the wooden cabinet, but they attacked the duplication that had kept the Idiot Index elevated. The key engineering trade-off was accepting a fixed IF bandwidth and tube complement that worked adequately across all thirteen VHF channels rather than optimizing each set for marginal picture improvements that most viewers would never notice.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
Average factory prices fell from one hundred eighty nine dollars in 1949 to roughly one hundred seventy eight dollars in the first ten months of 1950. Retail prices followed, with table models appearing below two hundred dollars in some markets by the early nineteen fifties. The Idiot Index of the original 630TS—finished price divided by the cost of its raw tubes, wire, and lumber—remained high because thirty separate tubes still had to be inserted, wired, and aligned by hand. Further reduction would have required replacing multiple tubes with fewer, more integrated devices or moving to printed wiring boards, steps that came later. The wooden cabinet also stayed expensive relative to its material content because each set was still assembled and finished one at a time. The 630TS approach proved that standardization across manufacturers could compress cost faster than any single firm could achieve alone, yet it left the fundamental part count and cabinet labor largely untouched. Licensees still ordered cabinets in small batches matched to local tastes, so the finishing labor never scaled the way the electronics did. The thirty-tube architecture itself set a ceiling on how far the Idiot Index could fall without a different circuit topology.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A chassis that stayed electrically identical while dozens of companies changed only the wood around it showed how much of the original price lived in duplicated engineering effort rather than in the signal path itself. Once the broadcast standard and the minimum tube complement were fixed, the remaining variable was how many times the same layout would be built. The same pattern appears whenever a new device moves from demonstration unit to commodity: the first question is which functions must be performed, and the second is how many times the same physical arrangement can be repeated before anyone redesigns it. Who will treat today’s custom assemblies as tomorrow’s shared chassis?
