Publishers begin with artwork and graphic design that must be licensed or commissioned, then move to small-batch printing runs that rarely exceed a few thousand copies. Each run carries setup charges for plates, dies, and color matching that stay fixed regardless of quantity.

Retailers add shelf fees, return allowances, and distribution markups that compound before the box even reaches the customer. Warehousing and inventory carrying costs rise because demand is seasonal and titles compete for limited shelf space. Rule books require professional editing and layout to meet expectations for clarity and illustration.

Component counts often include dozens of distinct token shapes and colors, each needing its own mold or cutting die. Small studios cannot amortize these fixed costs across millions of units the way a mass-market toy maker can. The result is a finished price that feels normal inside the industry because every participant has inherited the same sequence of steps.

No single actor sees an incentive to redesign the entire chain when margins already cover existing overhead. The baseline therefore remains thirty to sixty dollars at retail for a product whose physical contents are simple.

Printing plates and die-cutting tools are amortized over short runs, so each copy carries a disproportionate share of setup. Retail distribution layers on wholesale margins, store placement fees, and return reserves that together can exceed the material cost several times over.

Warehousing and transportation add further increments because games move in small lots rather than continuous high-volume flow. The gap therefore lives in the sequence of design, licensing, batch production, and channel markups rather than in any exotic material.

A high Idiot Index in this setting signals that the current process is far from the physical limit and that redesigning the production and distribution steps could close much of the distance.

One possible objection is that the floor estimate ignores quality differences between commodity stock and the coated, printed boards actually used; even doubling the material allowance still leaves the index well above ten.

A redesign would begin by standardizing component shapes across many titles so that the same molds and cutting dies could serve multiple games. That change would require publishers to coordinate on token geometries and card sizes, something that has happened in other commodity categories but not yet here.

Next would come longer print runs achieved by grouping titles or by shifting to print-on-demand systems that eliminate plate changes between jobs. Such systems would need reliable demand signals and perhaps direct-to-consumer channels that bypass traditional retail fees.

Third would be simplification of rule presentation so that core mechanics fit on a single folded sheet, reducing both paper and translation costs for international releases. Each of these moves would require either industry-wide standards or new scale that individual studios cannot achieve alone.

The hard constraints are the need for distinctive artwork to drive sales and the regulatory reality that each new title still requires its own intellectual-property protection.
