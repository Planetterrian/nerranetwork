> **McCormick's mechanical reaper sold for one hundred fifty dollars but contained under fifty dollars worth of iron and wood.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
Before the mechanical reaper, harvesting wheat on the prairie meant crews swinging scythes across a field for days. Cyrus McCormick's 1831 machine cut the same grain at roughly seven times the speed. The finished device sold for one hundred fifty dollars once production scaled, yet the iron, wood, and castings inside it amounted to well under fifty dollars at commodity prices. That gap between the cost of the raw materials and the price paid by farmers came from the accumulated labor of hand fitting every component to the next.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Farmers and implement makers had long treated harvesting as a task performed by teams of men with handheld tools. Each crew member cut a narrow swath, bound the stalks by hand, and moved forward in coordinated steps that had changed little for centuries. The convention assumed that grain must be gathered in small, careful increments because any mechanical substitute would damage the crop or jam in uneven prairie soil. Local blacksmiths built or repaired scythes and cradles to fit individual users, so every tool carried the cost of hand fitting and personal adjustment. That approach kept output low and labor high; a typical crew might clear only a few acres in a long day. The finished cost of wheat therefore included the wages, food, and lodging of many workers plus the time lost to weather before the grain could be brought in. No one questioned the underlying assumption that harvesting remained an artisanal process best left to human rhythm and local craftsmanship. The baseline price of grain reflected that labor intensity rather than any physical limit on cutting speed. Implement makers copied existing hand-tool designs and simply made them larger or added minor attachments, never asking what the raw motion of severing stalks actually required. Because each season's crew had to be assembled, fed, and housed again, the expense compounded year after year without any path to reduce the number of people required. The scythe itself remained a simple forged blade because the surrounding social and economic system rewarded incremental sharpening of familiar methods over any redesign of the motion. When early experimenters tried larger versions of the same hand tools, they still relied on the same crew coordination, so the fundamental labor multiplier stayed in place.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
McCormick began with the physical action needed: a blade moving through standing grain at a consistent height and speed. The magic-wand floor for such a device was a few hundred pounds of cast iron for the frame and cutting apparatus plus seasoned wood for the platform and drive wheels, together valued at under fifty dollars in the commodity markets of the time. The conventional hand-built reaper or improved scythe rig carried an Idiot Index well above three because the selling price reflected repeated hand fitting and small-batch assembly rather than the material itself. McCormick's redesign attacked that gap through interchangeable parts so that any component could be produced in volume and swapped without custom filing. Regional assembly plants later placed final fitting closer to the farms that bought the machines, cutting the cost of shipping partially completed units and the labor of correcting misalignments after long transport. The cutting bar itself used a reciprocating sickle driven by a simple gear train from the wheels, eliminating the need for separate teams to push or pull the device. Each consolidation removed hours of bench work that had previously been required to match one-of-a-kind castings. The drive mechanism was simplified so that power came directly from the ground wheels, avoiding extra shafts and clutches that added weight and leak points in earlier experimental machines. These steps together lowered the part count and the assembly time per unit, moving the selling price toward the raw-material floor while preserving the sevenfold speed gain over scythe crews. The key engineering trade-off was accepting a heavier overall frame in exchange for rigidity that kept the cutting bar aligned without constant adjustment. One objection often raised is that prairie fields varied in moisture and terrain, yet the fixed geometry of the reciprocating sickle proved tolerant enough once the frame held its alignment; the extra mass paid for itself by removing the daily recalibration labor that had kept earlier prototypes from reaching farms. Another potential objection concerns the horses or oxen needed to pull the machine, but that power source already existed on every farm and replaced the larger human crews rather than adding new animals. By producing the sickle sections and guards as repeatable castings instead of individually forged pieces, McCormick removed the most variable step in the old supply chain. The same logic applied to the wooden platform: standardized plank dimensions allowed regional mills to supply cut lumber that required only bolting rather than shaping at the assembly site.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
Once volume production and regional plants were in place, the reaper reached farms at roughly one hundred fifty dollars, a price that still left headroom above the material floor but far below the labor cost of equivalent hand crews over a season. The Idiot Index of the finished machine fell closer to three, reflecting mainly the remaining hand finishing and distribution margins rather than the original artisanal fabrication. Prairie wheat output rose sharply because one operator and a few helpers could now clear many acres in the narrow window before storms or sprouting ruined the crop. Limits remained in the need for relatively level ground and the requirement that horses or oxen supply steady forward motion; very stony or hilly fields still demanded manual methods. The design also accepted higher initial capital outlay for farmers, shifting expense from daily wages to a single durable purchase. The speed advantage held only when the machine could run without frequent stops for clogging, which in turn depended on the grain standing at a uniform height that the fixed cutter bar could reach. Even with these constraints, the reduction in total labor hours per acre remained the decisive gain that made the purchase worthwhile for larger holdings.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A harvesting machine whose price once tracked the wages of many men rather than the cost of its iron and wood revealed that the real constraint was assembly labor, not the physics of cutting stalks. Standardized parts and nearby final assembly each removed a distinct layer of hand fitting that had accumulated under the old craft model. Which other seasonal field tasks still carry similar hidden labor multipliers, and what would the first working prototype look like if someone started from the motion of the tool instead of the shape of the crew?
