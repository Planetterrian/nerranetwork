> **Before Ford, a car cost a skilled worker years of wages; he cut the price by treating it as atoms in motion rather than a fitted craft object.**

---

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1908 a new Model T left the factory priced near $850. By the mid-1920s the same basic car sold for roughly $260. The drop did not come from cheaper steel or rubber; it came from refusing to accept the labor hours and part-by-part fitting that had defined every automobile built before it.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Before Ford, every automobile was assembled in one place by skilled fitters who filed, drilled, and adjusted each component until it matched its neighbors. The convention had grown directly from carriage and bicycle practice, where small teams produced low volumes and each vehicle was essentially unique. Under that method a finished car carried the full cost of those fitters’ time, the carrying cost of parts waiting for their mates, and the scrap generated when a single misfit ruined an assembly. The price therefore sat far above the value of the raw materials because the dominant expense was the hours required to reconcile non-interchangeable parts. No one questioned the premise that a car must be built the way a fine carriage had always been built; the assumption was simply inherited. As a result the industry treated high labor content as an unavoidable feature of the product rather than a variable that could be attacked. The baseline price reflected that inheritance: a machine that only the well-off could purchase, even though the steel, wood, rubber, and brass inside it were ordinary commodities. Ford refused that baseline. He asked what the car would cost if every part arrived ready to bolt or rivet without further fitting, and if the work itself moved past stationary workers instead of the other way around.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Ford began by insisting on interchangeable parts, which meant the magic-wand floor—the cost of the raw materials themselves—could finally be approached instead of being buried under fitting labor. A rough magic-wand estimate for the materials in an early Model T runs on the order of perhaps $50 to $80 in period commodity prices for the steel, rubber, brass, and wood; the finished price of $850 therefore implied an Idiot Index on the order of ten to seventeen before any redesign began. That ratio announced that the dominant cost was not the atoms but the hours spent reconciling them. Interchangeability attacked the ratio directly by forcing every supplier and every department to hold tolerances that had previously been left to the fitter’s file. Once parts could be drawn from bins at random and still assemble, the next move was to bring the work to the worker on a moving line. The line eliminated the time each chassis spent waiting while fitters walked between benches or searched for the next part. Early experiments showed assembly time falling from roughly twelve hours per car to a few hours; later refinements pushed it lower still. Vertical integration followed because Ford calculated that the cost of motion included every shipment and every markup between foundry and assembly. By owning the steel mill, the glass plant, and the rubber plantations, he removed the intermediate handling steps whose costs had been passed along in the finished price. Each of these moves was chosen because it reduced the labor multiplier that sat between raw material and finished car. The trade-off was rigidity: the line only worked once the product itself stopped changing. Ford accepted that constraint and froze the Model T design for nineteen years so the process could be driven toward its physical limit. The Idiot Index fell because the numerator—total cost—dropped faster than any change in the material denominator.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
The price trajectory is well documented: from the initial $850 down through successive cuts to the $260 range by 1925. Part counts and assembly hours fell in parallel, though exact contemporary figures vary by source. The new Idiot Index was still well above one, because steel, rubber, and fuel remained real costs and because the line itself required expensive tooling and supervision. The human trade-off was equally real: the pace and monotony of the line produced high turnover until Ford countered with the famous five-dollar day. Even after the wage increase, the fundamental gap between material floor and finished price had narrowed dramatically compared with the pre-Ford craft baseline. What remained hard was any further reduction once the design was locked; later competitors would have to rethink the car itself, not merely its assembly.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A part or assembly whose price greatly exceeds its materials is announcing a design or process problem, not a shortage of atoms. Question the inherited requirement—hand fitting, stationary assembly, fragmented supply—before optimizing within it. The same logic that turned an $850 car into a $260 car is available wherever the Idiot Index remains high because the process still copies yesterday’s convention rather than the physics of the materials. Who will apply it next, and to what product whose price today still looks inevitable?
