> **Cortez began heap-leaching two million tons of run-of-mine gold ore in 1971 without milling it first.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In the late 1960s the conventional route to gold still began with crushing followed by fine grinding in mills so that cyanide could reach the metal particles in agitated tanks. At the Cortez mine in Nevada, operators instead placed low-grade ore directly on asphalt pads and trickled dilute cyanide solution through it. The change eliminated the grinding step for millions of tons of material that would otherwise have been uneconomic. George M. Potter of the U.S. Bureau of Mines had run the laboratory and pilot work that made the shift possible.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Gold mines had long treated ore by reducing it to a fine powder so that cyanide could dissolve the gold in suspension. The process required ball or rod mills, thickeners, and large volumes of water and power. Only higher-grade material justified those costs; anything below roughly 0.1 ounce per ton was left in the ground or stockpiled. The assumption was that physical liberation of the gold particles had to precede chemical attack. That assumption had been inherited from decades of milling practice and was rarely questioned for marginal deposits. Carlin Gold Mining Company still ran its main ore through a full mill circuit even while testing heap methods on lower-grade open-pit material. The capital tied up in grinding equipment and the energy consumed by it set a practical floor beneath which conventional operations could not reach. Each stage of size reduction added motors, liners, pumps, and classifiers that had to be maintained and powered continuously. The fine particles also demanded careful handling to avoid dust losses and to keep the pulp at the right density for agitation. Because the entire ore mass moved through these steps, even modest daily tonnage multiplied the installed horsepower and the volume of water that had to be clarified and recycled. Operators accepted the expense because past practice had shown that coarser material left too much gold locked inside unbroken rock. The convention therefore carried forward the equipment list and the energy budget without re-testing whether every size-reduction stage was still necessary once the goal was simply to expose gold surfaces to dilute cyanide.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Bureau of Mines metallurgists asked what minimum physical work was actually required to bring cyanide into contact with the gold. Laboratory tests on Cortez samples showed that ore crushed only to three-quarter-inch size and stacked in heaps still released about 70 percent of its gold when leached for seven days with dilute sodium cyanide solution. The pilot heaps at Cortez confirmed the result at larger scale. Mine manager Don Duncan then placed roughly two million tons of run-of-mine cutoff ore on prepared pads beginning in 1971. No ball mills, no classifiers, and no counter-current decantation circuit were built for that tonnage. The sources supplied by the Bureau record labor, equipment, and reagent quantities for the heap operation but do not report the dollar cost of the conventional milling route or the precise reagent and pumping energy that would constitute a raw-material floor. Without those paired figures the First Principles Ratio cannot be calculated from the record. What the sources do show is the elimination of one entire class of equipment and its associated energy and maintenance. The key test was whether solution could percolate through the coarser fragments fast enough to reach most of the exposed gold surfaces within a practical time. By keeping the ore at three-quarter-inch top size the operators avoided the power draw of the final grinding stages that would have turned the rock into a slurry. They also avoided the capital cost of the thickeners and filters that normally separate pregnant solution from finely ground solids. The trade-off was a lower recovery than a full mill circuit could achieve on the same ore, yet the capital saved allowed the mine to process material that would otherwise have stayed in the ground. One obvious objection is that skipping fine grinding must leave some gold particles coated or enclosed, and the sources acknowledge that heap leaching works best on oxidized, non-refractory ores where the gold is already accessible along fractures. Another objection is that solution losses or channeling could waste reagent; the Bureau reports note that operators learned to manage percolation by proper heap construction and by adding agglomeration steps when fines were present. The redesign therefore succeeded because the ore at Cortez met the physical condition that allowed direct contact without prior ultra-fine size reduction. The decision sequence ran from laboratory confirmation, to pilot heaps, to full-scale placement of two million tons, each step removing another piece of the inherited milling train rather than copying it at smaller scale.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
Heap leaching at Carlin and Cortez brought previously sub-economic ore into production with far lower installed capital than a new mill circuit would have required. Recovery on the 0.07-ounce-per-ton material reached roughly 70 percent after seven days of leaching. The method spread to other low-grade deposits in the 1970s because the capital barrier had dropped. It could not treat refractory sulfide ores that still needed roasting or pressure oxidation, and solution losses or poor percolation remained operational problems at some sites. The physical limit set by reagent consumption and pumping energy is therefore still present, but the sources give no measured value for it. The approach also required suitable pad foundations and solution containment that added their own costs, though these were smaller than the avoided grinding plant. Over time the industry added carbon adsorption and other refinements, yet the core simplification of skipping fine grinding for suitable ores stayed in place. The gap that remains is the portion of gold still locked inside unbroken fragments, which no amount of heap redesign can recover without reintroducing some form of size reduction.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
When the chemical reaction can occur without first reducing the entire mass to powder, the equipment that performed that reduction becomes optional rather than obligatory. The same logic applies wherever an intermediate physical step is retained only because past practice treated it as unavoidable. Which other mineral or chemical processes still carry an inherited grinding or size-reduction stage that a direct-contact test would show is unnecessary?
