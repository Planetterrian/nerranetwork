> **Hoe's 1847 cylinder press printed eight thousand sheets an hour where flatbeds managed only a few hundred.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
Before the cylinder press, a single newspaper page required a flatbed machine that pressed one sheet at a time against locked type. Richard March Hoe placed the type on a revolving cylinder instead, so the paper could meet the image continuously rather than in stops and starts. The change replaced repeated manual alignment with steady rotation, and circulation numbers that had been limited by press speed began to climb into the tens of thousands. The shift showed that the bottleneck had never been the paper or ink themselves but the mechanical pauses built into every prior cycle.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Newspapers had long been printed on flatbed presses descended from Gutenberg’s design. A bed held the type, a sheet of paper was laid on it by hand, and a heavy platen came down to make the impression before the sheet was lifted away. Each cycle took the coordinated effort of several workers, and the press could manage only a few hundred sheets per hour under the best conditions. The assumption was that printing remained a craft of setting type, inking, and pressing one sheet at a time. That assumption carried forward because every improvement stayed inside the same cycle of feeding, pressing, and removing. Labor costs and time per sheet therefore stayed high, and publishers accepted modest daily circulations as the natural limit of the technology. The finished cost of each page reflected the hours of skilled work rather than the price of the paper and ink alone. No one questioned the underlying sequence because it had always been done that way. The flatbed’s repeated stops also meant that any increase in demand simply required more presses and more operators rather than a change in the motion itself. This kept the entire economics of newspaper production anchored to human handling speed instead of the physical requirements of ink transfer.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Hoe asked what physics actually required to transfer ink to paper. The essential actions were placing ink on type and bringing paper into contact under pressure. Once those two facts were isolated, the flatbed and its repeated stopping motion looked unnecessary. Hoe mounted the type on a large revolving cylinder and added impression cylinders that pressed the paper against it as it turned. The paper now met the type in one continuous motion instead of discrete cycles. His 1847 patent and the first installation at the Philadelphia Public Ledger reached eight thousand sheets per hour on one side. The raw-material floor remained the cost of the paper, the ink, and the modest energy needed to turn the cylinder. The old flatbed design carried a high Idiot Index because most of its expense came from the repeated manual handling and the slow mechanical cycle rather than from those materials. Bullock took the same cylinder principle further in 1863 by feeding paper from a continuous roll instead of individual sheets. Curved stereotype plates replaced individual type on the cylinder, allowing both sides to be printed in one pass. The web moved through the rollers, was cut, and was folded automatically. Each of these steps removed a separate handling operation that had previously added labor and time. The trade-off was the need for precise tension control and durable stereotype plates that could survive the continuous run. Hoe later combined the web feed with perfecting in his 1870–1871 press, reaching eighteen thousand papers an hour at the New York Tribune. Every consolidation reduced the number of times a sheet had to be touched by a person or stopped by a mechanism. One objection often raised is that continuous motion would smear ink or tear paper, yet the solution lay in matching cylinder surface speeds exactly and using stereotype plates cast from the original type to withstand the run without loosening. Another concern was that feeding from a roll would require new paper-making capacity, but the press itself created the demand that justified larger rolls and faster papermaking upstream. By removing the discrete sheet-feeding station, the design also eliminated the time lost to each sheet’s acceleration and deceleration, converting that lost interval directly into additional impressions per minute.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
The cylinder and web designs raised output from hundreds of sheets per hour to twelve thousand or more, and later to thirty thousand. Publishers could therefore support daily circulations in the hundreds of thousands without a proportional rise in labor. The Idiot Index fell because the dominant cost shifted closer to the price of paper and ink, yet it never reached the pure material floor. Mechanical wear on cylinders and plates, the need for skilled pressmen to maintain register, and the limits of paper strength at high speeds remained real constraints. Bullock himself was killed in 1867 while adjusting one of his own machines, a reminder that the new speeds introduced their own hazards. The redesign proved that the cost of a printed page was not fixed by the physics of ink and paper but by the number of manual steps that could be eliminated. Even after the gains, the process still required operators to monitor tension and plate wear, and paper breaks at speed could halt an entire run, showing that some overhead had simply moved from feeding to supervision and maintenance.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A press whose speed is set by how many times a sheet must be picked up and aligned is paying for handling rather than for ink transfer. Once the cylinder turns the contact into a continuous process, every added mechanical stop becomes visible as an extra cost. The same logic applies wherever a sequence of discrete hand-offs can be replaced by steady motion through a single station. Who will next treat an entire production line as one rotating surface instead of a series of separate stations?
