Drawloom weaving had evolved over centuries as a direct extension of earlier hand-loom practice.

A master weaver or designer first created a point-paper diagram showing the desired pattern repeat.

That diagram was then translated into a sequence of individual cord pulls performed by an assistant who sat above or beside the loom.

Because each pick required a unique combination of lifts, the assistant’s memory or the paper sheet had to be consulted continuously.

The convention treated the pattern as inseparable from the weaver’s craft knowledge.

No one questioned why the information had to travel through a living person for every single row.

Under this regime a complex brocade or damask might require four to six people.

Jacquard asked what physical action actually determined which warp threads rose for each pick.

The answer was a binary choice per thread.

Lift or leave, repeated across hundreds of threads and thousands of picks.

Once that choice was recognized as information rather than craft, it could be stored on a durable medium.

He used rectangular cards pierced with holes.

Each hole allowed a needle to pass and lift its corresponding thread.

An unpunched spot kept the thread down.

A chain of such cards advanced automatically after every pick.

The loom itself read the pattern without further human interpretation.

In the old drawloom the equivalent information cost was paid in skilled wages on every single pick.

The price of a meter of figured silk stood many times above the sum of its thread and loom depreciation.

Most of the expense sat in repeated human selection rather than in the fibers themselves.

The redesign also required solving a mechanical trade-off.

The needles and hooks had to be light enough to be moved by the card yet sturdy enough to survive thousands of cycles.

By mounting the selection mechanism on the loom frame and letting gravity and springs return the needles, Jacquard kept added mass low while eliminating the assistant entirely.

Later refinements replaced individual cords with a grid of hooks.

This further reduced the number of moving parts that had to be adjusted by hand.

Each of these steps attacked a different layer of the original labor stack.

Memory, coordination, and physical selection were addressed rather than merely speeding up the old sequence.

The arithmetic therefore shifts dramatically.

The cards themselves could be stored, copied, and transported.

The information remained outside the operator’s head and could be advanced mechanically.

This separation of the instruction set from the execution mechanism is what drove the Idiot Index downward.

The dominant recurring expense had been replaced by a one-time encoding step whose material cost was negligible compared with wages.

Within a decade of its commercial introduction the Jacquard attachment spread through Lyon’s silk workshops and then to other textile centers.

Output per weaver rose sharply.

A single operator could now manage patterns that previously needed several people.

The price of figured fabrics fell enough that they moved from court and church use into broader markets.

The new Idiot Index remained above one because thread, wood, and card stock still had to be purchased.

The loom itself required maintenance.

Yet the dominant term in the cost equation had shifted from recurring expert time to one-time card preparation.

Limits persisted.

The cards still had to be punched by hand or by a separate machine.

Very large patterns demanded long chains that were heavy to handle.

Skilled weavers also lost work.

This produced documented protests in Lyon in 1801 and again in the 1830s.

Those frictions were real.

Even after the cards were prepared, the loom still consumed the same physical thread.

It still required periodic repair of wooden frames and metal hooks.

The residual gap between finished price and raw-material value therefore reflected these continuing inputs plus the cost of maintaining the card-reading mechanism itself.

Nevertheless the magnitude of that residual gap was far smaller than the labor multiple that had characterized the drawloom era.

The variable most sensitive to scale had been removed from the per-pick equation.

A pattern that must be recreated by hand on every row is paying for memory rather than for material.
