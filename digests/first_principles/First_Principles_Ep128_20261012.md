> **Moving companies bill $90–200 per crew hour while truck fuel and depreciation average roughly $0.90 per mile.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
A household move across town is quoted by the hour at rates that quickly reach several hundred dollars even for modest homes. The truck itself, however, burns diesel at about 48 cents per mile and carries equipment ownership costs near 40 cents more. That spread between the billed rate and the measured operating cost is the opening for first-principles examination. The gap appears because the price tag is built around the presence of people rather than the incremental consumption of fuel and the gradual wearing of tires and brakes.

### Segment 2 — Why It Costs What It Costs Today
Local residential moves are priced by crew time rather than distance or weight. Service Anchor reports typical 2026 rates of $90–150 per hour for a two-person crew with truck and $130–200 for three people, with two- or three-hour minimums. The clock starts when the crew arrives and stops only after the last item is placed inside the new residence. Every added flight of stairs, narrow hallway, or packing request extends that clock and raises the total. Carriers must also carry liability insurance, workers-compensation coverage, and seasonal capacity buffers that sit idle for months. Dispatch and sales overhead, vehicle licensing, and the need to turn away work during peak summer weeks all fold into the hourly quote. Customers accept the structure because most moves happen only once or twice in a lifetime and comparison shopping feels costly in time. The result is a market in which the final bill reflects coordination layers and risk allocation more than the incremental cost of rolling the truck another mile. When a two-person crew spends an extra forty minutes wrestling a sofa around a tight corner, the customer pays for those forty minutes at the full crew rate even though the truck’s engine is idling and its depreciation continues at the same steady pace. The minimum-hour rule further protects the carrier against the possibility that a job finishes faster than expected, yet it also prevents any customer from capturing the savings when the physical work proves lighter than average. Seasonal demand compounds the effect: in many cities the number of available crews halves between January and July, forcing summer quotes to embed the cost of trucks that sit parked through the winter. Because each job is still treated as a one-off transaction, the carrier cannot spread the fixed expense of maintaining a dispatch office or renewing annual permits across a steady stream of moves. The hourly model therefore survives not because it matches the physics of moving mass from one address to another, but because it allocates every layer of uncertainty and idle time directly onto the customer who happens to need the service that day.

### Segment 3 — The Magic Wand Number & The Idiot Index
The American Transportation Research Institute’s 2026 update places the 2025 industry-average marginal operating cost for for-hire trucks at $2.336 per mile. Fuel accounts for $0.482 of that figure and truck-plus-trailer ownership or lease payments another $0.404. The remaining $1.45 covers maintenance, tires, insurance, tolls, and driver wages when they are treated as variable. A rough magic-wand estimate for the physical floor of moving one household therefore begins with those two dominant line items: the energy in the diesel and the capital recovery on the vehicle itself. For a typical three-hour local job covering perhaps 25 miles round-trip, the fuel component sits near $12 and the ownership component near $10. Adding modest allowances for tires and unscheduled repairs still keeps the physics-based floor below $40 for the vehicle portion alone. The billed price for the same job commonly lands between $400 and $900 once crew time, minimums, and overhead are included. Dividing those two numbers produces an Idiot Index on the order of ten to twenty. The gap does not sit in the steel of the truck bed or the energy content of the fuel; it lives in the scheduling friction, the insurance stacking, the seasonal mismatch between demand and fleet size, and the hand-assembly nature of each job. Because every move is still treated as a bespoke event, fixed costs such as sales, permitting for temporary parking, and carrying inventory of unused trucks during slow months are amortized across very few billable hours. The index therefore signals that redesign of coordination and utilization, not new materials, offers the largest lever. If a carrier could run the same truck 300 miles in a day instead of 25, the per-mile ownership cost would drop by more than half even before any change in fuel efficiency, yet the current pricing model gives the carrier no direct incentive to achieve that higher utilization. The same arithmetic shows why the insurance layer matters: a policy written for an entire year spreads its premium across every mile the truck actually rolls, but when utilization falls the premium per mile rises sharply and that increase is passed straight into the hourly rate.

### Segment 4 — The First-Principles Opportunity
A redesign would first attack the minimum-hour rule by shifting more moves to a weight-and-distance quote even inside a single metro area, which requires only accurate portable scales already common on long-haul trailers. Second, it would standardize truck configurations and loading patterns so that the same vehicle can be turned in four hours instead of sitting through multi-hour gaps between jobs. Third, it would move routine insurance and liability into a shared pool or usage-based product that charges per mile rather than per annual policy, trimming the fixed-cost layer that now inflates every quote. Each of those steps needs reliable real-time demand data, standardized cargo manifests, and either regulatory acceptance of digital bills of lading or carrier willingness to self-insure a larger slice of risk. The hardest constraint is seasonal peaking: summer demand can exceed winter supply by factors of two or three in many cities, so any operator that smooths that curve through off-peak pricing or shared municipal staging yards captures most of the remaining gap. None of these moves requires new physics, only consistent measurement of the per-mile floor already published by ATRI and the willingness to price against it rather than against last year’s hourly precedent. A carrier that published a flat per-mile rate for local work would still have to solve the problem of matching trucks to jobs in real time; without that matching layer the trucks would simply spend more time empty between loads. Likewise, any attempt to pool insurance across multiple small operators would require verifiable mileage records and loss data that most local moving companies do not currently collect in usable form. The prize remains visible once those data and coordination problems are solved: the same 25-mile job whose vehicle floor sits near $40 could be priced at something closer to $120–150 once crew time is paid only for actual loading and unloading rather than for the entire elapsed appointment.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
One principle is that an hourly meter running on human presence will always diverge from a cost meter running on fuel and steel unless the two are deliberately reconciled. Another is that any service whose price is set by calendar friction rather than physical throughput carries an embedded seasonal tax that only shared capacity or demand shifting can remove. Tomorrow the series continues with one further concrete case or one further industry whose raw-material floor is still hidden by accumulated habit. The first visible signal that someone is attempting the move in household goods transport will be a published per-mile quote that stays flat when crew size changes but rises only with actual distance and weight.
