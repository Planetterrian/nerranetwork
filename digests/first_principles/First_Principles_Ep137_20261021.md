> **The 1977 Apple II sold for twelve hundred ninety eight dollars when its processor and memory came from standard catalogs.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
Steve Wozniak designed the Apple II around parts anyone could buy from distributors. The finished machine reached stores in June 1977 at a price more than double the cost of its separate board. That gap reflected choices about case, power supply, and the decision to sell a complete appliance rather than another kit. The contrast between the catalog price of the 6502 and the retail sticker on the assembled system showed how much of the final figure came from integration steps that hobbyist builders had previously performed themselves.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Before the Apple II, most microcomputers were sold as bare boards or required builders to add their own enclosures, power supplies, and interfaces. Hobbyists at clubs such as the Homebrew Computer Club assembled systems from published schematics and whatever chips they could source. The convention treated the computer as an ongoing project rather than a finished product. Prices for those kits often started several hundred dollars lower because buyers accepted the assembly work and the risk of incomplete function. Steve Jobs had seen this pattern with the Apple I and wanted the next machine positioned for households and schools instead. The assumption baked into the older approach was that serious users would tolerate exposed boards and custom wiring. That assumption kept part counts low on paper but kept volumes low as well. The Apple II therefore entered a market where the default path was incremental improvement on existing hobbyist designs. Wozniak refused to accept that limit when he laid out the new board. The earlier machines had treated the television set as an optional accessory that required an extra RF modulator or dedicated monitor, so builders carried that cost separately. The Apple II moved the video circuitry onto the main board and made the television a standard display, removing one layer of additional hardware that every buyer had previously sourced on their own. This shift changed the economic baseline because the machine now arrived ready to produce color graphics without further purchases. The older kits had left those decisions to the owner, which lowered the initial sticker price but also limited who would buy one.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Wozniak started with the MOS Technology 6502 running at one megahertz, a part already proven in other devices and available for modest cost. He added circuitry that produced color graphics on a standard television without extra hardware. The design incorporated eight expansion slots so owners could add memory or peripherals later rather than buying a larger machine up front. A switching power supply designed by Rod Holt replaced the heavier linear supply common in other systems, cutting weight and heat. Jerry Manock shaped the foam-molded plastic case that gave the machine a finished appearance instead of an open chassis. These decisions were made one at a time during 1976 while Wozniak still worked at Hewlett-Packard. Each choice was tested against the goal of a machine that could be unpacked and used with minimal extra purchases. The board-only version listed at five hundred ninety eight dollars for four kilobytes of RAM. The complete system with case, keyboard, and power supply reached twelve hundred ninety eight dollars. That difference shows where the remaining cost lived: enclosure, assembly, documentation, and the Integer BASIC already in ROM. A rough magic-wand estimate for the silicon, discrete components, and bare board alone would sit well below the board-only price, though exact commodity values for 1977 DRAM and the 6502 are not recorded in public price lists. The Idiot Index of the finished system therefore remained high because the largest markups sat in integration and presentation rather than in the raw chips. Wozniak’s later comments in Byte magazine stressed that a personal computer should be small, reliable, convenient, and inexpensive; the redesign attacked the convenience and reliability parts directly by moving functions onto the motherboard. The trade-off was that early production volumes stayed modest until demand proved itself, so the initial price could not yet reflect the learning that later high-volume runs would bring. The eight slots themselves represented a deliberate refusal to lock the buyer into a fixed configuration at purchase. Instead of soldering additional memory or interfaces at the factory, the board left those decisions open, which kept the base bill of materials lower but required the case and power supply to support future additions. This approach avoided the cost of populating every possible option on every unit while still delivering immediate usability. The switching supply further reduced material use because it operated at higher frequency and therefore needed smaller transformers and capacitors than the linear supplies found in most contemporary kit machines. Those material savings appeared in the lower weight of the finished product, yet the retail price still reflected the full cost of designing and qualifying the supply for safety and reliability in a consumer setting.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
The Apple II reached schools and small businesses once VisiCalc appeared, yet its margins stayed wider than those of later commodity machines. The new Idiot Index, measured against the board-only price, was roughly two to one for the lowest configuration. Further compression would have required either higher volumes or changes in how cases and power supplies were produced, neither of which occurred at Apple in 1977. The design still carried eight slots and a molded case that later clones would simplify or eliminate. Those choices preserved expandability at the expense of the lowest possible price. The foam-molded case added both material and tooling cost that a simple sheet-metal enclosure would have avoided, yet it also removed the need for buyers to fabricate or source their own housing. The power supply, while more efficient, still required separate certification and sourcing that a kit builder would have handled individually. These elements kept the gap between board cost and system price from closing further in the first year of production. The machine succeeded because it delivered immediate function without requiring the purchaser to solve those integration problems, but the same decisions left room for later competitors to attack the enclosure and supply costs directly.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A machine whose visible cost is driven by its enclosure and integration rather than its processor tells you the real constraint is assembly, not silicon. Treating the television as the display removed an entire category of dedicated hardware that hobbyist designs had carried forward. The same pattern of questioning which functions truly need separate parts appears again whenever a new platform moves from kit to appliance. What would change if the next generation of educational hardware started from the same list of catalog parts instead of from last year’s case?
