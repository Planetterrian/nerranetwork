For most of the twentieth century an accurate watch was assembled from dozens of tiny machined and jeweled parts.

The final price reflected the cumulative hours of hand fitting and regulation that went into each one.

Then a handful of engineers asked what physics actually requires to count seconds.

They answered with a quartz crystal oscillating at a fixed frequency, counted by a simple integrated circuit.

The result was a timepiece whose raw-material cost sat near the floor of a few dollars while its accuracy surpassed anything the old mechanical craft could sustain at comparable price.

That single substitution collapsed the cost curve of reliable timekeeping and left an entire national industry without a product that anyone needed to buy for the sake of knowing the hour.

Watchmaking had evolved by incremental refinement of the same basic architecture for more than two centuries.

A balance wheel and hairspring provided the oscillator.

An escapement converted that motion into discrete advances of the hands.

A mainspring supplied the stored energy.

Each of these elements demanded precision machining, polishing, and adjustment.

Jewel bearings reduced friction at critical pivots.

The final regulation required repeated observation against a reference.

Because every step added skilled labor, the finished price tracked the number of hours required rather than the value of the brass, steel, and ruby inside.

Swiss and Japanese houses alike accepted this cost structure as the natural price of accuracy.

The assumption was that better timekeeping meant tighter tolerances and more careful assembly within the existing mechanical framework.

No one inside the industry treated the oscillator itself as a variable that could be replaced by an entirely different physical phenomenon.

As a result, an accurate mechanical watch stayed expensive enough that most people owned only one or two.

The industry measured success by how many such labor-intensive instruments it could still sell.

The key engineering trade off at every stage was accepting a battery and a chip in place of a mainspring and jewels.
