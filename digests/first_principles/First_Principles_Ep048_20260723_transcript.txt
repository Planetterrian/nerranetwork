Today we reason from raw materials, not analogy.
Satellites that reach orbit often cost millions, yet contain only thousands of dollars of

The real expense sits in the process, not the atoms.
A modern small satellite can deliver communications or imaging for a finished price in the low millions
of dollars.
Yet the atoms inside it, solar grade silicon, structural aluminum, and ordinary electronics
could be put together for only a few tens of thousands if every other rule disappeared.
The difference does not hide in rare materials.
It sits inside the habit of treating every spacecraft as a one-of-a-kind artifact that
must survive launch, radiation, and years of operation with no chance of repair.
That gap is where First Principles Thinking starts to open new space.
Reusable rockets have already cut the price of reaching orbit, but the satellites themselves
still carry prices set when each flight was the only one that would ever happen.
Satellites are built today under a set of constraints that grew together and now hold
one another in place.
Most spacecraft remain one-off machines because launch slots were once so costly that losing
the vehicle was unthinkable.
That scarcity forced extreme reliability rules, which demanded long-testing campaigns,
radiation-hardened parts, and extra layers of redundancy.
Those choices add both mass and assembly hours.
Insurance models still price policies against the old record of rare flights and high stakes,
so another large share of cost appears before the rocket leaves the ground.
Supply chains stay fragmented because each mission carries slightly different instruments
or orbits, so the same qualification work repeats instead of building on itself.
Regulatory steps add calendar time that raises the carrying cost of engineering teams.
The finished price therefore feels fixed inside the industry even though the physics of
silicon cells or aluminum structures has stayed the same.
Reusable rockets have begun to shrink the transport cost.
Yet the spacecraft have not been redesigned around the new reality of frequent, cheaper rides.
One common objection is that every orbit or payload differs, so standardization cannot work.
The variation, however, usually lives in the instruments rather than in the core bus that supplies
power, attitude control, thermal management, and communications.
Another objection is that later testing would produce too many failures.
Yet the present testing depth was set when losing a vehicle meant waiting years for the next chance.
With more frequent launches, the arithmetic of replacement changes, but the design rules have not caught up.
A rough, magic wand estimate begins with the main physical parts.
A typical small satellite might use a few hundred kilograms of aluminum alloy whose commodity price
runs only a few dollars per kilogram.
The solar arrays rest on silicon wafers whose raw material cost before any cell work
sits in the range of tens of dollars per square meter at current commodity levels.
Electronics boards draw from the same microcontrollers, power converters, and memory chips used in
ordinary industrial gear. Each worth only single digit dollars once the silicon die and packaging
are removed. Adding modest amounts of copper wiring, thermal coatings, and battery cells
brings the total raw material floor to roughly the low tens of thousands of dollars
for a several hundred kilogram spacecraft. Against a finished price still measured in the low
millions, the implied idiot index therefore falls somewhere between 50 and several hundred.
The exact ratio matters less than where the gap lives. Most of the multiplier comes from custom
design work, repeated qualification, low rate assembly, and risk reserves that sit between the
commodity feedstock and the finished article. Launch cost cuts have already reduced one slice of
that multiplier. The spacecraft manufacturing term has not moved in proportion because the design
rules still assume each vehicle is the last one that will ever be built. Even if the aluminum
structure costs a few thousand dollars in raw stock, and the solar cells add another few thousand
before processing, the remaining electronics and wiring might add 10 to 20 thousand at commodity
prices. The rest of the finished price comes from engineering labor, repeated environmental testing,
radiation-hardened substitutions, and insurance reserves sized for missions where replacement
is measured in years rather than weeks. One objection claims that radiation-hardening itself
demands exotic parts whose material cost is already high. Many hardening steps however rely on
added shielding mass or circuit level fixes rather than costly atoms, and those steps could be applied
to higher volume parts once the qualification burden spreads across many identical units.
A redesign would start by locking a small number of standardized satellite buses whose mechanical,
thermal, and electrical interfaces are built at industrial volumes instead of mission-specific
volumes. Once the bus is common, the same radiation tolerant parts can be qualified once and
bought in larger lots, lowering both unit price, and the repeated engineering effort that now inflates
each program. Accepting a higher replacement rate in exchange for lower per unit cost
becomes rational once launch prices drop far enough that losing one vehicle no longer ends the
mission. The arithmetic is simple, 10 satellites at one tenth the price can deliver more total on
orbit time than a single heavily protected unit. Additive manufacturing and automated electronics
assembly lines already used for cars and consumer devices can be applied to structural brackets
and avionics trays, cutting the hand-fitting steps that dominate current integration.
Policy steps that shorten qualification for proven parts or that create shared orbital debris
standards would further trim insurance and testing overhead. The hard limits stay real. Total ionizing
dose and single event effects still need some shielding or part selection that ordinary hardware
can ignore. Thermal cycling and vacuum remains unforgiving and orbital debris still requires
collision avoidance measures that cannot be removed. Those constraints set a floor higher than
pure terrestrial electronics, yet they do not justify the present multiplier once the launch
term itself has been compressed. Another practical move would be to shift from custom machined
propellant tanks and reaction wheel assemblies to designs that share the same cast or printed
geometry across missions. So the same tooling produces hundreds of units rather than one.
The objection that each payload needs unique mounting points can be met by standardized
interface plates that attach to a common bus frame, keeping mission flexibility while holding the
core structure identical. The remaining difficulty is that debris mitigation and collision avoidance
still need onboard propulsion and sensing that cannot be eliminated. Even those subsystems
become cheaper when the same valve, thruster and sensor designs are qualified and produced at
scale rather than reinvented for each program. One principle is that every kilogram kept off the
spacecraft multiplies across the entire cost stack because launch price, structure and propellant
margins all scale with mass. Therefore, the first redesign moves should target mass removal even
before unit cost is attacked. A second principle is that the decision to accept occasional loss
must be made explicit in the architecture rather than hidden inside reliability margins.
Only then can part counts testing depth and replacement cadence be optimized together.
