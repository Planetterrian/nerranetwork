> **Most collected recyclables cost more to sort than the raw materials they contain are worth — except for a few that already beat that floor.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
A ton of mixed household recyclables arrives at a modern sorting facility carrying perhaps thirty or forty dollars of recoverable aluminum, a few dollars of glass cullet, and a few tens of dollars of PET and HDPE once they are cleaned and pelletized. The facility then spends more per ton on labor, energy, maintenance, and residue disposal than most of the recovered fractions are worth. Surveys of single-stream sorting facilities put average processing costs above one hundred dollars per ton, and rising. The gap between those two numbers explains why large fractions of what residents carefully separate still end up landfilled: the sorting step itself exceeds the value of the output. First-principles reasoning begins by asking what the materials alone would be worth if separation were free, then traces exactly where the excess cost accumulates. That framing reveals why aluminum already clears the economic test while glass and most plastics do not, and why simply collecting more material cannot close the difference.

### Segment 2 — Why It Costs What It Costs Today
Single-stream collection, adopted widely in North America after 2000, lets households place all recyclables in one bin. That convenience raised participation rates but delivered a heavily contaminated stream containing food residue, broken glass mixed with paper, and non-recyclable plastics. Material recovery facilities responded by installing more screens, magnets, eddy-current separators, and optical sorters, yet the equipment still requires human quality-control pickers at multiple points because contamination rates remain high. Each additional handling step adds direct wages plus the carrying cost of slower throughput. Markets for the separated commodities are volatile; when virgin resin or glass prices dip, buyers simply refuse loads that contain even modest contamination, forcing facilities to pay tipping fees to landfills instead. Regulatory definitions of what counts as recycled content further encourage collection of low-value materials whose recovered price never covers the processing bill. The result is an industry whose headline recovery percentages mask large internal subsidies from municipalities and whose economics remain tethered to the cost of virgin materials rather than to any intrinsic advantage of reuse. One common objection is that the environmental benefit of keeping material out of landfills justifies the subsidy, yet that argument still leaves the physical cost of separation unaddressed; the atoms must be cleaned and moved regardless of the social goal. Another objection holds that rising landfill fees will eventually make recycling pay, but those fees themselves are an added cost layered on top of an already negative-margin process for most fractions. The entrenched pattern therefore persists because every participant optimizes for the visible collection metric rather than the hidden commodity floor.

### Segment 3 — The Magic Wand Number & The Idiot Index
Consider a representative ton of single-stream residential recyclables. A rough magic-wand estimate starts with the commodity values of the major fractions: aluminum at roughly fifteen hundred dollars per ton of recovered metal, clear glass cullet at about forty dollars per ton, PET and HDPE at roughly $1,300–2,600 per ton once cleaned and pelletized, and the remaining mixed low-grade plastics near zero or negative once cleaning and disposal of residues are included. Weighting by typical composition yields a blended raw-material floor on the order of sixty to ninety dollars per incoming ton. Against that floor the current delivered cost of separated commodities runs several times higher once collection, sorting, and residue handling are counted. The resulting Idiot Index therefore sits between two and four for the overall stream, but the index splits sharply by material: aluminum’s index is already near one because its market price comfortably exceeds sorting cost, and clean PET commands real money, while glass and mixed low-grade plastics carry indexes well above ten. The excess lives in three concrete stages. First, manual and optical separation labor adds roughly eighty dollars per ton. Second, the energy and water required to wash contaminated streams adds another thirty to forty dollars. Third, the transport and disposal of the fifteen-to-twenty-five percent residue fraction adds still more. Because these costs are incurred before any sale occurs, the net revenue from glass and plastic fractions frequently turns negative, which is why facilities increasingly landfill them when virgin prices are low. To see the arithmetic another way, imagine a bale of mixed PET and HDPE that sells for two hundred dollars per ton after sorting; the same bale required perhaps one hundred fifty dollars in upstream labor and energy to reach market purity, leaving a margin too thin to cover collection or capital recovery. An objection sometimes raised is that technological improvement in optical sorting will lower the labor line item, yet the sensors still confront the fundamental variability introduced by food residue and label adhesives that no current machine fully removes without added washing energy. The gap is therefore structural rather than merely technical.

### Segment 4 — The First-Principles Opportunity
A redesign would begin by attacking the contamination that drives the labor and residue costs. Standardized packaging made from fewer resin types and designed with easily removable labels would let optical sorters achieve higher purity without hand intervention. Deposit-return systems for beverage containers already demonstrate that removing mixed streams at the point of sale can cut sorting expense by more than half while delivering cleaner aluminum and PET. Automated robotic picking guided by computer vision is advancing, yet its economics still require high-volume, low-variety input streams; policy that mandated minimum recycled-content thresholds only for materials whose recovered value exceeds sorting cost would steer investment toward aluminum, steel, and a narrow set of rigid plastics rather than diffuse subsidies for all resins. The hardest remaining constraint is the dispersed geography of collection itself: long-haul transport of low-density bales erodes margins unless regional preprocessing hubs are co-located with manufacturing. None of these moves requires new physics, only alignment between product design, collection logistics, and the actual commodity floor. The prize is an industry whose economics no longer depend on external subsidies once the Idiot Index for each material falls below two. One practical objection is that consumers resist changes to familiar packaging formats, yet the same objection applied to returnable glass bottles in earlier decades and was overcome once deposit incentives aligned behavior with the economics of recovery. Another objection notes that global trade in waste once masked domestic costs, but tightening import rules have simply exposed the underlying mismatch between sorting expense and material value.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
When the price of separating a material exceeds the value of the atoms it contains, the activity is performing economic theater rather than resource recovery. The second principle is that redesign must begin with the commodity price list, not with collection targets, because only materials that already clear their own sorting cost can scale without perpetual subsidy. The first concrete signal that someone is applying this logic will be a packaging redesign that eliminates one entire resin family from household streams while publishing the resulting change in net processing cost per ton.
