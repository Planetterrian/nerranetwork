A six-stop elevator that costs that much here can be put in for the European figure across the Atlantic. The same global manufacturers supply the core components on every continent, yet the finished price splits once the equipment reaches the job site.

Stephen Smith’s twenty twenty-four report for the Center for Building in North America traces that split to rules, assembly practices, and labor arrangements, not to any difference in steel, motors, or controllers. Fewer mid-rise buildings get built, because each added elevator shaft consumes budget that could otherwise support more units or lower rents.

Developers facing these numbers often default to four-story walk-ups even when land-use rules now permit taller infill.

North American elevators follow the American Society of Mechanical Engineers safety code, ASME A17.1, which diverges from the EN 81 standard used almost everywhere else. That separate code requires larger cabin dimensions to accommodate a stretcher and a wheelchair turning radius, adding floor area that must be carved out of every shaft.

The extra space forces thicker structural walls around the hoistway and larger counterweight assemblies, both of which increase the steel tonnage per installation. Manufacturers therefore produce North-American-specific variants instead of the standardized global models, which shrinks the supplier pool and raises parts prices.

Installation remains largely on-site and bespoke. European crews routinely assemble two floors per week while North American crews average one. The slower pace stems from sequential field wiring, custom bracket fabrication, and repeated inspections that cannot be batched, because each jurisdiction interprets the same ASME clauses differently.

The International Union of Elevator Constructors holds exclusive jurisdiction over most work, limiting the labor pool and resisting prefabrication that would move tasks into factories.

Maintenance contracts follow the same pattern. New York developers budget seven thousand five hundred dollars a year per unit while German firms quote around four hundred fifty dollars. The higher figure covers more frequent on-site testing and proprietary diagnostic tools that remain tied to the non-standard North American designs.

Because few buildings receive elevators at all, the market stays small, which further discourages standardized production. High prices limit demand, and limited demand keeps prices high.

Smith’s data show the United States has only three point one elevators per thousand residents, compared with twenty-seven in Switzerland and twenty-three in Spain. Canada sits at four point one per thousand. Developers therefore design around elevators whenever codes allow, favoring low-rise walk-ups that need none.

When elevators are required, the added cost is simply accepted as the price of compliance.

The magic wand number is what the finished thing would cost if you paid only for arranging the raw atoms, the price of the materials alone.

What the sources do document is where the premium lives. It sits entirely in the stages after the hardware leaves the factory. Cabin-size mandates force larger shafts and custom car enclosures that add structural steel and finishing work not required under EN 81.

Jurisdiction-specific code interpretations require each installation to receive separate engineering review and testing, lengthening permitting and inspection sequences. On-site assembly performed by a narrow pool of union labor stretches schedules and multiplies carrying costs for the developer.

Retrofit work faces the same constraints, which is why China installed fifty-one thousand elevators into existing buildings in twenty twenty-one while comparable retrofits remain rare in North America. Maintenance and modernization follow the same cost curve because replacement parts and service protocols remain non-standard.

The sources therefore locate the entire premium in regulatory fragmentation, labor-market restrictions, and the absence of factory-finished standardization rather than in the physical content of the machine itself. One might ask whether the larger cabins deliver measurable safety gains that justify the added expense.

Passenger fatality rates show no corresponding reduction on either side of the Atlantic, and worker injury statistics during installation actually run higher under the North American regime. The gap is not a verdict on the people who build elevators. It is a measurement of where the price sits, and the sources put that place after the factory door.

Stephen Smith’s Center for Building in North America has already mapped the concrete steps that would shrink the gap. Harmonizing North American rules with EN 81 would let builders adopt the same Type 2 cabin dimensions used globally and open the market to the full range of international suppliers.

Prefabricated shaft modules and pre-wired controllers could move the majority of assembly into controlled factory conditions, cutting field labor hours by half. A larger installed base would support dedicated training pipelines outside the current union structure, easing the skilled-labor bottleneck.

Smith notes that even modest moves toward these changes would lower the installed price toward the sixty-thousand-dollar range observed in Europe.

The hardest remaining obstacles are political. Rewriting ASME A17.1 to accept EN 81 conformity assessments. Persuading labor organizations to accept factory-built units. Updating accessibility rules that currently embed the larger cabin requirement. Countries that have already aligned their codes with the global standard show that safety records remain comparable while costs fall.

The first visible signal of progress would be a state or province publishing a side-by-side comparison of ASME and EN 81 requirements with a proposed amendment package. Such a document would need to quantify the exact square-footage savings per shaft and the resulting reduction in structural steel before it could move from analysis to legislative text.
