> **Cordless drill battery packs cost buyers roughly ten times the lithium cells and electronics inside them.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
A finished 18-volt cordless drill battery pack reaches the shelf at a price far above the sum of its five lithium cells, wiring, and protection board. The gap appears because manufacturers tie every tool purchase to one brand’s charger interface and voltage rating, locking users into repeated pack replacements rather than letting raw material costs set the floor. That structure turns a simple collection of cells and a motor into an ongoing platform commitment whose price reflects interface standards and ecosystem rules more than the physics of energy storage. The same cells that power a laptop or flashlight become, inside the tool ecosystem, a recurring purchase whose cost is set by the need to keep the charger and the connector shape unchanged across years of product releases.

### Segment 2 — Why It Costs What It Costs Today
Cordless platforms are built around a shared physical connector and a fixed number of cells in series. Most mainstream lines use five lithium-ion cells to produce an 18-volt nominal pack, though marketing often lists the 20-volt peak voltage measured right after charge. Once a user buys two packs and a charger, every new tool must accept the same slide or rail interface or the investment is stranded. Manufacturers therefore design entire ranges—drills, saws, grinders—around one cell count and one connector shape so that adding tools feels cheaper than starting over. The result is that retail pricing bundles the tool with one or two packs whose cost already includes the charger circuitry and the brand’s future compatibility promise. Legacy assumptions from nickel-cadmium days still influence packaging and perceived value even though lithium cells need no memory-effect conditioning. Supply chains remain fragmented because each brand maintains its own tooling for the plastic housing, terminals, and battery-management board. On-site assembly and testing add further steps that compound carrying costs through distribution. Retailers price the complete kit to reflect the long-term lock-in rather than the marginal cost of another pack. Users accept the structure because switching platforms means discarding working batteries and chargers. The finished pack therefore carries the accumulated weight of interface standards, marketing voltage labels, and ecosystem continuity rather than simply the cost of five cylindrical or pouch cells plus copper and silicon. One objection often raised is that safety testing and transport rules already force high costs; yet those rules apply to any lithium pack of the same chemistry and capacity, so they cannot explain why one brand’s pack sells for several times the price of an electrically identical generic module. Another objection is that the plastic housing and over-mold must be custom for ergonomics; the tooling amortization for that housing, however, is paid once per platform generation and then spread across every pack sold, turning a one-time capital expense into a permanent per-unit adder that would shrink if the same housing geometry were adopted across multiple brands.

### Segment 3 — The Magic Wand Number & The Idiot Index
If the cells, copper windings, and basic protection circuitry could be arranged instantly into a finished pack, the material cost would be dominated by the five 18650-format or pouch lithium cells plus modest amounts of copper, aluminum, and circuit-board laminate. Commodity lithium-ion cells trade in the low tens of dollars per kilowatt-hour in bulk, so a typical 2–3 amp-hour pack contains roughly a few dollars of active material once the metal cases and separators are included. Adding the small printed circuit board for over-current and temperature protection and the slide terminals brings the theoretical floor to perhaps one-fifth to one-tenth of today’s retail pack price. The Idiot Index therefore sits in the range of five to ten, meaning the finished pack sells for five to ten times the raw inputs. That multiplier lives in three distinct places. First, the custom plastic housing and over-molded grip are molded to match one brand’s tool line, adding tooling amortization and low-volume molding costs that would disappear at commodity scale. Second, the battery-management board carries proprietary firmware that enforces charger communication and prevents cross-brand use, requiring extra qualification and software development that a generic cell pack would skip. Third, the retail channel prices the pack as an entry ticket into an expanding tool ecosystem rather than as a replaceable energy module, so margin is taken at every layer to cover future tool sales that never appear in the material bill. Each of these layers is traceable to decisions made when the platform was first defined: the connector geometry, the cell series count, and the decision to sell tools only with matching batteries. Because those choices were made once and then defended across product generations, the cost structure feels inevitable inside the industry even though the underlying cells and copper remain ordinary commodities. A further objection is that the firmware and communication protocol add real engineering value; the value, however, is almost entirely in maintaining exclusivity rather than in any measurable improvement to energy delivery or safety, which could be achieved with open, published specifications. The arithmetic is straightforward once the cells are priced: five cells at roughly two to three dollars each in volume, a few cents of copper strip and nickel strip for connections, a dollar or two for the protection board laminate and components, and another dollar for the slide terminals and housing resin yields a materials total well below ten dollars for a typical consumer pack. Retail prices several times that amount therefore reflect the accumulated decisions about interfaces and lock-in, not the cost of the lithium, cobalt, nickel, or graphite inside the cells.

### Segment 4 — The First-Principles Opportunity
A redesign would begin by separating the energy storage from the brand interface. Standard cylindrical or pouch cells could be placed in an open mechanical format that any manufacturer could adopt, with only the electrical terminals left as a commodity connector. The second move would be to publish the charger communication protocol so that third-party packs could safely interoperate without reverse-engineering firmware. The third step would require scale in cell purchasing and automated pack assembly lines that treat the battery as a standardized module rather than a branded accessory. For any of these changes to close the gap, cell suppliers would need steady high-volume orders, testing standards would have to move from brand-specific to industry-wide, and retailers would have to accept thinner margins on the energy module itself. The genuinely hard parts are the certification burden for safety and transport regulations, the capital cost of retooling molds across multiple factories, and the risk that any single brand loses the lock-in that currently protects its installed base. None of these barriers are physical limits on the lithium or copper; they are coordination and contracting problems that have persisted because the present pricing already covers the cost of maintaining them. One practical objection is that open protocols could allow unsafe or counterfeit packs to enter the market; yet the same safety standards that already govern cell transport and charger interaction could be enforced by independent testing laboratories rather than by each brand’s proprietary firmware, shifting the verification cost from repeated per-brand development to a shared compliance regime.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
When a battery pack’s price is set by the need to protect a charger interface rather than by the cost of its cells, the excess is carried in the plastic housing and the firmware handshake. When manufacturers treat voltage class and connector shape as permanent, every later tool inherits the original multiplier instead of letting cell prices fall on their own curve. The same pattern appears whenever an energy or motion component is sold inside a closed mechanical envelope. Who will publish the first open pack specification that lets cells and tools meet on commodity terms, and what will be the first measurable drop in pack price once that specification ships?
