Insurance shows the same arithmetic. A policy written for a year spreads its premium across every mile the truck actually rolls. When utilization falls, the premium per mile rises sharply, and that increase passes straight into the hourly rate.

A redesign starts by attacking the minimum-hour rule.

Shift more moves, even inside one metro area, to a weight-and-distance quote. That needs accurate portable scales, already common on long-haul trailers.

Next, standardize truck configurations and loading patterns so the same vehicle can turn in four hours instead of sitting through multi-hour gaps between jobs.

Third, move routine insurance and liability into a shared pool or a usage-based product that charges per mile rather than per annual policy. That trims the fixed-cost layer now inflating every quote.

Each step needs reliable real-time demand data, standardized cargo manifests, and either regulatory acceptance of digital bills of lading or a carrier willing to self-insure a larger slice of risk.

The hardest constraint is seasonal peaking. Summer demand can exceed winter supply by factors of two or three in many cities.

An operator that smooths that curve with off-peak pricing or shared municipal staging yards captures most of what is left.

None of this needs new physics. It needs consistent measurement of the per-mile floor already published by the American Transportation Research Institute, and a willingness to price against that floor instead of last year’s hourly precedent.

A flat per-mile rate for local work still has to match trucks to jobs in real time. Without that matching layer the trucks simply spend more time empty between loads.

Pooling insurance across small operators needs verifiable mileage records and loss data. Most local moving companies do not collect those records in usable form.

Once those data and coordination problems are solved, the prize is visible. One principle sits under the whole case. An hourly meter running on human presence will always diverge from a cost meter running on fuel and steel unless someone deliberately reconciles the two.

Leave them separate and the customer pays for waiting, for winter trucks, and for the chance that the sofa sticks in the stairwell, all at the same rate as useful work.

A second principle: any service whose price is set by calendar friction rather than physical throughput carries an embedded seasonal tax. Only shared capacity or demand shifting removes it.

You cannot wish July into January. You can price the empty months so some moves slide, or you can let several carriers share a yard and a truck instead of each holding a private buffer.

The same split shows up wherever a person-hour stands in for a physical unit. The unit might be a mile, a kilogram, a kilowatt-hour. If the invoice ignores the unit, the Idiot Index hides inside the schedule.
