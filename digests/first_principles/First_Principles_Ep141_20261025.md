> **A handset priced at nearly four thousand dollars in 1984 reached wholesale prices below thirty dollars by early 2006.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
In 1984 a Motorola DynaTAC 8000X sold for $3,995. Twenty-two years later the same basic ability to place and receive calls fit inside the Motorola C113, which the GSMA Emerging Market Handset programme bought wholesale for less than thirty dollars. The drop did not come from cheaper raw materials alone. It came from repeated redesigns that asked what functions the radio actually required and then removed every part that did not perform one of those functions.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Early cellular phones followed the pattern set by car radios and military transceivers. Designers started with existing modules—an RF power amplifier here, a frequency synthesizer there, a separate nickel-cadmium battery pack—and bolted them together inside a cast-metal housing. Each module had already been qualified for another product line, so the only question asked was whether the module would fit inside the new case. The result was a device that weighed nearly two pounds and contained dozens of discrete boards, cables, and connectors. Because every supplier quoted its piece as a finished sub-assembly, the final price stayed close to the sum of those supplier prices. No one in the chain had an incentive to ask what the radio would cost if the functions were merged onto fewer silicon dies or if the antenna were stamped from sheet metal rather than wound by hand. The convention therefore reproduced itself: each new model simply shrank the same collection of purchased blocks. The nickel-cadmium cells themselves carried a material cost that already exceeded two hundred dollars in the DynaTAC era, yet the surrounding discrete circuitry, wiring harnesses, and machined enclosure added far more. Suppliers had no reason to redesign their own modules because the handset maker treated those modules as fixed inputs. This created a self-reinforcing loop in which every incremental improvement preserved the original partition of functions across separate boards and connectors.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
The decisive shift occurred when teams stopped treating the handset as an assembly of proven modules and instead treated it as a set of required electrical functions. The radio still needed to generate, amplify, and filter signals at cellular frequencies; it still needed to convert voice to digital bits and back; and it still needed a stable power source. Once those functions were listed without reference to existing hardware, engineers could ask which of them could share the same silicon. Baseband processors that had previously lived on separate chips were integrated with the RF transceiver. That single move eliminated multiple interconnects, reduced board area, and removed the cost of testing each interface twice. At the same time the antenna changed from a discrete helical element to a stamped metal pattern printed directly onto the plastic housing. The stamped pattern performed the same radiation task with far fewer assembly steps and no separate procurement line. Battery chemistry also moved from nickel-cadmium to lithium-ion packs whose energy density allowed smaller cells while keeping talk time acceptable. Each of these changes attacked a different term in the cost equation. Integration reduced the number of purchased components. Stamping replaced precision machining. Higher-density cells cut the volume and therefore the plastic and metal required for the case. Because the GSMA programme later aggregated demand from ten operators for roughly six million units, the same redesigned platform could be produced on high-volume lines in Asia, where the marginal cost of each additional handset fell further. The programme’s second-phase target of sub-thirty-dollar wholesale pricing, announced in late 2005 for delivery in early 2006, reflected the cumulative effect of those steps rather than any single breakthrough. A natural objection is that integration raises upfront mask-set costs; the six-million-unit commitment spread those fixed costs across enough volume that the per-unit penalty disappeared. Another objection is that stamped antennas lose efficiency; the design traded a few decibels of gain for the elimination of an entire purchased part and its associated labor, and the resulting handset still met the talk-time and standby requirements set by the operators.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
By the first quarter of 2006 the C113 and its variant C113a met the sub-thirty-dollar wholesale price while delivering up to 450 minutes of talk time and 330 hours of standby. The earlier phase-one target of below forty dollars ex-factory had already been reached in 2005 with the same platform family. The remaining gap to a theoretical material floor is still large because semiconductors, even when integrated, retain substantial value added by design and fabrication. Trade-offs accepted along the way included reduced feature sets—no color screens, no cameras—and a focus on durability over refinement. Those choices kept the design inside the cost envelope demanded by operators serving previously unconnected populations. The new Idiot Index, measured against the raw silicon, metals, and battery materials inside each unit, sits far lower than the DynaTAC’s index, yet the absolute distance to the floor remains wide because lithography, doping, and packaging still add layers of value that cannot be removed without losing the functions themselves.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
One principle is that integration only pays when volume is large enough to amortize the mask sets and test development; the GSMA’s six-million-unit commitment supplied exactly that volume. A second principle is that the largest cost reductions come from removing entire categories of parts rather than negotiating lower prices on the parts that remain. The same sequence—list the required functions, merge what silicon can merge, stamp or print what metal can replace—now applies to any radio that still carries legacy module boundaries. Which other high-volume electronic product still prices itself as an assembly of yesterday’s proven blocks?
