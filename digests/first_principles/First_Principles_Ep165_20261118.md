> **Two motors and a battery worth three hundred dollars power a fifteen-thousand-dollar wheelchair.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
A power wheelchair moves on two brushless motors and one lithium pack whose combined factory price sits between three hundred and four hundred dollars. Yet the same chair, once it reaches a user through the dealer and insurance channel, routinely clears fifteen thousand dollars. The gap is not hidden in exotic alloys or secret physics. It sits in the layers of certification, billing codes, small-volume assembly, and restricted parts access that have grown around the product.

### Segment 2 — Why It Costs What It Costs Today
Power wheelchairs reach the market through a narrow set of durable-medical-equipment suppliers whose business model depends on Medicare and private-insurance reimbursement codes. National Seating & Mobility and Numotion together control the majority of U.S. supply, each posting annual revenue above four hundred million dollars. Under this structure a manufacturer ships a chair that meets HCPCS code requirements, then the dealer adds seating, programming, and documentation that trigger higher reimbursement tiers. Repairs follow the same path: the same two firms hold the parts and the service contracts, so independent shops cannot obtain motors, controllers, or harnesses. A 2022 survey by U.S. PIRG found that forty percent of users waited seven weeks or longer for a fix. State right-to-repair bills, including New York’s S.4500 passed in 2026, attempt to force release of documentation and parts, yet the installed base still moves through the original channel. The result is that the same commodity motors and batteries used in e-bikes and scooters acquire layers of regulatory clearance, custom seating molds, and dealer margins before they reach the user.

The billing codes themselves add steps that multiply cost. Codes such as E2377 for an expandable controller or E2310 for actuator connections require specific documentation of medical necessity before reimbursement flows. Dealers therefore maintain large inventories of pre-certified configurations rather than ordering commodity parts on demand. This inventory ties up capital and creates carrying costs that get passed forward. When a motor fails, the same documentation chain restarts, extending downtime. The structure rewards firms that control both the hardware and the reimbursement paperwork, because any outsider attempting repair must first obtain the same clearances that the original supplier already holds. Over time the channel becomes self-reinforcing: small production runs keep per-unit certification costs high, which in turn discourages new entrants who might otherwise compete on price.

### Segment 3 — The Magic Wand Number & The First Principles Ratio
The dominant physical inputs are two 24-volt 250-watt brushless motors, one 24-volt 10–20 Ah lithium-ion pack, an aluminum or steel frame, and a basic electronic controller. Source data from 1000Mobility’s October 2026 factory pricing give the motors at forty-five to eighty dollars each and the battery at one hundred twenty to two hundred fifty dollars. Adding a conservative allowance for frame tubing, wiring harness, and controller board brings the sum of these commodity inputs to roughly four hundred fifty dollars at the high end of the quoted ranges. A finished power wheelchair that clears fifteen thousand dollars after all mark-ups therefore carries a First Principles Ratio of roughly thirty-three. The largest slice of that multiplier sits in regulatory clearance, insurance coding, and the dealer channel rather than in the cost of shaping aluminum or winding copper. Raw materials themselves account for thirty-five to forty percent of the factory FOB price, electronics twenty-five to thirty percent, and labor fifteen to twenty percent; the remaining steps—certification, small-batch seating, and distribution—multiply the factory figure several times more before the chair reaches the end user.

To see where the arithmetic lands, consider the motors first. Each 250-watt brushless unit is already produced at scale for e-bikes; the quoted OEM range of forty-five to eighty dollars reflects that volume. Doubling the figure for two motors yields ninety to one hundred sixty dollars. The lithium pack at one hundred twenty to two hundred fifty dollars supplies the energy storage; even at the upper end the pack remains a standard 24-volt module interchangeable with scooter packs. Frame tubing, whether aluminum or steel, adds another one hundred to two hundred dollars once cut and welded. The controller board and basic harness close the electrical loop for perhaps fifty dollars more. Summing these four categories at their higher bounds still stays below four hundred fifty dollars. The ratio of thirty-three therefore measures everything that happens after those parts leave the factory floor: the cost of obtaining Medicare coding approval, the cost of custom-molded seating that satisfies documentation rules, the dealer margin required to maintain service contracts, and the carrying cost of chairs that sit in warehouses while paperwork clears. Each of those steps is real and necessary under current rules, yet none of them alters the atoms inside the motors or the cells inside the battery.

### Segment 4 — The First-Principles Opportunity
Open-source projects already demonstrate one route around the current stack. The Lafayette College wheelchair team built a prototype assist using two ODrive motors at ninety-nine dollars each, a forty-eight-volt ten-amp-hour pack at one hundred eighty-nine dollars, and an ODrive controller at one hundred fifty-nine dollars, for a total bill of materials of one thousand three hundred twenty-seven dollars. An Instructables builder reached a complete chair for roughly two thousand dollars using off-the-shelf scooter parts. Right-to-repair statutes now advancing in seven states would let independent technicians and owners buy the same motors and harnesses that manufacturers currently withhold. A larger redesign would first standardize the controller interface so any certified electronics package could plug into any frame, second publish the actuator and harness pinouts required by the E2310–E2377 billing codes, and third move seating from one-off foam molds to modular, adjustable modules that still satisfy Medicare documentation. Each of those steps requires policy change and volume commitments before the ratio can fall; without them the regulatory and channel costs remain the binding constraint.

One objection is that medical devices must meet higher safety thresholds than consumer scooters. The counter is that the same motors and cells already pass UL and CE testing when sold for e-bikes; the additional wheelchair-specific requirements center on documentation of medical necessity rather than on new material properties. Another objection is that custom seating addresses individual anatomy. Modular frames with adjustable mounting points and interchangeable cushions could still accommodate that variation while allowing larger production batches of the core drive unit. The open-source examples show that the electronics and powertrain can be assembled and tested by small teams once the interface specifications are public. Scaling that approach would require manufacturers or regulators to release the pinouts and firmware hooks that currently sit behind service contracts. Once released, independent fabricators could produce replacement controllers or seating modules that still qualify for the same HCPCS codes, cutting the time between failure and repair from weeks to days.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A chair whose motors and battery already exist as e-bike commodities shows that the expensive part is the permission structure wrapped around those commodities. Releasing the same parts under published interfaces would let repair cycles shrink from months to days without new materials science. Who will publish the first open controller pinout that still satisfies the existing HCPCS codes, and what volume commitment would make that pinout the default for the next Medicare cycle?
