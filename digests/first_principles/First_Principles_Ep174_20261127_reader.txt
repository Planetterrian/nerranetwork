In practice the treatment occurs inside outpatient clinics that maintain nursing ratios, infection-control protocols, and real-estate overhead.

Payment systems reimburse per session rather than per outcome or per home treatment, so the incentive structure rewards the existing site model.

Regulatory rules on water quality, machine maintenance, and emergency response add further layers that clinics must satisfy regardless of patient volume. These layers feel normal inside the industry because the original chronic programs were built around hospital-adjacent centers.

The result is a service whose price reflects organization and compliance more than the cost of the filter or the treated water. When a clinic must keep a fixed number of chairs ready for staggered appointments, any unused slot still incurs the same rent, utilities, and standby staff costs.

Infection-control rules require dedicated water-treatment loops and regular validation testing that cannot be skipped even on low-volume days. Because reimbursement is tied to the clinic visit rather than the delivered filtration, there is little financial reward for shifting suitable patients elsewhere.

Stanley Shaldon began training patients for unattended overnight home dialysis in London between nineteen sixty-one and nineteen sixty-four, shifting the site from clinic to residence. That change cut facility and staffing expense while keeping the same filter and fluid, and it removed the clinic overhead by moving overnight treatments into patients’ homes.

Those limits show that technology alone is not enough. Payment and regulatory structures must also change for the cost to move closer to the physical floor.

A from-scratch redesign would first standardize compact machines that patients or caregivers can set up with minimal training. A compact machine would need built-in sensors that automatically pause treatment and alert a remote monitor if pressure or temperature drifts outside safe limits.

Second, it would move water treatment and dialysate preparation to point-of-use cartridges that meet regulatory standards without central pharmacy oversight. Those cartridges would have to carry pre-sterilized fluid paths whose integrity can be verified by the patient with a simple visual or electronic check rather than laboratory sampling.

Third, it would adjust payment rules so that reimbursement follows the patient rather than the clinic chair. Reimbursement that follows the patient would let a verified home setup receive the same or greater payment than a clinic session, removing the financial penalty that currently discourages the shift.

Each step requires reliable low-maintenance hardware, clear infection-control protocols that families can follow, and regulatory acceptance of distributed water quality testing. The hard parts are infection risk, machine failure during unsupervised hours, and the need for rapid emergency response when something goes wrong outside a clinic.

Infection risk remains the clearest barrier, because any breach in sterile technique at home lacks the immediate backup of on-site staff. Machine failure during the night would require either redundant systems or a response protocol that reaches the patient faster than typical emergency services.

Regulatory bodies would need to accept patient-performed water tests as equivalent to clinic-grade validation, which demands documented proof that such tests catch the same contaminants with comparable reliability.

When a medical service keeps its price high long after the core materials become inexpensive, the binding constraint is usually the rules that decide where and by whom the treatment can occur. A licence can outlast the room that first made the treatment possible. Once the check can happen at the bedside, the remaining expense is permission, not the membrane.

The open question is which payer or regulator will first test a reimbursement model that rewards verified home setups rather than clinic volume.
