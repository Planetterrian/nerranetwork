A rocket engine once priced in the millions

This is a daily show that reasons about the world
from first principles.
Most decisions in engineering and manufacturing begin
by studying what already works
and then trimming a few percent here or there.
That habit feels prudent because it limits immediate risk,
yet it also carries forward every assumption
that was baked into the original design.
First Principles reasoning begins instead
with the atoms that are actually required,
the energy that must be moved,
and the strength that must be achieved.
The method itself is not new.
Henry Ford calculated the raw price of steel
and gasoline needed to move a passenger
rather than accepting carriage prices as given.
Henry Bessemer asked how little pig iron
truly had to be processed to produce usable steel
and thereby lowered its price
by roughly an order of magnitude inside a decade.
The solar and semiconductor industries
have applied the same pattern for 60 years,
driving costs down measured curves in sense per watt
or sense per transistor.
Elon Musk later gave the two analytical tools,
their current shorthand names,
and applied them publicly to rockets and vehicles.
The underlying habit of thought reaches across more than a century
and across many fields.
The first tool is the magic wand number.
That is the price you would pay
if a wand instantly assembled the finished item
from its constituent atoms at commodity rates,
with no labor, no tooling, and no overhead.
The second tool is the idiot index.
It is simply the finished price
divided by that raw material floor.
A part that sells for $1,000
but contains $10 of aluminum
carries an idiot index of 100.
The number itself is only a signal
that the design or the process, not the material,
is carrying the cost.
Consider a simple aerospace bracket
often cited in public cost audits.
The finished machined titanium bracket
might be purchased for roughly $800.
The same volume of titanium alloy in plate form
trades at commodity prices near $40.
Dividing $800 by 40 yields an idiot index of 20.
That ratio tells an engineer
the bracket is announcing a manufacturing
or design problem, not a shortage of metal.
A high index is therefore an invitation
rather than a fixed fact of nature.
Lowering it requires changing how the part is conceived
or produced.
This show returns every day to either one concrete case
in which someone has driven an idiot index downward
or one domain where the gap between current price
and material floor remains large enough to be interesting.
The goal is simply to make the arithmetic visible
so listeners can judge the reasoning themselves.
SpaceX's Raptor engine illustrates the same logic
applied to a far more complex system.
The engine uses a full-flow stage combustion cycle.
In that cycle, both fuel rich and oxidizer-rich pre-burners
drive separate turbo pumps before all propellant flows
through the main chamber.
The architecture extracts more energy from each kilogram
of methane and liquid oxygen than a gas generator cycle can.
It therefore allows chamber pressures
above 250 bar and specific impulse values competitive
with far more exotic propellants.
Methane and oxygen were selected because they can be produced
on Mars from local resources.
Methane also avoids the coaking
that kerosene leaves on injector surfaces.
Cryogenic handling from methane and liquid oxygen
is simpler than the extreme insulation required
for liquid hydrogen.
Across successive versions, the design team
attacked part count and mass by consolidating manifolds
that had previously been separate machined forging.
Where earlier prototypes used dozens
of individual propellant lines and valves,
later iterations merged those lines
into single printed or cast housings.
That step removed dozens of potential leak paths
and hundreds of assembly hours per engine.
Vowel families were reduced by altering sequencing logic
so that a smaller set of actuators could serve multiple functions
without sacrificing safety margins.
Additive manufacturing replaced many traditionally machined
components whose internal passages
had required extensive drilling and welding.
The printed parts weigh less because excess material
can be omitted from locations that finite element analysis
shows carry little load.
Each of these steps move the engine closer
to its material floor by eliminating labor,
fasteners, seals, and inspection points
rather than by substituting exotic alloys.
Public statements and regulatory filings place early raptor
unit costs in the low millions.
Later versions are described in the same sources
as having reached the mid-six figure range,
though exact current figures remain proprietary.
Comparable legacy booster engines using similar thrust classes
have historically carried higher per unit prices
when measured on the same accounting basis.
The idiot index for raptor is therefore lower
than for engines whose designs froze decades ago.
It is still well above one because turbo machinery,
chamber liners, and nozzle extensions
continue to require precision forming and joining
that exceed the raw cost of the nickel alloys
and copper alloys involved.
The trajectory matters more than any single snapshot.
Each consolidation move demonstrably reduced
both mass and recurring labor.
That proves the original high index was a design artifact
rather than an immutable physical limit.
Residential construction offers a contrasting case
in which the gap between finished price
and raw material cost remains large and persistent.
A typical new single family home in North America
carries a construction cost, excluding land,
reported by industry surveys in the range
of 200 to $400 per square foot,
depending on region and finishes.
The major raw materials dimension a lumber,
concrete, oriented strand board, drywall, copper wire,
and asphalt shingles can be priced at commodity levels
whose sum for an average sized house
falls on the order of 50 to $80,000
according to public wholesale indices.
Dividing a $300,000 construction cost
by a $70,000 material estimate produces a rough
idiot index between four and five.
The true figure is higher once land, financing,
and permitting are included.
The difference arises from multiple stack stages
rather than from any single villain.
On-site stick framing requires skilled labor
for each custom cut,
whether delays compound carrying costs on borrowed capital.
Fragmented subcontractors each add margin
for coordination risk, permitting timelines measured
in months rather than days freeze capital
and inflate interest expense.
Factory built approaches have demonstrated lower costs
and controlled settings by moving
those same operations indoors.
They still confront building codes written around
site built assumptions and local inspection regimes
that treat each house as a one off.
A first principles redesign would therefore begin
by fixing the structural frame and utility runs
in a repeatable module whose interfaces are standardized
across many units.
That allows the same pressure tested assemblies
to be inspected once at the factory
rather than repeatedly in the field.
Next would come substitution of high volume processes
such as automated framing tables
or continuous panel lines for hand cut lumber,
provided the modules remain compatible
with existing transport limits and crane capacities.
Policy changes that allow performance-based codes
instead of prescriptive material rules would be required
before those modules could be deployed at national scale.
The genuinely hard constraints are not physics
but coordination.
Lenders, insurers and code officials
must be convinced that a factory certified assembly
meets safety thresholds without requiring
the same sequence of field inspections
that evolved for site built homes.
Where those barriers have been lowered
