The motors are brushless. The pack is lithium. Their combined factory price sits between three hundred and four hundred dollars, and the chair that carries them still has to pass a dealer and an insurance channel before anyone rides it. Nothing in that passage is an exotic alloy or a hidden law of physics.

The added layers are certification, billing codes, small-volume assembly, and parts an outsider is not allowed to buy.

Power wheelchairs reach people through a narrow set of durable-medical-equipment suppliers whose model depends on Medicare and private-insurance reimbursement. National Seating and Mobility and Numotion together control the majority of United States supply, and each posts annual revenue above four hundred million dollars. A manufacturer ships a chair that already meets the code requirements.

The dealer then adds seating, programming, and documentation that trigger higher reimbursement tiers.

Repairs walk the same path. Those two firms hold the parts and the service contracts, so an independent shop cannot obtain the motors, the controllers, or the harnesses. A twenty twenty-two survey by U.S. PIRG found that forty percent of users waited seven weeks or longer for a fix.

State right-to-repair bills, including New York S.4500, passed in twenty twenty-six, try to force the release of documentation and parts. The installed base still moves through the original channel. Commodity motors and batteries pick up regulatory clearance, custom seating molds, and dealer margins before they reach the person who needs the chair.

The codes themselves add steps that multiply cost. Under the Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System, HCPCS, code E2377 covers an expandable controller and code E2310 covers actuator connections. Each one demands specific documentation of medical necessity before reimbursement flows.

Dealers therefore keep large inventories of pre-certified configurations instead of ordering commodity parts when a chair is ordered. That stock ties up capital. The carrying cost is passed forward. When a motor fails, the documentation chain starts over, and the downtime stretches.

The structure rewards the firm that holds both the hardware and the paperwork, because an outsider who wants to repair must first obtain the clearances the original supplier already has. Small production runs keep the certification cost per chair high, which discourages a new entrant who might otherwise compete on price. The channel reinforces itself.

Set the channel aside for a moment and price the atoms. The physical inputs that actually move the chair are two twenty-four-volt, two-hundred-fifty-watt brushless motors, one twenty-four-volt lithium-ion pack of ten to twenty amp-hours, an aluminum or steel frame, and a basic electronic controller.

If you could wave a magic wand and arrange those raw materials into the finished thing for free, the bill would be the price of the commodities alone.

Factory pricing published by 1000Mobility in October twenty twenty-six puts each motor at forty-five to eighty dollars and the battery at one hundred twenty to two hundred fifty dollars. Those motors are already built at scale for e-bikes, which is why the original-equipment range sits there. Two motors, then, run from ninety dollars to one hundred sixty.

Even at the top of its range the pack is a standard twenty-four-volt module, interchangeable with a scooter pack. Frame tubing, aluminum or steel, cut and welded, adds another one hundred to two hundred dollars. A controller board and a basic harness close the electrical loop for perhaps fifty dollars more.

Add the high ends: one hundred sixty for the motors, two hundred fifty for the pack, two hundred for the frame, fifty for the controller and harness. The sum still stays under four hundred fifty dollars. Call that the magic wand number at the cautious top of the quoted ranges, about four hundred fifty dollars.

A finished power wheelchair that routinely clears fifteen thousand dollars, divided by that four-hundred-fifty-dollar floor, carries a First Principles Ratio near thirty-three. The ratio is a measurement of where the price sits. It is not a verdict on the people who design, fit, or service the chair.

The largest slice of the multiplier sits in regulatory clearance, insurance coding, and the dealer channel, not in the cost of shaping aluminum or winding copper. None of those later steps changes the atoms inside the motors or the cells inside the battery. Each step is real under the rules as they stand.

A different cut of the same factory, not the retail price, shows how the invoice itself is built. Of the factory free-on-board price, raw materials account for thirty-five to forty percent, electronics for twenty-five to thirty percent, and labor for fifteen to twenty percent.

Certification, small-batch seating, and distribution then multiply that factory figure several more times before the chair reaches the user. The First Principles Ratio of near thirty-three is that later multiplication, stacked on a commodity floor, not a claim that the factory invoice is already thirty-three times the metal.

What the ratio is counting, after the parts leave the floor, is the cost of Medicare coding approval, the cost of custom-molded seating that satisfies the documentation rules, the dealer margin required to keep service contracts alive, and the carrying cost of chairs that sit in a warehouse while paperwork clears.

Open-source work already shows one way around that stack. The Lafayette College wheelchair team built a prototype assist on two ODrive motors at ninety-nine dollars each, a forty-eight-volt, ten-amp-hour pack at one hundred eighty-nine dollars, and an ODrive controller at one hundred fifty-nine dollars. Their bill of materials totaled one thousand three hundred twenty-seven dollars.

An Instructables builder reached a complete chair for about two thousand dollars with off-the-shelf scooter parts. Right-to-repair statutes now advancing in seven states would let independent technicians and owners buy the motors and harnesses that manufacturers currently withhold.

A larger redesign has three moves, and each one needs a policy change and a volume commitment before the ratio can fall. First, standardize the controller interface so any certified electronics package can plug into any frame. Second, publish the actuator and harness pinouts that codes E2310 through E2377 already assume.

Third, shift seating from one-off foam molds to modular, adjustable modules that still satisfy Medicare documentation. Without those moves, regulatory cost and channel cost stay the binding constraint.

One objection is that a medical device must clear a higher safety bar than a consumer scooter. The same motors and cells already pass Underwriters Laboratories and CE testing when they are sold for e-bikes. The extra wheelchair-specific requirements center on documentation of medical necessity, not on new material properties.

Another objection is that custom seating has to match an individual body. A modular frame, with adjustable mounting points and interchangeable cushions, can still take that variation, while the core drive unit is built in larger batches. The open-source chairs show that a small team can assemble and test the electronics and the powertrain once the interface specifications are public.

Scaling that approach means a manufacturer or a regulator releases the pinouts and the firmware hooks that now sit behind service contracts. Independent fabricators could then build replacement controllers or seating modules that still qualify for the same HCPCS codes, and the wait between failure and repair could shrink from weeks to days.

The portable lesson is about permission, not a new material. A chair whose motors and battery already exist as e-bike commodities is a designed thing, so the First Principles Ratio has a job to do. The job is to locate the gap. Here the expensive part is the permission structure wrapped around those commodities: who may code the chair, who may stock the part, who may touch it when it fails.

That measurement does not scold the clinician who fits a seat or the technician who answers a service call. Those tasks stay necessary. It says the multiplier lives after the copper is wound.

Some prices should never be told as engineering stories. A scarce view, a captive audience, a brand — those are market power, and this show says so and moves on. This chair is mixed, and the mix is the point. Two suppliers and a closed parts bin are market structure. Documentation that must exist before reimbursement moves is a rule. Under that rule the steps are genuine.

A from-scratch rethink has room only in the slice that is process, organisation, or rules, not in a pretend shortage of lithium. Naming the slice wrongly sends the next builder hunting for a lighter alloy when the binding constraint is a locked interface.

Releasing the same parts under published interfaces would let repair cycles shrink from months to days without new materials science. A pinout is a map of which wire does which job. A harness spec is that map for the actuators. Modular cushions on adjustable mounts keep the fit to a person’s body without sending every frame through a one-off mold.

None of that asks the cells to become a different chemistry. It asks someone to make the specification buyable, so more than one pair of hands can meet a code that already exists. Prototypes have shown the drive unit can be built once the parts are on the open market. What a prototype cannot do alone is make that interface the ordinary path through the next reimbursement cycle.

That takes volume, committed in advance, large enough that the code expects the open pinout rather than a closed service contract.
