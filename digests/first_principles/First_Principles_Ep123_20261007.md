> **The pneumatic tire cut raw material costs to under two dollars, but early versions sold for over twenty because of manual assembly and patents.**

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
Before John Dunlop’s 1888 patent, bicycle tires were solid bands of rubber that transmitted every jolt from the road. Dunlop’s design swapped that solid mass for a thin envelope of cotton canvas, sulfur, and a small amount of natural rubber inflated with air. The raw ingredients for one tire fell to roughly the price of a few pounds of those commodities, yet the first commercial versions still commanded prices several times higher because every step remained hand work protected by patents.

### Segment 2 — The Old Way (Reasoning By Analogy)
Solid rubber tires had been the accepted solution since the 1860s because they were simple to mold and required no inner pressure. Makers copied the same thick cross-section year after year, varying only the rubber compound or tread pattern. The finished tire therefore carried the full cost of a large volume of expensive natural rubber plus the labor of building up layers by hand on a mandrel. Early bicycle manufacturers accepted this cost structure because no one had yet asked what minimum material was actually needed to carry the rider’s weight. The convention also suited the small production runs of the time; each tire was essentially a custom article. When Dunlop introduced the pneumatic alternative, the industry’s first reaction was that the new tire was fragile and complicated rather than that the old tire had been wasteful. Patent holders reinforced the status quo by charging royalties that further discouraged volume production. As a result, the price of a pneumatic tire stayed well above the cost of its ingredients for more than a decade. The default approach treated the tire as a single molded block whose thickness had to supply both cushioning and strength. That assumption locked in high material use because no one separated the functions of load support and shock absorption. Hand lay-up on wooden forms added hours of skilled labor per tire, and the small market kept presses and calendering equipment from reaching economical scale. Makers therefore viewed the high price as an unavoidable feature of the product rather than a signal that the design itself carried unnecessary mass and steps.

### Segment 3 — The First-Principles Move
Dunlop began by asking what physical job the tire had to perform: support load, absorb shock, and roll with low resistance. The answer pointed to an air cushion rather than a solid block of rubber. Once air was accepted as the primary structural element, the remaining materials shrank to a thin, airtight envelope. A rough magic-wand estimate for that envelope starts with a few pounds of natural rubber at roughly thirty cents per pound in 1890 prices, a yard or two of cotton canvas at a few cents per yard, and a small quantity of sulfur for vulcanization. Adding those commodity values yields a floor well under two dollars per tire. The early selling price above twenty dollars therefore implied an Idiot Index on the order of ten or more. The first decisive redesign step was to replace the solid rubber mass with an inflatable tube made from rubber-coated canvas. This single change eliminated most of the rubber volume while preserving load-carrying capacity through air pressure. The second move was to develop a separate outer cover that could be removed for repair; that separation let the inner tube be made lighter and the outer tread thicker only where wear occurred. The third step was to mechanize the application of rubber to fabric using early calendering machines instead of brushing it on by hand. Each of these changes attacked a different part of the old Idiot Index: the tube redesign attacked material volume, the cover redesign attacked waste in the wear surface, and the calendering step attacked labor hours. The key engineering trade-off in every case was maintaining airtightness and structural integrity while reducing total material and assembly time. One objection often raised is that air pressure itself introduces new failure modes such as punctures; the redesign addressed this by making the inner tube replaceable without discarding the entire tire. Another concern is that the cotton canvas would stretch or leak under repeated flexing; vulcanization with sulfur cross-linked the rubber to the fabric, creating a composite that held pressure while remaining flexible. Scaling the new process required presses capable of consistent heat and pressure across larger batches, which arrived only when bicycle and later automobile demand justified the capital investment. The same logic later extended to car tires once Ford-era volume made continuous calender lines and molded sidewalls practical. Each incremental consolidation reduced the number of hand operations per tire and thereby lowered the multiplier between raw-material cost and finished price.

### Segment 4 — The Result & The Limits
By the early 1900s the combination of volume production and the expiration of key patents had driven tire prices down sharply, though still above the raw-material floor. The Idiot Index fell as presses and calender lines replaced hand lay-up, yet it never reached one because vulcanization, quality control, and distribution still added real cost. The remaining gap reflected genuine process requirements rather than simple inertia. Even today, commodity tires carry an Idiot Index well above the theoretical minimum because the manufacturing sequence must guarantee safety under high-speed and high-load conditions. The redesign proved that air could carry the load with far less rubber, but it also showed that the new process introduced its own irreducible steps such as curing under pressure and testing for leaks. Those steps keep the finished price several times the commodity floor even after more than a century of refinement.

### Segment 5 — The Lesson
A tire whose price is set by how many hands touch it rather than by how little rubber it contains is announcing that the process itself is the dominant expense. The same logic applies whenever a product’s cost is dominated by assembly steps that could be collapsed by changing the form of the materials.
