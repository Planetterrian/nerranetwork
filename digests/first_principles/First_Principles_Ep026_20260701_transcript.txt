Single-stream collection spread across North America after 2000, letting people drop every recyclable
into one bin. Participation climbed, yet the arriving stream carried food scraps, shards of glass
tangled with paper, and plastics that never belonged in the system. Recovery plants added screens,
magnets, eddy current devices, and optical readers. Yet human pickers still stand at several stations
because contamination often tops 15%. Each extra touch adds wages and slows the whole line.
Commodity markets swing sharply, so when prices for new resin or glass drop, buyers reject
even slightly dirty loads and the plant pays landfill fees instead. Rules that count any recovered
content toward mandates push facilities to keep accepting low-value items whose sale price never
covers the work required. The headline recovery numbers therefore hide ongoing support from city
budgets, and the whole business stays tied to the price of virgin materials rather than any built-in
advantage of reuse. Some argue that keeping material out of landfills supplies enough public
benefit to justify the support, yet that view leaves the physical cost of cleaning and moving the
items untouched. Others expect rising landfill charges to flip the economics, but those charges
sit on top of an already negative margin for most streams. The pattern holds because every player
focuses on the visible collection rate rather than the hidden value of the raw materials.
Take one representative ton of single-stream residential material. A rough, magic wand estimate
begins with the market prices of its main pieces, aluminum near $1,500 for each ton of recovered
metal, clear glass cullet near $40, PET and HDPE together near $200 once turned into pellets,
and the leftover mixed plastics near zero or below zero after cleaning and residue handling.
Waiting those values by how much of each fraction usually appears produces a blended raw material
floor on the order of $60 to $90 for each ton that arrives. Current delivered costs for the
separated streams run several times higher once collection, sorting and residue handling enter
the picture. The overall idiot index therefore lands between 3 and 5, yet the number splits sharply
by material. Aluminum sits near 1 because its sale price already covers sorting, while glass and
most plastics sit above 10. The extra expense appears in 3 clear stages. Manual and optical separation
work adds roughly $80 for each ton process. Washing the dirty streams with energy and water adds
another $30 to $40. Moving and disposing of the 15 to 25% residue fraction adds still more.
Because every one of those steps happens before any sale, net revenue from glass and plastic portions
often turns negative, which is why plants increasingly send them to landfills when virgin prices are
weak. Consider a bail of mixed PET and HDPE that sells for $200 after sorting. Reaching market purity
may have required $150 in upstream labor and energy, leaving too little margin to recover collection
or capital costs. Faster optical sorters may trim the labor line, yet the sensors still face the
same variability from food residue and label glue that no present machine removes without extra washing
energy. The mismatch is therefore built into the process itself rather than waiting for one more
piece of equipment. A redesign would start by cutting the contamination that drives labor and residue
expenses. Packaging made from fewer resin types and fitted with labels that peel away cleanly would
let optical readers reach higher purity without hand sorting. Deposit return programs for drinks
already show that pulling mixed streams out at the point of sale can have sorting expense while
supplying cleaner aluminum and PET. Robotic picking guided by vision systems is moving forward,
but its numbers still need steady, low variety input. Policy that set recycled content rules only
for materials whose recovered value exceeds sorting cost, would direct spending toward aluminum,
steel, and a narrow group of rigid plastics instead of spreading support across every resin.
The remaining hard limit is the scattered geography of collection.
Hauling low-density bales long distances eats margins unless regional pre-processing hubs sit
near manufacturing sites. None of these steps needs new physics, only better alignment between how
products are made, how they are gathered, and the actual commodity prices. The goal is an industry
whose numbers no longer rely on outside support once the idiot index for each material drops below
two. Consumers sometimes resist shifts in familiar packaging, yet the same resistance once met
returnable glass bottles and faded once deposit rules matched behavior to recovery economics.
Earlier global trade in waste once hid domestic costs, but tighter import rules have simply made
the mismatch between sorting expense and material value impossible to ignore. When the price of
separating a material exceeds the value of the atoms it contains, the activity is performing
