Medieval book production treated each volume as a unique object.
Its cost was dominated by the time required to form every letter by hand.
Monasteries and later commercial scriptoria organized labor around the assumption

Illumination and binding added further weeks or months.
Because the process scaled only by adding more scribes,
the finished price of a book remained high even when the raw materials were comparatively modest.
Those materials were parchment or early rag paper and simple carbon ink.
That price was accepted as natural because every workshop had inherited the same workflow

The result was an idiot index measured not in dollars but in labor hours.
The finished book embodied perhaps 50 to 100 times the material cost
simply because every character had to be drawn anew.
The convention rested on an unspoken premise that legibility and accuracy demanded
continuous human judgment at every stroke.
A scribe could adjust spacing, correct errors on the fly,

All of this happened without separate tools.
When a workshop considered producing two identical copies,

No one searched for a way for one set of letter forms to serve both.
This approach made sense under the constraint that each page was an original performance.
The labor term could not be amortized because the performance itself was the product.

The assumption that text reproduction must be a repeated act of writing was never tested
against the physics of a reusable mark. Gutenberg began by asking what the irreducible physical
requirements of a reproducible page actually were. Once the question was posed that way,
the expensive term became obvious.
The repeated formation of each letter shape was the term that mattered.
The solution was to make the letter itself a durable, interchangeable object
rather than a fleeting gesture of the hand. He needed four tightly coupled inventions.
First came the type metal alloy. It was roughly led with tin and antimony.
That alloy could be cast repeatedly into crisp, reusable letters without rapid wear or distortion.
Second was the hand mold that allowed a single skilled worker to produce hundreds of identical
sorts per day. Third was an oil-based ink that would adhere to metal yet transferred
cleanly to paper under pressure. This differed from the water-based inks used for woodblock printing.
Fourth was the screw press, adapted from existing wine and olive presses. It could exert even force across
an entire form of type. Each element attacked the same underlying constraint. That constraint was
the cost of forming one character on one page. With movable type, the labor of designing the letter
shape was paid once, when the punch was cut. Every subsequent impression became a matter of composition,
inking and pressing. A rough magic wand estimate for the materials in a 300-page book
points to several reams of rag paper plus a few ounces of ink. That estimate suggests a floor,
perhaps 120th the price of a comparable manuscript. The old manuscript therefore carried an idiot index
on the order of 20 to 50. The exact ratio varied with illumination and binding. It was always
dominated by scribe time rather than material scarcity. The new system moved that index downward by
converting the expensive variable into a fixed capital cost for the type itself. Human attention
per character became that fixed capital cost. Later refinements such as the shift from wooden to
metal type and the gradual improvement of paper quality continued the same logic. They did so without
changing the core decomposition. The hand mold itself embodied a further decomposition. A matrix
carrying the letter shape was struck once from a hardened punch. Molten alloy was then poured into
the mold cavity and cooled under controlled pressure. This kept the face flat and the shank
dimensionally consistent. The same matrix could generate thousands of sorts before it wore.
That spread the cost of the original punch across an entire font. The oil-based ink required its own
chemistry. Linced oil boiled with resins produced a viscous vehicle. It stayed on the raised metal
surface yet released under the pressure of the press. This avoided the feathering that water-based
inks produced on paper. The press itself had to be modified so that the platen descended parallel
and with repeatable force. Too little pressure left faint impressions. Too much crushed the paper
or damaged the type. Each of these steps addressed a specific failure mode that would have kept the
idiot index high if left unsolved. Without a durable alloy the sorts would have deformed after
a few hundred impressions. That would force frequent recasting and erase most of the labor savings.
Without compatible ink the metal type would have been no better than wood blocks that already
existed. The four inventions therefore had to arrive together. Anyone alone would have left the
per-page costs still dominated by manual formation of characters. One might object that block printing
already existed and could have been scaled instead. Yet a wood block or a full-page metal plate
still required carving or engraving every character for every new title. The labor was merely shifted
from the scribes pen to the cutter's chisel. Only when the unit became the single letter cast
from a reusable matrix did the design cost amortize across many different texts. The arithmetic is
straightforward once the unit is isolated. If a punch set for a full alphabet cost the equivalent
of several months of a skilled worker's time that cost divided across 10,000 impressions per sort
and then across dozens of books drops the per-page contribution of type creation to a small fraction
of the original scribe hour expense. Within a generation the price of an ordinary book fell by roughly
an order of magnitude. The number of titles in circulation rose sharply. Literacy spread beyond
clerical and aristocratic circles. Vernacular Bibles and political pamphlets became practical tools
rather than luxuries. The new idiot index for a plain printed volume approached the cost of paper
and ink plus the amortized cost of type and press time. That was a far smaller multiple than the
scribe-based process had required. Yet the floor was never reached. Paper remained the largest single
material expense. Its production still depended on regs, water power and skilled vat work. Type
eventually wore or broke. Composition itself stayed a hand process until the 19th century.
The system also inherited the limitations of the substrate. Books were still physical objects that

problems rather than invisible assumptions baked into the price of every page. The decisive step
