**Japan’s 10-year bond coupon tops budgeted rate in ‘sci-fi’ spike: Asahi**
In Japan, in Asia, the Finance Ministry set the coupon on newly issued 10-year government bonds at 3.1 percent a year at an October 6 auction, the highest in 30 years and above the 3 percent assumed for fiscal 2026 debt-servicing costs. The coupon is the interest promised to investors, so the rise means higher payments on new government debt and could narrow fiscal room. The rate was 2.1 percent from January to March, 2.4 percent in April and 2.7 percent in July, after the Bank of Japan began raising rates in 2024 and long-term rates rose by more than 1 percentage point since Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi took office last October. A senior Finance Ministry official said the pace felt like science fiction, because the coupon caught the budgeted rate in the middle of the fiscal year. The ministry said the higher coupon will not immediately block financing, since bonds issued in the first six months of fiscal 2026 came in below the 3 percent assumption.

**Bolivia lands $214M from IMF. The full $1.9B comes with strings attached: Latin Times**
In Bolivia, in South America, the IMF Executive Board approved a 1.9 billion dollar, 36-month Extended Fund Facility on October 2, releasing 214 million dollars at once, the country’s first multi-year IMF arrangement since 2006. The Finance Ministry said that first tranche was set to reach government accounts on Tuesday or Wednesday. Further payments depend on reform benchmarks, including zero central-bank financing of the deficit and sustained fiscal consolidation, and the program is expected to catalyze about 4 billion dollars more, for potential support near 5.9 billion dollars. Latin Times reported that liquid dollar reserves have shrunk to a fraction of total holdings, leaving the central bank little room to manage a newly flexible exchange rate. The lower chamber approved the deal on September 17 and the Senate ratified it the next day, with support from across the legislative spectrum.

### Both Sides: Should Washington state keep its tax on earnings above one million dollars?
Voters in Washington state will decide in November whether a 9.9 percent tax on earnings above 1 million dollars stays in force. The tax was signed into law in March. A conservative-backed initiative on the ballot would repeal it.

The initiative’s strongest case is that the tax is a slippery slope. Its backers claim that a levy written for earnings above 1 million dollars would eventually result in taxes for people who are not millionaires, and they are asking voters to stop that path before it widens.

The strongest case for keeping the tax comes from a coalition of advocacy groups and unions opposing the initiative. They say taxing the state’s roughly 20,000 highest-earning households could raise about 3.5 billion dollars a year. They say that revenue could fund education and healthcare and expand tax credits and sales-tax exemptions. Their best reason is money for those services from a narrow set of households, not a rate on everyone.

Both sides accept the same design: 9.9 percent, only on earnings above 1 million dollars, already signed into law, and now on November’s ballot. The next step is that vote. This edition does not say which side will prevail.
