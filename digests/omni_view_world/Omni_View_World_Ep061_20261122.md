**Bahrain says Iranian attack caused fire at industrial facility: Al Jazeera**
Bahrain said an Iranian attack on a civilian industrial facility caused a fire, and The National reported that Bahrain said two people were injured. The Bahraini Defence Force said defences intercepted several Iranian aerial attacks late on Saturday, after sirens sounded, the ministry told residents to move to the nearest safe place, and an all-clear followed. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps said the operation answered continued violations of Iranian airspace, including by U.S. MQ-1 and MQ-9 drones, and an attack on a radar installation on Hengam Island. The IRGC said missile and drone strikes targeted U.S. military positions at the Al-Riffa and Awali bases, that two radars belonging to U.S. Army Patriot air defence systems were struck, and that any further airspace violation would meet a harsh and decisive response. Bahrain hosts the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet and has been attacked repeatedly since the U.S.-Israel war on Iran began in late February, including three drone attacks in recent days after a lull of several weeks; Al Jazeera reported that other Gulf states have also come under repeated fire from Tehran since that war began. The Bahrain attack came as multiple tankers were reportedly struck in the Strait of Hormuz, through which about one-fifth of the world’s oil passed before the war; U.S. Central Command said last month it had helped Gulf countries export 1.5 billion barrels of oil via Hormuz since the summer, while the IRGC claimed U.S. escort and aerial protection had failed and caused significant losses to equipment and vessels under U.S. protection. Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said a Qatari delegation visited Tehran the previous evening and held two hours of talks, and state television quoted Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi saying ongoing regional talks must be given a chance and the blockade must be lifted rationally. Iran’s demands, as reported, include ending the war on all fronts, unfreezing Iranian assets, lifting oil sanctions and ending the U.S. counterblockade of Iranian ports, and Araghchi’s reported line is that discussion of opening the Strait of Hormuz is pointless until those demands are met. Bahrain’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs called on the U.N. Security Council to take firm measures to compel Iran to implement Resolution 2817.

**The U.S. has sufficient munitions to fight Iran — but what about China?: Deseret News**
The United States has sufficient munitions to renew the fight with Iran, according to an analysis the Center for Strategic and International Studies released this month, but the same analysis says there is great concern about a war with China. Rebuilding inventories, CSIS said, will require a multiyear commitment extending beyond the current crisis, and the report calls for a long-term national commitment to aggressive munitions production. Some inventories are in good shape, others are not, and a few are critically short; of high concern are costly ballistic missile defence systems, Patriot and THAAD, used heavily in the opening stages of the Iran war. Ballistic missile defence interceptors are in critical short supply, CSIS noted, and even before the Iran war such weaponry was already considered inadequate for a war against China, with no battlefield substitutes once supplies are diminished. Only highly specialized munitions can intercept theater ballistic missiles that reach a velocity of 5,000-plus mph in their terminal phase, the report noted, while aircraft and cruise missiles fly at about 800 mph and drones at 150-250 mph. An estimated two-thirds of the country’s prewar Patriot interceptor inventory have reportedly been expended defending against Iranian ballistic missiles, and Lockheed Martin has a goal to produce 2,000 Patriot interceptors each year, up from around 600. About half of the prewar THAAD inventory is estimated to have been expended in the Iran war, according to the CSIS analysis; Standard Missiles have not been used as widely, partly because they launch from naval vessels and U.S. ships have been stationed outside the Persian Gulf. A large supply of air-to-air missiles remains in U.S. inventory. Last summer on CBS News’ “Face the Nation,” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth labeled claims of a munitions stockpile shortage a “manufactured story” and said supplies “are only getting stronger,” while Senator Mark Kelly, Democrat of Arizona, said it is widely understood that attacking 10,000 targets from the air uses a lot of munitions and “we do not have an endless supply of these things.” It is unclear if or when President Donald Trump will order resumption of major military actions against Iran.

**Europe sees new protests over rising costs, inequality and public services: France 24**
France saw the sixth consecutive weekend of marches over the cost of living, public services and inequality, as Sunday marked a third consecutive day of protests across Europe, from Portugal to France, after large demonstrations on Friday and smaller rallies on Saturday. France 24 reported that the protests have largely focused on the rising cost of living and access to affordable housing. The movement, sparked in mid-September by high school students calling for improved learning conditions, has since fuelled a nationwide campaign against soaring prices, failing public services and police brutality, Al Jazeera reported. Across France, organisers reported 150,000 protesters on Saturday, down from 200,000 last week and 800,000 during the first of the weekly demonstrations on October 17. The Ministry of the Interior said numbers have “significantly declined,” putting Saturday’s turnout at 82,500 people, compared with 106,000 a week earlier. The protests have been largely peaceful but have seen sporadic violence on the margins and repeated accusations of police brutality. Last month, huge rallies across France descended into the worst rioting seen in years, with hundreds of shops looted and buildings attacked and set on fire. Unions and activists have called for further protests in the coming weeks.

**South Korea education minister resigns over exam cheating scandal: BBC**
South Korea’s education minister, Choi Kyo-jin, resigned on Sunday over an exam-cheating scandal, and President Lee Jae Myung, who has been calling for an overhaul of the exam system, accepted the resignation. Choi said he took full responsibility for confusion caused by last-minute measures aimed at stopping AI-assisted cheating during Thursday’s college entrance exam, known as the Suneung, telling Yonhap he could not escape criticism that the ministry failed to prepare in advance. The Suneung takes up to nine hours and plays a big role in the career trajectories of millions of South Koreans; about 500,000 students sat it this year. The education ministry said 111 candidates were found with unauthorised smart devices. A week earlier, Han Won-seok, 34, the top scorer in April’s national tax accountancy exam, with one wrong answer out of 160, admitted using a microcamera, a mobile phone and AI. In a video message he said he photographed the questions, sent them to an AI, and checked answers on his smartphone. Authorities then sent three nationwide emergency alerts between 08:00 and 15:00 local time on Thursday, the first use of the national disaster alert system to catch exam cheaters; phones blared even on silent, including tourists on data roaming, but not if switched off or on airplane mode. Candidates had to cover buttons on shirts and jackets, and authorities inspected eyewear. France 24 reported that the controversy centred on that tax-exam admission of a miniature camera and smartphone.

**Ontario Liberals choose Navdeep Bains as new leader: The Globe and Mail**
Canada’s Ontario Liberals chose Navdeep Bains as leader on Saturday, the third leader since 2020 elected without a seat in the provincial legislature. Bains, a former industry minister in Justin Trudeau’s government, won on the third ballot with 50.3 per cent, to 49.7 per cent for Toronto MPP Lee Fairclough, before about 1,000 Liberals at the Metro Toronto Convention Centre. The party is trying to rebuild after three consecutive election losses and present an alternative to Premier Doug Ford’s Progressive Conservatives. Bains told reporters he will seek a seat and will not ask one of the current 14 MPPs to resign; he was elected five times federally in Mississauga and Brampton and left his post as chief corporate affairs officer at Rogers Communications earlier this spring. Fairclough, a first-term MPP for Etobicoke-Lakeshore, is a former hospital executive and national women’s rugby player; Stephanie Bowman finished third and Dylan Marando fourth. In his victory speech Bains said people have fallen behind economically and cannot find a family doctor, and that Ford and the Conservatives “have abandoned the fight” and “are in it for their buddies.” He cited the previous Liberal government’s creation of full-day kindergarten, reduced class sizes, and the Greenbelt, an environmentally protected area of more than 800,000 hectares around Greater Toronto; the RCMP has for three years been investigating the Ford government’s decision to open that region to development, a move since reversed, and Ford has consistently denied wrongdoing. Bains also said the party will “reverse OSAP, ASAP,” after the government scaled back the Ontario Student Assistance Program and now faces questions about alleged abuse by private career colleges. The Globe and Mail reported he is the first racialized person to lead any major party in the province; the Progressive Conservatives criticized his ties to Trudeau, who formally resigned as federal Liberal leader in March 2025, with Citizenship and Multiculturalism Minister Graham McGregor among those speaking.

### Both Sides: Where Colorado conservatives put their money
What is being argued now, after the votes are in, is where Colorado’s conservative donors should spend next: more ballot measures, or the Republican Party’s own candidates and infrastructure. Advance Colorado and its allies spent millions to place six measures before voters, on taxes, energy, criminal justice and elections. Michael Fields, the group’s president, and former state GOP chair Dick Wadhams say the losses follow the scale of the Democratic wave. Wadhams’s best reason is the record he cites: nobody wins everything, and Advance has been an important counterweight to Democratic control of the state. Fields adds that the measures were identified broadly as a conservative slate, which made them an easier target in a blue environment. Frank McNulty, a University of Colorado regent and the last Republican speaker of the Colorado House, says that playbook has come to an end. His best reason is tactical: throwing fifteen ballot initiatives at the wall, not funding the campaigns behind them, and hoping they pass does not work anymore. Colorado GOP chair Craig Steiner tells donors that ballot fights must be repurchased every two years, while a larger presence in the statehouse could do some of the same work in law. The election is the shared ground. All but one Advance measure was trailing on election night. The one that passed, Proposition 132, increases criminal penalties for possessing or distributing fentanyl, and Steiner notes Democrats’ minority-proof legislature can amend it. Two measures the group opposed passed, setting aside billions for schools and healthcare and raising income taxes on the highest earners, which The Denver Post says upends decades of revenue restrictions conservatives had defended. Steiner said he will make the case that the party and the candidates are the better cost-benefit.
