**Global bond sell-off intensifies, as UK long-term borrowing costs pass 6%: The Guardian**
In Britain, in Europe, long-term government borrowing costs rose to a twenty-eight-year high as a global bond sell-off intensified. The Guardian reports that Britain's thirty-year bond yield reached a level not seen since 1998 and passed six percent. The paper links the move to fears that the United States deficit is unsustainable and to the persistently high cost of oil, which it says has spooked investors who expect central banks to raise interest rates in the coming months. Dearer long-term yields raise what the British state pays to borrow for decades.

**Explosions heard in Ethiopia's capital after drone flights banned: BBC**

**Tennessee death row inmate Christa Pike survives execution attempt after two lethal doses: The Globe and Mail**
In the United States, in North America, Tennessee failed to execute Christa Gail Pike on Wednesday after two doses of pentobarbital, and Governor Bill Lee halted the remaining execution for the rest of the year. Pike, 50, scheduled to die for a 1995 murder committed at 18, was alive and loudly snoring and was taken by ambulance to hospital, her attorneys said; she would have been the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years. An appeals court stopped the injection an hour before the 10 a.m. schedule, and the US Supreme Court later overturned that stay. Lee ordered a third-party review, while the Department of Correction said it followed the lawful protocol, which calls for a second set of drugs if the inmate is not dead and does not say what follows if the person is still alive. Robin Maher of the Death Penalty Information Center called the survival singular, saying seven other people had survived failed attempts to place a vein but none had stayed alive after receiving the drugs.

### Both Sides: Should a state use lethal injection again after two doses fail?
Tennessee has to decide whether lethal injection can still carry out a death sentence after two doses of pentobarbital left Christa Gail Pike alive. Governor Bill Lee has stopped the one remaining execution set for the rest of 2026 and ordered a third-party review of what occurred.

The case for treating the sentence as still executable, and the method as lawfully used, comes from the state. Lee said carrying out a lawfully imposed sentence is among Tennessee's most serious responsibilities, and that people expect it to be legal, constitutional, and effective. The Department of Correction said it followed every step of the protocol approved by the Attorney General's office. That protocol requires a second set of syringes if the inmate is not dead after the first, which is what officials administered.

The case against using the method again comes from outside government. Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, said survival after the drugs were given is singular: seven other people have lived because a team could not find a vein, but she said no one had stayed alive once the drugs were administered. Clive Stafford Smith, a human rights lawyer, told Al Jazeera he has been in the execution chamber with clients and that it is never the clinical process officials describe, and he asked why a state kills to prove that killing is wrong. UN special rapporteur Morris Tidball-Binz told the UK's Times, Al Jazeera reported, that executing Pike would be torture and a gross human rights violation.

Both sides accept that two doses were given and that Pike was still alive, snoring, and taken to hospital. Next is Lee's outside review; no further Tennessee execution is set for this year.
