# Omni View Top World News
> **More than eight thousand two hundred U.S. flights were canceled Friday, stranding passengers nationwide.**

The Federal Aviation Administration’s rare nationwide ground stop, the longest of its kind in the country since September 11, 2001, has been lifted, while airlines and airports were still working through the delays. This edition also draws on the Omni View Omni View North America desk.

### The Ten
**What to Know About Friday’s Nationwide Ground Stop: The New York Times**

The United States stopped departures across the country on Friday afternoon. The Federal Aviation Administration issued a rare nationwide ground stop after outdated air-traffic communications equipment became overloaded with data and began to fail, The New York Times reported. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy told reporters at the White House on Friday night that radar and other air-traffic communications started to falter at 3:36 p.m., when old routers became overloaded with data. He said the failures were not universal. He said they were pervasive enough that officials issued an unusual nationwide moratorium on takeoffs.

France 24 reported the scale of what that moratorium did to the day’s flying. More than 8,200 flights were canceled. That was over 31 percent of the flights scheduled in the United States. The ground stop lasted nearly five hours. France 24 said it was the longest nationwide ground stop in the country since September 11, 2001. Officials blamed outdated air-traffic communications equipment. France 24 said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. The airspace has since reopened, and flights have resumed.

The Times described what the stop left behind once the clock on those nearly five hours had run. The outage stranded thousands of passengers. It caused widespread delays. Airlines and airports were still struggling to recover from those delays. The afternoon, the Times wrote, highlighted persistent vulnerabilities in the aging U.S. air-traffic infrastructure. It also opened a funding fight. Administration officials demanded that lawmakers approve tens of billions of dollars more to accelerate systemwide upgrades. That demand is a request put to Congress. It is not an appropriation. It is not a completed upgrade. No vote on the demand is reported in today’s articles.

Two officials who appeared together at the White House drew different lines around the same failure. Duffy insisted that the failure was caused by the antiquated, legacy communications equipment, not by recent upgrades to the system. Markwayne Mullin, whom the Times identified as the homeland security secretary, stood with him. Mullin said investigators had seen zero evidence that a cyberattack triggered the technology failures. He suggested that new equipment and new technology Duffy’s department had added to the old infrastructure was what had overwhelmed it. France 24’s account, that officials blamed outdated communications equipment, sits beside that split. Neither official described a finding that every communications site had failed. Both described a failure wide enough to stop departures everywhere. Duffy’s 3:36 p.m. mark is the time he gave for the start of the faltering, not the time the stop was lifted. France 24’s nearly five hours is the length of the ground stop. Together they place the failure in a Friday afternoon and evening, when a national hold on takeoffs hits the day’s remaining departures at once.

For a passenger booked on one of the canceled flights, the change was immediate: a departure that did not leave, then a restart into a system already out of position. For an airline, more than 8,200 cancellations in one country, on one day, is not a local weather bank. It is a broken schedule at national scale, with aircraft, crews and connecting passengers left in the wrong places when the hold lifted. The Times’ account that carriers and airports were still struggling to recover is the part that outlasts the nearly five hours of the stop itself. A ground stop ends when takeoffs are allowed again. The delays it creates keep moving through later banks of flights. France 24’s report that flights have resumed and the Times’ report that recovery was unfinished can both be true at once: the moratorium is over, and the timetable is not yet whole.

The share France 24 gave — over 31 percent of flights scheduled in the United States — is a share of the day’s U.S. schedule, not a count of people. The Times’ figure for people is thousands of passengers stranded. The articles do not convert either number into a single total of travelers turned away. What they do fix is the reach of the order: a moratorium on takeoffs that was nationwide, unusual, and long enough for France 24 to set it against the ground stop of September 11, 2001, as the longest of its kind in the country since that day.

What the episode changes, beyond those hours, is the argument over the equipment the country flies on. The Times tied the stop to vulnerabilities that officials already describe as persistent, and to a demand for tens of billions of dollars more so that systemwide upgrades can move faster. Duffy’s account points that money at replacing legacy routers and communications gear he says failed on their own. Mullin’s account points it at a different risk: new technology added onto old infrastructure, overloading what was already there. The listener does not have, in either official’s remarks as published, a joint finding that settles which account is the operating one. What both put on the record is the absence, in their telling, of evidence of a cyberattack, and the presence of a failure serious enough to stop takeoffs nationwide.

The next step the articles name is the one already taken and the one still asked for. The stop has been lifted, and flights have resumed. Until Congress acts or declines to act, the system that failed on Friday afternoon is the system airlines are using to rebuild Friday’s broken day. That is the consequence that does not wait on the argument: people who were meant to fly did not, crews and aircraft are still being put back in order, and the officials responsible for the network disagree, in public, about whether the failure was the old equipment or the new gear laid on top of it.

### Both Sides: What overloaded U.S. air-traffic communications
What is being decided is not whether Friday’s stop happened. It happened. What is in dispute is the mechanism of that nationwide ground stop, and therefore what tens of billions of dollars in faster upgrades would be buying. The Federal Aviation Administration ordered the stop after communications equipment overloaded with data and began to fail. No vote on that demand, and no date for one, is reported in today’s articles. The operational next step is already on the record: the airspace has reopened and flights have resumed, while airlines and airports were still struggling to recover.

Sean Duffy, the transportation secretary, told reporters at the White House on Friday night that radar and other communications started to falter at 3:36 p.m. when old routers became overloaded with data. His case is that the failure belongs to antiquated, legacy communications equipment. He insisted it was not caused by recent upgrades. On that account, the reason to spend is to replace gear that has already shown it can force a nationwide moratorium on takeoffs. France 24 called that stop the longest in the country since September 11, 2001, and reported more than 8,200 flights canceled, over 31 percent of those scheduled in the United States. Duffy also said the failures were not universal, only pervasive enough to justify stopping takeoffs everywhere. His strongest reason, stated in his own terms, is that the old routers overloaded, and that recent upgrades were not the cause.

Markwayne Mullin, identified by the Times as the homeland security secretary, made the other case while standing with Duffy. On that account, the risk is not only age. It is the way new systems are laid onto old ones. A large new appropriation, spent the same way, could repeat the overload rather than end it. His strongest reason is the one he offered at the White House: the added technology, not a hostile intrusion, is what he suggested had overwhelmed the old infrastructure.

France 24 reported that officials blamed outdated air-traffic communications equipment and said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. That reporting aligns with Duffy on age and with both men on the cyber question. It does not choose between Duffy’s rejection of recent upgrades and Mullin’s suggestion that added technology was the overload. The listener is left with two official accounts of one Friday afternoon, a canceled share of the U.S. schedule above 31 percent, a stop of nearly five hours, and a funding demand whose size the Times put at tens of billions of dollars more, still unvoted in these reports. ### Progress Watch
Microsoft said on Saturday morning that it had restored service to the remaining customers and regions hit by one of the worst information-technology failures in years. On its Microsoft 365 status page, the company said it had completed its efforts to address the underlying issue of the incident and restored service to the remaining impacted customers and regions. The Guardian reported that Microsoft described the service as fully restored after a massive outage that caused widespread disruption to governments, businesses and public services around the world.

The company said the outage had affected governments, courts, healthcare services, businesses, airlines and banks in many places. Governments including those of Japan, Singapore and Australia reported that the hours-long episode had affected some of their operations. In the United Kingdom, hospital staff resorted to paper records and courtrooms scrambled to locate fax machines. Users reported that Microsoft 365 services, including Outlook, Teams and the artificial-intelligence assistant Copilot, were interrupted. Downdetector, an online tracker, said it received outage reports from users in more than 70 countries and territories, and that it received reports from 74 countries and territories within an hour after problems first emerged.

Microsoft said on its website that more than 430 million people use Microsoft 365 apps. The company said in January that it had more than 450 million paid commercial subscriptions to its 365 services. The Guardian said the total number of people affected was not the figure those subscription counts settle; the counts are Microsoft’s own measures of the installed base the outage could reach. Problems started appearing at about midday on Thursday in the United States and 6 p.m. in the United Kingdom. The Washington-based company apologised on Friday. It said the outage was not related to cyber or to artificial-intelligence agents. It said the incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced after a recent network change.

The restoration is the measured change: a failure Microsoft itself ranked among the worst information-technology failures in years, declared over for the remaining customers and regions on Saturday morning. What the same reporting leaves in place is the dependence the outage made visible. The Guardian said the disruptions were the latest example of cascading problems when so many communications and technology platforms depend on the same provider, and that the outage fuelled concerns in the United Kingdom about an over-reliance on a small number of technology companies. Tim Clement-Jones, a former chair of the House of Lords artificial-intelligence select committee, called the widespread outages of vital government services an “I told you so moment” highlighting Britain’s dependence on U.S. technology. Jeremy Singer, a professor at the University of Glasgow’s school of computing science, said the problem emphasised the dependence built on one company’s digital infrastructure, and might lead people to question whether more resilience is needed in the information-technology ecosystem. Those are arguments about what should follow a restoration Microsoft says is already complete.
