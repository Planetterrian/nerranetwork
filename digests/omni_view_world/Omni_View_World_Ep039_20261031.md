# Omni View Top World News
> **More than 20,000 California mail ballots stay counted after courts blocked a federal set-aside.**

A second Friday order limits prosecution threats against election workers, with voting set for November 3. This edition draws on 1 of the 5 Omni View regional desks live by press time.

### The Ten
**Court Rejects Latest Trump Effort to Impound California Ballots: The New York Times**

In the United States, Friday brought two court losses for the Trump administration’s attempt to intervene in California’s elections, with voters going to the polls on Tuesday, November 3.

The New York Times reports that a federal appeals court again refused an order that would have made California officials set aside more than 20,000 mail-in ballots. The court called that notion unconstitutional. Late the same Friday, a federal judge temporarily stopped the Justice Department from threatening election officials with criminal prosecution if they decline to follow the department’s directives on ballots. The Times says the administration could now ask the Supreme Court to step in.

The Times treats the lawsuit as one piece of a wider push, built up over recent weeks, to raise doubts about whether the vote will be counted fairly. It ties that push to voter discontent and to what it describes as President Trump’s sagging approval ratings. It also says Republican candidates are struggling to keep an edge in a series of red states before Tuesday, while the administration looks to the courts for what the Times calls a structural advantage.

The Justice Department has widened its California investigation by sending federal grand jury subpoenas to local election officials. The Times describes that expansion as an effort to prove claims, which it calls unsubstantiated, that thousands of noncitizens sit on the state’s voter rolls. The union that represents California election workers sued over those subpoenas. Its filing accuses the department of “unprecedented and unlawful threats” that “risk interfering with the lawful administration of the ongoing 2026 midterm elections and the counting of ballots.”

The same live report carries a separate Justice Department move in Alaska. On Thursday the department issued dozens of subpoenas linked to what the Times calls a Democratic plan to stack the ballot in a close Senate contest that could help decide which party controls the chamber. Democratic candidate Mary Peltola said late Thursday that she had not known about the effort. The Times had reported that operatives aligned with her campaign aimed to recruit extra candidates in order to peel votes from Republican Senator Dan S. Sullivan. At a fund-raiser in Juneau on Thursday night, Peltola said: “I knew nothing about any other candidates on the ballot except for me and the incumbent. That is all I have ever known about. I am not interested in tricking voters.” Her former campaign manager, Elisa Rios, told the Times that Peltola was not directly part of the ballot-packing effort but was “definitely aware.” Those two accounts sit in the same article and do not agree.

Campaign travel is also moving. A White House official told the Times that Trump will hold rallies in Kansas and Iowa on Sunday. The paper calls that a sharp reversal: the rallies had been canceled a day earlier in two states it describes as traditionally Republican, where polls indicate tight races. On Friday Trump said he had wanted to try tele-rallies in those states, and that Republicans in both had called to say an in-person visit would be better.

Other named campaign stops sit in the same report. Former President Bill Clinton was scheduled to appear on Friday in Tupelo, Mississippi, for Senate candidate Scott Colom. Former Vice President Kamala Harris will deliver the keynote at a Democratic fund-raising dinner in Fulton County, Georgia. In South Carolina, the Times reports, the contest between Republican Senator Darline Graham and Democratic challenger Dr. Annie Andrews is effectively tied.

France 24, in a separate report, says Americans vote on November 3 in midterms that could see Republicans lose control of Congress and put President Trump’s legislative agenda in danger. It notes that the Constitution leaves the running of elections to the states, and that this has not stopped the president from trying to use his influence before the vote. Jonathan Diaz, director for voting advocacy and partnerships at the Campaign Legal Center, told France 24 that the administration has tried to insert the federal government into almost every aspect of the election process. Diaz said the long-term risks are very significant, and that even if the president has not succeeded in changing the rules or in coercing state and local officials, the effort has created an atmosphere of fear and uncertainty.

France 24 cites a new poll by the Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs. In that poll, 55 percent of Democrats said they are confident the final count after the midterm will be accurate, compared with 38 percent of Republicans.

France 24 reports that Trump has repeatedly claimed large numbers of noncitizens vote in United States elections, while the figures it cites show this is exceedingly rare. Voting is already limited to citizens and already requires identification. A noncitizen who tries to vote can face fines, prison, or deportation. France 24 says even think tanks that support Trump’s voting restrictions have failed to find evidence of widespread fraud. It quotes an April New York Times account of the Heritage Foundation’s database: 100 documented cases of noncitizens voting from 1982 to 2025, about 0.000008 percent of more than 1.3 billion votes cast in presidential elections across that span.

The bill Trump is pressing is the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act, which would require documentary proof of United States citizenship in order to vote. The United States does not issue a national identity card. Under the act, a driver’s license or other official identification would not be enough if it does not show a place of birth. France 24 lists the documents that would count: a valid United States passport, a military identification card, a valid government-issued photo identification showing the person was born in the United States, or a birth certificate, but only if that certificate matches the person’s current name exactly. France 24 says the exact-name rule could be a problem for the 69 million women who changed their names after marriage, and for the trans community.

The Campaign Legal Center, as cited by France 24, says more than 21 million Americans, or nearly 10 percent of voting-age Americans, lack access to documentary proof of citizenship. It names young people, people in lower income brackets, and people of color as the groups most affected. A 2024 report by the Center for Democracy and Civic Engagement, also cited by France 24, found that 3 percent of people of color lack proof such as a birth certificate, a passport, or naturalization documents. By comparison, 1 percent of white Americans have no such documentation. France 24 reports that only 1 percent figure in the sentence that follows the 3 percent finding.

A Republican amendment to the legislation also aimed to limit voting by mail or online, by rebuilding those systems so they comply with the stricter identification rules. The bill passed the House of Representatives in February and then languished in the Senate for lack of support. France 24 says Trump’s allies are still trying to move it through the budget reconciliation process. It also reports that last year the administration expanded the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements database, known as SAVE, which exists to check whether someone is a citizen and therefore qualifies for government benefits. The expansion allowed states to use that database to check whether someone is eligible to vote.

The Guardian, in a third report dated the same day, says government corruption has emerged as a key issue for midterm voters and points to what it calls an exclusive poll. The article frames its examples as alleged self-dealing, not as a court finding. It writes that the Qatari government gifted Trump a luxury jet, potentially violating the emoluments clause of the Constitution. It writes that the United Arab Emirates gained access to some tightly guarded, high-end American artificial-intelligence chips soon after an investment firm linked to its ruling family secretly bought a 500 million dollar stake in World Liberty Financial, the crypto company funded by the families of Trump and Steve Witkoff. It writes that people facing criminal charges have taken to donating to Trump’s political operations and then seeking pardons or clemency. Most recently, it says, Wanda Vázquez Garced, the former governor of Puerto Rico, and Julio Herrera Velutini, whom the Guardian describes as the banker who paid her bribes, were pardoned after Velutini’s family gave a 50 million dollar deal to Alina Habba, Trump’s former personal lawyer, and donated 3.5 million dollars to Trump’s Maga Inc super PAC. The Guardian also writes that the Pentagon lent more than 600 million dollars to Vulcan Elements, a rare-earths startup, three months after Donald Trump Jr.’s venture capital firm, 1789 Capital, bought a stake in the company. It further writes that Powerus, a drone maker backed by Eric Trump and Donald Trump Jr., is selling drone interceptors to Persian Gulf allies of the United States that are under attack by Iran.

For voters and election workers in California, the immediate change is that the mail ballots the administration wanted set aside remain in the count, and that prosecution threats tied to the department’s ballot directives are blocked for now. The national electorate votes on Tuesday. The next legal step the Times names is a possible request to the Supreme Court. In Congress, the citizenship-document bill is still not law.

### Both Sides: Documentary proof of citizenship to vote
Congress is deciding whether a voter must produce documentary proof of United States citizenship before casting a ballot. President Trump is pressing the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act. France 24 reports that the bill cleared the House in February and then stalled in the Senate, and that his allies still want it attached to budget reconciliation.

The case Trump is making, as France 24 reports it, is that large numbers of noncitizens are voting and that identification already required at the polls is not a sufficient check. His stated reason is that the franchise belongs to citizens. The administration has already widened the SAVE database so a citizenship test built for benefits can be used by states on voter rolls. Documents the bill would accept are a valid passport, a military identification card, a government photo identification that shows birth in the United States, or a birth certificate whose name matches the voter’s current name exactly. A Republican amendment would also rebuild mail and online voting around those same papers.

The case against, from Jonathan Diaz at the Campaign Legal Center, is that the administration has not rewritten the rules for this midterm but has produced fear and uncertainty among the officials who run the count. The center’s numbers, cited by France 24, say more than 21 million Americans, nearly one in ten voting-age adults, cannot produce the papers, with the burden falling on the young, people on lower incomes, and people of color. The 2024 Center for Democracy and Civic Engagement report puts the share without a birth certificate, passport, or naturalization papers at 3 percent among people of color and 1 percent among white Americans. France 24 also notes that an exact-name birth certificate could shut out many of the 69 million women who took a new name after marriage. On the fraud claim itself, France 24 points to the Heritage Foundation database, via an April New York Times report: 100 noncitizen-voting cases from 1982 through 2025, about 0.000008 percent of more than 1.3 billion presidential votes in those years. It says think tanks that favor the restrictions have still not found fraud on a wide scale.
