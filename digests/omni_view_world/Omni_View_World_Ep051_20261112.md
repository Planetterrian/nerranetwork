**Ethiopian men abducted for military service as tensions rise in Horn of Africa: The Globe and Mail**
In Ethiopia, night raids in Addis Ababa are forcing young men into the army as Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed expands the armed forces. The Globe and Mail reports the military is now estimated at half a million soldiers, one of the largest in Africa, with a fast-growing stock of drones, fighter jets, artillery and armoured vehicles. Azeb Girma said men in civilian clothes pushed her only child, 19, onto an unmarked truck; after a March conscription and training, he was deployed to a northern border town to fight Tigrayan rebels and called her on a stolen phone. Etsegenet Tadios, 55, said two sons were taken in September from a bar where they were playing pool, then phoned from an unknown number, sounding terrified, as they headed north. The army is fighting insurgencies in Tigray, Amhara and Oromia. Cameron Hudson, a US-based Horn of Africa analyst, estimates the force has more than doubled since the last Tigray war ended in 2022. The paper reports that thousands of soldiers marched into Tigray in September and that drone strikes in northern regions have killed hundreds of civilians and rebels. Kjetil Tronvoll, a Norwegian professor of peace and conflict studies, said heavy equipment has been arriving in huge shipments, though government secrecy makes the full scale hard to confirm. Abiy has vowed to win access to the Red Sea, which the paper says puts Ethiopia in direct conflict with Eritrea, holder of the nearest ports; Egypt objects to Ethiopia's Nile dam, and the paper reports drone bases have been provided for Sudan's Rapid Support Forces. Abiy has also sent cabinet ministers through military training and posted photographs of them exercising at an army base.

**Strike on Gaza Hospital Kills at Least 6 and Injures 20, Rescuers Say: The New York Times**
In the Gaza Strip, an overnight Israeli strike hit Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis. The New York Times reports Gaza's Civil Defense said at least six people were killed and more than 20 injured; CBC, citing Gaza's Health Ministry, said six were killed and at least a dozen wounded. The Israeli security agency and military said they targeted Muhammad Abu Ismail, whom they identified as a Hamas commander responsible for holding hostages, and said Hamas had set up a command centre at the complex, was storing weapons there, and that steps were taken to limit harm to civilians. Hamas said that account was a blatant attempt to justify a heinous crime against a civilian medical facility. Gaza's health ministry called the strike a flagrant violation of international laws and conventions and did not answer the Israeli claims. Paramedic Naseem Hassan told the Times that at least two missiles hit the roof of a four-storey building in the complex and exploded in the surgery department on the second floor. The attack comes more than a year into a ceasefire Israel and Hamas agreed in October 2025, a period in which the health ministry says Israeli fire has killed more than 1,600 people, without separating civilians and combatants. The ministry says more than 74,000 people have been killed in the war that followed the Hamas-led attack of 7 October 2023. In August last year a strike on the same hospital killed 20 people, and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu called that strike a tragic mishap. The Times says it is trying to verify who was killed.

**War in Ukraine: MEPs demand Russia returns deported Ukrainian children: European Parliament**
In Belgium, the European Parliament on Thursday adopted a resolution demanding that Russia immediately return Ukrainian children it has deported or forcibly transferred. The text passed 536 to 8, with 37 abstentions, and condemns what members called systematic deportations, forced transfers, illegal adoptions, forced Russification, indoctrination and militarisation. Members said return to unoccupied Ukraine must be safe, immediate and unconditional, and that it is a duty under international law, not a bargaining chip for territory, prisoner exchanges or other political deals. The resolution says that, as of September 2026, 20,651 cases of deportation or forced transfer had been verified, while about 1.6 million Ukrainian children remain under Russian occupation. It also says that, as of 28 October 2026, 2,752 children who had been deported, forcibly transferred or rescued from occupied territory had returned. Members said a safe, unconditional return must be central to any real peace agreement ending Russia's war, and that no durable peace can ignore the children's rights or the responsibility of those who violated them. The Parliament restated support for the International Criminal Court and for arrest warrants against President Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova, the Russian president's commissioner for children's rights. It said accountability should run beyond Russia's top leadership, urged EU states to speed identification and return and to widen sanctions, and backed cooperation among Europol, Eurojust and Ukraine's human-rights ombudsman. The vote is a parliamentary demand, not a treaty and not a completed return.
Ranks: deported and occupation-held Ukrainian children, numbered in the resolution at 20,651 verified cases and about 1.6 million under occupation · a political and legal demand, not a return carried out today · the vote is done; return still unmet Source: [europarl.europa.eu](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/pl/press-room/20261110IPR48737/war-in-ukraine-meps-demand-russia-returns-deported-ukrainian-children)

**Democrat Scott Colom makes history as Mississippi's first black senator since 19th Century: BBC**
In the United States, Democrat Scott Colom has won Mississippi's Senate race, defeating Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith after a long count. The BBC reports he took 49.2 percent to her 48.7 percent, fewer than five thousand votes out of nearly a million, and that Democrats will hold 55 seats in the 100-member Senate, with Alaska still uncalled. Hyde-Smith congratulated him on Wednesday evening and said she respected Mississippi voters' decision though it was not the outcome she had wanted. Colom, 43, spoke at Tougaloo College and said, "They did not believe a moment like this was possible, but I kept the faith," and "It's something we did." The BBC says he will be the first Black person from Mississippi seated in the Senate since 1874, when Blanche Bruce was chosen by the state legislature; France 24, citing Time, says he will be the first Black senator to serve from the state since 1881, and its press review calls him the first Black senator the state's voters have directly elected. He visited all 82 counties in a state President Donald Trump won in 2024 by more than 22 points. The BBC says he turned Hyde-Smith's line that voters should "pick another protein," given when she was asked about beef prices, into an advert, and that polls and exit polls point to voter anger over the economy and the Iran war. At the NAACP's Jackson office, board chairman Leon Russell called the result the work of organising, particularly in the Black community. The outcome is a counted result, not a forecast.
Ranks: Mississippi voters and the US Senate, where Democrats will hold 55 seats with Alaska uncalled · one seat flips and a 145-year gap in Black representation from the state ends on the counts reported · the result is in; seating is the next step the BBC describes Source: [bbc.co.uk](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cw7vppyr80eeo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss)

**Ottawa mandates a merger of EDC and BDC: The Globe and Mail**
In Canada, Ottawa has mandated a merger of Export Development Canada and the Business Development Bank of Canada, both Crown corporations that finance businesses. The Globe and Mail reports that together they hold $128-billion in assets, employ 5,100 people and operate in more than two dozen countries, and that the mandate is one of the steps in the federal budget unveiled earlier this month. Alison Nankivell, EDC's chief executive, said the point is more Canadian companies of a certain size with a genuine footprint in several markets. She said the combination is not a cost-cutting exercise but a bigger balance sheet with more room to move resources. The paper calls the project, to date, little more than a concept, and reports that two people familiar with the plans said the combined body will sit under the minister of finance. In a September white paper, EDC ranked Canada 35th for the complexity of what it produces, behind Japan, South Korea, Germany, China, Sweden, Italy, the United States and France, and estimated a $100-billion gain in GDP if Canada made more complex goods. Nankivell named critical minerals, the energy transition, digital tools including artificial intelligence, agri-food and forestry as strengths to scale, and pointed to Austria's engineered-wood industry as a model. She said Canada is following countries such as France, Japan and China in gathering government-backed finance in one place. The merger is a mandate and a plan, not a completed institution.

### Both Sides: Should the public boycott Starbucks until a first union contract?
Starbucks Workers United is asking the public to boycott Starbucks until a first contract is reached, and rallies were set for Thursday on more than 40 US college campuses. The union wants better pay and staffing, including a $17-an-hour minimum for baristas and at least three workers on duty in a store. More than 700 stores have voted to unionise, covering more than 12,000 baristas. The first store, in Buffalo, New York, voted to unionise in December 2021, and the union says it typically takes more than 450 days to reach a first contract.

The union's case is that nearly five years without a contract leaves baristas unprotected. Andrew Trinklein, a barista taking part in an event at the University of Texas at Austin, said workers are employed at will and could be fired over something like failing to write a message on a cup, and that a contract would stop that. The union's campus push, begun in 2023, has already seen Cornell University and Pomona College decline to renew Starbucks contracts, and UCLA stop offering Starbucks products in its cafes. The National Labor Relations Board has found Starbucks guilty of violating federal labour law in several cases about its response to organising. Talks that had a path forward in February 2024 broke down after Brian Niccol became chief executive. At Chipotle, where he was chief executive from 2018 to 2024, the company agreed to pay $240,000 to former workers in a settlement over a Maine store closing after a union vote, which the labour board said was illegal; Chipotle has denied wrongdoing.

Starbucks' stated case is that the protests pull attention away from bargaining. A company spokesperson said Workers United represents about 5 percent of US partners, that actions like the rallies divert attention from the bargaining table, and that stores are focused on the holiday season. The next dated pressure point in the reporting is Red Cup Day on 17 November, when customers receive a reusable holiday cup. No new bargaining date is set out in today's account.
