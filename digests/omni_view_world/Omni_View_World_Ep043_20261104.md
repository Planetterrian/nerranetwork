**Democrats win control of House and Senate: Key US midterms takeaways: Al Jazeera**
In the United States, Democrats have won both the Senate and the House of Representatives, taking control of Congress for the rest of Donald Trump's presidency, which France 24 described as his final two years. Not every seat is called. Al Jazeera, citing the Associated Press, reported Democrats on 228 House seats and leading in 34 more, a total that could pass 260 and would be their largest House strength since 1990, when they won 267; after the 2024 election Republicans held 220 House seats and Democrats 215. In the Senate, Democrats have won 54 seats and lead in another, while Republicans have won 44 and lead in one; control takes 51, and Democrats last had a majority this clear in 2008, when they won 56. It is the first time in 20 years a Republican president has lost both houses, after George W. Bush in 2006, and Democrats have already flipped 25 House seats, more than the 21 they took in 2008, including seats in Texas, South Carolina, Iowa, Michigan and Kansas. Al Jazeera also reported 28 Democratic governors to 20 Republicans, with two races uncalled, against 27 Republican and 23 Democratic governors after 2024. Hakeem Jeffries told supporters, "Today begins the great American comeback"; if colleagues keep him as House leader, he would be the first Black Speaker, succeeding Republican Mike Johnson. Abdul El-Sayed, the son of Egyptian immigrants, defeated Mike Rogers in Michigan and is the first Muslim elected to the US Senate; he is to be sworn in on January 3. Trump called him a "jihadist" without citing evidence, and El-Sayed told supporters they could turn "inshallah" into "alhamdulillah," adding, "For the right-wing ops, that just means 'praise be to God.'" Susan Collins, Republican senator for Maine for three decades, conceded to Democrat Troy Jackson and said it had been the "honour of my life" to serve 30 years. Trump said Democrats did a "good job," that he would accept the results, and that "Iran was a piece of it," while noting he would still have the power, as president, to veto legislation; he also said that in some cases Republican candidates "were not my friend." Voters decided 145 ballot questions in more than three dozen states: Missouri rejected a proposed amendment that would have limited most abortions, with the Associated Press putting the "no" vote at 55.5 percent, and rejected a 2025 congressional map with 61.8 percent voting no; Virginia wrote in a right to reproductive freedom covering the first two trimesters, and Nevada a right to abortion until fetal viability, generally after 21 weeks, while Idaho defeated a measure that would have created abortion rights in state law. France 24 reported Keisha Lance Bottoms became the first Black woman elected governor of Georgia, and Byron Donalds the first Black man elected governor of Florida.

**Persian Gulf leaders fear a renewed U.S.-Iran war as diplomacy sputters: Los Angeles Times**
In Bahrain, diplomats at the Manama Dialogue warned that another round of fighting between the United States and Iran could be more destructive than the war the Los Angeles Times said began on February 28. Anwar Gargash, a presidential adviser in the United Arab Emirates, said the Iranian and American stands are far apart and that "if these positions are not bridged, then we have to worry about the worst," adding that regional states are seeking a "political offramp" in a "protracted crisis of no war, no peace." Egyptian Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty said governments must "push very hard in order to prevent the escalation," because a second round "will be more dangerous, more destructive and it will lead to nowhere," and because more fighting would affect food and energy security. President Trump has hinted he could launch "another little Operation Epic Fury" after Tuesday's midterms, referring to the US-Israeli operation that opened the war, but on Monday he wrote, "Iran wants a settlement, as soon as possible. It could happen!" Hours later, Iranian state media reported explosions on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, and the Associated Press quoted an unnamed US official saying the military had carried out "defensive strikes." The Times reported that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, after an inconclusive election last month, is said to be pressing Trump to finish off the Islamic Republic. Last week Saudi, Pakistani and Turkish officials flew to Tehran and met Iranian military officials; Qatar is leading talks on a de-escalation plan Tehran has presented and Trump has not accepted. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told the Islamic Republic News Agency it is up to the Americans to decide "whether they have the will to take the wise path of diplomacy or, as in the past, seek to drag the situation into aggression and military operations." He added, "If they dare to test us again," they "will receive a much more bitter and severe result than before," after a first round in which Iran struck American bases in the region with drones and cruise and ballistic missiles.

**Houthis’ monthlong siege on Taiz creating hell for trapped Yemenis: Al Jazeera**
In Yemen, the Houthis' siege of Taiz has lasted 30 days and, Al Jazeera reported, has affected hundreds of thousands of people. The United Nations has repeatedly warned of a catastrophe inside the city, where food and medicine are becoming scarce. The blockade has affected the 1.5 million people in Taiz City and others in rural districts, including about 180,000 displaced people trapped by clashes along the main road between Lahj and Taiz. The siege tightened when the Houthis seized al-Turbah, in the far southwest of the governorate, cutting the city's main artery to Aden. The Houthis reached Taiz's outskirts in 2015 after pushing the government out of Sanaa in September 2014; their takeover of the western countryside has now encircled the city. Salah Ahmed Ghaleb al-Majidi, acting director of the governorate's Siege Affairs Department, called it "the worst catastrophic siege imposed on a Yemeni city since the war began in 2014," with food, fuel, medical supplies and water not reaching the city and rubbish piling up. He said cut roads are also keeping families from relatives. Ahmed al-Mikhlafi, whose name Al Jazeera shortened for security, returned from Umrah in Mecca to find himself stuck in the western countryside; he described nearly 20 new Houthi checkpoints and a stop about 15 kilometres from the city, where fighters ordered his vehicle to turn back because of clashes.

**Netanyahu’s political rivals seize on Democrats’ sweep, seeking his ouster: The Globe and Mail**
In Israel, a week after an election the Globe and Mail described as leaving Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing coalition without a parliamentary majority, his rivals seized on the Democratic capture of Congress as proof he had squandered generations of bipartisan American support. The same account said the coalition still performed well enough to deny his main opponent, Gadi Eisenkot, a clear path to unseat him. Unlike earlier Israeli leaders who courted both US parties, Netanyahu has openly backed Republicans. The Globe and Mail reported that support for Israel among Democrats and some Republicans wavered after Israel's assault in Gaza following the 2023 Hamas attack, and fell further after Trump and Netanyahu ordered a joint attack on Iran. Some Democratic candidates made criticism of American military aid to Israel a campaign argument. Trump, who had endorsed Netanyahu, made an election-day pledge to halt 4 billion US dollars in annual funding to Israel, which he said would take away a "bad talking point" used by Democrats. Yair Golan, head of the centre-left Democrats party, wrote that the US results "reveal just how toxic Netanyahu is among both Democrats and Republicans." Yair Lapid said "Israel cannot afford" for Netanyahu to remain, because "It endangers our security, it jeopardises our democracy, and it will ruin our standing on the global stage." Netanyahu has so far remained silent on the US vote.

### Both Sides: Should the NBA keep Rwanda's paid partnership while US military sanctions stand?
The question is whether the National Basketball Association should keep a paid partnership with Rwanda, including finals at Kigali's BK Arena, while United States sanctions on Rwanda's military remain. The army-funded APR club has already been withdrawn from the 2026 Basketball Africa League. Democratic Senator Jeffrey Merkley and Republican Senator Marsha Blackburn wrote on 23 October that the NBA risked "becoming complicit in sportswashing by accepting money from a Rwandan government that systematically abuses human rights," and asked, "What is the NBA red line for accepting money from foreign governments that commit human rights abuses?" Their best reason is that a league that speaks of justice cannot keep taking government money after Washington sanctioned the Rwandan army in March, accusing it of fuelling the war in the Democratic Republic of Congo, and last week sanctioned the defence ministry over support for the M23 group, which has seized large parts of the east. DR Congo has asked the NBA, European clubs and Formula 1 to end Rwandan campaigns, arguing the deals clean Rwanda's image while fighting continues. NBA deputy commissioner Mark Tatum said the team "had been withdrawn" after those earlier sanctions, and the club stays barred while the sanctions last. Rwanda's stated case is a denial: Rwanda denies backing the rebels. That denial, if accepted, undercuts the charge that the partnership launders a war role. What is not in dispute in the reporting is the commercial fact: Rwanda is an official league partner, has hosted all but one final and playoff series since 2021 in the 10,000-seat arena, and the NBA reportedly receives millions of dollars a year. APR, third in 2024 and 2025, was replaced this year by the RSSB Tigers. The senators' 2 November deadline for a written answer has passed. The team remains out for as long as the sanctions stand; the articles do not set a date for ending or keeping the Kigali finals. ### Progress Watch

DW reported that data show children at participating schools miss fewer classes and perform better, and that founder Wawira Njiru, 35, is receiving this year's German Africa Prize; the organisation has 6,000 staff, more than 70 percent of them women.
