**Kenya confirms its first Ebola death as outbreak spreads: BBC World**
Kenya, in East Africa, confirmed its first Ebola death after a citizen who had lived in the Democratic Republic of Congo for seven years died in Nairobi, Health Minister Aden Duale said. Duale said the man fell ill a month ago, was treated in several Congolese hospitals, travelled by road to Kampala, flew to Nairobi on Saturday, passed ordinary airport screening, and was taken by a relative to hospital, where he tested positive for the Bundibugyo species. The BBC reports more than 4,000 people have died of Ebola in Congo this year, in the second most deadly known outbreak; Bundibugyo has no approved vaccine or treatment, though four vaccines are in development and human trials for one began in July. He died at 23:30 local time on Monday, and burial was planned for Tuesday under public-health protocols; the virus spreads by contact with infected bodily fluids such as blood or vomit.

**Serbia ties overseas voting to local time, a move likely to spark backlash ahead of a key election: ABC News**
Serbia, in Southeast Europe, has tied overseas voting in the October 25 parliamentary election to the 7 a.m. to 8 p.m. window at home, rather than to local time abroad, the election commission decided on Monday. ABC News reports that voters in Chicago would cast ballots between 1 a.m. and 2 p.m., and that Serbs in parts of Australia and New Zealand would have to vote overnight at diplomatic missions. The commission approved 93 polling stations overseas, 12 more than in 2023, with Bosnia holding 28 of them, nearly 30 percent of the foreign stations, including nine of the added sites. At least 100,000 citizens have applied to vote abroad, nearly three times as many as in 2023. Aleksandar Vucic called the election before resigning as president on September 27, short of the end of his second and final legal term, and is seeking a return as prime minister; critics say the hours could make it hard for the diaspora on the other side of the world to vote.

**'Ghost particles' from space telescope wins physics Nobel: BBC World**
Belgium, in Europe, provided this year's physics laureate: the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Nobel Prize to Francis Halzen for IceCube, which uses a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice fitted with light sensors to detect neutrinos. The academy said his vision and scientific leadership were fundamental to the observatory; he first set out the South Pole idea in 1988. Halzen told a news conference he was surprised the idea worked so well, and said, "I have to emphasise how lucky I was." Neutrinos from violent processes far beyond the solar system usually pass through the ice; a rare strike on a nucleus produces charged particles and a blue flash that the sensors can record, which the Nobel committee's Mark Pearce called a new kind of astronomy.

### Both Sides: Should Argentina keep the 2011 cap on foreign rural land?
Argentina is weighing whether to keep the 2011 Land Law's cap on foreign ownership of rural land or to let President Javier Milei's deregulation decree cut that cap back. Milei, in office since December 2023, is opening resource-rich areas to foreign investment. The Guardian reports he is courting billionaire investors by removing a block on foreign ownership of land and natural resources.

The case for the change is the investment case attached to that policy: foreign buyers would be freer to put money into land and resources the 2011 rules limit. Those rules cap foreigners at 15 percent of any department, the subdivision below a province, and cap any single nationality at 30 percent of foreign-held land in that department.

The case against is being made in the street and in research. On 19 September, Marta Sahores, an 84-year-old chemistry professor, marched in Esquel, in Chubut province, during the marcha de la bronca, beside workers, retirees, students, Indigenous groups and human rights organisations. Signs read "The land is not for sale." Sahores said, "When foreigners buy land, they buy land with rivers and lakes. They take our water, they take our soil," and said she stands for environmental sovereignty. She and fellow activists want the decree overturned. Tomás Costa, a researcher with the Land Observatory at the University of Buenos Aires, says some departments already break the caps: in Lácar, in Neuquén, about 54 percent of land is foreign-owned. Advocates say rolling the law back deepens extractivism, adds to housing inequality and displaces Indigenous communities, and that the 2011 law is already weakened because it does not apply retroactively.

The baseline both arguments sit on is the same in the reporting: foreigners own about 13 million hectares, about 5 percent of Argentina, roughly the size of England, under a cap the decree would loosen. Sahores says opponents will keep going to the streets and to the courts. Source: [theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2026/oct/06/argentina-land-reform-protests-javier-milei)
