### The Ten
**Mixed reactions in Iraq on final day of US-led coalition withdrawal:** France 24
In Iraq, in the Middle East, the US military said on Wednesday that it has completed the withdrawal of all American troops, ending a twelve-year mission against the Islamic State group. The last US troops left an air base in northern Iraq under a deal between Baghdad and Washington. France 24 reported that the country's leaders celebrated the close of a US-led coalition mission deployed more than a decade ago to fight jihadists. The same account said some fear the departure will embolden pro-Iran armed factions.

**UK’s Burnham says he’ll consider push to rejoin EU:** Politico Europe
In Britain, in Europe, Prime Minister Andy Burnham told Labour's conference in Liverpool on Tuesday that the Brexit settlement struck by the Conservatives had done more harm than good. He said he would set out options for Britain's long-term relationship with European partners at a UK-EU summit later this year. In a BBC interview broadcast Wednesday, he said the government could look at a customs union, as former chancellor George Osborne has suggested, at the single market, as the Liberal Democrats have argued, or could go all the way, judging each by what is doable and by the pros and cons. Politico reported that his government will stick, before a general election, to predecessor Keir Starmer's promise not to rejoin the single market or the customs union, while the remarks prepare a post-election push. Conservative deputy leader Alex Burghart said Burnham cannot be trusted and is dragging Britain back into Brexit arguments after saying weeks ago that he wanted to move on.

**'Third country' deportation can resume, US supreme court rules:** The Guardian
In the United States, in North America, the Supreme Court has ruled the Trump administration can resume deporting immigrants to countries other than their homeland without a chance to contest the removal. A six-justice conservative majority temporarily lifted a lower-court decision that had required notice and an opportunity to claim fear of torture or persecution in the destination country. The Guardian reported the ruling, which the administration had asked the court to allow.

**Israel-bound passenger plane makes emergency landing in Saudi Arabia after reported fight between pilots:** The Guardian
In the Middle East, a flydubai flight from Dubai in the United Arab Emirates to Israel diverted to Tabuk in Saudi Arabia after transmitting three emergency signals over Jordan, one indicating possible unlawful interference. The Guardian reported the jet descended about 16,000 feet in two minutes, Israeli officials scrambled fighter jets, and the plane landed at Tabuk. A spokesperson for the Israeli prime minister's office first said the diversion was not a hijack attempt, and authorities later said they were investigating; France 24 reported that the Israeli government believes one of the pilots may have attempted a terror attack. A passenger told the Jerusalem Post that one pilot stabbed the other; the Guardian said that account could not immediately be verified. A Saudi aviation authority said all passengers were safe and the captain and co-pilot were taken to hospital, while the Israeli air force waited for Saudi permission to fly the passengers back.

**Government notifies new CAFE-3 norms for passenger vehicles:** The Hindu
In India, in South Asia, the government has notified Corporate Average Fuel Economy, or CAFE-3, norms that set carbon-emission limits for passenger vehicles. The rules take effect on April 1, 2027, and run until March 31, 2032, for new passenger vehicles manufactured or imported for sale. The Ministry of Power said the fuel-consumption benchmark tightens from 3.996 litres per 100 km in 2027-28 to 3.3273 litres per 100 km in 2031-32, about a 16.7 percent improvement. The norms cover hatchbacks, sedans, special utility vehicles and MPVs seating eight passengers besides the driver, and raise the reference weight from 1,082 kg to 1,229 kg. Manufacturers can claim 1 gram of CO2 per kilometre for each eligible cleaner-fuel technology, including ethanol-blended petrol, biofuels and compressed biogas, up to a cap of 9.0 grams.

### Both Sides: Is Ontario's tariff fund reaching businesses?
Ontario's government is being asked whether its five-billion-dollar Protect Ontario account is reaching firms hit by United States tariffs. At a Tuesday committee at Queen's Park, Finance Minister Peter Bethlenfalvy first called questions about the spending outside his brief, saying the Ministry of Economic Development ran the fund. Deputy Minister of Finance Gadi Mayman then answered: thirty-six applications, just under one hundred million dollars requested; eighteen approved, about forty million dollars; and one hundred million dollars more for Algoma steel. Global News reported the combined outlay at one hundred forty million dollars, nearly two years after Premier Doug Ford pledged the five billion in the February 2025 election, a pledge later written into the budget.

The strongest case that the program is failing comes from Liberal finance critic Stephanie Bowman. She said forty million dollars for Ontario businesses is 0.01 percent of GDP, and that what the government has on offer is not working.

The strongest case that the account still counts, as Bethlenfalvy framed it, is that other government supports have drawn positive uptake, so the forty million dollars are not the whole response. The government has also said it would shift billions from the account into an investment fund managed with a private-sector partner.

Both sides accept Mayman's split: about forty million dollars approved from applications, plus one hundred million dollars for Algoma, inside a five-billion-dollar account. Next, on the government's own account, is that shift of billions into the private-partner fund.
