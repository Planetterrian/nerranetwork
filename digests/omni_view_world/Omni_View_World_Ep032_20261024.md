### The Ten
**Brazil’s Lula and Bolsonaro debate policy proposals before run-off: Al Jazeera**

Brazil's presidential run-off reached its last televised argument on Friday in Rio de Janeiro, with voters due to choose on Sunday between the incumbent and a senator who finished ahead of him in the first round. Al Jazeera reports that President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and his right-wing rival, Flávio Bolsonaro, questioned each other about the economy, infrastructure and regional development. The debate was broadcast on TV Globo. Al Jazeera calls Friday's exchange notably more diplomatic than the candidates' initial face-off on Sunday, and also a debate that still carried personal attacks and tense moments. The New York Times, in a same-day report, identifies Flávio Bolsonaro as the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro and says the former president was jailed over a coup plot. That identification is background the Times states. It is not a finding about who will hold the office after Sunday, and no outlet in today's file predicts the result.

The count that put the two men in a run-off is already on the record. Bolsonaro finished first in the initial round of voting on October 4 with 47 percent of the vote to Lula's 45 percent. A Datafolha institute survey published on Thursday suggested the pair are now tied with 50 percent support each, compared with Bolsonaro's four-point lead in polls published two weeks ago. Al Jazeera's summary line says polls indicate the candidates are neck and neck before Sunday's voting. What the same article does name as the last campaign step is street politics: both candidates are expected to join supporters in Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo before Sunday's run-off.

The policy argument, as Al Jazeera records it, turned first on the size of public spending. Lula challenged his rival's plan to take a "chainsaw" to public spending in a manner similar to that of Argentina's president, Javier Milei. Bolsonaro answered in these words: "I'll have the same job here [as Milei in Argentina] because you're leaving the country bankrupt." His stated case is that the country he would inherit is bankrupt, and that the remedy is the spending cut he associates with Milei. Lula's challenge is to that plan. The report does not set out a line-by-line budget from either side. It places the chainsaw image and the bankruptcy reply at the centre of the economic exchange.

Bolsonaro, described as the son of former right-wing president Jair Bolsonaro, then accused the incumbent of allowing organised crime to expand, driving families into debt, and not addressing the higher cost of living. Those three charges are the case he made, in the order Al Jazeera gives them: crime, then household debt, then prices. Lula, whom the report calls Brazil's first working-class president, answered the personal edge of that attack with, "He doesn't know what hunger is." Bolsonaro replied, "Why are things so expensive?" The cost-of-living argument therefore sits in the record as an exchange. Lula questioned the senator's experience, saying, "He doesn't know Brazil." His challenger doubled down on presenting himself as a capable candidate who represented change for the country. Experience against change is the second fight the debate left with viewers, beside spending and crime.

Three tense exchanges followed, and each is a claim by a named person, not a court finding. Bolsonaro called Lula "Nine Fingers," a pejorative nickname Al Jazeera says is used by many of his supporters and that references an accident the president suffered during his time as a metalworker. Lula, described as 80, demanded an apology. Bolsonaro gave it. The nickname and the apology are both in the report.

Lula in response called Bolsonaro a vigilante, referring to allegations that his rival has ties to paramilitary groups in Rio de Janeiro. Bolsonaro said he would classify those groups as "narcoterrorists." The allegation of ties is reported as an allegation Lula referred to, not as a conviction. Bolsonaro's reply is his stated classification of those groups, not a judicial outcome. One candidate alleges paramilitary ties and uses the word vigilante. The other says he would classify those groups as narcoterrorists.

The third tense exchange was between Bolsonaro and the mother of a five-year-old who was killed in a shootout that involved a policeman who had the presidential candidate as his lawyer. Bolsonaro said he does not believe his client was guilty. The policeman is described only as someone involved in that shootout and as Bolsonaro's client. He is not described here as guilty. Bolsonaro's statement is a belief about his client, given in the debate, not a verdict. The mother's presence is part of what Al Jazeera calls the third tense moment.

Friday's record is therefore a debate that opened on policy and also put character, crime and a lawyer-client claim in front of a national audience. Voters were asked to weigh a proposed cut in public spending, modelled in Lula's challenge on Milei's approach and defended by Bolsonaro as the job of a country he says is being left bankrupt. They were asked to weigh Bolsonaro's charges that organised crime has expanded, that families have been driven into debt, and that the higher cost of living has not been addressed, against Lula's replies on hunger, prices, and whether his rival knows the country. They were also asked to weigh a nickname and an apology, an allegation of paramilitary ties against a promise to label those groups narcoterrorists, and a candidate's statement that he does not believe a former client was guilty.

The people who change something by acting are Brazil's voters, in a national run-off, on Sunday. How much changes is the occupancy of the presidency and, on the candidates' own terms in Friday's debate, the direction of spending, the handling of organised crime and the cost of living, and the experience they say the office requires. How soon is Sunday, two days after the Rio debate and after the street campaigning both sides are expected to hold in Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo. This account records the debate, the Datafolha reading, and the first-round count. It does not award the weekend to either candidate.

### Both Sides: New Jersey's voter-roll cleanup before early voting
New Jersey began early voting Saturday while officials and campaigns fight over who stays on the rolls, in the same window that New York also opened early voting. Democracy Docket reports that millions of voters across both states are preparing to cast ballots as disputes over voter eligibility, mail voting and federal access to voter data have intensified in the final weeks before Election Day. The question in New Jersey is whether correction of a Motor Vehicle Commission software error, and Ocean County's residency review, should keep running through those final days, or stay narrow enough that eligible voters are not swept into a cleanup or a challenge.

House Republicans make the case for more records and more scrutiny. On Wednesday they subpoenaed acting Secretary of State Shirley Emehelu, a Democrat, for additional documents about the glitch, arguing the state's response has been inadequate. The subpoena came after New Jersey refused to provide personally identifying information about the affected voters, and after the Department of Justice lost its own lawsuit seeking broader voter-registration data from the state in July. The underlying error, as the state describes it, ran from June 2023 to June 2024: roughly 6,600 people who answered "no" when asked whether they were U.S. citizens were nevertheless marked as citizens by the state's software and transmitted for voter registration. Republicans' stated reason for pressing now is that the response so far has not been enough, and that the immediate ballot-access question is whether efforts to correct the rolls remain limited to ineligible registrations.

Governor Mikie Sherrill, a Democrat, and voting-rights groups make the case for a narrow fix. Sherrill said fewer than 400 people who were newly registered because of the error later voted. The state says those votes did not affect any election. It has spent months removing registrations that resulted solely from the error, while separately reviewing people who may have another valid registration or evidence of eligibility. In Ocean County, a separate dispute tests how far local officials can go in questioning residency just before an election. The county Board of Elections has been investigating dozens of Democratic and unaffiliated voters in Bay Head whose property-tax bills are sent outside the borough. Voting-rights groups sued, alleging the process discriminated against those voters while excluding similarly situated Republicans. A judge declined last week to halt the investigation, allowing the review to continue. Their reason is that a last-minute cleanup, or a partisan residency challenge, can catch eligible voters, force them to prove eligibility shortly before an election, and expose them to extra scrutiny at the polls or even after Election Day. The dispute involves only a few dozen voters, but Democracy Docket says the mechanism has broader implications. Early voting is already under way. Both New York and New Jersey are also preparing to count mail ballots that are lawfully cast on time but may arrive after Election Day, a process the report says could again become a target of misinformation if close congressional races remain unresolved. ### Progress Watch
Ukraine's defence ministry says Starlink and its military version, Starshield, are again recording speeds over 400 kilometres per hour, with a stable connection, after more than a week of restrictions. Defence minister Evhenii Khmara told reporters that monitoring systems are recording a return to normal speed for Starlink and Starshield, and that the connection is stable. The ministry said speed metrics that had shown disruptions have now returned to normal. The restrictions were first reported on October 14. Ukrainian officials said they undermined Ukraine's ability to intercept Russian attack drones and disrupted frontline communications. Sources said connection speeds had been reduced to about 160 kilometres per hour or less. The New York Times describes Starshield as the Pentagon-funded satellite network that Ukraine relies on to thwart Russian attacks, and says the announcement followed more than a week of disruption. France 24 reports that neither SpaceX boss Elon Musk nor the United States government has acknowledged responsibility for restricting Starlink or Starshield in Ukraine, and that both have said publicly that any such decision could only have been taken by the other. Reuters has reported that the order to throttle Starshield came from inside the White House. President Donald Trump said on Friday that if problems with access to Starlink communications systems in Ukraine persisted, he would "get it straightened out."
