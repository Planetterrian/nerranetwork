**War in the Middle East continuing to disrupt Iran’s fisheries sector, with estimated millions in losses: SeafoodSource**
In Iran, the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs says the Middle East war has heavily disrupted fisheries in Hormozgan and Bushehr, two of the country’s top commercial fishing provinces. OCHA estimated direct and indirect losses in those provinces at around 105.9 million US dollars, or 94.4 million euros. More than 60 percent of the fishing fleet there remains inactive, directly cutting the income of an estimated 37,000 fishers and causing other harm to the livelihoods of an estimated 300,000 people. Hamzeh Rostampour, head of Iran’s Fisheries Organization, put the damage at more than 60 trillion Iranian rials, about 33.1 million US dollars or 29.5 million euros, a smaller total than OCHA’s. OCHA said communities that lost fishing assets, facilities, and post-harvest infrastructure, including damage to a major shrimp hatchery in Bushehr, have no clear indication of when meaningful production might restart or when fishers would be allowed time to recoup losses. The Food and Agriculture Organization is assessing the needs of conflict-affected fisheries in southern Iran, and FAO representative Farrukh Toirov said the agency is providing emergency support through its Technical Cooperation Program to restore essential fishing capacities, support affected coastal communities, and strengthen household food security. The World Forum of Fisher People, a 29-member group that says it represents at least 10 million small-scale fishers, said its members listed fuel as up to 50 percent of operating costs, and that Iran’s restrictions on the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint for roughly 20 percent of the world’s oil, lifted Brent crude from around 70 US dollars a barrel before the conflict to above 100 and as high as 119. WFFP said diesel and gasoline costs have risen 60 to 120 percent in many markets since late February, and SeafoodSource reports that the European Union, the United States, Thailand, and Ecuador have been enabling fuel aid for commercial fishers. Despite the conflict, Iran’s Fisheries Organization has moved forward with plans to inaugurate 122 fisheries projects across 23 provinces.

**Texas elections aren’t official until they get certified. Here’s how it works.: The Texas Tribune**
In the United States, the Potter County Republican Party in the Texas Panhandle filed a lawsuit on October 27 seeking to block certification of the November 3 midterm elections unless all machine-cast paper ballots are counted by hand. The Texas Tribune reports that the suit, without providing proof, warned of potential interference from artificial intelligence and foreign adversaries such as China. Jonathan Diaz, director of voting advocacy and partnerships at the Campaign Legal Center, said certification is a ministerial duty, mandatory, with no discretion, and that by then officials have already completed the post-election checks required by state law. Joshua Blank, research director of the Texas Politics Project at the University of Texas at Austin, said the Texas Election Code states that public officials “shall certify the tabulations,” an obligation rather than a choice. Kenneth Parreno, counsel at Protect Democracy, said voting-rights advocates are watching because state and federal officials have spread disinformation about who is voting, but that under the code those officials should not and cannot refuse a valid count. The code already requires a portion of paper ballots to be hand-counted to verify machine tabulations, and it makes it a felony if someone knowingly or intentionally refuses to count votes the person knows are valid or alters a report to exclude them. Parreno said failing to certify canvassed results would violate that provision, and that if an official declines, state law says candidates and voters may be entitled to a court order to prevent the violation. Certification is the last step of canvassing, which includes tabulating ballots and verifying the count, and a certificate of election is granted weeks after election night. The governor, House speaker, and county commissioners play a role by race, and local canvassing authorities, typically a county commissioners court, certify local contests such as city council and mayor, with experts saying they have little to do beyond approving the results.

**Hope floats on Louisiana’s shrimp boats, despite dire economic times: The Globe and Mail**
In the United States, Louisiana shrimpers are still enlarging boats while the Gulf and South Atlantic catch loses value. The Globe and Mail reports that the value of the domestic shrimp catch fell 65 percent between 2000 and 2024, from about 738 million US dollars to nearly 258 million US dollars. Captain Ronnie Anderson, 59, known as Chevo and vice president of the Louisiana Shrimp Association, is investing around 100,000 US dollars to lengthen his 81-foot trawler, Last of the Mohicans, so it can hold 80,000 pounds, after he estimates he has already spent 400,000 US dollars on the boat since buying it. The upgrade has taken more than two months of around-the-clock work at the Gulfbound shipyard in Bayou Little Caillou, which Lucy Pinell, 58, co-owns with her husband Randall, 61. Anderson told the Globe the industry’s turn came after European restrictions in 2002 on Asian shrimp found with prohibited antibiotics sent rejected shrimp into US markets, and that industrial aquaculture now supplies inexpensive shrimp. The Globe reports that a flood of imported shrimp, rising fuel and operating costs, and dwindling profits have driven fishermen to sell boats, leave the trade, and discourage their children from following them. Pinell said she wants the shrimping culture to stay. The paper says controversies over President Donald Trump’s immigration policies, what it calls his attacks on American democracy, and the war in Iran do not resonate in these communities, which have stayed loyal to him. Anderson, who has shrimped since childhood in Buras and whose boat was the only shrimping vessel in his hometown not destroyed in Hurricane Katrina, said he expects to survive and wants a next generation in the trade.

**Pakistan, Turkmenistan discuss transit trade deal and transport corridors: Trend News Agency**
In Pakistan, lawmakers and Turkmenistan’s ambassador discussed putting a transit trade agreement to work, not a pact signed on Thursday. Trend News Agency reports that Sanjay Perwani, head of Pakistan’s Parliamentary Friendship Group with Turkmenistan, met Ambassador Atadjan Movlamov at Parliament House in Islamabad. A National Assembly press release said the talks focused on expanding bilateral trade and on finalising and operationalising the Pakistan-Turkmenistan Transit Trade Agreement, particularly because Turkmenistan is landlocked and needs reliable transit routes. The sides also stressed regional transport corridors to improve connectivity and facilitate trade. Pakistan has been seeking a role as a transit and maritime gateway for landlocked Central Asian countries through the ports of Karachi, Port Qasim, and Gwadar on the Arabian Sea. In July 2026, President Asif Ali Zardari and Kyrgyz President Sadyr Zhaparov stressed multimodal transport and greater use of Pakistani seaports, and their joint statement reaffirmed existing agreements on transport, logistics, and transit. In September 2025, Pakistani officials discussed with a Kazakh delegation road, rail, and maritime corridors linking Central Asia with the Arabian Sea, including port operations and free trade zones. Trend reports that any practical benefit for Turkmenistan would depend on implementation, transit arrangements, and whether the routes are reliable and competitive.

### Both Sides: Should India open farm markets in a US trade deal?
India and the United States have not signed the trade pact whose framework they unveiled in February, a framework that brought down President Donald Trump’s punitive tariffs on most Indian goods. What is being decided is whether the first phase, which New Delhi says is nearly final, gives American farm goods new access to India.

Trade minister Piyush Goyal said in New Delhi on Thursday that India will not compromise the interests of its farmers or other stakeholders, and that once details are out Indians will celebrate that not a single sensitive farm or stakeholder interest was compromised. His stated reason is that a finished text can protect those interests and still leave India with a strong advantage.

The Samyukt Kisan Morcha, a coalition of more than 100 farm groups, said earlier this month that the deal put farmers for sale because it eliminated India’s high tariffs on maize, almonds, walnuts, apples, and soybean oil, among other products. Farming groups have repeatedly asked the government to share more of the text, arguing that imports of subsidised US farm products could depress domestic prices and hurt rural incomes. The union says it will organise further demonstrations in November to protest the deal and seek its withdrawal.

The next dated step the groups have named is those November demonstrations. The two governments have not signed off on the pact.
