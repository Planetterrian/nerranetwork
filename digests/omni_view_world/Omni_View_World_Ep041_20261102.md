**Egypt signs $1 billion World Bank deal as war compounds economic pressures: The National**
Egypt’s foreign minister, Badr Abdelatty, signed a $1 billion World Bank financing agreement on Sunday, the second of three concessional development-policy operations. The bank’s board approved it in May; it includes a $200 million British credit guarantee and is tied to reforms of state-owned enterprises, competition rules, tax collection and the cost of government borrowing. The bank said then that Egypt had endured two years of successive external shocks, and The National reports those pressures have deepened because the US-Israeli war on Iran has raised oil, shipping and insurance costs for a country that relies on imports. Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly told the Egypt Forum in late October that oil was trading around $110 a barrel, against $75 assumed in the budget, and that the government chose to absorb the gap “at this stage” rather than pass costs to citizens, borrow to cover them, or risk power cuts of six to eight hours a day. President Abdel Fattah El Sisi told the same forum that fuel and electricity prices were “completely uneconomic” and sold below cost. Finance Minister Ahmed Kouchouk put subsidies at 840 billion Egyptian pounds ($15 billion) this fiscal year, with borrowing costs adding about 20 percent and bringing the real bill near 1 trillion pounds ($17.9 billion). The dollar rose by almost 8 percent, from about 52 pounds at the start of October to a record above 56 pounds on October 26, and about $4 billion of foreign money is estimated to have left Egyptian government debt between October 15 and 26. Central Bank Governor Hassan Abdalla called a flexible exchange rate the “first line of defence” and said unifying the rate mattered more than the rate itself; on Thursday the bank raised rates by 100 basis points to 20 percent, the first increase since March 2024, and pushed its inflation target to late 2027. Trading halted for 30 minutes on October 25, the first suspension since 2021, after the EGX100 index fell 10 percent, while remittances hit a record $33.9 billion in the first eight months and Suez Canal income, near $10 billion a year before Red Sea attacks, has recovered only to about $4.4 billion.

**Nuclear radiation in Russia, Ukraine? No, just hacked warning alerts: Al Jazeera**
Ukraine’s Air Force said late on Sunday that users of its Air Alert app received fake warnings of a radiation threat, a chemical attack and street fighting in and around Kyiv, and of a Russian Oreshnik missile. One message was in Russian. Ukrainian authorities said within the hour that unauthorised parties had interfered and that the app had been taken offline, and the Air Force wrote that there was no radiation threat. Ukraine’s Center for Strategic Communications blamed Russia and pointed to the Russian-language message; the government said a separate app for the capital was working normally. Al Jazeera’s Audrey Macalpine, reporting from Kyiv, said speakers in the city centre also sounded the warnings and that most people understood within about half an hour that this was a hacking attack. The Guardian reported that the ministry took the app offline temporarily and said the wider system — texts, broadcasts, street sirens and loudspeakers — was still operating. Media reports cited by Al Jazeera said the Russian alerts warned of nuclear warheads on drones across all regions and told people to stock food for three weeks and hide. Al Jazeera said this appears to be the first time either country’s official alert app has been hacked, and that the fake threats began the same day a six-day local ceasefire in the Russian-occupied Kherson region ended, a truce the United States said was brokered by first lady Melania Trump.

**Sudan files complaints against Ethiopia with UN over deadly drone spree: Al Jazeera**
Sudan’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs said late on Sunday that it had lodged complaints with the United Nations Security Council and the African Union’s Peace and Security Council over drone attacks this year. The ministry accused the Rapid Support Forces of using Ethiopian territory to strike “civilian sites and power stations,” and said the incidents place Ethiopia before international law. Sudan’s military has repeatedly accused Ethiopia of aiding the RSF in a civil war under way since April 2023; Ethiopia has accused the Sudanese military of backing rebels linked to the Tigray People’s Liberation Front, a charge Khartoum denied. An army-led Transitional Sovereignty Council report alleged 70 assaults from February 15 to October 27 involving 187 one-way attack drones, saying the attacks killed or wounded 484 civilians, killed 176 soldiers and wounded another 200. The report said drones had taken off from Ethiopian soil since February 2026 and named launch sites at Asosa Airport, the Kurmuk-Gizen border area and Bahir Dar airbase. It said last month brought 28 attacks by 97 drones, and it cited an October 9 strike on displaced families in Wad el-Mahi that it said killed 30 civilians — 26 women and four children — and wounded 100. A spokesman for UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said on Saturday that Guterres was “appalled” and “strongly condemns the escalating drone attacks in Sudan by the parties to the conflict,” calling the strikes violations of international humanitarian law, days after another strike on a market in RSF-controlled Darfur killed more than 100 people.

**The DOJ plans to send poll monitors to Philly on Election Day. Here’s what we know: Inquirer.com**
The US Department of Justice plans to send election monitors to polling places in Philadelphia and in Allegheny, Luzerne and Lehigh counties on Election Day, according to an October 22 letter to Pennsylvania’s Department of State. Those counties include Pittsburgh, Allentown and part of Bethlehem. The department said the monitors’ job is to check compliance with the Voting Rights Act, the National Voter Registration Act, the Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act and other federal laws. Trial attorney James T. Catania wrote that monitors “only observe and take notes,” will not intrude, will identify themselves to each poll manager, will wear business casual clothes without DOJ insignia and will not carry firearms. The Inquirer, citing CBS News, reported that the department plans a record 1,000 monitors nationwide, up from 289 in the 2022 midterms and 714 in the 2024 presidential election. State Deputy Secretary Jonathan Marks told county officials there is no specific federal or state statute authorizing the monitors and that they have no greater access than the general public. A Department of State spokesperson said they may be inside polling locations but must stay at least 10 feet outside the area where voting machines are located, and Marks said they may not enter restricted areas, inspect equipment or demand records. Vic Walczak of the American Civil Liberties Union of Pennsylvania said the monitors are not a cause for alarm, and the Philadelphia mayor’s office assured people there was nothing to worry about; the Inquirer, citing Newsweek, reported that Indiana, Kentucky and one Arkansas county have told the department to remain outside.

**Ryanair says it may freeze workers' pay and raise airfares: BBC World**
Ryanair is considering a temporary wage freeze for staff and said airfares will be “materially higher” next summer because fuel costs have jumped after the US-Israel war with Iran. The airline said it expects fuel prices to stay high well into 2027 unless there is a swift resolution, and it forecast its fuel bill €2 billion (£1.6 billion) higher in the short term. Chief executive Michael O’Leary said fares would have to rise by double digits to help cover the cost, and he repeated an earlier forecast that Ryanair would need a 20 percent fare increase to cover jet fuel. He said he was not sure the airline would get to 20 percent, but that he was looking at double-digit fare increases across Europe from all airlines in 2027. Pre-tax profits fell 16 percent to €2.4 billion in the six months to the end of September, which the airline linked largely to fuel costs that jumped 15 percent. Ryanair said 80 percent of its fuel bill had been hedged and that the cost of the rest was elevated, and it tied the surge in aviation fuel to a sharp cut in refined products from the Gulf. It said a pay freeze could start in April as multi-year wage deals end, and that it would use more artificial intelligence in back-office and support work.

### Both Sides: Should the NHS end Palantir's data-platform contract?
Britain’s health secretary, Yvette Cooper, faces calls to end Palantir’s contract for the National Health Service federated data platform, built to join scattered medical files, speed discharges and improve surgery scheduling. NHS England’s website says 137 trusts are live and lists 29 as “realising benefits” from the £330 million system. Those 29, in freedom-of-information replies gathered by the charity Medact, said the system is not live or that they are not using its applications. Epsom and St Helier said it was not using the platform at the moment. University Hospitals of North Midlands said it is not live and that the trust does not intend to roll it out.

Chi Onwurah, chair of the Commons science and technology committee, argues that if many trusts do not see the value, serious questions follow about whether the hype matches reality. She wants ministers to activate the 2027 break clause and open a competition for a supplier owned and based in the United Kingdom. The Commons health select committee is also calling for the deal to be pulled. Dr Rhiannon Mihranian Osborne of Medact says there is no good reason to continue what she calls a failing project.

Louis Mosley, Palantir’s UK and Europe chief executive, says ending the contract would be “putting politics above patients,” and he accuses critics of “Palantir Derangement Syndrome.” His case is that cancelling the firm would sacrifice the gains the platform is meant to deliver for patients. The Office for Statistics Regulation said it will take the “realising benefits” claim into its casework, after NHS England conceded it could not prove that 117,539 more operations at trusts using the platform were caused by it.
