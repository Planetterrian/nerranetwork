**Russia bombs aid centre and shelter in Ukraine, killing at least 29 people: The Guardian**
In Ukraine, Russia bombed an aid distribution centre and a neighbouring shelter in Komyshuvakha, a village in the southern Zaporizhzhia region, killing at least 29 people, with 48 still missing. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia launched 289 attack drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles across nine regions, triggering emergency power cuts, and that the barrage coincided with the US elections. He said the Russians think America will not notice this signal, and that Ukraine needs help defending its skies or Russia will never stop its missile strikes. The guided-bomb attack first hit a school building used to distribute humanitarian aid, then a nearby shelter. Mykhailo Semikin, acting head of the regional military administration, said the dead included four police officers and two rescue workers, that at least five people were hurt, and that Russian drones launched further attacks that hindered rescue work on Monday evening. In the north-east Kharkiv region, local authorities said a combined missile and drone attack wounded at least 62 people, damaged a medical facility in the Nemyshlianskyi district, shattered windows in four apartment buildings and damaged cars; Oleh Syniehubov, head of the regional military administration, said more than 200 patients were transferred. In the Dnipro area, local authorities said an attack killed one person and wounded 17, and rescuers pulled a woman alive from the rubble on Tuesday morning. As night fell in Komyshuvakha, emergency responders used torches to search collapsed buildings, according to photos and video posted by local officials. Zelenskyy called the Komyshuvakha attack vile and said it showed Vladimir Putin does not want to de-escalate; he said residents remaining there gathered each week for aid packages, and that Moscow was killing people rather than building on last week’s ceasefire in the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky, brokered by US first lady Melania Trump. The Kremlin said no other similar missions were being planned at the moment. That pause was the longest in the war, now in its fifth year, which has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced millions. US special envoy Steve Witkoff said US-led talks were due on 10 and 11 November in Abu Dhabi; the Guardian reported that Kyiv and its European allies worry Donald Trump has increasingly sided with Moscow.

**Voters head to the polls as control of Congress is on the ballot: The New York Times**
In the United States, voters began heading to the polls on Tuesday in midterms that will decide which party controls Congress for the final two years of President Trump’s second term. All 435 voting House seats are contested; The Guardian reported that Republicans are defending a narrow majority and that Democrats need a net gain of four seats to take the House. Thirty-five Senate seats are up, 33 in regular elections and two special elections in Florida and Ohio; Republicans hold 53 seats, Democrats and independents who caucus with them hold 47, and Democrats need four seats for a majority, The Guardian reported. Voters are also choosing governors in 36 states and three US territories, plus thousands of state legislative seats and ballot measures on taxes, abortion rights and voting rules. The New York Times reported that President Trump has urged supporters to act as if he is on the ballot and has insisted Republicans would win if his base turned out, while Democratic campaigns have blamed his policies for the struggles of Americans trying to make ends meet, and Republicans have tried to frame the choice as one against opponents they cast as radical, far-left activists. A final New York Times and Siena University poll, published on Sunday, put Democratic candidates ahead in five of seven key battleground contests, including Texas and Kansas, where voters have not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1988 and 1932, respectively, The Guardian reported. The Times reported that early voting has broken recent midterm records, with more than tens of millions of ballots already cast, that early turnout exceeded the last midterm cycle in each major Senate battleground, and that the share of registered Democrats rose in the early vote in several key states. It also reported that this is the most expensive midterm in political advertising since at least 2018, according to AdImpact, and that Elon Musk contributed $474 million to Republican candidates and their allies, more than any donor has spent in a single cycle. In Michigan, Democratic congresswoman Haley Stevens, who lost her party’s Senate primary to Abdul El-Sayed, endorsed his Republican rival, Mike Rogers, on Sunday, The Guardian reported. The president’s party has lost House seats in 18 of the past 20 midterms, a 90 percent rate of loss back to 1946, The Guardian reported. The first polls close at 7 p.m. Eastern, and the Times said full results could take days or weeks; in Texas, the Texas Tribune reported, domestic mail-in ballots postmarked by election day count if they arrive by 5 p.m. on 4 November, while military and overseas ballots must be postmarked by election day and received by 9 November.

**US hits Qeshm Island, Pezeshkian signals more diplomacy: Iran war latest: Al Jazeera**
In Iran, US forces struck Qeshm Island late on Monday, the first attack there since June, in what officials called a defensive measure, as both sides kept military and diplomatic tracks open. Iran’s state news agency IRNA said the blasts came in rapid succession and that witnesses saw two planes over the island, off Iran’s southern coast along the Strait of Hormuz. The Wall Street Journal, quoting an unnamed official, said the Monday-night strikes appeared limited and were not a resumption of a major operation; France 24, citing the Journal and unnamed US officials, described the hit on the largest island in the strait as the latest in a series of limited strikes Washington has secretly carried out in recent weeks, even as President Trump signalled progress in talks. The Associated Press quoted an unnamed US official as saying Washington conducted the strikes against Iranians using surface-to-air missiles against US assets. IRNA reported that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps had destroyed a US MQ-9 drone east of the Strait of Hormuz. Al Jazeera reported that the strikes came even as Vice President JD Vance described progress in talks. The war began on 28 February, when the United States and Israel launched strikes across Iran, closing the strait, sending oil and gas prices up and damaging military, civil and energy sites around the Gulf; on 7 March the United States struck a desalination plant on Qeshm, which Tehran called a flagrant crime against civilians and which cut freshwater to 30 surrounding villages. Qatari mediators have passed messages in indirect talks, but those talks appeared to end on Monday with no breakthrough, Al Jazeera reported. An official briefed on the talks told Reuters that Iran’s delegation left Doha on Sunday while a draft was still circulating, and that Qatari mediators travelled to Tehran on Monday night to meet Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi on a draft under negotiation for eight days. Araghchi told IRNA that consultations through mediators are very intensive, that Iran has presented its conditions and proposals, and that everything depends on the political rationality of Washington. Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said the ball was in the US court, that Iran’s demands were clear and logical, and that a plan submitted in September could be shortened and implemented in two days, including reopening the Strait of Hormuz and returning to talks on Iran’s nuclear programme. President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei had given the government certain authorities over talks with the United States, and that Tehran would look to resolve the conflict through dialogue.

**Trump to remove Rwanda and South Africa from US trade deal: BBC**
In South Africa and Rwanda, President Donald Trump has notified Congress that he intends to remove both from the African Growth and Opportunity Act from 1 January 2027. The programme lets eligible African countries export thousands of products to the United States duty-free. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said an annual review found that both countries were engaging in practices inconsistent with the programme’s eligibility requirements, that both governments had been notified, and that the administration is working to ensure African partners meet those criteria. The administration cited barriers to US trade and investment and concerns over national security and foreign policy; for South Africa, it pointed to corruption. In a letter to President Cyril Ramaphosa, Trump said Pretoria’s actions on international security and its cooperation with governments of concern to Washington risked undermining US national security and foreign policy interests, and he accused South Africa of significant barriers to American trade and investment, including on agricultural market access, regulatory requirements, investment protections and intellectual property. Trade Minister Parks Tau called the decision unfortunate, said Washington was raising new issues that had not featured in negotiations begun in 2025, and said South Africa would accelerate diversification of export markets to protect jobs. Government figures he cited show about 89 percent of South African exports to the United States entered under ordinary World Trade Organization most-favoured-nation terms in 2025, while about 10 percent benefited from Agoa, down from 28 percent in 2024, and that the value of exports under the programme fell to about $1.7 billion in 2025 from $4 billion the year before. Bilateral trade reached $28.8 billion in 2025, and more than 600 American companies operate in South Africa, supporting about 134,600 jobs. Pretoria says South Africa supplies 12 of the 50 minerals the United States considers critical, and 70 percent of American isotopes used among other things in cancer treatment, and that South African manufacturers use American inputs in vehicles and boats exported under Agoa. US Ambassador Leo Brent Bozell III wrote on X that it was regrettable South African policies led to termination from Agoa, and that the outcome was predictable and preventable after the government rejected concerns raised by the United States. For Rwanda, Washington also raised human rights, political pluralism, the rule of law and workers’ rights. The BBC reported that the announcement comes amid strained relations, and that last year Trump accused South Africa of persecuting its white minority, launched a programme for white Afrikaners to seek asylum in the United States, and repeated claims of a genocide of white South Africans that the BBC described as widely discredited.
Ranks: Exporters and workers in South Africa and Rwanda · duty-free access to the US market · from 1 January 2027 Source: [bbc.com](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c9j346xg0mkvo)

### Both Sides: Should the United States end Agoa access for South Africa and Rwanda?
What is being decided is whether South Africa and Rwanda stay in the African Growth and Opportunity Act, the programme that lets eligible African countries export thousands of products to the United States duty-free. President Donald Trump has notified Congress that he intends to remove both from 1 January 2027. The removal is an intention notified to Congress and to the two governments, not a change already in force.

The case for ending access, as the Trump administration states it, is eligibility. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said an annual review found practices inconsistent with the programme’s rules, and that the administration wants African partners to meet those criteria. For South Africa, Washington pointed to corruption, to barriers on agriculture, regulation, investment protection and intellectual property, and, in Trump’s letter to President Cyril Ramaphosa, to Pretoria’s security actions and ties with governments of concern to Washington. US Ambassador Leo Brent Bozell III said South Africa’s exit was regrettable but predictable and preventable, because the government rejected concerns the United States had raised.

The case against, as South Africa’s government states it, is that the penalty is poorly matched to the trade. Trade Minister Parks Tau called the decision unfortunate and said Washington was raising new issues that had not featured in talks begun in 2025. He said South Africa would diversify export markets to protect jobs. Government figures he cited put Agoa’s share of exports to the United States at about 10 percent in 2025, down from 28 percent in 2024, and the value at about $1.7 billion, down from $4 billion, against $28.8 billion in bilateral trade. Pretoria also says it supplies 12 of 50 minerals the United States lists as critical, and 70 percent of American isotopes used among other things in cancer treatment. The next dated step in the articles is 1 January 2027, the date Trump gave Congress for the intended removal.
