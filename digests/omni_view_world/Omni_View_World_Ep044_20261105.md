**Dozens dead and missing after Russian strike on Ukrainian aid centre: France 24**
In Ukraine, officials say the death toll from a Russian bombing of an aid distribution point in the southeastern Zaporizhzhia region has risen to 42. Dozens of people are still missing, the same officials said. France 24 describes the strike as one of the deadliest on civilians this year. The bombing was on Monday. It struck people gathered at a point where aid was being handed out, not a military position named in the report. The attack came a day after a brief US-brokered local ceasefire expired. Less than a week on, the United States is due to bring Ukrainian and Russian delegations together in Abu Dhabi for talks on ending the war. That war is now in its fifth year. The talks are the next dated step the report names; the missing are still unaccounted for as the toll is given.

**New bomb threats bring fresh disruptions to flights across Europe: Al Jazeera**
Across Europe on Thursday, bomb threats forced about ten commercial flights into unplanned or high-alert landings, Al Jazeera's Frances Read said from London. An Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome was diverted to Evreux, west of Paris, escorted by French military aircraft, and landed safely. The office of Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu said it was monitoring the incident in real time, that a bomb squad was in place, and that 151 passengers and six crew were moved to safety. No one claimed responsibility, and officials did not attribute blame. In Bulgaria, Ryanair flights from Copenhagen and Madrid landed in Sofia under alert, and a Ryanair service from London Stansted diverted to Plovdiv, the airport's chief executive said. Czech police said four planes bound for Prague had been threatened with improvised explosive devices, judged the relevance of the threats low, and still prepared pyrotechnic inspections after landing so the effect on passengers and operations would be as small as possible. A LOT flight from Warsaw to Brussels landed at Zaventem after cabin crew found nothing suspicious and a further inspection found no dangerous items; the airport said no extra security measures were required. Wizz Air said a Milan-to-Warsaw flight diverted to Vienna after a hoax threat, and a separate Wizz Air service from Milan toward Bucharest was also listed among Thursday's alerts. A Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by a fighter jet and checked by bomb-disposal experts, while a threat against a Helsinki-to-Vienna flight, operated by Jettime, ended in a scheduled landing with no incident. Deutsche Welle reports the alerts follow threats earlier this week, including against LOT, Scandinavian Airlines, Wizz Air and Ryanair, that turned out to be hoaxes, and that no sign of dangerous materials had yet been reported on Thursday.
Ranks: Passengers and crews on about ten flights, plus airport staff in at least six countries · Diversions, escorts and searches, with no device reported so far · Thursday morning, and still unfolding. **A month after Yemen government offensive began, war of attrition takes hold: Al Jazeera**
In Yemen, a month after the internationally recognised government launched Operation Dawn of Yemen on October 5, Sanaa is largely calm apart from recurring air strikes, while fighting has not stopped in the south. Government forces, backed by a Saudi Arabia-led coalition, said they aimed to retake all territory held by the Houthis, including the capital, which the Houthis have held since late 2014 after seizing it in the opening phase of a civil war now more than a decade old. Al Jazeera reports the Houthis, also known as Ansar Allah, have since scored territorial gains in Taiz and Lahij, where fighting has not ceased. The Yemeni government said last month that its forces killed or wounded 1,540 Houthis in 24 hours. Abdulsalam Mohammed, chairman of the Abaad Studies and Research Center, said the offensive forced the Houthis to move forces and equipment, exposing them to air strikes, and that many field commanders and hundreds of fighters were killed, especially in Taiz and on the western coast. He said those deaths have not stopped the territorial gains, but that the gains could be short-lived if government air power and ground forces keep attacking together. Turki al-Goblan, head of the Saudi Daimumah Center for Studies, said the offensive has hit three pillars he described as Houthi fuel and supply lines, maritime routes around Bab al-Mandeb and the western coast, and reliance on tribes around Sanaa, some of which he said have begun mobilising for the recognised government. Bab al-Mandeb links the Red Sea and the Arabian Sea and is a route for Saudi oil exports and for Asian goods bound for Europe. He argued the pace of attrition has outrun the Houthis' ability to replace losses, and that this is the more likely path to a decisive result. Mohammed Ahmed, a 40-year-old Sanaa resident, told Al Jazeera his fear was fighting inside the country's most densely populated city, and that the fear has eased as the capital stayed mostly quiet.
Ranks: Residents of Sanaa, Taiz and Lahij, and shipping through Bab al-Mandeb · A new phase of attrition, with disputed ground and a government casualty claim · Ongoing, one month into the offensive. **Not just Congress: Democrats’ blue wave washes over state legislatures across US: The Guardian**
In the United States, Democrats gained hundreds of state legislative seats in Tuesday's midterm elections, The Guardian reports. Ballots are still being counted. The paper says the party's success appears historic, and that the wins were under the radar beside the congressional result. It reports Democrats have not held full control of government in Arizona in 60 years, in Pennsylvania since the 1990s, or in Wisconsin since their wipeout in 2010. In Michigan and Minnesota, The Guardian reports, they regained trifectas they lost after the 2024 election. In Texas, it reports, they will control the state house for the first time since 2002. The Guardian says those chamber wins may have major implications for daily life, because state legislatures write much of the law people actually live under. It also says the party is set to embark on an aggressive legislative spree once the new majorities are seated. The count is not finished, so the paper presents the scale as what the returns so far appear to show, not as a final national tally of seats.

**How elderly ‘unsung heroes’ powered Lula’s historic fightback in Brazil: The Guardian**
In Brazil, The Guardian reports that older voters helped President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva overturn a first-round loss in the October 25 runoff against Flávio Bolsonaro. Lula lost the October 4 first round. More than 1 million elderly people who missed that round voted in the second, among them Celia de Oliveira Soares, 101, who had not voted in nearly a decade and cast number 13 for Lula. Never since the return of democracy in the 1980s had a first-round loser come back, and Lula did, The Guardian reports, finding 10.3 million extra votes between the rounds. Of 3 million extra voters who skipped the first round and took part in the second, 1.4 million were over 70. Voting is mandatory in Brazil only for people aged 18 to 69, so the over-70 turnout was voluntary. Political journalist José Roberto de Toledo said turnout was measurably higher among older voters, particularly those aged 60 to 79, and that images of people in their 80s, 90s and 100s being carried to polling stations matched a statistical movement rather than a staged one. The Guardian lists other explanations still being weighed by election experts: a sharp rise in welfare benefits on the eve of the vote, a pro-Lula WhatsApp group that grew from 50,000 to nearly 11 million members in less than a month, and street protests it describes as involving millions. At his 81st birthday party, Lula himself credited elderly voters who reached polling stations with walking frames, sticks, stretchers and wheelchairs. At a victory celebration, former minister Simone Tebet called the result a cross-generational triumph and said young people hit the streets while older voters saved democracy.
Ranks: Brazil's electorate, including more than a million older people who voted only in the runoff · A completed presidential result, with 10.3 million extra second-round votes · Already decided on October 25. ### Both Sides: Should the United States end the Iran war now?
The question is whether Washington should end the Iran war now, after Republicans lost control of Congress. Vice President JD Vance makes the case for treating the war as the loss that has to be faced. He wrote on Wednesday that the reason the party lost is the war, and that the president himself said so the night before. He asked anyone who believes losing the Senate for the next six years was worth it to argue that case. Citing an unnamed pollster, he said that ending the Iran war and changing the relationship with Israel were among the top four answers given, ahead of this week's midterms, by Americans who voted for the Republican ticket in 2024 and planned to vote Democratic this year. President Donald Trump makes the case for continuing until a nuclear bar is locked in. IranWire reports that on Wednesday he said what the United States did with Iran had to be done and is continuing, and that the conflict was worth the political cost of losing congressional control. He said he can end the war whenever he wants, but that it has to be done right, so Iran can never resume a nuclear program after American forces withdraw. Al Jazeera reports he told The Wall Street Journal, "I did the right thing. I would have done it again," about launching the war alongside Israel in February, and told reporters that Vance was largely right that higher fuel prices turned voters, while insisting the war was necessary to stop a nuclear weapon. Senator Ted Cruz, speaking from a hawkish position rather than an end-it-now one, said the war should have been brief, decisive and over, that it dragged on too long, and that Washington should never give the Iranian leadership billions of dollars. Al Jazeera tied that remark to a 300-billion-dollar rebuild fund discussed in a June memorandum of understanding that quickly fell apart. Iran's parliamentary speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said Americans used their votes to say no to war. No end date is set. Trump's stated condition is a withdrawal that leaves Iran unable to restart a nuclear program.
