**Iran War passes 8-month mark: WLWT**
The war with Iran has passed its eighth month, and Pentagon figures now put a casualty count and a dollar cost on it for Congress and for U.S. forces. Twenty-three U.S. service members have died and more than 850 have been injured, according to Pentagon data as of October 30, WLWT reports. The latest Pentagon estimate to Congress puts the war’s cost at 47.1 billion dollars. WLWT says that bill is reaching U.S. consumers in everything from gas to mortgages. President Donald Trump has said the war will end when he can ensure that Iran will not have a nuclear weapon. At a campaign rally in Georgia two weekends ago, Trump said the country is making hundreds of billions of dollars because of what is happening in the Middle East, and that pump prices are going to come down, West Virginia Watch reports. He told that crowd he did not care if the headline was that he was talking about the money while people suffered at the pump, and said, “We’re making a freaking fortune.” That column reports that on Friday ExxonMobil’s profits doubled from the same period last year and Chevron’s nearly tripled. It says a major oil shipping lane has been closed, gas prices have risen, high diesel costs have lifted the price of goods through transport, and October inflation numbers were higher than usual. The columnist asks who is taking that fortune while U.S. soldiers have died and civilians in Iran and other Middle Eastern countries have died, and says national leaders are boasting of corporate profits while cutting food assistance and healthcare.

**Tanzanian opposition leader found guilty of inciting unrest but acquitted of treason: BBC**
In Tanzania, the High Court in Dar es Salaam found opposition leader Tundu Lissu guilty of inciting unrest and sentenced him to four years in jail, the BBC reports. A panel of three judges acquitted the 58-year-old of treason, saying prosecutors had failed to prove a key element of that charge. Treason is among the country’s most serious offences and can carry the death penalty. Lissu, who leads Chadema and has been detained since April last year, denied the charges and called the prosecution politically motivated. He was arrested six months before the poll, shortly after launching a campaign for changes to the electoral system. The court said his slogan “No Reforms, No Election” was enough for an incitement conviction, ruling that by uttering those words with the intention of disseminating them he incited the public to disrupt the 2025 elections. Lissu told the judges they had manufactured an offence that did not exist, on political directives, and said he had been charged only with treason, not incitement. He said he would apply for bail, which he said is available for incitement but not for treason, and he has leave to appeal to the Court of Appeal. A lawyer who represented himself, he argued that his remarks, also circulated online, were a lawful campaign, and that prosecutors had taken them out of context. Chadema’s demands included independently appointed electoral commissioners, police allowing parties to campaign free of intimidation, an end to government-appointed district chiefs acting as returning officers, party agents observing the count without harassment, and a right to challenge presidential results in court. President Samia Suluhu Hassan won the October 2025 election with 98 percent of the vote, a result the opposition called a mockery of democracy after her two main challengers, including Lissu, were excluded; her government has repeatedly denied targeting opponents. In remand at Ukonga Prison he went on hunger strike twice for brief periods, saying he was denied access to lawyers or relatives and that guards listened to consultations; the prison authorities have denied those allegations.

**Employers to be forced to put salaries on job ads, Andy Burnham announces: The Guardian**
In Britain, Prime Minister Andy Burnham said his government will make it a legal requirement for employers to state what they pay when they list jobs, the Guardian reports. He said a young person starting out, or a parent supporting a family, should know the pay before spending time on an application. Officials say publishing pay will also help people already in work see whether they are paid fairly beside others. Workers’ rights campaigners say the measure will particularly help young people and women seek more equal pay. Paul Nowak, general secretary of the Trades Union Congress, said anyone who has searched for a job knows how frustrating it is to read “competitive salary.” Experts say leaving pay off an advert makes it harder for young people to know which jobs to apply for, and makes it easier for employers to offer lower pay to some candidates, including women. Burnham has used his early months for smaller changes, including removing VAT from electricity bills, reducing bus fares, and a 100-pound council-tax rebate, while he works on a 10-year plan he says is aimed at ending what he calls 40 years of neoliberalism. He said this week that plan’s publication could be delayed until next year, having originally been expected next month. Ministers consulted in July on equal-pay changes and have not yet set final plans for greater protections for disabled people and ethnic minorities. They have promised further policy changes to help young people into work after a review by former minister Alan Milburn, due in the coming weeks. The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development found that less than half of employers always publish salary ranges, and less than a fifth always publish a specific salary. The government said 93 percent of businesses responded positively to the consultation; ministers have not yet decided what must be published, which size of company must comply, or how the rule will be enforced.
Ranks: jobseekers and workers across Britain · pay hidden on ads would have to be shown · announced, not yet in force Source: [theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/law/2026/nov/06/uk-employers-forced-put-salaries-job-adverts-andy-burnham)

**Two dead after bus crashes in France, officials say: BBC**
In France, one child and one young adult died after a bus crashed on Friday in Lent, in the east of the country, local authorities told the BBC. The bus was travelling between Lyon and Bourg-en-Bresse and crashed at about 7 a.m. local time, about 34 miles, or 55 kilometres, from the Swiss border. Mayor Yves Cristin told French media the bus left the road and hit a tree in a ditch. He told BFM TV he arrived minutes after the crash, that people were in shock, and that the side of the bus was torn off, so those seated on that side must have been seriously hurt. The Bourg-en-Bresse prosecutor’s office said the bus carried 43 passengers, including 15 children; three people are in a critical condition and a further 13 have sustained minor injuries. The driver was taken to hospital with injuries and has not yet been questioned by police, the prosecutor’s office said. Education Minister Édouard Geffray said the crash involved students, and told reporters it is always difficult when children are victims of accidents of this nature. Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot, on his way to the area, said more than 100 firefighters had been sent. Emergency services were at the scene, and medical helicopters took the seriously injured to a specialised hospital. The mayor said the local town hall was opened to shelter people and to coordinate with those services. Photos in French media show a blue and white bus tilted in a roadside ditch, trees along the road, and heavy damage to the front.

**Pollution, shortages, crime: Daily life in Pakistan's megacity Karachi: France 24**
In Pakistan, residents of Karachi blocked several major roads in recent days after hours of power cuts, France 24 reports. The city has more than 21 million people and is the country’s economic hub, home to a large share of its businesses, jobs, and wealth. The Economist Intelligence Unit’s 2026 Global Liveability Index ranks Karachi 170th of 173 cities, ahead of only Dhaka, Tripoli, and Damascus. France 24 calls that a striking contrast for the economic powerhouse. The city receives only about 55 percent of the water it needs. In some neighbourhoods, power cuts can last up to 19 hours a day. It generates nearly 15,000 tonnes of waste every day, and almost a quarter of that never reaches a landfill. Residents also face crippling traffic congestion, inadequate public transport, crime, and chronic shortages of basic services. Despite several major urban development projects in recent years, France 24 says the infrastructure is struggling to keep pace with rapid growth. The report is by Sonia Ghezali, Shahzaib Wahlah, Ondine de Gaulle, and Jamil Iqbal.

### Both Sides: Denmark’s “non-western” housing classification
Denmark’s supreme court is expected to rule on whether the state’s classification of residents with “non-western” backgrounds is covered by racial discrimination law, in an appeal by people evicted from the Nøjsomhed-Sydvej housing complex in Helsingør, north of Copenhagen. The Guardian reports that the case could reach others forced out under legislation once known as the ghetto law, which the government now describes as a policy on “parallel societies.”

The residents’ case, as the appeal frames it, is that the classification itself is the harm: they were removed from their homes under a rule that sorts people by a “non-western” background, and they are asking the supreme court to decide that this sorting falls under racial discrimination law. Their strongest reason, on the facts the court has been given, is that the label is what put them in the class of people who could be evicted, so ethnicity is not incidental to the removal.

The government’s case is that the removal of the former residents of that Helsingør complex is unrelated to their ethnicity. Its strongest reason, as stated, is that the policy is about neighbourhoods it calls parallel societies, not about the ethnic identity of the people who lived there.

What happens next is the supreme court’s ruling on whether that classification is covered by racial discrimination law. A finding that the classification is covered would bear on others already forced out under the same legislation; a finding that it is not would leave those evictions standing on the government’s stated ground.
