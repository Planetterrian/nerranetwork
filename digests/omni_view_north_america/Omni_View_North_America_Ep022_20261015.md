### The Closer Look
**Kenora residents applaud ‘fantastic’ move to permanent daylight time: Global News**
The Ontario government announced Tuesday that northwestern Ontario communities west of the 90th meridian would stop the twice-annual clock changes and stay on daylight time year-round. Kenora, Dryden, Sioux Lookout and Pickle Lake already observe Central daylight time, the same zone Manitoba uses. Come November 1, the time will match in Manitoba and all of Ontario. When clocks move forward an hour in March for much of Ontario, the northwest and Manitoba will not adjust.

Kenora–Rainy River MPP Greg Rickford said in a Tuesday news release that the change followed “unanimous consensus,” built through communities and First Nations across the northwest, with what he called detailed and nuanced considerations. Several communities surveyed residents. In Kenora, about 6,000 of approximately 16,000 residents answered. Mayor Andrew Poirier told Global News that 93 per cent supported keeping daylight time. He said council passed a resolution to align with Manitoba, as did neighbouring communities, and that Kenora is much more linked to Winnipeg than to the rest of Ontario. Travel between them, he said, is like the 401 for economic activity, tourism and visits. The same clock also covers medical appointments and tourist bookings.

Scott Green, owner and operator of Lake of the Woods Marina and Service Centre, said Wednesday he wants the match with Winnipeg and Manitoba to hold. His marina’s restaurant and bar hosts Winnipeg Jets watch parties. He said the permanent change makes the games time out better and makes a trip to Winnipeg for a game easier. Shared time, he said, will also make boat-taxi and tour bookings, and Zoom calls with people in Toronto, more efficient, because callers from Toronto have had to check the clock.

Northwestern Ontario is not the only pocket on a different clock from most of its province. The Kootenay region in British Columbia is on Mountain Time. Nunavik and the Lower North Shore in Quebec are on Atlantic Time, as are parts of Labrador, unlike the rest of Newfoundland and southeastern Labrador. In Golden, British Columbia, Councillor Wesley Routley said council last month had planned to shift to Mountain standard time permanently on November 1, then reversed course after hearing from hundreds of residents who wanted to stay aligned with the Regional District of East Kootenay to the south and with other communities on Mountain daylight time. A large share of Golden’s visitors come from Alberta, he said, so the alignment could limit confusion for people visiting. British Columbia, Alberta and Manitoba have each announced they will remain on daylight time starting November 1. Saskatchewan has observed central standard time year-round. The remaining provinces have said in recent months that no changes are expected, though discussions and analyses are ongoing. ### The Region and the World
**Carney, von der Leyen urged to draft ‘middle power’ charter at Canada-EU summit: The Globe and Mail**
A group of Canadian foreign-policy and trade experts is urging Prime Minister Mark Carney and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen to use their late October summit to launch a charter of shared principles for middle powers. Canada and the European Union are meeting in Montreal later this month to chart what the summit calls an “alliance for the future,” building closer ties as both try to reduce reliance on the United States under Donald Trump, whom The Globe and Mail describes in this context as protectionist and unpredictable. The Expert Group on Canada-U.S. Relations published the proposal Thursday. The charter would be open to other democracies and modelled on the 1941 Atlantic Charter between Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill, a statement that helped lay foundations for the United Nations and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization.

The Globe and Mail notes that Mr. Carney popularized middle-power co-operation in a January speech at the World Economic Forum in Davos, where he argued that the post-Cold War rules-based order has suffered “a rupture, not a transition,” and that middle powers like Canada must act together or risk being left on the menu. The Montreal summit is aimed at deepening trade ties and co-operation. The Expert Group argues Ottawa and Brussels should go beyond a transactional approach and draw up a shared set of political and economic purpose, what the paper calls a middle-power compact for today. Authors include former federal cabinet minister Perrin Beatty, onetime ambassador Jonathan Fried, former trade negotiator John Weekes, and former deputy chief of staff to the prime minister, Brian Clow. They argue that if Canada wants substantive relations with other middle powers, it has to lay out principles that would found that co-operation.

The 1941 statement had no formal status. It declared that Britain and the United States sought no territorial gains, opposed territorial changes made against the wishes of the peoples concerned, and supported every country’s right to choose its own government. The eight-point statement also pledged freer trade and economic co-operation, freedom of the seas, freedom from fear and want, and the disarmament of aggressor countries. It became a founding blueprint for the postwar order, the United Nations, the Bretton Woods economic system, and the alliance anchored by NATO. The Expert Group says that order has been severely compromised. It says the United States, a signatory to the Atlantic Charter, “no longer plays by the rules it itself developed and negotiated,” and that Washington has “weaponized the bonds of commerce forged over 80 years of interdependence to pursue economic advantage,” even though this causes economic harm globally.

The group says a new charter should reaffirm the Atlantic Charter and add five principles for a digital age, in which the defence of democracy depends on the integrity of information systems, data and digital infrastructure as much as on military strength. Canada and the European Union should commit, the group says, to ensuring technology does not undermine human dignity, human security and democratic self-government, “or for that matter,” threaten humanity’s very survival. The five principles, as the paper lays them out, start with defending democracy at home through free and fair elections, peaceful transfer of power, freedom of expression and a free press, and protection of minority rights. Second, every country must have the right to determine its future free from force or coercion, including a pledge of non-interference in other countries’ democratic processes and joint efforts against disinformation and anti-democratic movements. Third, middle powers should work together to reduce vulnerabilities in critical supply chains, financial services, energy, technology, infrastructure and defence industries, and pledge not to use trade as an instrument of coercion, while every country retains the sovereign right to choose its own economic or security partners. Fourth, they agree to combine strength through joint investment, production and shared capabilities in defence, critical technologies, energy, research and infrastructure, aiming at the scale needed to deter aggression. The proposal is a paper from the Expert Group, not a text Carney or von der Leyen have adopted. The dated step the Globe names is the late October summit in Montreal. ### Progress Watch
**How a contested B.C. supportive housing project won over its neighbours: The Globe and Mail**
In Maple Ridge, British Columbia, the 52-unit Rivers Reach supportive housing building has not produced the drug use, fires and noise that neighbours feared when it replaced a temporary modular site. Julia Clissold and her husband had just settled into a new condo in the summer of 2022 when they learned the residential development next door would be supportive housing. She recalled police “constantly” at the old modular site, and people who loitered and camped there. She told The Globe and Mail she and her husband would not walk their dogs down there or drive by, because people were on the road “in various states of what have you,” and that they were very scared. She is president of the building’s strata council. Others in the new-construction condominium, on a quiet residential street, were similarly apprehensive. Some felt deceived by realtors and developers who sold homes along with the idea that a complex similar to theirs was planned next door.

A year and a half later, Ms. Clissold has become a champion of Rivers Reach. She says the fears never materialized, and she attributes that to careful planning, attentive management and the operator’s responsiveness to community feedback. The Globe and Mail is reporting from cities and towns in Ontario, Manitoba and British Columbia ahead of municipal votes, and it says disputes over homeless encampments and supportive housing have dragged on for months or years across British Columbia, in big cities, sleepy suburbs and northern communities. This year alone, proposed supportive housing in Abbotsford, Burnaby and the District of North Vancouver has been cancelled amid backlash. The issue is a flashpoint in municipal debates before Saturday’s general vote. In Richmond, fierce opposition has put mayoral candidates under pressure, and some have explicitly declared their opposition. In Surrey, one candidate has pledged to build 1,000 new units annually for four years and has blamed the past council’s resistance for worsening street homelessness. Candidates in Vancouver have been grilled on whether they would reverse the incumbent administration’s freeze on net-new supportive housing.

In an interview with The Globe and Mail last week, NDP Leader David Eby said ahead of the October 24 provincial vote that his government is done with overriding municipalities that oppose supportive housing. He said that if a local government is happier with an encampment than with the hard conversation about the housing needed, he struggles to know why he would not dedicate that money to a local government that wants to close the encampment and open housing. Efforts in Maple Ridge stretch back more than a decade. Former mayor Nicole Read, who advocated for unhoused people and supported low-barrier options from 2014 to 2018, faced online harassment so heated she curtailed public appearances while the RCMP investigated threats to her safety. In May 2017, people began erecting tents at a vacant downtown site slated for a neighbourhood park. The encampment, called Anita Place, lasted more than two years. At its peak, nearly 200 people sheltered there. Firefighters said portable heaters, propane tanks and electrical connections sparked numerous fires. Police said they received dozens of calls about weapons and stolen items. Against that record, Rivers Reach is the completed 52-unit building whose neighbours now say the worst fears did not arrive.
