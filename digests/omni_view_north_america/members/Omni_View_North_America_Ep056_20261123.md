### Lead
**Daily cannabis use more common among Canadian adults than daily alcohol consumption, study finds: The Globe and Mail**
A study in the Canadian Medical Association Journal, released Monday, finds daily cannabis use is more common than daily alcohol use among Canadian adults, and equals or exceeds daily cigarette use among people younger than 35. Researchers used the 2023-2024 Canadian Community Health Survey, covering nearly 100,000 people age 18 and older. Among men aged 25 to 34, daily cannabis use was 10.8 percent, against 2.9 percent for daily alcohol and 8 percent for cigarettes; among women in that age group the figures were 6.8 percent, 1.2 percent and 4.5 percent. Among women 18 to 24, daily cannabis use was 6.6 percent, daily alcohol 0.4 percent and daily cigarettes 2 percent, with similar patterns among men in that age group. Lead author André McDonald, a psychiatric epidemiologist and Canadian Institutes of Health Research postdoctoral fellow at McMaster University’s Peter Boris Centre for Addictions Research, said the results suggest a generational shift with long-term implications for population health. Senior author Daniel Myran, the Cheesbrough research chair at North York General Hospital, said many daily cannabis users do not drink or smoke cigarettes every day; three in five reported no concurrent daily cigarette use, nine in ten reported no concurrent daily alcohol use, and 68 percent of daily users smoke cannabis. The work, funded through a Terry Fox Research Institute award to Dr. Myran, comes eight years after Canada in October 2018 became the first major industrialized nation to legalize non-medical cannabis; the authors cite risks including psychosis and schizophrenia and say further research is needed because a significant share of daily users started after legalization. ### The Closer Look
**High levels of toxic forever chemicals found near Newfoundland site, study finds: The Globe and Mail**
More than two decades after firefighters last sprayed flame-dousing foam at a Newfoundland practice facility, a Memorial University paper published earlier this month in Environmental Science & Technology finds high concentrations of toxic forever chemicals still turning up several kilometres away. The work appears to bolster residents living downstream of St. John’s International Airport who are suing the federal government, alleging the chemicals have fouled their drinking water. Ed Sheerr, a lead plaintiff in a proposed class action for roughly 700 households, said the contamination researchers found was significantly more widespread than the plaintiffs had recognized. The lawsuit argues that per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances from the airport training facility have contaminated local well water, imperiling health and cratering property values, and it asks for immediate remediation and compensation.

Memorial researchers began sampling in December 2024 after learning Transport Canada was not testing private wells outside a designated subdivision near the airport where PFAS had been found. Karl Jobst, a Memorial chemistry professor, said the team, led by PhD student Emmanuel Tolefe, wanted to understand the spread and offer free testing. The highest readings were in foams that form on surface waters near the airport, reaching 2,000,000 nanograms per litre, against a Health Canada drinking-water guideline of 30 nanograms per litre. Water samples near the airport ranged from 318 to 2,302 nanograms per litre. Levels fell with distance but stayed above the drinking-water standard more than four kilometres away. Researchers detected the chemicals in 32 of 42 private wells sampled, and in seven of those wells the levels exceeded the guideline. PFOS, a major component of foams used at the airport between 1982 and 2004, made up 62 percent of the PFAS detected near the defunct practice facility. The paper describes PFAS as a family of more than 6,000 synthetic chemicals, linked in recent years to kidney and testicular cancer, metabolic disruption, immune suppression, pregnancy complications and other harms.

Transport Canada has acknowledged using PFAS-laden foams. In its statement of defence it denies that any PFAS migrated from the airport to drinking-water wells, and spokesperson Sau Sau Liu said the department will not comment on the paper because of the proposed class action. The agency is delivering bottled water to Mr. Sheerr and other residents whose water exceeds the Health Canada threshold. A Globe and Mail analysis last year found eight communities across six provinces in a similar position, relying on federal bottled water, and Ottawa has identified 86 federal properties where PFAS has fouled groundwater. Mr. Sheerr said he hopes the study will push the government to work with residents rather than deny responsibility. ### The Region and the World
**Carney’s South East Asian deals only a first step to expanding trade: The Globe and Mail**
During a visit to the Philippines, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that Canada had wrapped up bilateral trade negotiations with the Philippines and reached the substantial conclusion of talks with the 11-member Association of Southeast Asian Nations. The fine print is still being worked out, and the agreements are expected to be ratified next year. Goods exports to the 11 ASEAN countries doubled over the past decade to 11.6 billion dollars in 2025, while imports nearly tripled to 41 billion dollars — 1.5 percent of Canadian merchandise exports and 5.2 percent of imports. Mr. Carney said the Philippines deal, which would give preferential tariff treatment to 97 percent of Canadian products, could triple exports over the next decade from 1 billion dollars in 2025, led by pork, wheat and wood products; a 2017 Global Affairs Canada feasibility report estimated an ASEAN agreement could lift Canadian exports by 2.67 billion dollars a year. Jeff Nankivell, president and chief executive of the Asia Pacific Foundation of Canada, said Canada is playing catch-up and must keep up the engagement, while Wayne Farmer, president of the Canada-ASEAN Business Council, said President Donald Trump’s pressure on Malaysia, Cambodia and Indonesia is making Canada look more appealing as a partner that does not weaponize trade. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/business/article-carneys-south-east-asian-deals-only-a-first-step-to-expanding-trade/)

### Both Sides: Senate passage of Bill C-22
The Senate is weighing the Liberal government’s proposed national security bill, C-22, which would change how companies retain and hand over user data to Canadian police and intelligence officers. CBC reports the bill is nearing its last legislative hurdle.

Law enforcement agencies are appealing to senators to pass the bill as soon as possible. The case they are putting, as CBC describes it, is that police and intelligence officers need the new rules on how companies keep and provide user data, and that the Senate should not hold that change at the final stage.

Big tech is making what CBC calls a final salvo to scale the same legislation back. The case on that side, as far as today’s reporting states it, is that a contentious bill changing retention and handover of user data should be narrowed before it clears the Senate.

The decision in front of senators is whether to pass the Liberal text or cut it back. Source: [cbc.ca](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/bill-c-22-lawful-access-privacy-senate-9.7430455?cmp=rss)
