Ottawa is now hearing a sharper warning from the Pentagon, CBC reported. Further measures, still unnamed, are on the table unless Canada puts continental defence first in its military buildup. Two cancer patients have taken Ontario to court, CBC reported, after the province refused to pay for treatment outside the country. What is under appeal is a refusal to fund, not a decision to send them.

The Progressive Conservative government said in early October it was stopping those payments at every private postsecondary school, and for their students, while an outside auditor looked at suspicious activity in some private, for-profit career colleges. A preliminary audit over the summer had already found activity that looked suspicious and possibly fraudulent.

At some of those colleges, enrolment in mostly online courses paid for with student aid had shot up. Some of what investigators found was serious enough to go to the Ontario Provincial Police. Nolan Quinn, the minister responsible for colleges and universities, has not said why schools like Tyndale were pulled into a freeze aimed at career colleges.

His spokeswoman, Bianca Giacoboni, told The Globe and Mail that the first inquiries focused on a few schools, that the ministry has since seen signs of wider problems, and that auditors have not assured the government the trouble stops there.

She said the blanket pause is meant to protect taxpayer money and to hold bad actors to account, and that officials are trying to put students and schools that are acting properly back on aid while they set rules for third-party loans.

Seven private Christian schools, divinity schools and seminaries among them, have joined to ask the government to let them out. Their case is that they already meet a high bar to award undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral degrees, and that they do not run for profit. They are private because the public treasury does not subsidize them the way it subsidizes public universities and colleges.

Ambrose Thomson, who runs student services at McMaster Divinity School in Hamilton, said the school is a not-for-profit and that the fraud risks under investigation are simply not the same. Last week the government tabled changes meant to protect student aid, including a cap on how fast career colleges can grow enrolment and an end to aid for mostly online programs in that sector.

Quinn has said he hopes the freeze lifts around the start of the new year.
