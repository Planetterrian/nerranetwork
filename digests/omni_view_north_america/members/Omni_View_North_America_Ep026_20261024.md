### Lead
**Trump Officials Seek Block on Alaska Senate Race Results if the Republican Dan Sullivan Loses: The New York Times**
The Justice Department has gone to court over one Senate seat in Alaska, and the remedy it seeks is aimed at the count itself. In what The New York Times calls a highly unusual lawsuit, the department asked to block the state's Senate result if Republican Dan Sullivan loses. The department's stated ground is confusion: candidates with similar names, it argued, could taint the election result. The Times reports that President Trump told the Justice Department to intervene in the race. In a companion account, the Times says polling shows Republicans at risk of losing the seat, and that Trump directed the department in. A separate Times report says legal experts see the challenge as dubious. No obvious legal authority, that report says, supports a pre-emptive effort to block certification of Alaska's Senate election if the Republican incumbent narrowly loses. The challenge is framed around a narrow loss, not a completed recount the articles describe as already finished. PBS put the same threat on its broadcast, with Jonathan Capehart and Ramesh Ponnuru discussing Trump's threat to block the Alaska election results. ### The Closer Look
**Tighter immigration rules put nursing homes at risk of losing foreign workers, long-term care providers say: The Globe and Mail**
Nursing homes in several provinces say tighter federal immigration rules now put them at risk of losing significant numbers of temporary foreign workers, The Globe and Mail reports. The homes look after seniors who have cognitive problems or dementia and who need help dressing, bathing, or feeding. Local hiring often falls short, so the homes recruit abroad to fill the gap on those shifts.

Lisa Levin, chief executive of AdvantAge Ontario, which represents the province's municipal and non-profit long-term care homes, said changes Ottawa has made to immigration policy have made a difficult staffing situation in health care worse. Mary Polak, chief executive of the BC Care Providers Association, called the result an unintended consequence. She said the government may not have been aware of the whole scale of the impacts.

The Globe and Mail reports that Ottawa has moved since 2024 to curb the number of international students and temporary foreign workers, after criticism that their influx created housing problems and made it harder for Canadians to find jobs. Levin singled out two measures that hit long-term care. The first is the Labour Market Impact Assessment, the document an employer completes to confirm that no Canadian is available for the job. That assessment is what lets foreign personnel apply for a work permit. Each application carries a $1,000 processing fee. The assessments are now valid for six months, down from a year. The second measure is the work permit itself, shortened from two years to one.

Gavin McIntosh, operations and finance vice-president for The Care Group, which runs nine facilities in British Columbia, said homes are paying overtime to cover the shortages. "It's not sustainable," he said. Levin said other homes have turned to temporary staffing agencies. She said that route is costly, and that it is also detrimental, because vulnerable residents need stable care personnel. "They're working with people who have cognitive issues, and then you have to bring someone else in, you have to train them all over again on what the needs are of the residents," she said. Each resident has a care plan and needs of their own, and in many cases cannot communicate those needs.

The federal government answered that the Temporary Foreign Worker Program should be only a last-resort solution for short-term problems. Samuelle Carbonneau, a spokesperson for Employment and Social Development Canada, said in an email: "It is not a substitute for Canadian talent and is conditional on ensuring that measures have been taken to recruit Canadian workers." She said assessments are not based on job titles. They are assessed individually. She noted that other pathways exist for hiring foreign workers.

One of those pathways is in Quebec. Employers there have a priority process called traitement simplifié that extends to foreign applicants for personal support workers, who provide basic care in nursing homes. Jean-François Blanchard, executive director of two long-term care homes west of Montreal, said some of his foreign candidates have been stumped because that Quebec priority stream does not recognize training they acquired in private establishments. Blanchard, who is also president of a group representing Quebec private nursing homes, said his members could potentially lose 650 of their 6,000 workers because of immigration issues.

Carbonneau pointed to another option: a temporary extension that lets rural employers retain their number of foreign workers until next March. The measure still requires new Labour Market Impact Assessments. Ontario and Alberta are not participating. Ashley Hoffmeister, human resources director for the District of Kenora Homes for the Aged, said 28 of the 400 employees in her three facilities in northwest Ontario are facing expiring papers. She tried another route, the Significant Benefit to Canada permit, commonly known as the LMIA exemption C10. That permit costs $230. The employer has to explain how the foreign workers will make a contribution so significant that they can be exempted from the assessment. Hoffmeister said some C10 applications were turned down because bureaucrats did not recognize the foreign workers as health-care personnel. One rejection letter she shared said the personal support workers "appear to be highly/mainly specific to individual's personal care and not health care."
