### Lead
**Democrats Gain Hundreds of State Legislative Seats: The Guardian**
Democrats gained 258 state legislative seats in Tuesday’s midterm elections, according to Chaz Nuttycombe, executive director of State Navigate, creating six new governing trifectas, taking 11 legislative chambers from Republicans and breaking Republican supermajorities in 10 states. The Guardian reports that ballots are still being counted, and that the party has not held full control of government in Arizona in 60 years, in Pennsylvania since the 1990s, or in Wisconsin since 2010, while Michigan and Minnesota regained trifectas lost after the 2024 election. In Texas, Democrats will control the state house for the first time since 2002. The New York Times reports they won control of state governments in Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, Pennsylvania and Nevada, and won chambers in Texas, Georgia and North Carolina. They also won gubernatorial races over Republicans in Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas, a majority of governorships for the first time since 2010, the Guardian reports. Mandara Meyers, executive director of the States Project, said it was the most significant night for state legislative power since the Republican wave of 2010, and that Democrats now have more power in the states than at any other point in the 21st century. Nuttycombe predicts about 76 percent of the population will live under a Democratic governor and about half will be represented by a Democratic lawmaker in their legislature, and he said roughly 80 to 85 percent of the wave is the economy and dissatisfaction with the president. The Guardian says Democrats are on course for a Senate majority of at least 54 seats and 237 House seats. Andy Beshear of Kentucky and Gretchen Whitmer of Michigan, leaders of the Democratic Governors Association, said more Democratic governors mean better lives for the millions of Americans who live in their states. ### Across the Region
**Mississippi Senate Race Leads by 1,138 Votes, and the State Has No Recount Law: The Guardian**
Almost 24 hours after Democrats secured control of Congress, two Senate seats were still in play, with counting in Alaska expected to continue into Thursday. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by 1,138 votes with almost 96 percent counted, the Guardian reports, and new batches had not been reported for several hours even as Colom’s votes had been outpacing hers. Mississippi has no law for initiating a recount; The New York Times reports the state does not allow vote recounts in statewide elections. Democrats have not won a U.S. Senate seat in Mississippi since 1982, Donald Trump carried the state by 22 points in 2024, and the state has not elected a Black person to that office since 1875. Colom, a seventh-generation Mississippian whose father was a Republican, became the first Black district attorney for the 16th circuit in 2015 and, as district attorney, had a 94 percent success rate for convictions. ### The Closer Look
**What to watch for as Carney introduces his 2nd federal budget: CBC**
Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government tables its second federal budget on Thursday, since taking office in March 2025. The CBC says the budget is the next step in a plan to support the country through an affordability crunch and the trade war. A Globe and Mail headline in the same day’s coverage says Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne has said the budget will focus on affordability and cultural sovereignty.

Jake Fuss, director of fiscal studies at the Fraser Institute, argues in The Globe and Mail that Canadians should expect more new spending and large deficits. He writes that Carney promised on the campaign trail a “very different approach” to federal finances from Justin Trudeau, and later said he would be “spending less,” then increased spending significantly. Fuss says the government posted a $66.9-billion deficit for 2025-26, a projected deficit of $65.3-billion in 2026-27, and deficits of at least $53-billion for each of the subsequent four years. Those cumulative deficits from 2025-26 to 2029-30, he writes, are double what Trudeau planned before leaving office. Ottawa has not balanced a budget in nearly 20 years, he notes, after the Trudeau government borrowed hundreds of billions and pushed inflation-adjusted per-person spending and debt to historically high levels.

To cover the deficit, Ottawa must borrow. Fuss says total federal debt will eclipse a projected $2.9-trillion by 2029-30 — almost $300-billion more than Trudeau planned — and that debt interest costs will hit a projected $75.7-billion, more than the combined total Ottawa plans to spend that year on employment insurance and the Canada Child Benefit. He argues the burden lands disproportionately on younger Canadians, who are likely to pay higher taxes, enjoy fewer services, or both. He also argues that when the government borrows, it competes with individuals, families and businesses, and that lenders raise interest rates, making mortgages, cars and renovations more expensive. He says today’s higher interest rates exist in part because of federal and provincial spending and borrowing.

Last year, Fuss writes, the Carney government separated “operating spending” — day-to-day programs such as Old Age Security and employment insurance — from “capital spending,” defined as “anything that builds an asset,” and said last month it would balance the operating budget by 2028-29. He says both categories affect the bottom line, and that the split obscures the true state of federal finances. The Parliamentary Budget Officer has criticized how the government classifies capital spending, he writes, because the government has an incentive to shift items from operating to capital. A separate PBO report last month said the government is not on track to balance the operating budget by 2028-29 regardless of that classification. Fuss’s conclusion is that, absent substantial spending reductions or huge jumps in revenue, the overall deficit remains larger than the Trudeau government’s projections, and that younger Canadians inherit the bill.
