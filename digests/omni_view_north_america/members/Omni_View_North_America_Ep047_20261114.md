### Lead
**What to Know About Friday’s Nationwide Ground Stop: The New York Times**
The Federal Aviation Administration issued a rare nationwide ground stop Friday afternoon after outdated air-traffic communications equipment became overloaded with data and began to fail, The New York Times reported, with NPR also covering the halt. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said at the White House that night that radar and other communications started to falter at 3:36 p.m. when old routers overloaded, pervasive enough to stop takeoffs even though the failures were not universal. The Times said the outage stranded thousands and left airlines and airports still recovering; NPR said millions of passengers were stranded and that no planes were taking off at any airport. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was zero evidence of a cyberattack and suggested new equipment and new technology Duffy’s department had added to the old infrastructure had overwhelmed it, while Duffy insisted the cause was antiquated legacy communications equipment, not recent upgrades. In a separate account Mullin called the stop a telecommunication outage and said he did not know the cause; speaking at the White House briefing NPR attended, he said there was not a threat to the United States. Duffy said that during the intermittent outage controllers could still reach pilots. Radio transmissions had a Newark Liberty controller sharing the stop with pilots, then Atlanta and Chicago controllers saying they had limited information, then talk of significant gate trouble and a tarmac compared to a parking lot; one pilot told a Newark controller it was not lost on him that it was Friday the 13th. Administration officials demanded that lawmakers approve tens of billions of dollars more to accelerate systemwide upgrades. The last nationwide ground stop, in 2023, came after a pilot warning system went down and lasted around 90 minutes. Travelers whose flights were canceled are entitled to refunds regardless of the reason, though airlines will not guarantee a hotel room or meal vouchers. Some professional and college sports teams were stuck on runways or turning to buses ahead of weekend games now uncertain, and airports filled with stuck families and stranded travelers; passengers on tarmacs told NPR they were stuck without information. In Denver, where Democrats had gathered for a conference after midterm wins, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg stood at a hotel bar as CNN carried the administration news conference and told a Times reporter he had questions, offering comic-book swearing characters in place of a printable reaction. At a table sat Governors Abigail Spanberger of Virginia and Mikie Sherrill of New Jersey and Senator Elissa Slotkin of Michigan, eating Parmesan fries and checking phones; Senator Raphael Warnock of Georgia scanned Senate group chats for updates from secure channels and from Senator Mark Warner of Virginia. A New England operative booked a rental car for Boston; others looked up Amtrak and found the East Coast more than 40 hours away; aides to a Democratic governor who had arrived on a private jet fielded hitch-a-ride questions. Anne Caprara, chief of staff to Illinois Governor JB Pritzker, who was not in Denver, worked two phones and said Pritzker was furious about the lack of information from the Trump administration, calling the bar Denver’s hottest and likening it to the Mos Eisley Cantina, with a Veep cast and a Terminator 2 mood. ### Across the Region
**Canada making progress in USMCA talks to ensure long-term viability of auto sector, Carney says: The Globe and Mail**
Speaking to reporters in Manila, where he announced Canada had wrapped up talks on a trade agreement with the Philippines, Prime Minister Mark Carney said Canada is making headway with the United States on terms that would leave a path to profitability for Canadian auto production, The Globe and Mail reported, and UA.NEWS carried his statement that the Philippines talks had concluded. He called talks over autos one of the most challenging issues in the negotiations with Washington, said he will not accept a US deal that would kill the Canadian auto sector, and said this government will not settle for a deal that is not consistent with a viable Canadian auto sector. He said he had a long, substantive conversation with President Donald Trump just before leaving on a trip that has taken him from Dublin to Asia for three summits, with White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer among those on the call. He said he sees a one-on-one deal with the United States to scale back the US tariffs that currently cover steel, aluminum and autos, and that there is the possibility of a bilateral deal but Canada is not yet there. He said the sustainability of the auto industry, premised on continued market access to the United States, is non-negotiable, and that Canada is making gains on auto terms in part because longer-term competitiveness of the US auto sector depends on factors that include the degree of integration with the Canadian sector. He said the North American industry as a whole faces significant competitive challenges because of the speed of change and the shift to electric vehicles, and that this will require big investment. A 25 percent US tariff on Canadian-made autos, minus a carve-out for US parts in the vehicles, has put severe pressure on an industry that typically exports nearly 90 percent of production to the United States; the value of Canadian passenger car and light truck exports to the US tumbled nearly 12 percent from 2024 to 2025, and exports are tracking even lower in 2026. In August, Washington offered to lower the headline tariff rate to 15 percent as part of a broader trade deal that would have reduced Section 232 duties on several key Canadian industries in return for concessions from Ottawa; the US parts carve-out would have been maintained, but the relief would not have been extended to medium- and heavy-duty trucks. Many in the Canadian auto industry balked at that proposal and applauded Carney’s decision to walk away, a choice the Globe said has left an unsustainable status quo. Stellantis is trying to sell its idled Brampton, Ontario, plant to armoured-vehicle manufacturer Roshel. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Friday that Ottawa, the Ontario government and the union representing workers at the plant have put an aggressive package in front of Stellantis and remain in solution mode to prevent the sale; Carney declined to offer details of the package and said the details and implications will come out in due course. **Trump Administration Delays Enforcement of Medicaid Work Requirement: The New York Times**
The federal government had planned to require poor adults to prove they were working or lose insurance coverage starting January 1. It will now let states wait until July. The change pushes back the date those adults can lose coverage for failing to document work, from the first day of the year to midsummer, and it is a delay in enforcement rather than a repeal of the requirement itself. **Alaska Senate Race Between Dan Sullivan and Mary Peltola Still Counting Votes: The New York Times**
In the only Senate race still uncalled, Democrat Mary Peltola widened her lead over Republican Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska’s latest tally, The New York Times reported Friday night. She had 49.8 percent of the vote and led Sullivan by nearly four percentage points in incomplete results as of Friday night. If neither candidate wins an outright majority, the race will be decided next week by ranked-choice tabulation. A New York Times analysis said she is on track to eventually prevail; Sullivan spokesman Nate Adams said Friday night, after the latest release of results, that the campaign is still waiting for every ballot to be counted and will see what happens from there. The delay is in part because of the time Alaska allows for mail ballots to be received and counted and in part because of the state’s ranked-choice system. Sullivan, a two-term incumbent, and Peltola, a former congresswoman, faced two other candidates after emerging from a nonpartisan primary in August. The contest sits inside a midterm cycle the Times described as featuring sweeping Democratic victories, and it remains the only Senate battle still uncalled.
