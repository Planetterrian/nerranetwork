### Lead
**Saskatchewan, Newfoundland push Ottawa for greater control over immigration: The Globe and Mail**
Saskatchewan and Newfoundland are asking Ottawa to hand them more immigration powers, while Alberta renews its demand for greater autonomy over which foreign nationals become permanent residents. The Globe and Mail reports that those demands drew fresh attention after a majority in Alberta voted on Monday for more control over who may settle there. That vote was part of a referendum in which Albertans rejected separating from the rest of Canada and endorsed several measures aimed at curbing inflows of newcomers. Critics warn the shift could produce a fragmented, patchwork system. Federal Immigration Minister Lena Metlege Diab is preparing to announce within weeks new federal targets for permanent residents, including how many newcomers each province will be able to nominate. Under the Provincial Nominee Program, a province nominates and the federal government ultimately approves who is accepted. The provincial streams are a favoured route for former international students seeking permanent residence. Quebec and Nunavut are the only jurisdictions that do not use the program. In last year’s immigration levels plan, Ottawa set provincial nominee spots at 91,500 people, rising to 92,500 next year. Premiers have been pressing Ms. Metlege Diab to raise the number of permanent-residence spaces available for those nominations. Provinces argue they are best placed to decide which immigrants should settle in order to fill local labour gaps. Saskatchewan’s minister of government relations, Eric Schmalz, said the province will keep pushing for greater control, with the focus on immigrants who can fill job gaps so industries can keep providing jobs for Saskatchewan people and generating revenue for public services. He said that push complements, and does not replace, training more people at home, which he called the first pillar of Saskatchewan’s Labour Market Strategy. Newfoundland and Labrador, through spokesperson Allison King, said it wants both more control and a greater number of nominations, and that it supports increased economic-immigration allocations that reflect each jurisdiction’s labour-market needs. Ms. King said recent reductions under the Immigration Levels Plan constrain the province’s ability to meet employer demand, support rural communities and sustain population growth. She noted immigration is a shared constitutional responsibility, said provincially driven programs have succeeded in attracting and retaining newcomers, growing the population and filling job gaps, and said Newfoundland, as provincial-territorial co-chair of the Forum of Ministers Responsible for Immigration, is working with other governments toward a collaborative approach that recognizes national priorities while giving provinces and territories more flexibility. Prince Edward Island spokesperson April Gallant said the province also supports a greater provincial and territorial role, because provinces deliver health care, housing, schools and roads, understand labour-market needs and the pressure on public services, and should have a meaningful role in shaping levels and programs so allocations reflect workforce demands, economic priorities and population goals. Premiers meet Monday in Ottawa, where greater control over immigration is expected to be raised. At a Wednesday news conference with Prime Minister Mark Carney, Ontario Premier Doug Ford said economic immigration is needed to keep economies growing across the board, and that Ontario looks forward to working with the federal government on that. ### Both Sides: Whether Canadian outreach counts as US election interference
The question is whether Canadian political contacts inside the United States count as interference in the midterms or as ordinary diplomacy. American voters are preparing to cast ballots in tight midterm elections that, The Globe and Mail reports, will turn on a small number of closely fought races.

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s case, made at a news conference in Istanbul on Friday, is that allegations from Donald Trump’s administration have no merit. He said he is disappointed that US ambassador Pete Hoekstra treats making those suggestions as his role, and he called the charges unsubstantiated claims. Canada, he said, keeps a very deep diplomatic presence in the United States, and its hundreds or thousands of interactions are normal efforts by a foreign government to build relations. He said Ottawa has lots of contact with American companies, citizens, individuals and stakeholders, is very careful not to be seen to interfere, and has no intention of interfering with US democratic processes. He said he would not point fingers or get into tit-for-tat, but that there are lots of concerns about American interference in Canadian political processes, and that the media can judge how far very senior US officials have directly made suggestions to intervene in Canadian sovereignty and democratic processes, encourage certain outcomes and encourage certain people. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly is rejecting the accusation. She says she would never interfere, because she would expect the Americans not to interfere in Canada’s own elections. Anadolu Ajansı reports that Mr. Carney, while rejecting the interference claims, also said Canada will never be the 51st state.

The US embassy in Canada this week circulated what it called a fact sheet accusing Ottawa of trying to sway US politics. The statement alleges that the Canadian government brushes off concerns about potential interference even as its ministers and premiers openly describe plans to influence American politics in official documents and public appearances. Mr. Hoekstra, in an interview on the Canadian podcast The Really Big Show, said the United States is closely watching Canadian activities, referenced Industry Minister Mélanie Joly among other politicians, and said some of this, from the US view of its elections, could be inferred to be election interference. He cited Ms. Joly talking about targeting retaliatory tariffs in politically sensitive states, and an Ontario government television advertisement that ran in the United States last year criticizing US tariffs on Canadian goods. He said foreign-policy leaders coming into the United States are appreciated, but that they should keep their nose out of American politics, and that Washington is monitoring and evaluating and will respond if it judges the conduct to be election interference. Those remarks followed accusations last month by Trump adviser Peter Navarro, who told Canadian lobbyists to get the hell out of the US and warned Canada against interfering in the midterms. Canada has since warned its diplomats to avoid US candidates, Bloomberg reports, and The Globe and Mail reports diplomats were told not to meet with US midterm candidates.
