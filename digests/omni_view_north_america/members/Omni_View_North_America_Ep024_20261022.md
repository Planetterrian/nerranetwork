### The Closer Look
**Scientists report ‘substantial’ declines in loons across Canada: The Globe and Mail**

A study published Thursday in the journal FACETS finds substantial long-term declines in loon productivity in all but one of 12 breeding regions. Matthew Fuirst, a research ecologist with Birds Canada who led the work, said loons are not doing as well as researchers would like. More than 20 researchers in Canada and the United States joined a three-year effort that pulled together decades of data.

The steepest declines were in Canada's Atlantic and Prairie provinces, Dr. Fuirst said. Loons are not currently endangered. An estimated 254,000 breeding pairs live in Canada, about 94 percent of the species' total population. The team used more than 45,000 breeding records from 3,421 lakes across North America. About half the data came from volunteers in a long-running Birds Canada loon survey, with some observations reaching back to the 1980s.

Productivity was defined as the number of chicks per adult pair that survive beyond six weeks after hatching. Declines ranged from 0.5 to 2.5 percent a year, over two to four decades depending on the data. Northern Maine was the exception, where productivity has been relatively stable. The northern half of the breeding range is a blind area: fewer people live there, and data have been hard to obtain.

Walter Piper, a co-author and a biology professor at Chapman University in Orange, California, said it was not likely that northern data would show an increase offsetting the south, or that loons were moving farther north. He said the study also indicated a decline in chick quality that could signal poorer nourishment. "We are probably detecting the leading edge of a broad decline in the loon population that is likely to continue in coming decades," Dr. Piper said. Loons are long-lived, he said, so the decline will take a while to become obvious, and causes should be sought now.

One possible cause the researchers flag is increased precipitation linked to climate change. Unlike ducks in some of the same habitat, loons are built to catch fish in relatively clear water, and their bones are denser than those of many other species so they can stay submerged. More flooding and churning of lake water could lower visibility and impede hunting. James Paruk, a loon biologist at St. Joseph's College in Standish, Maine, who was not part of the study, said Maine's stability may reflect relatively low human and shoreline development in the area the study covered. He said the time span and geographic scale make the implications far-reaching. Dr. Fuirst said he has already started a follow-up study on the causes. For many Canadians the bird is tied to wilderness and national identity, including its place on the one-dollar coin. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/science/article-scientists-report-substantial-declines-loons-across-canada-atlantic/)
