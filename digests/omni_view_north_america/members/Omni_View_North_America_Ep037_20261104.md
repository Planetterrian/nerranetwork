> **Democrats won both the House and the Senate, ending Republican control of Congress.**

**What You Need to Know:** Democrats won both the House and the Senate, ending Republican control of Congress and, as The New York Times reported, the party's hold on the House, the Senate and the presidency together. President Trump attributed the losses to his name not being on the ballot; The New York Times reported that others in his party dismissed that explanation and blamed him. The same night reset governor's offices from Texas outward, put a Canadian submarine purchase back in play, and put Hakeem Jeffries in line to lead the House.

### Lead
**Democrats Seize Control of Congress, Ending a G.O.P. Trifecta: The New York Times**
The New York Times reported that the Democratic Party wrested control of Congress from Republicans, and that Republicans lost both the House and the Senate, in what the paper called a wave of midterm victories and a stinging rebuke to President Trump's agenda. The Times reported that the House result ended a Republican trifecta and cemented divided government and a freshly assertive Congress after two years in which, the paper wrote, Republicans ceded power to President Trump. In a separate account, the Times reported that the backlash extended even into deep red states and, in the paper's reading, indicated that President Trump has lost his hold over the coalition that lifted him to the White House twice. The Times also reported a string of Senate victories in Iowa, Texas, and North and South Carolina, and wrote that Democrats flipped the Senate and the House with ease, leaving the president weakened. President Trump attributed the sweeping losses to the fact that he was not on the ballot. The Times reported that others in his party dismissed that explanation and pinned the blame squarely on him. He told the Times, "There was a mood in the air, I guess." On Truth Social, the BBC reported, he wrote that Republicans would have won "in a landslide" if his name had been on the ballot. The BBC's account was headlined that he takes aim at Republicans but says Democrats did a "good job." ### The Closer Look
**Public menopause care is scarce, and private care is costly. Why Canadians pay either way: The Globe and Mail**
Angela Rimmer has spent more than five thousand dollars out of pocket on menopause care that Ontario does not cover. She is forty-two, a single mother of four in the London, Ontario, area, and a full-time dental assistant. She told The Globe and Mail that brain fog, difficulty sleeping, night sweats, hot flashes, mood changes and exhaustion were debilitating, and that she needed treatment to keep working and to keep caregiving at home. A family doctor initially misdiagnosed her and wanted to prescribe antidepressants. She turned to two different private clinics for menopause hormone therapy. At one clinic she paid six hundred dollars for an initial assessment, two hundred dollars for each six-month follow-up, seventy dollars for lab tests, and about one hundred and twenty dollars a month in prescriptions. She has no private health insurance, and the province does not cover the treatments. She said, "I just wish that I didn't have to access this through private. I wish that it was in the public system, because we're having to pay so much." She said access, including the hormone therapy that has helped her, should be available without reaching so far into pocketbooks at the time when women should be saving for retirement and for their children's schooling. Private care, she said, has been costly and time-consuming to find.

The Globe and Mail reported that more than five million women in Canada are between forty and sixty, and that the majority will experience menopause symptoms, which can run from mild to overwhelming. Symptoms the paper listed range from fatigue to headaches, anxiety, muscle aches and heart palpitations. An Ipsos survey this year for GreenShield found that four in ten Canadian women do not know where to go for treatment, and that a third of respondents waited more than two years to get effective menopause care. The paper listed the barriers as a shortage of family physicians, doctors who lack training in menopause, long waits to see specialists, a dearth of research funding, and an explosion of misleading information online. Desperate for treatment, some women turn to private or virtual clinics. Some are run by physicians or nurse practitioners; others are operated by naturopaths or tech entrepreneurs. Women's health experts told the paper that the standard of care varies, and so do the prices, that many remedies are costly, and that not all are effective or safe. The paper reported that effective treatments exist that alleviate symptoms, and that most cases are not so complex that a family doctor cannot handle them. Currently, just two provinces, British Columbia and Manitoba, cover the costs of menopausal hormone therapy. Newer nonhormonal medications, Veozah and Lynkuet, which alleviate hot flashes and night sweats, are approved by Health Canada but are not fully covered by the public system. The Globe and Mail reported that Canada is behind Britain and Australia in taking steps to improve access. In June, the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada warned that a two-tiered health system is emerging in which women are forced to pay for basic health care that should be free.

The Menopause Foundation of Canada says access should be universally available to all Canadians. It started a national campaign last month urging governments to bolster support for women experiencing menopause. The foundation says the widest gaps are for Indigenous, Black and racialized women, people living in rural communities, gender-diverse people and those with lower incomes. It said, "Access to safe, evidence-based menopause care should never depend on a person's ability to pay." Gynecologist and author Jen Gunter, of The Menopause Manifesto, said that whether someone lives in Flin Flon, Manitoba, or in downtown Toronto, they should be able to get excellent first-line menopause care, just as people should have access to care for blood pressure, and that these are things that should be available to everybody. In Lindsay, Ontario, Monika Kerschl-Sales, co-owner of Thairapy Beauty Bar, said she hears about menopause all the time, and that hundreds of women in the region who sit in her stylist chair have shared their struggles in getting care. She described her own perimenopause — low moods, fatigue, hot flashes, night sweats and brain fog — as a stretch when she did not feel like herself and could not bring her best to work. ### Progress Watch
**Haaland and Flanagan, Two Native American Women, Break Political Barriers: The New York Times**
The New York Times reported that Deb Haaland of New Mexico will be the nation's first woman Native American governor, and that Peggy Flanagan, Minnesota's next senator, is the first Native American woman to win election to that chamber. The Times reported Flanagan, the state's lieutenant governor, defeated former sports broadcaster Michele Tafoya and keeps the Minnesota Senate seat in Democratic hands. Those results put a Native American woman in a governorship and another in the Senate for the first time the paper records.
