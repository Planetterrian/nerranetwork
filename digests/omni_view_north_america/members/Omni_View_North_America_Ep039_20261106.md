### Lead
**Newsom Commutes the Sentences of California’s 548 Death Row Inmates: The New York Times**
Governor Gavin Newsom commuted the sentences of California’s 548 people on death row, The New York Times reports. He called capital punishment morally objectionable. The Times reports he is considering a run for president, and that California has not executed anyone for 20 years. Those 548 sentences are about a quarter of death sentences nationwide, the Times reports. In The Guardian, Austin Sarat writes that the decision to empty the state’s death row frees hundreds of people from the threat of execution. Sarat, treating Newsom as a politician who may seek the presidency, argues that the move signals opposition to the death penalty is no longer a third rail in US politics. ### The Closer Look
**Online gambling more than doubles among young Montreal men, study finds: Global News**
Online gambling has more than doubled among men aged 18 to 34 in Montreal, even as the share of adults who gamble at all has fallen, Global News reports from a new public health study. Researchers surveyed 8,500 Montreal adults. Thirty-nine per cent said they had gambled in some form in 2025, 15 per cent fewer than in 2018 and in 2002. The authors say the line runs the other way for younger men: the number who reported gambling online has more than doubled.

Sylvia Kairouz, a Concordia University gambling researcher and one of the authors, said the phone is the mechanism. When a person only has to pick up a phone and push a button, she said, resisting is very difficult. She said researchers have seen more interest in online gambling than in casinos or any other physical location.

Anne Elizabeth Lapointe, chief executive of Maison Jean Lapointe, which offers treatment for gambling addiction, said the study matches the people coming through the door. She told Global News on Thursday that more and more young men are seeking help for online gambling addiction. Since the COVID pandemic, she said, the organization has been helping far fewer people struggling with in-person slots or video lottery terminals, while online gambling has become the growing concern. She said even minors are finding ways to gamble online. For the last two years, she said, kids have come to the office with a gambling addiction or with serious consequences from the habit, and young people with little or no income can face serious consequences from their losses.

The earlier steps the sources name are that pandemic-era shift away from physical machines, and the spread of advertising. Kairouz said the volume of gambling advertising is making sports betting more socially acceptable. The study found 75 per cent of those surveyed wished there were no advertisements at all for gambling sites. Lapointe said gambling advertising needs to be banned or regulated more effectively.

The figures land on men 18 to 34, on minors who reach the sites, and on the clinics treating them. For anyone who still bets, Kairouz recommends setting a budget and sticking to it. She said that over the long term, people who bet on sports will lose money because unpredictable outcomes are unavoidable, and that the house always wins. Help is available across Canada for gambling-related problems. In Quebec, Gambling: Help and Referral offers free, confidential support around the clock, in Montreal and elsewhere in the province, including online. ### Both Sides: Whether a president can lock the Oval Office as a national monument
President Donald Trump has signed an executive order declaring the Oval Office a national monument, and the fight is over whether that order can stop a successor from altering it. Trump’s case, as he put it to reporters, is that the room is a symbol of the country and should not be touched, destroyed or ripped down by a later president in order to build something in a slightly different shape. He said the White House and the Oval Office had been badly damaged before his changes, describing broken windows, cracked walls and broken stone, and he said the order would make it impossible for anybody to destroy the space.

The order’s own text, as the BBC reports it, cuts the other way: it does not restrict a president’s authority to redecorate, select furnishings, or make ordinary operational and security changes that do not substantially impair the historic structure. Greg Werkheiser, of the law firm Cultural Heritage Partners, told the BBC that furniture and decor are already up to the president so long as permanent art and architecture are not damaged, and he called the order legally meaningless because Trump cannot bind a successor this way. Werkheiser called it hypocrisy beside the demolition of the East Wing, a project the BBC reports was not approved by Congress and is continuing as a ballroom, and beside what he described as lawsuits aimed at weakening preservation law. The grounds are already a National Historic Landmark. Trump himself scaled back two national monuments in Utah in 2017, and the BBC reports that revoking a monument designation has not been tested in court, so it is unclear whether a future president or a lawsuit could undo this order.
