### Lead
**Kelowna hospital among longest waits for CT scans, new data reveals: Global News**
Health Data BC figures, obtained through freedom-of-information requests and reported by Global News, show Kelowna General Hospital had the longest median waits in British Columbia in 2025 for both routine and urgent CT scans. The median wait for a routine CT was 210 days, more than three times the provincial target of 60 days, and one in ten people waited longer than 607 days. For urgent CT scans the median was 76 days, against a seven-day target, and one in ten patients waited longer than 245 days. Median waits are the midpoint, so half of patients waited longer than those figures. MRI waits at the hospital also missed targets: 160 days for a routine scan against a 60-day target, with one in ten waiting more than 322 days, and 27 days for an urgent MRI against a seven-day benchmark, with one in ten waiting longer than 78 days. Suzanne Summers-Gill said her doctor suggested she go to Salmon Arm; she made a round trip of more than 200 kilometres, got a CT within a couple of weeks, and paid her own gas. Health Minister Ryan Peterson said the ministry plans a comprehensive review, including access to imaging, that there is no solution today because the ministry needs to listen to workers, and that the system needs better throughput and investment in MRI and CT; he said a plan will be communicated to the public once there is one. ### The Closer Look
**York University’s Glendon College to vote on the future of its bilingual location: The Globe and Mail**
York University’s Glendon Faculty Council is scheduled to vote Friday on a proposal to move the college’s bilingual academics and activities off the Glendon campus to a dedicated building at the main Keele Street campus. Interim provost Laina Bay-Cheng submitted the plan in September. If the council rejects it, that decision is final.

Glendon is a small liberal-arts college in one of Toronto’s wealthier neighbourhoods, and for students such as Samuel Nieva it has been a place to keep French. Nieva, 22, from Pickering, is president of the Glendon College Student Union. He chose Glendon for a double major — economics in English, history in French — and for everyday French with classmates, professors and staff. Enrolment has fallen to 1,419 in 2025-26, down nearly 50 percent from a peak of 2,770 in 2016-17. The college’s accumulated deficit was $52.4 million at the end of the last academic year, a heavy load for a university trying to get out of the red by 2028-29. York’s written proposal says more students would take French at Keele, which has more programs and easier transit, and that an estimated 70 percent of York’s francophone students are already in Keele-based programs. It says the move would open bilingual pathways, widen postgraduate prospects, and give more than 52,000 Keele students access to French-language courses, services and community.

Nieva and the student union, with some professors and alumni, are fighting the move. More than 2,800 people have signed a petition against it. Contract philosophy instructor Imola Ilyes calls Glendon a small bilingual ecosystem — small classes, and a library, café and offices that work in both official languages — and says that would be lost at Keele. Jérémie Séror, a professor in the University of Ottawa’s Official Languages and Bilingualism Institute, says students are more likely to use French where hearing and seeing it is normal. Linguistics professor Marcia Macaulay, who has taught at Glendon for 35 years, says students almost unanimously report finding the campus by accident and that recruitment is the problem. Deputy spokesman Yanni Dagonas told The Globe and Mail by email that recent years have brought a concerted effort to raise visibility and enrolment through campus-specific marketing and advertising. Supporters of keeping Glendon where it is have also raised a worry the article does not confirm as fact: that a motive for the shift could be redevelopment of the Glendon site, a green swath near private schools and clubs on Bayview Avenue. The council’s vote on Friday is the dated step the university has set. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-york-university-glendon-college-future-of-bilingual-location/)

### Both Sides: Should Parliament question Canada’s unpaid ocean envoy?
What is being decided is whether Diana Fox Carney, named Canada’s Ocean Envoy, should answer questions before parliamentary committees about a job Conservatives say is more than ceremonial. A mandate letter dated September 16, the day the appointment was announced, and obtained by The Globe and Mail, says she will serve “as an independent steward and champion” of the 12th Our Ocean Conference in Halifax next spring, “in an advisory, convening, and ambassadorial capacity,” according to Paul MacKinnon, deputy minister of Fisheries and Oceans. The letter calls the post voluntary, unpaid and time-limited through the conference, sets aside no budget, including for travel, and mentions support from a departmental planning committee, not the Prime Minister’s Office. The fall budget includes $8 million over two years for Fisheries and Oceans Canada to host the conference. A briefing note says she had expressed interest in chairing it, and that she and Prime Minister’s Office staff met the World Resources Institute on August 6. Since a Halifax trip in October she has travelled to Iqaluit for federal Arctic conservation announcements and to the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, as part of Canada’s official delegation.

Conservative MP Branden Leslie told reporters before Question Period on Thursday that if she is travelling abroad and speaking on behalf of Canadians, she has to be answerable to Canadians. Conservative MPs Clifford Small and Connie Cody have filed written questions about the appointment. Liberals have blocked motions to call her before the House fisheries committee, and on Thursday blocked a similar motion at the environment committee. In Question Period, Fisheries Minister Joanne Thompson said the envoy does not manage $1.2 billion in ocean-protection funding announced two weeks after the appointment, and that the funding is the minister’s responsibility. The Ethics Commissioner’s office told The Globe in October that an unpaid ministerial appointee without an order-in-council generally would not be considered a public office holder under the Conflict of Interest Act. The next step named in the letter is her work through the planning and delivery of the Halifax conference.
