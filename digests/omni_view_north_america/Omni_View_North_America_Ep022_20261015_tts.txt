In Montreal, with the wider world in view, Canadian foreign-policy and trade experts are urging Prime Minister Mark Carney and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen to launch a middle-power charter at their late October summit, The Globe and Mail reports.

Canada and the European Union meet there later this month to map what the summit calls an alliance for the future.

The aim is closer ties while both try to rely less on the United States under Donald Trump, described in that report as protectionist and unpredictable.

The Expert Group on Canada-U.S. Relations published the proposal Thursday.

The charter would be open to other democracies and patterned on the nineteen forty-one Atlantic Charter between Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill.

That statement helped lay groundwork for the United Nations and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization.

Carney popularized middle-power co-operation in a January speech at the World Economic Forum in Davos.

There he argued the post-Cold War rules-based order has suffered what he called a rupture, not a transition.

He argued middle powers such as Canada must move as a group or else end up on the menu.

The Montreal summit is aimed at deeper trade ties and co-operation.

The Expert Group says Ottawa and Brussels should go past a deal-by-deal approach and write a shared political and economic purpose, a middle-power compact for today.

The authors include former federal cabinet minister Perrin Beatty, onetime ambassador Jonathan Fried, former trade negotiator John Weekes, and Brian Clow, a former deputy chief of staff to the prime minister.

They argue that if Canada wants substantive relations with other middle powers, it has to set out principles that would found that co-operation.

The nineteen forty-one statement had no formal status.

It said Britain and the United States sought no territorial gains.

It opposed territorial changes made against the wishes of the peoples concerned.

It backed every country's right to choose its own government.

The eight-point text also pledged freer trade and economic co-operation, freedom of the seas, freedom from fear and want, and the disarmament of aggressor countries.

It became a founding plan for the postwar order, the United Nations, the Bretton Woods system, and the alliance anchored by NATO.

The Expert Group says that order has been severely compromised.

It says the United States, a signatory, has stopped following rules Washington itself wrote and bargained.

It says Washington has turned commerce built across eight decades of interdependence into a lever for economic gain, even when that harms economies worldwide.

The group says a new charter should restate the Atlantic Charter and add five principles for a digital age.

In that age, it says, defending democracy depends on the integrity of information systems, data and digital infrastructure as much as on military strength.

Canada and the European Union should pledge, the group says, that technology will not undercut human dignity, human security and democratic self-government, or threaten humanity's survival.

The principles, as laid out, start with defending democracy at home through free and fair elections, peaceful transfer of power, freedom of expression, a free press, and protection of minority rights.

Second, every country must be able to set its future free from force or coercion, including a pledge against interference in other countries' democratic processes, and joint work against disinformation and anti-democratic movements.

Third, middle powers should reduce weak points in critical supply chains, financial services, energy, technology, infrastructure and defence industries, and pledge not to use trade as a tool of coercion, while each country keeps the sovereign right to choose its own economic or security partners.

Fourth, they would combine strength through joint investment, production and shared capabilities in defence, critical technologies, energy, research and infrastructure, at a scale meant to deter aggression.

The proposal is a paper from the Expert Group, not a text Carney or von der Leyen have adopted.
