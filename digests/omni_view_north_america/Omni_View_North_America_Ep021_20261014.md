### Lead
**OPP yet to launch formal probe into alleged “egregious” OSAP use at career colleges: Global News**
Ontario’s provincial police say they are reviewing allegations about a handful of private career colleges and have yet to decide whether to launch a formal criminal probe, as the Ford government faces increasing scrutiny over how those colleges tapped the Ontario Student Assistance Program. In early October, the Ministry of Colleges and Universities sounded the alarm about six private career colleges. The ministry said those colleges were engaged in “egregious use of taxpayer dollars” and accused them of criminality by taking advantage of the student assistance program. Minister Nolan Quinn said an internal investigation found “unusually high concentrations of OSAP applications submitted from the same geographic locations.” He also pointed to “immigration services firms” directly tied to, or co-owned by, some of the colleges in question. After referring the findings to the auditing firm KPMG for a deeper dive, Quinn said the government had “reported it to the OPP and the Auditor General.” The OPP confirmed that it “received a referral from the Ontario government” and that the allegations are “currently under review.” An OPP spokesperson said, “I am not able to provide an estimated timeline for the review,” and added that, because the matter remains under review, the force could not provide any further detail or comment. Ontario’s Auditor General said the office received only a “notification” from the government, and that the province stopped short of requesting a formal value-for-money audit. The auditor’s office quoted Minister Quinn’s October 6 statement that the government “notified the Auditor General last week and has also referred this matter to the Ontario Provincial Police.” The office confirmed it received that notification last week, and said this was separate from the request for an audit received from Liberal MPP Tyler Watt. A minister has the power to request a “special assignment.” Global News reported that it is unclear why the government chose only to notify the auditor rather than request a performance audit of the kind Watt sought. The auditor’s office added that it has been notified about similar issues in the past, and stressed that its “expectation” is that the government would inform it, “as we are its independent auditor of the public accounts of Ontario.” There are currently 242 career colleges eligible to receive OSAP in Ontario. All of them have now been cut off from the provincial program while the province examines the scope of the problem. Career Colleges Ontario, the association representing hundreds of institutions, is urging the Ford government to reinstate student financial aid for career-college students who were already approved for funds and are actively attending in-person classes. The association says it had been warning the government about possible misuse of OSAP since 2023. It says the government should take targeted action against those concerns, not disrupt the education of students who have followed the rules. The minister has indicated only that the pause in OSAP grants will remain in effect until the government’s third-party auditors can complete a wider probe. On October 2, Quinn said, “The auditors, the third-party auditors, haven’t given us assurances yet, and I will respect the experts.” He said that whenever they give him assurance “to be able to release all of the institutions,” that is “when we’ll move forward with OSAP for career colleges.” Source: [globalnews.ca](https://globalnews.ca/news/12101191/opp-yet-to-launch-formal-probe-into-alleged-egregious-osap-use-at-career-colleges/)

### The Closer Look
**What about Alberta’s nine other referendum questions?: The Globe and Mail**
Albertans head to the polls in a provincial referendum next Monday, October 19, and they will face ten questions, not one. The last question, on whether Alberta should remain in Canada or trigger a secession vote, has dominated headlines. The Globe and Mail’s account today is about the other nine, including migrants’ access to social programs, judicial appointments and the future of the Senate. Voters answer each proposal by checking yes or no. Those nine, the Globe reported, could lead to consequential changes in the province or a push to reopen the Constitution. Ahead of the vote, Premier Danielle Smith has spent four million dollars on government advertising focused mostly on the first nine questions. She says that to implement her political agenda she needs direct input from the electorate. Her detractors argue the referendum is harmful, that its questions stoke anti-immigration rhetoric, and that they feed oxygen to the province’s separatist fire. The Globe and Mail said its Alberta reporters and editors will answer reader questions on Thursday, October 15, at 11 a.m. MT.
On immigration, Smith has asked voters to look at Quebec. That province reached an agreement with the federal government in 1991 that gave it exclusive control over the selection of incoming economic migrants, a carve-out she hopes to mirror in Alberta. “We have to make sure that we’re involved at the very beginning stages of determining what the market need is,” she said. The Globe reported that she has provided only loose details on how it would be managed. Her government has not said what “sustainable” immigration levels would be, nor who would qualify as an “Albertan.” The question proposes decreasing immigration levels, but the premier has recently suggested she wants to admit more people under the provincial nominee program. Between 2022 and 2025, provincial nominees comprised roughly 58 per cent of economic migrants to Alberta, according to figures from Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada. The proposals are being floated as migration into Alberta is nosediving after surging from 2022 to 2024, and as Ottawa has curbed the number of non-permanent residents entering Canada.
A separate proposal would narrow which newcomers are eligible for social services and, in the government’s words, allow Alberta to “distinguish between the temporary residents it has approved and those it has not.” Unapproved temporary residents would be ineligible for certain provincial benefits, according to a website created by Smith’s government. All temporary residents are currently eligible for those services. Smith has not set criteria for how people with “Alberta-approved immigration status” would be selected, and she has not committed to whether the change would affect non-permanent residents already living in Alberta. In February, when she unveiled a projected 9.4-billion-dollar deficit for the current fiscal year, she blamed two things: low oil prices and immigration. She has since justified the proposals by arguing that non-permanent residents are a drag on the province’s finances. According to the government’s website, the proposal would exclude all non-permanent residents from benefits such as child-care subsidies, student aid, income supports and other benefits, but not health care and education. The province estimates that non-permanent residents’ use of these services costs roughly 100 million dollars annually. The premier frequently points to a statistic that non-permanent residents cost the public system roughly one billion dollars for health care and education, while delivering 150 million dollars in taxes in return. Mikal Skuterud, an economist at the University of Waterloo, disputes that number. He said many Canadian citizens cost the public system more than they pay in taxes, and gave children as the example: they do not pay taxes but are expected to contribute later in life. “That’s just not the right way to do the fiscal impact analysis,” said Skuterud, who has advised Prime Minister Mark Carney and his predecessor, Justin Trudeau, on immigration policy. Experts have also pointed out that immigrants often arrive in better health than Canadian-born populations and use fewer health services than non-immigrants. As with the other questions, Smith has not said whether this proposal would affect current non-permanent residents or only those who move to Alberta after the province changes the law. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/alberta/article-what-are-the-alberta-referendum-questions/)
