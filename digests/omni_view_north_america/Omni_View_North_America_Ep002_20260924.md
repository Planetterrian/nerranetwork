**Blanche Vows Supreme Court Review in Third-Country Removal Case: Bloomberg Law News**
Attorney General Todd Blanche said on Thursday that the Department of Justice will immediately ask the Supreme Court for relief, after the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit on third-country removals take effect. That ruling requires meaningful notice that a person will be sent to a country they are not connected to, and a chance to claim they would be persecuted there. Blanche wrote on X that the court acted without giving the government a chance to respond, “in the dark of night,” and blocked all third-country removals, which he called an entirely legal and invaluable tool to stem illegal immigration. A tracker from Refugees International and Human Rights First counts more than 25,000 people sent to 29 third countries; The Guardian, citing Third Country Deportation Watch, says that in many cases the country is Mexico, and that some people have been sent to the Central African Republic and South Sudan. ### Across the Region
**Institutional failures, dismissed warning signs factors in deaths of eight children, report says: The Globe and Mail**
A report published Thursday by the National Association of Women and the Law and law professors at the University of Sherbrooke says institutional failures, and warnings from mothers that officials dismissed, played a role in the deaths of eight children in five cases in Canada. The authors write that significant warning signs were present before each death but did not bring adequate protective action, and that mothers — and in one case a grandmother — who identified safety risks were disbelieved or penalized while the children stayed in contact with fathers whose abusive conduct institutions already knew. In a 2019 case in Granby, Quebec, a seven-year-old girl was found unconscious in her room and later died in hospital after placement in her father’s custody; her grandmother had warned of a serious risk of sexual abuse and then lost custody after being seen as hostile for reporting those dangers. The report says the director of youth protection did not push for home visits and that nothing followed a month out of school; it says the stepmother was found guilty of manslaughter and false imprisonment and the father pleaded guilty to false imprisonment. The authors are scheduled to present the report on Parliament Hill Thursday morning with Liberal MP Lisa Hepfner, whose private member’s bill would, with some exceptions, bar a court from considering an allegation that a spouse deliberately manipulated a child into resisting the other spouse. **Kentucky Supreme Court upholds opinion on Fayette Co. judge’s impeachment: WKYT**
The Kentucky Supreme Court said Thursday it will not change its April 6 opinion voiding the impeachment of Fayette County Circuit Judge Julie Muth Goodman, after the justices taking part split evenly and court rules left that order standing. In April the court had ruled 5-1 to halt the proceedings. The Kentucky House passed an impeachment resolution on March 20, claiming Goodman abused judicial discretion and authority. Thursday’s order keeps the General Assembly from any further impeachment proceedings in this matter, while a separate judicial conduct investigation can still go forward. One case in the petition is the 2020 hit-and-run death of Tammy Botkin: Goodman dismissed a murder indictment against Cornell Thomas II, and in December the Kentucky Court of Appeals unanimously ruled to re-indict him. ### Both Sides: Impeaching a judge over her rulings
Kentucky’s Supreme Court has left in place an order that stops lawmakers from continuing the impeachment of Fayette Circuit Judge Julie Muth Goodman. The fight is whether the General Assembly may remove a judge for her rulings, or whether those disputes belong in appeals and judicial discipline.

The House says Goodman abused judicial discretion and authority, and it passed an impeachment resolution on March 20. After Franklin Circuit Judge Phillip J. Shepherd ruled on March 31 that the proceedings were null and void, lawmakers told the Lexington Herald-Leader he has no constitutional authority to void an impeachment and that a Senate trial should still happen. Their best reason is institutional: impeachment, they argue, is a legislative power a trial court cannot cancel.

The Supreme Court says this petition was not a lawful impeachment because it was never sworn or verified by affidavit, as state law requires. The justices said the allegations were disagreements over rulings in six cases, not impeachable offenses, and that such complaints must go through appeals and the judicial conduct commission. They warned that impeaching judges over controversial decisions would violate the separation of powers, threaten judicial independence, and could lead to what the court called tyrannical control of the courts. They also said the process denied Goodman due process, because five of the six cases were still pending and ethics rules barred her from publicly defending her decisions.

Both sides are contesting the same March 20 resolution, and neither disputes that a separate judicial conduct investigation into Goodman can still move forward. Thursday’s order records an even split among the justices taking part, so the April 6 opinion stands and the injunction against further impeachment proceedings remains.
