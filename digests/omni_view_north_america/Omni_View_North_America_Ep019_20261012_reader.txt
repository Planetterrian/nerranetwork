The Federal Communications Commission is asking the U.S. Supreme Court, in an eleven-page filing, to preserve discounted rates for certain political party ads and for joint fundraising committees.

Solicitor General D. John Sauer filed over the weekend and is urging the Court to block a lower-court order that would require the agency to unwind guidance on what local radio and television stations may charge for political advertising, The Desk reports.

The dispute turns on a March 30 Public Notice that extended the lowest unit charge to qualifying political party advertisements and to certain joint fundraising committees.

Chief Justice John Roberts on Thursday temporarily stayed a Fourth Circuit order that had required the FCC to decide the dispute by noon Eastern Time on Friday, after the federal government sought emergency relief.

Four Democratic candidates — former Ohio Senator Sherrod Brown, Georgia Senator Jon Ossoff, former North Carolina Governor Roy Cooper, and Michigan Representative Kristen McDonald Rivet — argue the March guidance unlawfully widened a benefit federal law reserves for legally qualified candidates.

That statutory benefit is the lowest available rate in the 45 days before a primary and in the 60 days before a general election.

The FCC says the March guidance clarifies existing requirements rather than creating new rules.

Opponents of the change say it could benefit Republican candidates in battleground states ahead of the midterms, and that it would cut revenue stations need to operate.

In August the Fourth Circuit struck down the guidance, finding that political parties and joint fundraising committees with non-candidate members were not entitled to the discounts.

In Colorado, The Colorado Sun reports that Proposition 137 would, starting next year, send all state sales tax collected on sporting goods to land conservation, access, and wildfire mitigation.

The paper estimates that amount at about $175 million at the start.

The diversion would shrink or turn off in any year it would affect the Family Affordability Tax Credit or the Earned Income Tax Credit.

Those two credits totaled nearly $1 billion in 2024.

The family credit offers households earning as much as $96,000 up to $3,200 per child under 6, and up to $2,400 per child ages 6 to 16.

Tarn Udall, an attorney with Western Resource Advocates, a main backer, says the measure was built to fund protection from wildfire and drought while recognizing budget pressure.
