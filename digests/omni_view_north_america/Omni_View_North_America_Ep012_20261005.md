### Lead
**Quebec heads to the polls with focus more on Trump than independence: The Straits Times**
Quebec's nine million people vote today for a provincial government, and The Straits Times reports the campaign has turned more on President Donald Trump's tariffs than on independence. Polls cited by the paper put support for separation at 30 percent, against a peak of nearly 50 percent in a 1995 referendum. The Parti Québécois, which says breaking away would better protect French culture and language, had promised a referendum in a first term and now says it will wait until Trump is out of office. Premier Christine Frechette of the Coalition Avenir Québec, in power eight years, has called Trump "the real adversary" and the one who "truly threatens Quebec." Polling firm Leger found 77 percent of voters wanted a change, amid high prices, a strained healthcare system and tariff damage to aluminum, steel and manufacturing. Parti Québécois leader Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has ruled out bilateral talks with Washington for tariff relief and has called Trump "extremely unpopular" and "very toxic" in Quebec. The Straits Times says analysts read a vote for the Parti Québécois as disappointment with the Coalition Avenir Québec rather than an endorsement of independence, and says a Parti Québécois win could challenge Prime Minister Mark Carney, who stresses national unity; Alberta holds a non-binding independence vote in October. Source: [straitstimes.com](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/quebec-heads-to-the-polls-with-focus-more-on-trump-than-independence)

**Supreme Court Allows Appeal on Second Degree Murder Case: Butler Radio**
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has allowed an appeal on whether its March ruling — that life in prison for second-degree murder is cruel and unusual punishment — applies to earlier sentences. Jerome Coffey, convicted over a 1994 killing and imprisoned for 33 years, is seeking resentencing; Butler Radio reports a key witness and no physical evidence connected him to the killing. More than 1,100 people in Pennsylvania are serving life without parole, and lawmakers and the attorney general have urged the General Assembly to redefine second-degree sentencing guidelines to limit a backlog of appeals. ### Both Sides: Should a Canadian launch licence require a security consult?
The Commons is debating Bill C-28, the Canadian Space Launch Act, sponsored by Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon. The bill would authorize, license and regulate the safety of commercial rocket launches from Canada. No dedicated federal law governs those launches now, and companies that want to put payloads into orbit have generally had to use foreign sites.

The case for passing the bill as written, as Liberal parliamentary secretary Mike Kelloway put it in the House on April 28, is sovereignty in a growing industry. He argued that Bill C-28 is about whether Canada becomes a sovereign, space-faring nation that launches from its own soil, builds its own capabilities and competes in one of the fastest-growing sectors on earth, or whether it continues to rely on others.

The case for rewriting the text before licences go out, as Conservative Dan Albas told the House in that debate, is a security gap. The NDP has questioned how much of the framework is left to regulation rather than written into the act.

The Hub reports that the opposition case has centred less on the goal than on what the text leaves out, so the shared ground is a domestic launch regime under Canadian rules. With a majority government, The Hub says eventual passage is not in doubt. The next step is whether the government accepts amendments tightening national security review of launch applicants; those amendments would be decided in committee, not on the floor, as debate resumes.
