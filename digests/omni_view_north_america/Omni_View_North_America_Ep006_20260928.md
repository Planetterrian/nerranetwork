> **Six months after a softer-touch pledge, ICE still arrests people in daily American life.**

**What You Need to Know:** Six months after a softer-touch pledge, ICE is still arresting people during ordinary errands across the United States, The Guardian reports. A Venezuelan man was put on a flight and then taken off before takeoff after officers said they may have erred, and an agent shot an asylum seeker in Austin last week. Colorado voters will decide whether the state may keep more revenue for schools, and Walmart’s chief executive said digital shelf tags will not be used to charge different people different prices.

### Lead
**Gone in 90 seconds: an ‘invisibilized’ ICE is arresting more people than ever: The Guardian**
President Donald Trump said in February that he had learned the agency “could use a little bit of a softer touch,” after officers killed two U.S. citizens during an ICE operation in Minneapolis and public backlash followed, The Guardian reports. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, at his Senate confirmation hearing in March, said his goal in six months was that the department not lead the news every day, after earlier raids that residents described as a siege, with armored vehicles in neighborhoods and agents outside elementary schools. The Guardian describes a Cuban man detained in Miami just after he took out the trash, an Ecuadorian father arrested in Danbury, Connecticut, after he saw his six-year-old onto the school bus, and people taken while pumping gas, their cars left behind. A Venezuelan man identified as Y was stopped at Phoenix Sky Harbor; he said officers would not say why he was being arrested or accept a judicial order barring deportation to Venezuela, and four days later he was placed on a plane, then removed before takeoff after ICE officers said they may have made a mistake. Last week an ICE agent shot Wilber Rafael Garcés Pérez in Austin, Texas; the Venezuelan asylum seeker, who had a deportation order after missing a letter to appear in immigration court, was discharged with a bullet still lodged near his spine and then taken to ICE detention. ### Across the Region
**Cap on state revenue collection would rise under Colorado ballot measure: Colorado Newsline**
Proposition NN, the only measure Colorado lawmakers referred to this year’s ballot, would raise the revenue cap set by the 1992 Taxpayer’s Bill of Rights, Colorado Newsline reports. Democratic majorities sent it to voters in May, a break from 2024, when lawmakers referred seven questions, and from an average of about five in every even-year election since 2016. Nonpartisan state analysts estimate the higher cap would increase K-12 funding by about $500 million a year and, depending on later revenue growth, could shrink Taxpayer’s Bill of Rights refunds. The measure passed the Legislature mostly on party lines, with nearly all Democrats in favor and Republicans unanimously opposed; Colorado Education Association president Kevin Vick has said it does not raise taxes and would let the state use revenue it already collects for students and educators. Yes For Colorado Kids, the committee registered in support, had reported just under $2 million in contributions as of Sept. 1. Source: [coloradonewsline.com](https://coloradonewsline.com/2026/09/28/cap-revenue-colorado-ballot-measure/)

**Walmart vows no surge pricing as digital shelf tags sweep stores: The Independent**
Walmart chief executive John Furner wrote on the company website Friday that the retailer does not set different prices based on who a shopper is or the time of day, and will not, The Independent reports. He said Walmart does not use income, shopping history, or a customer’s willingness to spend to set prices, and will not do so, and that the same rule binds Sparky, its artificial-intelligence shopping assistant, which he said will not raise a price for one customer or hide a lower-priced option that fits the search. Furner said those practices would conflict with the company’s “everyday low prices” model, while prices can still fall when costs fall or rise when goods cost more to source or ship. In March, Walmart said 2,300 of its U.S. stores were using digital shelf labels, with a plan to expand them company-wide over the next year. Last month the Federal Trade Commission warned that personalizing prices could violate consumer-protection standards. ### Both Sides: Whether Idaho should host spent nuclear fuel
Idaho legislators are making the case for a federal Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus, and the decision in front of the state is whether to seek a U.S. Department of Energy site that would store spent fuel. The Idaho Capital Sun reports that if Idaho were selected, the state would have to accept and store spent nuclear fuel and radioactive waste.

Senator Dave Lent, a Republican from Idaho Falls and a retired Idaho National Laboratory training manager, says the state is unusually well placed because of the laboratory’s 75-year record of research, safety, and innovation. He says researchers and engineers there already take spent fuel, reprocess it, and turn it into new reactor fuel. On workforce, location, facilities, and expertise, he calls Idaho a standout possible host, and he says the hard part is helping residents understand what high-level waste is and how it can be handled safely. More than 40 Idaho legislators have voiced support. Governor Brad Little has said, in the campus talks, that protecting the Snake River aquifer is nonnegotiable.

Representative Monica Church, a Democrat from Boise, opposes the project. She says that if three states are chosen, each could be responsible for accepting about 33,000 tons of the country’s spent nuclear fuel and radioactive waste. The Sun’s series says opponents argue against bringing the project to Idaho. Laboratory officials declined to be interviewed. The shared ground is that hosting would mean accepting and storing spent fuel; the dispute is whether Idaho should take that on. The Sun’s report on the opponents is set to conclude Tuesday.
