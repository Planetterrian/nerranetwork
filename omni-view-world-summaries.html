Both Sides: Should a state use lethal injection again after two doses fail?

Tennessee has to decide whether lethal injection can still carry out a death sentence after two doses of pentobarbital left Christa Gail Pike alive. Governor Bill Lee has stopped the one remaining execution set for the rest of 2026 and ordered a third-party review of what occurred.

The case for treating the sentence as still executable, and the method as lawfully used, comes from the state. Lee said carrying out a lawfully imposed sentence is among Tennessee's most serious responsibilities, and that people expect it to be legal, constitutional, and effective. The Department of Correction said it followed every step of the protocol approved by the Attorney General's office. That protocol requires a second set of syringes if the inmate is not dead after the first, which is what officials administered.

The case against using the method again comes from outside government. Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, said survival after the drugs were given is singular: seven other people have lived because a team could not find a vein, but she said no one had stayed alive once the drugs were administered. Clive Stafford Smith, a human rights lawyer, told Al Jazeera he has been in the execution chamber with clients and that it is never the clinical process officials describe, and he asked why a state kills to prove that killing is wrong. UN special rapporteur Morris Tidball-Binz told the UK's Times, Al Jazeera reported, that executing Pike would be torture and a gross human rights violation.

Both sides accept that two doses were given and that Pike was still alive, snoring, and taken to hospital. Next is Lee's outside review; no further Tennessee execution is set for this year.

Government notifies new CAFE-3 norms for passenger vehicles: The Hindu

In India, in South Asia, the government has notified Corporate Average Fuel Economy, or CAFE-3, norms that set carbon-emission limits for passenger vehicles. The Ministry of Power said the fuel-consumption benchmark tightens from 3.996 litres per 100 km in 2027-28 to 3.3273 litres per 100 km in 2031-32, about a 16.7 percent improvement. The norms cover hatchbacks, sedans, special utility vehicles and MPVs seating eight passengers besides the driver, and raise the reference weight from 1,082 kg to 1,229 kg. Manufacturers can claim 1 gram of CO2 per kilometre for each eligible cleaner-fuel technology, including ethanol-blended petrol, biofuels and compressed biogas, up to a cap of 9.0 grams.

Mexico's Pacific coast braces for Hurricane Polo: BBC World

In Mexico, on the Pacific edge of the Americas, residents are bracing for Hurricane Polo, which is forecast to make landfall later on Monday or early on Tuesday. The category three storm, with maximum sustained winds near 115 mph (185 km/h), is expected to move over Baja California Sur, including the resort of Los Cabos. Eight dead in Thailand floods since mid-September: CNA

The capital declared a city-wide disaster on Saturday after almost 48 hours of heavy rain; schools are closed and people have been urged to work from home. The EU is preparing "road maps" for accession for Ukraine and three other countries: Українські Національні Новини

In Europe, the European Commission is drawing up proposals for step-by-step membership paths for Ukraine, Montenegro, Moldova and Albania, in an account Українські Національні Новини carried from Politico. Germany’s government published a roadmap that for the first time explicitly commits to transitioning away from fossil fuels by 2045, with renewables targeted to reach 80 percent of electricity by 2030. It reaffirms the coal exit by 2038 with potential for 2035 and a 30 percent cut in methane emissions by 2030. Andreas Sieber of 350.org called the explicit phase-out goal a notable shift but warned the economic ministry is pushing for greater gas use.
