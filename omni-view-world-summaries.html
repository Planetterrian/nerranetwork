1. Nigeria's first Ebola patient holidayed in Uganda, officials say: BBC World

Nigeria has its first confirmed Ebola case since the East African outbreak began in May: the wife of a diplomat based in Abuja, who travelled to Kampala for a holiday, Dr Jean Kaseya of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention told the BBC.

She is originally from Uganda, and she fell ill three days after arriving home in the Nigerian capital on 3 October.

Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate told the BBC the patient is 42, spent two and a half weeks in Kampala, and made a two-hour stopover in Ethiopia's capital on the return; he said she is stable and improving.

She tested positive on Tuesday. Pate said authorities had identified 44 people who had contact with her, not all of them close, because the net was cast wide.

Aviation Minister Festus Keyamo said 46 people — 44 who met her at hospital and two who worked in her home — are banned from boarding flights, and Nigeria has added airport health screenings, temperature checks, movement tracking and digital health declarations.

Neighbouring Cameroon has also stepped up surveillance at its airports and ports.

Pate said none of the contacts now in isolation are known to have symptoms, including the person she travelled with, and that she has not travelled outside Abuja since landing.

Kaseya said symptoms began on 6 October, that she first sought care thinking she had malaria, and that she was admitted to a second private hospital on the 11th. Pate said public health authorities were alerted on 19 October and three laboratory tests confirmed the diagnosis the next day.

She has the Bundibugyo species, the same as the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo, which the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control reports has claimed at least 4,600 lives, with more than 9,500 cases. That species has no approved vaccine or treatment, though trials are under way. The incubation period is two to 21 days.

Uganda, where at least two people died after the outbreak was also reported there, declared itself free of the disease in August after its last patient was discharged.

4. Experts alarmed by 'less transparent' CDC as US measles deaths rise: The Guardian

5. Commentary: The Arizona-Nevada-California water war is heating up, and all three states will end up parched: Los Angeles Times

Arizona has moved to join Nevada's federal lawsuit against a United States Interior Department plan that cuts Colorado River water for Arizona, Nevada and California, the downstream basin states, through 2028, with harsher cuts over the following eight years.

Nevada sued to block the plan in late August. Arizona told the court its interests are unique and that it cannot entrust them to another state suing to protect its own.

California, the largest consumer of Colorado River water, has not expressed an interest in joining the lawsuit.

Lake Mead and Lake Powell are at their lowest levels since they began filling. As they fall further, Hoover and Glen Canyon dams could lose the capacity to provide water and power downstream.

The government got the three states to agree to reductions over the next two years: through 2028, California is to cut use by about 12%, Arizona by 31% and Nevada by 28%.

After that, total allotments to the three states could be cut by as much as 40% through 2036.

The 1922 Colorado River Compact guaranteed the lower three states at least 7.5 million acre-feet a year. A Los Angeles Times commentary argues the engineering estimate used to support that guarantee was a lie, not a mistake, because it rested on a short, unusually wet sample of the river's flow.

Both Sides: California's one-time billionaire tax

Californians vote early next month on Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on the roughly 250 billionaires who live in the state. Meta, the company behind Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, has struck a deal with chief executive Mark Zuckerberg to keep him a resident of California, or wherever Meta keeps its base, for eight years even if the tax passes. He would receive new non-voting shares, created by a two-for-one split for all shareholders, that could be sold to pay a potential multibillion-dollar bill without diluting his control. Zuckerberg, worth $239bn according to Forbes, told Meta he was considering moving his primary residence because the measure could force him to sell voting shares.

The case for the tax, as Congressman Rohit Khanna of California's 17th district put it, is that every society is taxed and the only question is who pays. He argues that working people pay more than they should while billionaires are becoming trillionaires, and that billionaires should pay what he calls their fair share so working people are unburdened. He has called the vote a choice between standing with the working class or the oligarchs.

The case against, as Zuckerberg put it to his own company, is that the bill could be large enough to force a sale of the shares that control Meta, or a move out of the state where the headquarters sit. Sergey Brin, Peter Thiel and Eric Schmidt are among tech billionaires spending heavily to fight the measure. Recent polling has shown Proposition 40 still short of majority support. The next step is that vote early next month. The result is not known.

Is Ebola under control in Kenya after nine cases confirmed?: BBC

Kenya is facing its first Ebola outbreak, with nine confirmed cases all linked to a man who travelled from the Democratic Republic of Congo and died two weeks ago, the BBC reported. Wall Street Journal: Regional Oil Exports Exceed Pre-War Levels: IranWire

Middle Eastern oil exports, including shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and alternative routes, have risen above pre-war levels even as attacks on shipping continue, IranWire reported from a Wall Street Journal account of Kpler tracking data.
