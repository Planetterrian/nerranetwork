Microsoft outages extend into 2nd day, company not aware of malicious activity: CBC

A failure in Microsoft services tied to Exchange Online and email is in a second day. CBC reported Friday that an hourslong outage was affecting a wide range of Microsoft 365 offerings that interact with those email services. Users reported Outlook, Teams and the Copilot assistant down. France 24 reported that the break began on Thursday and was still hitting people around the world on Friday, November 13. Microsoft blamed server problems and said it was struggling to fix them. The company said malicious activity was not to blame, and that it was not aware of malicious activity. France 24 said businesses, governments and likely millions of people have been without services like Outlook email for over half a day, triggering wide-ranging consequences. Oil supply to exceed global demand for first time since Iran war: Belga

The International Energy Agency said on Friday that more oil will be available worldwide than is needed to meet demand in the fourth quarter. It is the first time supply has exceeded demand since the war in Iran began in February. Belga reported the agency’s line that the market is showing tentative signs of rebalancing. Gulf exports are now at 74 percent of pre-war levels. Belga reported that Saudi Arabia and Iran have increased production, and that more tankers are passing the Strait of Hormuz even though attacks are still taking place there. The agency said Gulf exports have recovered much faster than global oil demand. Brent crude is trading at 96 dollars a barrel, down from more than 117 dollars at the end of October. Belga tied the drop to higher Middle East exports and hopes of a diplomatic solution. Rigzone, on the same monthly report, said the agency raised its estimate of global oil supplies in 2026 by 920,000 barrels a day. This year’s average deficit is now 685,000 barrels a day, against 1.7 million a day in last month’s projection, and the annual shortfall is 60 percent lower than that earlier report. This quarter shows an overhang of roughly 2 million barrels a day where a deficit of similar size had been projected a month ago. Saudi production reached 10 million barrels a day in October. Iraq’s output hit a seven-year high of 4.6 million a day. Gulf shipments excluding Oman reached 18.4 million barrels a day last month. Observed inventories rose in October for the first time since June, mostly as barrels at sea, while onshore stocks remained at their lowest in a decade. Global demand is forecast to fall by 2.68 million barrels a day this year, to an average of 102.3 million. IEA members will speed the release of remaining emergency stockpiles, discharging a further 71 million barrels through the end of February. Benchmark crude is down about 18 percent this month. The agency said the outlook for gasoil and diesel has marginally improved as high prices reined in consumption, and it expects the surplus emerging this quarter to widen next year.

Ethiopian men abducted for military service as tensions rise in Horn of Africa: The Globe and Mail

In Ethiopia, night raids in Addis Ababa are forcing young men into the army as Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed expands the armed forces. The Globe and Mail reports the military is now estimated at half a million soldiers, one of the largest in Africa, with a fast-growing stock of drones, fighter jets, artillery and armoured vehicles. Azeb Girma said men in civilian clothes pushed her only child, 19, onto an unmarked truck; after a March conscription and training, he was deployed to a northern border town to fight Tigrayan rebels and called her on a stolen phone. Etsegenet Tadios, 55, said two sons were taken in September from a bar where they were playing pool, then phoned from an unknown number, sounding terrified, as they headed north. The army is fighting insurgencies in Tigray, Amhara and Oromia. Cameron Hudson, a US-based Horn of Africa analyst, estimates the force has more than doubled since the last Tigray war ended in 2022. The paper reports that thousands of soldiers marched into Tigray in September and that drone strikes in northern regions have killed hundreds of civilians and rebels. Kjetil Tronvoll, a Norwegian professor of peace and conflict studies, said heavy equipment has been arriving in huge shipments, though government secrecy makes the full scale hard to confirm. War in Ukraine: MEPs demand Russia returns deported Ukrainian children: European Parliament

In Belgium, the European Parliament on Thursday adopted a resolution demanding that Russia immediately return Ukrainian children it has deported or forcibly transferred. The text passed 536 to 8, with 37 abstentions, and condemns what members called systematic deportations, forced transfers, illegal adoptions, forced Russification, indoctrination and militarisation. Members said return to unoccupied Ukraine must be safe, immediate and unconditional, and that it is a duty under international law, not a bargaining chip for territory, prisoner exchanges or other political deals. The resolution says that, as of September 2026, 20,651 cases of deportation or forced transfer had been verified, while about 1.6 million Ukrainian children remain under Russian occupation. It also says that, as of 28 October 2026, 2,752 children who had been deported, forcibly transferred or rescued from occupied territory had returned. Members said a safe, unconditional return must be central to any real peace agreement ending Russia's war, and that no durable peace can ignore the children's rights or the responsibility of those who violated them. The Parliament restated support for the International Criminal Court and for arrest warrants against President Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova, the Russian president's commissioner for children's rights. It said accountability should run beyond Russia's top leadership, urged EU states to speed identification and return and to widen sanctions, and backed cooperation among Europol, Eurojust and Ukraine's human-rights ombudsman. The vote is a parliamentary demand, not a treaty and not a completed return.
