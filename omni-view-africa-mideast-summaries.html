Both Sides: Whether a lender may sell a hire-purchase car after two-thirds is paid

Whether Co-operative Bank of Kenya could repossess and sell Peter Musya Kiteme's hire-purchase vehicle without High Court leave, after he had paid two-thirds, is what the Court of Appeal has decided.

Kiteme's strongest case, which the High Court accepted and the appeal court has upheld, rests on Section 20 of the Consumer Protection Act. That section restricts a supplier from repossessing or disposing of goods once a consumer under a future-performance agreement has paid two-thirds or more, unless the High Court gives leave. He financed a Toyota Harrier valued at 2.95 million shillings, paid 718,000 shillings down, and was to make 48 instalments of about 60,434 shillings. The court found he had paid more than two-thirds. The bank took the vehicle in May 2021, claiming arrears of 455,029 shillings. Kiteme said he had cleared that sum and the auctioneer and storage charges by 15 June 2021. The bank did not return the car and sold it in September 2021 for 1.52 million shillings. A three-judge bench upheld an award of 1,777,541.28 shillings, exemplary damages of 300,000 shillings, interest and costs, and said the High Court judge had not erred.

The bank's strongest case was that the Consumer Protection Act did not apply, because the Hire Purchase Act governed the deal. The Court of Appeal rejected that argument and dismissed the appeal with costs to Kiteme.

Both sides accept the financing, the May 2021 repossession and the September sale. The appeal is finished, and the compensation order stands.

Both Sides: Whether Britain should refuel Saudi jets in Yemen

Britain has decided, for now, to put Royal Air Force tankers behind Saudi fighter jets while fighting with the Houthis is at its worst since the 2022 truce. Prime Minister Andy Burnham approved the refuelling. The question is whether that help stays on the defensive side of the war or ties London to a wider air campaign.

The British government's case is that the support is time-limited and defensive. Its best reason, as Middle East Eye reports the government's position, is that a bounded refuelling role can be separated from offensive bombing: help Saudi jets stay in the air for defence, and stop when the limit runs out. On that account, the approval answers a present threat without reopening the full Saudi-led air campaign Britain has backed before.

Campaign groups and academics reject that split. In an open letter they warn the approval could make the United Kingdom complicit in war crimes. Their best reason is that fuel for Saudi fighters cannot be walled off from how those jets are used once they are airborne, and that Britain's long record of backing the Saudi-led air campaign makes a "defensive" label too thin to carry the legal risk. They are arguing about complicity, not about whether the fighting is real.

Both sides are working from the same reported facts: Burnham has approved the refuelling; the government calls it time-limited and defensive; fighting is the worst since the 2022 truce; and nearly 130,000 Yemenis have been displaced in recent weeks.
