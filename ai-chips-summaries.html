The Teardown: Four Supplies for Eight Slots

A six-U chassis that holds eight full accelerators is a power problem before it is a compute problem. INNO3D’s announced AGS-6220V2, in TechPowerUp’s item on the product notice, pairs dual Intel Xeon 5th Gen Scalable processors and up to 32 DDR5 RDIMMs with eight PCIe Gen 5 x16 slots for NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPUs. It feeds that stack from four 2700 W redundant Titanium power supplies.

The slot and the supply do different jobs. A PCIe Gen 5 x16 link is the attachment for data and for a limited amount of slot power. A server GPU at a sustained training or high-batch inference load does not live on that slot budget. Board power has to come from the chassis rails. Eight of those boards in six U put the rails, the bus bars and the converters in the same short column of air as the GPUs, so the supply count is as much a specification as the slot count.

Titanium, on the 80 Plus ladder, is the efficiency band in which the supply turns the least of each incoming watt into heat in its own magnetics and switches. Six U leaves no spare volume for a lazy converter. Redundant, on a four-supply set, means the nameplate is not the continuous budget: the chassis is built so one module can drop and the others still hold the rail. The notice lists four 2700 W supplies, eight GPU slots, two processors and up to 32 DIMMs, and it does not assign a wattage to each GPU. The nameplate is shared headroom and a spare, not a per-chip rating.

The tradeoff flips when the GPUs are not the load and the rack feed is already full. Extra Titanium modules then buy a hot-swap and a cleaner efficiency curve, not another accelerator. They also take airflow and rear cable space a denser tray would have given to another GPU. On a light inference or visualization node, the spare supply is insurance. On a fully loaded eight-GPU box sitting on a constrained rack circuit, those four modules are what let a supply be serviced without dropping the rail, and they are why a ninth card does not fit the power story. Source: [techpowerup.com](https://www.techpowerup.com/353391/inno3d-announces-6u-rtx-pro-6000bse-gpu-server)

The Teardown: When a Wafer Chain Goes Offline

A wafer-scale part is sold as one computer the size of a wafer. Chaining is the next claim: stitch many of those wafers into one logical machine so a single job can see all of them. Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis, writing on X on October 4, pushed back on a reading that treats such a chain as delivered and fully utilized. His point was narrow and physical: chaining dozens and dozens of wafers together brings quite a few offline and utilization issues.

The mechanism is a failure domain, not a brochure diagram. Each wafer is its own yield lottery. A conventional packaged chip throws out the bad die and ships the good ones. A wafer that is the product cannot do that as cleanly. Defects, a dead link, a power fault or a cooling fault take that wafer out of the chain. Once wafers are wired as one machine, the job scheduler either shrinks the job to the wafers still answering or waits on the missing slice. Waiting is idle silicon that still occupies power, network ports and floor space. Shrinking the job means the nameplate — dozens and dozens of wafers — is not the capacity a customer can book every hour.

Utilization then splits in two. Delivery utilization asks whether every wafer in the purchase order is on the floor and powered. Online utilization asks whether those wafers are in the same chain at the same time, with links healthy enough to pass traffic. A chain multiplies the second problem. One offline wafer does not merely subtract one wafer’s compute. It can force a rebalance across the remaining chain, and a second fault in another wafer can split the machine into islands that cannot see one another. Spares help only if they are already cabled, already cooled and already in the address map. A spare in a crate is not a spare in a chain.

That is why “all delivered and fully utilized” is a stronger claim than “wafers shipped.” Shipment counts wafers that left the dock. A chained system counts wafers that are up, linked and scheduled together. Patel’s objection is that the second number is the one that pays for the power and the building, and chaining dozens and dozens of wafers makes that second number harder to hold. The tradeoff flips when the job can be cut into pieces smaller than the chain, so one offline wafer costs one wafer, not a stall of the whole machine. Until the software can do that, the chain’s headline size and its usable size are different machines.

The Teardown: Why a Limb Log Wears Out Flash

A joint that has to keep the last safe pose does not lose that pose in the neural network. It loses it in the log. Take the write path apart at the limb microcontroller, not at the vision computer. The same piece says logging motor torque, velocity and limb position at sampling rates above 10 kilohertz can exceed the endurance of conventional non-volatile memory. The wear mechanism is block erase. Embedded flash does not rewrite a single sample in place. It erases a whole block, and a shift-long sample stream never pauses long enough for that block to be written once and left alone. Small log lines therefore become repeated erases. That is write amplification, and it spends the cell’s life on bookkeeping rather than on the sensor count. Memory sized for the vision workload does not stand in. The article separates high-bandwidth memory, used for large networks on the central processor, from latency-optimized memory on the distributed controllers. A wide inference path does not hold the last torque command through a power cut beside the motor driver. Goldman Sachs figures cited there put annual humanoid shipments at fewer than 15,000 units in 2025 and possibly more than 6 million by 2035. Endurance that looks acceptable on a demo stand is a fleet problem at the second figure. The tradeoff flips when logging stops being a continuous stream and becomes a checkpoint on a fault or a mode change. The Teardown: When the Rack's Coolant Has to Change

Heat leaves the rack, or the accelerators throttle. Data Center Knowledge, citing Bill Kleyman, reports AI workloads driving typical rack densities from roughly 10–30 kilowatts into 80–150 kilowatts, with advanced deployments approaching 1 megawatt per rack.

Air cooling is a volume problem. Fans push room air across a heatsink; the air carries heat to a coil elsewhere in the building. The ceiling is flow. More flow means more pressure drop through the cabinet, more risk that hot-aisle air comes back to the inlet, and a point where the electrical feed and the airflow fail together. Another card in that rack then adds temperature, not useful work.

Liquid cooling changes the carrier, not the need to reject the heat. A pump moves water or a dielectric across a cold plate, or the board sits in a bath, and a coolant distribution unit hands the load to the building loop. A small liquid flow moves heat that would have required a large air flow. Kleyman tied that shift to AFCOM's State of the Data Center 2026 survey: 69 percent of respondents expect rack density to increase like nothing seen in the past, and liquid-cooling adoption is showing 36 percent growth.

The parts in the rack show why the old air budget breaks. Kleyman said a standard rack can hold six Nvidia DGX-class systems, each built around eight H100 accelerators, with individual accelerator cards costing up to $40,000. That population does not fit a 10–30 kilowatt cabinet as a full set. It starts to fit between 80 and 150 kilowatts. Near 1 megawatt per rack, the design is busbar plus coolant loop, not a deeper air plenum.

The tradeoff flips inside the old 10–30 kilowatt band. Pumps and plates then add leak paths without buying accelerators the air system could already feed. Once a deployment approaches 1 megawatt per rack, staying on air means leaving some of those $40,000 cards out, or asking the hall for airflow it cannot move.

The Teardown: CXL Memory Pooling in the MX1

Take it apart and the MX1 places RISC-V cores directly alongside DDR5 and SSD arrays connected through CXL links. This layout lets the cores perform local reductions and filtering before any data crosses the host PCIe bus. Each CXL-attached module therefore cuts the volume of traffic that would otherwise traverse the narrower host interface. The design also lets the SSDs serve as a slower but larger tier that the RISC-V cores can address without first staging everything in host DRAM. When the working set fits inside the pooled DDR5, latency stays comparable to local memory; once the set spills to the SSD tier, bandwidth drops but capacity scales without adding more host sockets. The tradeoff flips when the ratio of compute to data movement exceeds the CXL link rate, at which point the added cores no longer offset the extra hop.

An in-row coolant distribution unit takes heat off a secondary loop at the end of the aisle, so the cold plate on the package is not the building heat exchanger. The Teardown: One Laser, Many Wavelengths

Wavelength-division multiplexing puts several colours of light on one fibre so a data-centre link carries more traffic without another strand. The conventional build gives each colour its own laser, plus a driver and a control loop. Channel count and component count move together. Add a colour and you add another laser to power, tune, and watch fail.

An optical frequency comb breaks that pairing. One source produces many evenly spaced wavelengths. On a spectrum analyzer those lines look like the teeth of a comb. Data Center Knowledge reports that AI data centres are scaling toward 16 or more wavelengths per fibre, which is where a laser-per-channel build gets heavy: each added colour brings another laser, driver, and control loop, and with them more power, cost, and failure points.

Marcello Girardi, CEO and co-founder of Solinide, told Data Center Knowledge that four wavelengths is a solved problem and that arrays solve it well. He said the difficulty starts at eight, and that by 16 the component count is what limits you. His account of the comb is specific: one pump laser and one silicon nitride die produce all the wavelengths at once, on a grid set by the resonator itself, so the hardware does not grow as channels are added.

Frank Smyth, founder and CTO of Pilot Photonics, told Data Center Knowledge that comb lasers potentially pack many more wavelengths much tighter together without fear of interference. Spacing comes from the resonator, not from a rack of independently locked lasers drifting toward each other.

The same article draws a line on maturity. Comb sources have circulated in telecom research for years, but the co-packaged optics many of these designs envision remain closer to pilot than to volume production. That is the flip. At four wavelengths, an array already does the job, and a single pump plus still-pilot co-packaged optics is a harder bet. Toward 16 wavelengths per fibre, the array's part count — laser, driver, and lock loop per colour — is the constraint the comb is built to remove, provided the single source and its packaging actually reach volume. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/networking/the-role-of-optical-frequency-comb-generators-in-ai-data-centers)

The Teardown: Through-Glass Vias in Glass Interposers

Take it apart and the bottleneck appears at the moment a femtosecond laser must drill a precise via through glass without cracking the surrounding material or leaving debris that blocks later copper plating. The laser fires pulses lasting only femtoseconds so heat does not propagate into the glass matrix, allowing the beam to ablate a clean cylindrical hole while the rest of the interposer stays intact. Litlit’s factory in Vilnius will produce up to 3,000 such lasers annually once it opens in October, a volume that directly limits how many advanced packages can receive through-glass vias each year. RayVen’s two-micron systems add the ability to focus inside silicon itself, turning the same short-pulse mechanism into a tool for backside structuring and stacked-chip signaling without chemical etch steps. Menhir’s 1.5-micron units target the synchronization role in co-packaged optics, where the laser acts as a timing reference rather than a drill, yet still relies on the same femtosecond stability to keep jitter low enough for 6G and AI fabric links. The global ultrafast-laser market sits at roughly $2.4 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach over $10 billion by the early 2030s, with semiconductor miniaturization cited as the leading driver. When demand spikes, the constraint flips from laser power to the number of field-deployed units that can run 24 hours without maintenance, which is exactly the qualification Litlit, RayVen, and Menhir are now racing to complete.

The Teardown: CoWoS Capacity Scaling Limits

CoWoS advanced packaging throughput is constrained by the number of usable interposer reticles that can be processed per month on 300 mm wafers and the subsequent TSV reveal and hybrid bonding steps. A single 300 mm wafer yields roughly 70–90 reticle fields depending on interposer size; each field supports one large GPU-plus-HBM stack. Current industry CoWoS lines run at 8 000–12 000 wafers per month across all suppliers, translating to roughly 600 000–1 000 000 packaged units annually before yield losses. Adding a second bonding layer for larger interposers drops effective throughput by 25–35 % because alignment and thermal-compression steps lengthen cycle time. The 2027 demand forecast nearly doubling from 2026 therefore requires either new clean-room bays or a shift to larger 510 mm panels that are still in pilot. Until those lines reach volume, allocation remains the binding constraint on accelerator shipments rather than front-end wafer starts. Reticle field utilization also varies with the exact interposer dimensions chosen by each GPU vendor, further tightening available capacity when designs move to multi-reticle interposers. Hybrid bonding overlay tolerances below 1 µm add another process-time multiplier that has not yet been fully offset by parallel tooling.
