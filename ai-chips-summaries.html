The Teardown: When the Film Has to Survive the Hole

A stacked package only prints if the wafer under the lens is flat enough that the next pattern lands on the last one. That alignment is overlay. Bow is the warp that film stress puts into the wafer: the surface leaves the focal plane, and features shift sideways from the grid the scanner is trying to hit. EE Times, reporting Zeiss Semiconductor Manufacturing Technology at SEMICON West, quotes US sales head Raghu Nayak calling wafer flatness a huge issue, and describes Zeiss DUNE as a tool that measures topography and corrects it wafer by wafer so the next fabrication step can still overlay.

The same account puts a second job beside that correction. Zeiss NLX is specified for 300-millimeter wafers. It is built to make internal structures in a modern package visible without destroying the package, so connections between stacked dies can be checked and a defect caught before the wafer is scrapped. DUNE tries to put the surface back where the scanner expects it. NLX asks whether the bonds inside the stack are actually there. Those are sequential gates, not substitutes: a flat wafer can still hide an open bond, and a bond map does not flatten the wafer the scanner is about to expose.

Panel-level packaging changes the shape of the substrate the film has to cover. EE Times reports that Von Ardenne is extending its deposition portfolio from the 300-millimeter wafer to panel-level packaging, and that US chief executive Stephen Strausser told the magazine the company has a one- to two-year lead in coating and deposition for panel-level glass substrates. The process named is HiPIMS, high-power impulse magnetron sputtering, aimed at high-aspect-ratio barrier and seed layers inside through-glass vias. Those vias are holes through a glass panel that later take metal so a redistribution layer can land on a pad. The claim EE Times relays is dense, highly adherent film in those holes, so a deposition recipe already running on 300-millimeter wafers can move to a larger panel, including through-glass vias and redistribution layers.

The figures in that account are the format and the lead, and they are enough to see where time sits. NLX's inspection field is the 300-millimeter wafer. Von Ardenne's stated lead is one to two years, and the migration it describes is away from that same 300-millimeter format onto a panel. A panel is the larger form factor, so each coating pass addresses more package area than a 300-millimeter disc, but only if the film stays continuous down a hole whose depth is large relative to its width. That continuity is the whole engineering content of the high-aspect-ratio claim here. One to two years is the gap Strausser claims over other coaters on panel-level glass. A packaging line that wants a second source of that barrier-and-seed step is waiting on that interval, not on a lithography node announcement.

The stage is a separate limit, and EE Times puts it on the same show floor. Heidenhain unveiled the METIS HP, a full air-bearing planar platform for the most demanding step-and-scan work, including direct laser writing and high-resolution optical inspection. The company supplies sub-nanometer motion control and position feedback for wafer manufacturing. An air bearing takes mechanical contact out of the loop that has to hold position while the tool steps. Sub-nanometer is the scale the article gives for that feedback. Overlay fails when the stage and the bowed wafer disagree by enough to miss the last layer; DUNE is trying to remove the wafer's share of that miss, and the air bearing is trying to remove the stage's.

SEMICON West 2026 ran October 13 to 15 at Moscone Center in San Francisco. SEMI chief executive Ajit Manocha opened it by calling semiconductors a trillion-dollar industry. Beside the metrology, EE Times notes equipment installer ABM, after its merger with WGNSTAR, pointing at a technical training center in Boise, Idaho, with room for about 500 participants a year. Five hundred people a year is a training throughput. It only binds if the tools above ship into fabs that need installers who can keep a sub-nanometer stage and a glass-via coater inside the process window those tools were sold on.

DUNE is the correction that matches a round wafer whose defect is bow: measure this wafer, flatten this wafer, send it back into a 300-millimeter flow. That choice stops matching the package when the interconnect leaves the wafer and enters a glass panel. The clock then is Von Ardenne's one- to two-year lead on panel coating, because a wafer-flatness tool does not put barrier metal down a through-glass hole. NLX stays the inspection that matches the problem only while the defect is an internal open inside a 300-millimeter package. Once the yield loss sits on a panel, outside that wafer field, a wafer-format internal camera is aimed at the wrong object, and the 300-millimeter correction loop no longer bounds the part.

The Teardown: Why the Helios Switch Tray Still Needs a Retimer

Two Broadcom Tomahawk 6 switch chips, each rated 102.6T, sit side by side in a double-wide 1OU tray. ServeTheHome opened that tray on the HPE booth floor at OCP 2026. It is the HPE Juniper QFX5252, built as an AMD Helios switch tray, and the layout is the opposite of a chassis switch whose value is a wall of front-panel ports. The fabric connectors are at the back. They blind-mate into the rack’s cable cartridges. The electrical problem is the run from each switch chip to those connectors at the SerDes rate Helios actually uses.

That rate is 224G PAM4. The switch that sounds like the scale-up part is Tomahawk Ultra, and ServeTheHome spells out why it is not in this tray. The current Tomahawk Ultra is a 112G PAM4 SerDes switch: 512 lanes of PAM4-112G. A lane specified at 112G does not carry a 224G PAM4 signal, so those 512 lanes are not a Helios fabric. The tray uses Tomahawk 6 because Helios is on 224G PAM4 SerDes. Two of those 102.6T devices in a 1OU tray are why the assembly is liquid cooled. Internal manifolds feed the cold blocks on the chips.

The connectors do not sit next to the silicon. ServeTheHome notes that the cabled connections to the rear high-density fabric connectors are quite far from the Tomahawk 6 chips, and some are very far. The liquid-cooling blocks in that gap are retimers. A retimer takes a degraded high-speed waveform, samples it, and launches a clean edge so the next stretch of copper can still meet the eye opening the SerDes requires. At 224G PAM4 the channel budget is short enough that a long board-plus-cable run inside one rack fails unless something rebuilds the edge. AMD is using retimers for that reach. NVIDIA has worked to remove them. After the retimer, the tray still needs what ServeTheHome calls an enormous array of copper cables into the cartridges. Retimers buy the reach; copper still carries the lanes. That combination is the picture the site says it will use when explaining why co-packaged optics is the next simplification: move the optical engine next to the switch chip and the long electrical run goes away.

The cooling split matches the power split. The two switch chips and the retimer blocks are on liquid. Fans and airflow guides remain over heatsinks on other components. NVIDIA has been pushing fans out of the AI rack; this Helios tray still air-cools those other bits. The front latch levers are very long because the back panel is long, has to blind-mate, and the insertion force into the Helios rack is significant. ServeTheHome had already covered the QFX5252 at HPE Discover 2026. The show-floor difference is the assembled tray, built to scale up inside the rack rather than out of front-panel connectors.

The copper-plus-retimer choice stops being the right one when the cartridge connectors have to sit so far from the Tomahawk 6 that a 224G PAM4 lane only closes inside the rack if a retimer rebuilds the edge and a full copper array finishes the run. That is the condition already sitting in the open QFX5252: the retimers are there because the connectors are far, and co-packaged optics is the simplification once that distance is what in-rack copper cannot close on its own.

The Teardown: Why the Weight File Sets the Memory Floor

An open-weight release puts the trained parameters where a buyer can load them onto hardware the buyer controls. Semiconductor Engineering's account of that shift starts from the memory those parameters occupy. Ashish Darbari, chief executive of Axiomise, notes in the piece that OpenAI reports gpt-oss-120b runs on a single 80 GB GPU and gpt-oss-20b can run on a device with 16 GB of memory, and that both use MXFP4 quantization. The weights are the file. The GPU or the smaller device is the shelf. If the file does not fit the shelf, that model does not run there, whatever else the chip can do at peak.

Sharad Chole, chief scientist and co-founder at Expedera, tells the same article that publishing the weights cuts vendor lock-in. The user can host the model or pick a service provider, and no single company owns the next token. He also dates the map. As of September 2026, he said, the larger open-weight models come primarily from outside the United States, from Europe or Asia, and the field is moving toward trillion-parameter models. Guillaume Boillet, vice president of strategic marketing at Arteris, draws the line the file does not cross: an open-weight release is not necessarily open source, because training data, code, and usage rights may remain restricted.

The worked example is the pair Darbari cites, and it should be read as a fit check rather than a benchmark. A model at the 120-billion-parameter scale, stored in MXFP4, is reported to fit one 80 GB GPU. A model at the 20-billion-parameter scale, in the same format, is reported to fit 16 GB. The article is explicit that memory fit alone does not establish useful performance. Architects, the piece says, still have to account for memory capacity, sustained performance, changing models, and deployment security. Chole's catalog count makes the churn concrete: about 250 open-weight models from major companies landed on Hugging Face in the last two years, not counting quantized variants or models from smaller firms. He describes an effort to distill a 400-billion-parameter DeepSeek-C model into an 8-billion-parameter Qwen model, and he cautions that the industry is not about to see 100-billion-parameter models breaking every device benchmark. The Teardown: Why the Replay Never Sees the Missing Call

A graphics trace benchmark does not launch the application. It replays a recorded stream of graphics API calls and times how quickly the driver and the GPU finish that fixed script. The result is a playback rate. It is not a training step, and it is not a measure of how a chip moves tensors.

ServeTheHome reports that the Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation has released SPECviewperf 15.0.1 Linux Edition, the first Linux Edition in almost half a decade. SPEC first shipped a Linux build in 2019, as SPECviewperf 13, because many workstation applications are OpenGL-based or are not available on Linux at all. The new Linux suite carries 8 of the Windows edition's 11 benchmarks. The traces are from Blender 3.6, 3DEXPERIENCE CATIA and CATIA V5, Creo 9, Maya 2025, Solidworks 2024, and NX 2406, plus visualizations from OpendTect and the Tuvok library. Against the previous Linux suite, 2020 3.0, Blender is new and the CATIA, Creo, Maya, Solidworks, and NX workloads are updated. There is no DirectX path.

That gap is the mechanism. A replay can time only calls that were captured. If the application never issued the call on the platform SPEC ported, the call is not in the script, and no driver optimization on the machine under test can make it appear. The Linux score therefore describes the OpenGL subset in those eight traces, on the CPU architecture the binary actually runs. This release adds native Arm AArch64 support, the first time the graphics benchmark has been natively available on an Arm-based system, so the timed path need not include an x86 translation layer. The score still says nothing about a collective, a memory stack, or a rack.

The Linux edition stops being the right instrument when the frames that matter live in DirectX, or in one of the three Windows traces this port leaves out. Those calls never enter the script, so they cannot show up in the result. The Teardown: When the Module Price Is the Allocation

A finished memory module does not record whether its die was bid against an accelerator. The choice is made at wafer start and in the back-end that turns a die into something a board can accept. High-bandwidth parts take advanced DRAM, stacking and test time. Ordinary DRAM and NAND still need cleanroom hours, probe capacity, substrates and module assembly from an industry already steering people and tools toward the higher-margin AI package. When that shared capacity tightens, the line that used to be a small item on a bill of materials becomes the line that sets the shelf price.

TechPowerUp, reporting Circana figures that analyst Mat Piscatella shared on Bluesky, measured the US console market for 2026 through August. Xbox hardware unit sales were down 33 percent from a year earlier. The average price paid for a new Xbox was 529 dollars. A new PlayStation averaged 597 dollars. Total hardware unit sales in August were 559,000, the lowest August total since 2013. Piscatella tied the slump to price hikes that followed the memory and storage shortage.

The mechanism is a queue. A fab books a wafer start well ahead of the shelf. A start aimed at a stacked DRAM die does not later become a console chip. A start that is standard DRAM or NAND still competes for testers, engineers and often the same substrate vendors the AI package is already loading. The device maker then meets two constraints at once: a higher contract price, and a shipment slot that can slip after that price is accepted. Unit sales fall when either constraint binds. The average selling price in the Circana data is what remains after the buyer who would not pay has already left the sample.

That pass-through stops clearing the market when the missing constraint is no longer price. If the module is simply not on the line — testers full, substrates late, NAND held for data-centre drives — a discount does not create a unit. Availability, not the sticker, is what then decides whether a launch weekend ships.

The Teardown: Why the Void Is Paid For Before It Is Seen

A hybrid-bonded stack does not fail like a wafer that can be scrapped at the lot. Value is already in the piece. Semiconductor Engineering describes advanced packaging as a place where cost accumulates layer by layer, component by component, and step by step, so that by the time chiplets, high-bandwidth memory stacks, or hybrid-bonded devices reach assembly, the manufacturer has already spent the material and the process time. An undetected defect at that point puts the larger assembly at risk, not a single die that never left the front end.

The defect list is not one optical problem. The same article names surface particles, cracks, embedded defects, microbump irregularities, and bonding voids, plus the need to measure bump height and surface profile. A particle on a bond pad and a void inside a bond are different failures, and a tool that only looks at the top surface will miss the second. High-sensitivity two-dimensional inspection is aimed at surface particles and pattern anomalies. High-speed infrared inspection is aimed at voids and embedded defects that stay hidden to a visible-light camera. Three-dimensional metrology is what measures microbump height and surface profile so the process engineer can tell a short bump from a dirty one.

That split is the mechanism. Hybrid bonding and fine-pitch bumps have pulled package tolerances toward the range historically associated with front-end fabrication, so the inspection problem is no longer "is the package cracked." It is whether a sidewall particle, a low-contrast defect, or a subsurface void is visible before the next layer is committed. Multi-angle illumination is described as a way to make sidewall and low-contrast defects show up when conventional brightfield or darkfield misses them. Electrical process control sits beside that optical stack: a later Semiconductor Engineering note on the same day frames the next process-control frontier as electrical as well as visual, because a bond that looks closed can still fail as a connection.

Sensitivity is constrained by the line, not by the microscope. If inspection throughput is too low, it becomes a bottleneck in a flow that is already capital-intensive. The design choice — catch the void with complementary optical, infrared, and height metrology before the stack is closed — stops being the right one when the only tools that can see the defect cannot keep up with the assembly rate, and the line either slows to the inspection step or ships assemblies whose internal bonds were never measured.

The Teardown: Why the Cell Loses a Track When p Sits on n

A standard-cell track is a wiring lane that sets how tall the cell is. Gate-all-around nanosheet transistors, in imec's roadmap as published by Semiconductor Engineering, scale through the A10 logic generation and there enable 5.5-track cells. From the A7 node, imec says industry may move to a complementary FET: pMOS stacked on nMOS, so the two polarities stop sitting side by side and the cell can drop to 4.5 tracks and below. Push that stack further and the same roadmap puts cells at 3 tracks, which imec says can carry classical CMOS logic out to at least the A3 node. The height cut is not free silicon. Contacts, gates, and power still have to reach the buried device, which is why imec's design-technology work treats a backside contact and a gate stack that can set more than one threshold voltage as CFET-specific modules. Part 2 of that work, the piece dated for this window, adds a sequential-CFET cell that can be built as split-gate or common-gate. Split-gate keeps the two devices independently controllable, which logic and SRAM both need; common-gate is the tighter option when the pair should switch together. The SRAM result in that study is a 5-track-plus-one CFET bit cell. A second study in the same article works on back-end-of-line routability, because a shorter cell only helps if the wires above it can still escape. At A7, imec also leaves another path open: CMOS 2.0, partitioning a system-on-chip into heterogeneous tiers and stitching them with 3D interconnects, with power from the front or the back if nanosheets are pushed instead. The stacked cell stops being the right next step when the buried device cannot get an independent gate and a backside contact without giving the track height back to extra routing.

The Teardown: Four Supplies for Eight Slots

A six-U chassis that holds eight full accelerators is a power problem before it is a compute problem. INNO3D’s announced AGS-6220V2, in TechPowerUp’s item on the product notice, pairs dual Intel Xeon 5th Gen Scalable processors and up to 32 DDR5 RDIMMs with eight PCIe Gen 5 x16 slots for NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPUs. It feeds that stack from four 2700 W redundant Titanium power supplies.

The slot and the supply do different jobs. A PCIe Gen 5 x16 link is the attachment for data and for a limited amount of slot power. A server GPU at a sustained training or high-batch inference load does not live on that slot budget. Board power has to come from the chassis rails. Eight of those boards in six U put the rails, the bus bars and the converters in the same short column of air as the GPUs, so the supply count is as much a specification as the slot count.

Titanium, on the 80 Plus ladder, is the efficiency band in which the supply turns the least of each incoming watt into heat in its own magnetics and switches. Six U leaves no spare volume for a lazy converter. Redundant, on a four-supply set, means the nameplate is not the continuous budget: the chassis is built so one module can drop and the others still hold the rail. The Teardown: When a Wafer Chain Goes Offline

A wafer-scale part is sold as one computer the size of a wafer. Chaining is the next claim: stitch many of those wafers into one logical machine so a single job can see all of them. Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis, writing on X on October 4, pushed back on a reading that treats such a chain as delivered and fully utilized. His point was narrow and physical: chaining dozens and dozens of wafers together brings quite a few offline and utilization issues.

The mechanism is a failure domain, not a brochure diagram. Each wafer is its own yield lottery. A conventional packaged chip throws out the bad die and ships the good ones. A wafer that is the product cannot do that as cleanly. Defects, a dead link, a power fault or a cooling fault take that wafer out of the chain. Once wafers are wired as one machine, the job scheduler either shrinks the job to the wafers still answering or waits on the missing slice. Waiting is idle silicon that still occupies power, network ports and floor space. Shrinking the job means the nameplate — dozens and dozens of wafers — is not the capacity a customer can book every hour.

Utilization then splits in two. Delivery utilization asks whether every wafer in the purchase order is on the floor and powered. Online utilization asks whether those wafers are in the same chain at the same time, with links healthy enough to pass traffic. A chain multiplies the second problem. One offline wafer does not merely subtract one wafer’s compute. It can force a rebalance across the remaining chain, and a second fault in another wafer can split the machine into islands that cannot see one another. Spares help only if they are already cabled, already cooled and already in the address map. A spare in a crate is not a spare in a chain.

That is why “all delivered and fully utilized” is a stronger claim than “wafers shipped.” Shipment counts wafers that left the dock. A chained system counts wafers that are up, linked and scheduled together. Patel’s objection is that the second number is the one that pays for the power and the building, and chaining dozens and dozens of wafers makes that second number harder to hold. The tradeoff flips when the job can be cut into pieces smaller than the chain, so one offline wafer costs one wafer, not a stall of the whole machine. Until the software can do that, the chain’s headline size and its usable size are different machines.

A joint that has to keep the last safe pose does not lose that pose in the neural network. It loses it in the log. Take the write path apart at the limb microcontroller, not at the vision computer. Semiconductor Engineering describes a zonal machine: a central compute core runs vision, model execution and motion planning, and a time-sensitive network pushes commands to microcontrollers in the limbs, the joint actuators and the battery system. Those edge controllers are picked for deterministic low latency and for power-loss persistence, so a brownout does not bring a joint back in an unknown state. The same piece says logging motor torque, velocity and limb position at sampling rates above 10 kilohertz can exceed the endurance of conventional non-volatile memory. The wear mechanism is block erase. Embedded flash does not rewrite a single sample in place. It erases a whole block, and a shift-long sample stream never pauses long enough for that block to be written once and left alone. Small log lines therefore become repeated erases. That is write amplification, and it spends the cell’s life on bookkeeping rather than on the sensor count. Memory sized for the vision workload does not stand in. The article separates high-bandwidth memory, used for large networks on the central processor, from latency-optimized memory on the distributed controllers. A wide inference path does not hold the last torque command through a power cut beside the motor driver. Goldman Sachs figures cited there put annual humanoid shipments at fewer than 15,000 units in 2025 and possibly more than 6 million by 2035. Endurance that looks acceptable on a demo stand is a fleet problem at the second figure. The tradeoff flips when logging stops being a continuous stream and becomes a checkpoint on a fault or a mode change. Block erases then fall back inside what ordinary flash is built to survive, and the high-endurance part on the limb is no longer what limits the design.

The Teardown: Moving the job instead of the megawatt

Carbon intensity on a data-centre feeder is an hourly quantity, not a nameplate on the building. Take the flexible queue apart and the lever is which work is allowed to wait. Data Center Knowledge describes carbon-aware computing as scheduling compute-intensive but time-flexible tasks when grid carbon intensity is lower, or when on-site renewable or stored energy is available. The tasks it names are data backups, batch analytics, AI model training, report generation, and non-urgent patching. The comparison is emissions from running the workload at one time or one place versus another, then the option that cuts emissions inside the rules the operator already has.

Operators have to know which jobs can move without breaking service-level agreements, latency requirements, data-residency constraints, or change-control policies. A backup, a batch analytics pass, a training run, a report, or a non-urgent patch might move. The grid side of that comparison moves because generation moves. Solar production peaks in daylight. Wind output varies by season and by hour. Some regions sit on geothermal, hydro, or nuclear baseload. Demand, transmission constraints, and market dispatch then change what is actually serving the next kilowatt-hour, so the emissions intensity of that kilowatt-hour can swing significantly inside a single day.

The policy that falls out of the comparison is a filter on incremental demand. Flexible load is pointed at cleaner windows so it does not force extra consumption in higher-emitting hours. The Teardown: When the Rack's Coolant Has to Change

Heat leaves the rack, or the accelerators throttle. Data Center Knowledge, citing Bill Kleyman, reports AI workloads driving typical rack densities from roughly 10–30 kilowatts into 80–150 kilowatts, with advanced deployments approaching 1 megawatt per rack.

Air cooling is a volume problem. Fans push room air across a heatsink; the air carries heat to a coil elsewhere in the building. The ceiling is flow. More flow means more pressure drop through the cabinet, more risk that hot-aisle air comes back to the inlet, and a point where the electrical feed and the airflow fail together. Another card in that rack then adds temperature, not useful work.

Liquid cooling changes the carrier, not the need to reject the heat. A pump moves water or a dielectric across a cold plate, or the board sits in a bath, and a coolant distribution unit hands the load to the building loop. A small liquid flow moves heat that would have required a large air flow. Kleyman tied that shift to AFCOM's State of the Data Center 2026 survey: 69 percent of respondents expect rack density to increase like nothing seen in the past, and liquid-cooling adoption is showing 36 percent growth.

The parts in the rack show why the old air budget breaks. Kleyman said a standard rack can hold six Nvidia DGX-class systems, each built around eight H100 accelerators, with individual accelerator cards costing up to $40,000. That population does not fit a 10–30 kilowatt cabinet as a full set. It starts to fit between 80 and 150 kilowatts. Near 1 megawatt per rack, the design is busbar plus coolant loop, not a deeper air plenum.

The tradeoff flips inside the old 10–30 kilowatt band. Pumps and plates then add leak paths without buying accelerators the air system could already feed. Once a deployment approaches 1 megawatt per rack, staying on air means leaving some of those $40,000 cards out, or asking the hall for airflow it cannot move.

The Teardown: CXL Memory Pooling in the MX1

Take it apart and the MX1 places RISC-V cores directly alongside DDR5 and SSD arrays connected through CXL links. This layout lets the cores perform local reductions and filtering before any data crosses the host PCIe bus. Each CXL-attached module therefore cuts the volume of traffic that would otherwise traverse the narrower host interface. The design also lets the SSDs serve as a slower but larger tier that the RISC-V cores can address without first staging everything in host DRAM. When the working set fits inside the pooled DDR5, latency stays comparable to local memory; once the set spills to the SSD tier, bandwidth drops but capacity scales without adding more host sockets. The tradeoff flips when the ratio of compute to data movement exceeds the CXL link rate, at which point the added cores no longer offset the extra hop.
