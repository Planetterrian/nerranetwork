The Teardown: One Laser, Many Wavelengths

Wavelength-division multiplexing puts several colours of light on one fibre so a data-centre link carries more traffic without another strand. The conventional build gives each colour its own laser, plus a driver and a control loop. Channel count and component count move together. Add a colour and you add another laser to power, tune, and watch fail.

An optical frequency comb breaks that pairing. One source produces many evenly spaced wavelengths. On a spectrum analyzer those lines look like the teeth of a comb. Data Center Knowledge reports that AI data centres are scaling toward 16 or more wavelengths per fibre, which is where a laser-per-channel build gets heavy: each added colour brings another laser, driver, and control loop, and with them more power, cost, and failure points.

Marcello Girardi, CEO and co-founder of Solinide, told Data Center Knowledge that four wavelengths is a solved problem and that arrays solve it well. He said the difficulty starts at eight, and that by 16 the component count is what limits you. His account of the comb is specific: one pump laser and one silicon nitride die produce all the wavelengths at once, on a grid set by the resonator itself, so the hardware does not grow as channels are added.

Frank Smyth, founder and CTO of Pilot Photonics, told Data Center Knowledge that comb lasers potentially pack many more wavelengths much tighter together without fear of interference. Spacing comes from the resonator, not from a rack of independently locked lasers drifting toward each other.

The same article draws a line on maturity. Comb sources have circulated in telecom research for years, but the co-packaged optics many of these designs envision remain closer to pilot than to volume production. That is the flip. At four wavelengths, an array already does the job, and a single pump plus still-pilot co-packaged optics is a harder bet. Toward 16 wavelengths per fibre, the array's part count — laser, driver, and lock loop per colour — is the constraint the comb is built to remove, provided the single source and its packaging actually reach volume. Source: [datacenterknowledge.com](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/networking/the-role-of-optical-frequency-comb-generators-in-ai-data-centers)

The Teardown: Through-Glass Vias in Glass Interposers

Take it apart and the bottleneck appears at the moment a femtosecond laser must drill a precise via through glass without cracking the surrounding material or leaving debris that blocks later copper plating. The laser fires pulses lasting only femtoseconds so heat does not propagate into the glass matrix, allowing the beam to ablate a clean cylindrical hole while the rest of the interposer stays intact. Litlit’s factory in Vilnius will produce up to 3,000 such lasers annually once it opens in October, a volume that directly limits how many advanced packages can receive through-glass vias each year. RayVen’s two-micron systems add the ability to focus inside silicon itself, turning the same short-pulse mechanism into a tool for backside structuring and stacked-chip signaling without chemical etch steps. Menhir’s 1.5-micron units target the synchronization role in co-packaged optics, where the laser acts as a timing reference rather than a drill, yet still relies on the same femtosecond stability to keep jitter low enough for 6G and AI fabric links. The global ultrafast-laser market sits at roughly $2.4 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach over $10 billion by the early 2030s, with semiconductor miniaturization cited as the leading driver. When demand spikes, the constraint flips from laser power to the number of field-deployed units that can run 24 hours without maintenance, which is exactly the qualification Litlit, RayVen, and Menhir are now racing to complete.

The Teardown: CoWoS Capacity Scaling Limits

CoWoS advanced packaging throughput is constrained by the number of usable interposer reticles that can be processed per month on 300 mm wafers and the subsequent TSV reveal and hybrid bonding steps. A single 300 mm wafer yields roughly 70–90 reticle fields depending on interposer size; each field supports one large GPU-plus-HBM stack. Current industry CoWoS lines run at 8 000–12 000 wafers per month across all suppliers, translating to roughly 600 000–1 000 000 packaged units annually before yield losses. Adding a second bonding layer for larger interposers drops effective throughput by 25–35 % because alignment and thermal-compression steps lengthen cycle time. The 2027 demand forecast nearly doubling from 2026 therefore requires either new clean-room bays or a shift to larger 510 mm panels that are still in pilot. Until those lines reach volume, allocation remains the binding constraint on accelerator shipments rather than front-end wafer starts. Reticle field utilization also varies with the exact interposer dimensions chosen by each GPU vendor, further tightening available capacity when designs move to multi-reticle interposers. Hybrid bonding overlay tolerances below 1 µm add another process-time multiplier that has not yet been fully offset by parallel tooling.
