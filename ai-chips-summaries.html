Sixteen months after working transistors, Rapidus still has no SRAM for 2027.

What You Need to Know: Sixteen months after Rapidus showed working two-nanometre transistors, it still has no functioning SRAM, and a 2027 mass-production start is at risk. Nvidia has now named a science accelerator, Rubin HPC, with more than 50,000 CUDA cores and a 2028 arrival. Treasury says older-chip rules need no further tightening, while a House tally counts 879,868 Nvidia and AMD chips already licensed toward Chinese-tied buyers.

Top Story

Rapidus has missed a summer target to show a working SRAM cell on its two-nanometre-class gate-all-around process, and Tom's Hardware, drawing on Bloomberg, reports that the government-backed startup has not produced functioning static random-access memory cells. The company did make test structures with gate-all-around transistors at its IIM-1 fab in Hokkaido in July 2025; chief executive Atsuyoshi Koikem told Hot Chips 2025 that on July 10 those transistors showed the expected electrical characteristics. A single working transistor is a much smaller circuit than a six-transistor SRAM bitcell with contacts, wordlines, bitlines and power rails, and SRAM arrays are how process teams usually expose defects and variability before a real processor will yield. The same report says Rapidus is also fighting contamination in its cleanroom. In a Japan Times interview, Rapidus said it was on track to mass-produce chips in the second half of fiscal 2027, which ends on March 31, 2028; earlier targets had been stated without separating fiscal years from calendar years. For scale, Tom's Hardware notes that TSMC's N2 showed more than 50 percent yield on a 256-megabit SRAM test chip in April 2023, more than 80 percent SRAM yield by April 2024, and over 90 percent average yield on that vehicle by May 2025, about half a year before mass production. Only Intel, TSMC and Samsung have begun mass production on nodes that use gate-all-around devices. Source: tomshardware.com

The Teardown: Charge on the Gate, Not on a Capacitor

A Z2RAM cell holds its bit as charge on the gate of a read transistor, and that read does not empty the charge. IEEE Spectrum describes the prototype from Nagare Labs, spun out of Georgia Tech by Suman Datta and Shimeng Yu. A conventional DRAM cell stores the bit as charge on a capacitor. The act of reading drains the capacitor, so the array must write the bit back before it can be trusted again. Datta puts that write-back loop at 40 to 50 nanoseconds. Z2RAM throws out the capacitor and uses two transistors. One writes. The other reads without disturbing what was stored, so read and write no longer share a single port the way they do when every read forces a restore. Charge sitting on the read transistor's gate also amplifies the signal, which means a smaller stored charge can still be sensed quickly. Datta's best-case estimate for that read is one to two nanoseconds. SRAM remains the faster cell, because it is built in the same front-end process as the processor it serves. It is also the expensive cell: every extra bit competes for area on a logic die, and the SRAM cell has proved hard to shrink.

The density argument is vertical. Datta says the memory transistors can be built with plasma-enhanced atomic layer deposition at less than 450 degrees Celsius, cool enough to add layers on a finished CMOS wafer without degrading it. Nagare's plan is to tape out the control circuits first on a mature logic process, build the array above them with multiple layers per lithography step, and bond the finished chip onto a processor as a chiplet. The first generation targets six layers and 185 megabits per square millimeter, about five times what SRAM achieves. Datta projects that the same approach could eventually stack up to 106 layers, which would put density at about 90 times SRAM. Take the near-term claim first. Six layers and five times SRAM is the number a customer could be asked to design around. The 90-times figure is not six layers repeated; it is the same cell carried to 106 layers, a stack more than seventeen times as tall as generation one, and the article does not publish a per-layer bit count a buyer could multiply alone. A patented metal shield inside each cell is there because the write transistor, when it switches off, couples into the stored charge and narrows the margin the read needs. The shield separates the two devices. It also makes each cell wider. Stacking is how Nagare expects to earn that width back.

This added layer stops being the right one on the package when the voltage that turns those oxide transistors on drifts under bias and temperature. Datta calls threshold-voltage stability the biggest challenge for this class of device. If that turn-on point moves, a stored bit is misread. The shield does not restore a threshold that walked, and another deposited layer copies the same film. Nagare is still tuning indium and gallium oxides, sometimes with zinc, and tungsten-doped indium oxide. There is also no agreed way to attach this memory to an AI chip; Datta's opening is only that high-bandwidth memory for accelerators is already moving from standard parts toward custom ones. Gelsinger's gate, before any of that attach work counts, is a device out of the university lab, a fabrication partner, and a demonstration on standard production wafers. Until a returned wafer holds its threshold, the one-to-two-nanosecond read remains a cell estimate, not a latency a scheduler can book. Source: spectrum.ieee.org

The Teardown: Reading another PC's memory over USB4

Remote Direct Memory Access lets a workload on one machine touch memory on another without the usual copy through the operating system. Microsoft has turned that path on for Windows 11 over USB4, so several PCs can be daisy-chained and share a job. TechPowerUp's account of the work is a latency measurement, not a bandwidth bake-off, and the number that matters is how long a message takes to go and come back.

USB4 is the pipe, rated at up to 80 gigabits per second. Ethernet can already run over that same cable, and a UDP packet on that Ethernet path is the comparison Microsoft rejected. UDP still climbs the network stack: the message is wrapped, queued, copied, and unwrapped on the far side before the other PC's memory is involved. RDMA is built so the transfer lands in the other machine's memory with the CPU on that machine mostly out of the way. The engineering bet is that the stack, not the copper, was the wait.

The worked example is two PCs and a sweep of message size. TechPowerUp reports round-trip latency of 28 to 50 microseconds for RDMA over USB4. The same sweep on Ethernet over USB4 using UDP landed between 116 and 194 microseconds. At the fast end, 28 against 116; at the slow end, 50 against 194. The outlet calls the RDMA path roughly four times faster. Those four bounds are the whole published result: two machines, one cable family, two protocols, latency versus message size. The illustration of DGX Spark boxes linked by Ethernet is explicitly not this USB4 setup, so it does not add a third data point.

Eighty gigabits per second is the other limit, and it does not show up in a microsecond measurement of a short message. A short message finishes before the pipe is full, so the 28-to-50-microsecond access is what the user feels, and the 116-to-194-microsecond UDP path is the tax of the stack. Stretch the message and the bytes have to occupy that 80-gigabit USB4 pipe for longer, while the protocol gap stays in the same band the measurement already drew. Daisy-chained Windows 11 PCs stop being the right fabric when the job is limited by how many bytes that 80-gigabit pipe can move, not by the 28-to-50-microsecond memory access — a wider network then finishes the step, and the fourfold latency win no longer decides it. Source: techpowerup.com

Top Story

Samsung’s named Chinese customers include Baidu’s Kunlun unit and ByteDance. TSMC supplies smartphone processors to Xiaomi, Oppo, and Vivo; an exemption that had covered chips designed for non-AI uses such as smartphones is gone, and an industry official predicted Commerce would likely approve smartphone chips while warning that the process could delay production. The Teardown: The Rack That Outgrew Ten Kilowatts

A GPU server changes the colocation decision at the electrical service, before lease rate or city enters the comparison. Data Center Knowledge sets the older test as location, how much infrastructure was going in, and how the operator expected to grow. Those questions remain. What moved is how much of the answer is now owned by power delivery, heat removal, the wait for a utility interconnection, and whether the accelerators stay busy enough to earn their cost.

The mechanism is density stacked on duty cycle. Power density is not a larger number on the same busbar. A rack that draws several times a conventional load needs feeders, breakers, and a cooling path sized for that rack, then a contract for the energy, then a utility willing to interconnect on a timeline the project can survive. Utilization sits on top of that physics. Accelerator servers cost enough that an idle machine is a capital loss, so a hall built for a peak and left dark between runs fails the return test even when the megawatts exist on paper.

Walk the figures Data Center Knowledge actually gives, as a planning case rather than a measured hall. The historical ceiling it states is no more than about 10 kilowatts for a typical rack. GPU-enabled servers, it says, push that figure higher, often by a factor of ten. Ten times about 10 kilowatts is about 100 kilowatts at the rack — the article’s multiplier applied to the article’s ceiling, not a vendor nameplate and not a utility filing. That product is the load the power train, the cooling plant, and the interconnection request have to be ready for if the “often” case is the one you design to. The second figure in the piece splits the fleet, not the wattage. If only 10 percent of planned servers will include GPUs, GPU-specific power and cooling need not decide the whole campus. The other share of the plan can still turn on cost structure, location, and room to expand. The article’s contrast is binary after that: once GPUs are a substantial portion of the environment, power and cooling become central. It does not publish the percentage at which “substantial” begins, so the worked line stops at the 10 percent case it does publish.

Duty cycle then chooses the owner of the hall. For training, where demand may be bursty rather than constant, Data Center Knowledge argues colocation can be the cheaper path: deploy in a building already equipped for high density rather than fund a GPU-ready private hall that sits idle for long stretches. For GPU-intensive work that runs continuously, such as large-scale inference, the private hall can win over time if the operator buys power more cheaply than a colocation provider that builds a markup into the power price. The higher upfront capital is then a bid to cut operating cost, not a bid to arrive sooner. Speed runs the other direction. A new private data centre can take years, longer if permitting slips, and a finished building can still wait on utility power. In many markets, the piece says, grid interconnection timelines are now a major constraint. A colocation provider that already holds power capacity, cooling, and staff can bring the same dense racks up sooner.

Sustainability follows the same pipe, because the accelerators consume far more power than conventional infrastructure and so raise the stakes on the mix behind the meter. Colocation can be the nearer path to renewables when the provider already holds renewable contracts, a low-carbon mix, or offsets. Matching that in a private hall is harder where renewable supply is limited or on-site generation is impractical. An aggressive sustainability target can therefore pick the landlord even when the long-run power price would have favoured owning the building.

The private build stops being the right choice when the accelerator share is large, the workload is bursty, and the interconnection is measured in years rather than in capacity a provider already holds. In that case the capital buys a dense hall that waits — on the utility, and on the next training run — while a colocation contract rents density that is already energized. The private hall fits again only when the GPUs run continuously and the operator’s own power price undercuts the provider’s markup by enough to repay those years of construction. The Teardown: The Recipe Space an Etcher Actually Has

An etch step is a point picked from RF power, chamber pressure, gas chemistry, temperature, and time, and moving one of those knobs changes more than one thing the metrology will later score. Critical dimension can tighten while a film somewhere else in the stack thins, or a profile that looked clean at one pressure bows once the incoming wafer is a little different. Semiconductor Engineering's account of SEMICON West 2026, drawing on Lam Research and Intel Foundry, treats that as a search problem before it is a machine-learning problem: the tool already exposes a space no engineer can walk by habit.

David Fried of Lam Research's Semiverse group described, on a panel, how a process engineer looking at a data set reaches for the region that worked a decade ago — lower pressure, higher power — because that is the bias the last fix left behind. The current Lam Research etcher he described has a potential recipe space on the order of 10²³ combinations. That is not a menu of twenty-three settings. It is the product of every knob the recipe can turn, each with its own legal range, multiplied out. A human team samples the neighborhoods it already trusts. An optimizer that is allowed to ignore that history can land in an operating region the team would not have opened, because the interactions were not visible in the plots they were used to reading.

Intel's side of the same problem, from Vikas Sharma of Intel Foundry R&D, is larger still and already has a clock on it. One process he discussed has 350 parameters and about 10⁷⁰⁰ theoretical combinations. Engineers working with the equipment suppliers spent about a year getting a minimally acceptable recipe for that tool and that layer, then kept working with limited success. After AI-assisted optimization they were finding better results within a few months, and more than one of those results beat the original baseline. The search did not stop at "better." Sharma's point, as reported, is that AI could identify thousands of candidates, and each one still had to be judged with silicon experiments that are expensive. Parameters in that space are nonlinear: a relationship that holds in one corner of the recipe does not hold in another, so a model tuned on the first corner mis-ranks the second.

Two recipes can hit the same performance point in development and then diverge once the line is real. Sharma's framing is stability, not the hero number: minor perturbations in incoming material or at the tool can drop performance sharply. Fried's separate caution is that there is no single AI method for every layer. Full physical simulation, surrogate models, machine learning, deep learning, large language models, and agentic setups each cover a different job — exploring conditions versus coordinating the engineering workflow — and the time is going into choosing which form addresses which workflow, not into declaring the search finished. Handing one model's recommendation to the next model imports the first model's assumptions whether or not anyone wrote them down.

Inspection is the place this already touches production, and advanced packaging is where the count gets physical. Some packages contain hundreds of thousands of interconnects, each a possible open or short. Automated optical inspection and X-ray collect the images; machine learning can separate shades a person reads as one gray. Ingu Yin Chang of ASE put that at a thousand shades of gray against the few a human eye sorts. Finding the shade is not the same as authorizing a process change. The candidate still has to be a defect, not a tolerable variation, before anyone moves a knob.

The design choice — let the optimizer nominate the recipe, then confirm it on wafers — stops being the right one when the nominated point is only stable inside the slice of incoming material and tool condition the model saw. Nonlinear regions mean a ranking earned in one corner does not travel. Thousands of candidates against a silicon budget means most of the 10²³, and essentially all of the 10⁷⁰⁰, will never be run. Authority to change the line belongs to the experiment that still fails the perturbation, not to the search that found the point. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: The Film Between the Resin and the Mold

Release film is the sheet that keeps epoxy off the tool. In compression molding for IC packaging, hot resin is pressed around the die and the substrate. If that resin wets the mold, the next shot is contaminated and the demold can tear the package. BigGo Finance, reporting chairman Yang Yun-pin’s pre-listing presentation, describes Suregiant’s film as the layer that isolates epoxy from the mold under high temperature and high pressure so the part releases clean.

The process choice is how the film is made. Yang said Japanese peers mostly extrude film. Suregiant coats it, drawing on more than 25 years of precision coating and formulation, from an optoelectronics start that entered semiconductors in 2014. Coating is what lets a supplier cut a custom sample for one customer’s automated line. The line cares about three outcomes: non-stick sheeting, no adhesive residue, and a release force that matches the tool. An extruded film can post the same force number and still fail if the surface chemistry is not the one that mold, resin and cycle were qualified against. The sample, not a catalog roll, is the product.

The package stack changed the mold face the film has to leave clean. Suregiant’s release film entered mass production for 2.5D advanced packaging in 2024, was applied to panel-level fan-out in 2025, and is advancing to 3D packaging in 2026. A 2.5D compression is wide and relatively flat. Fan-out on a panel moves more resin across a larger area. A 3D stack adds height and more interfaces that can trap residue. The Teardown: A Million Dollars a Megawatt in the Tank

Immersion cooling removes heat by putting the server in a dielectric liquid instead of blowing air across a heatsink. The fluid touches the board, so the thermal path is component to liquid to a heat exchanger, not component to air to a coil. In single-phase immersion the liquid stays liquid; pumps move it, and a heat exchanger dumps the heat elsewhere. Because the bath does the cooling, the hall no longer has to be designed around airflow, and the method uses no water for evaporative cooling. Data Center Knowledge notes that this also cuts some operational risk by removing fans and airflow dependencies, and that the technique has existed for decades. As of 2026 it is still not how major operators cool production halls. Headlines have been pilots, demonstrations and proofs of concept, including Microsoft’s experiment earlier this decade. Direct-to-chip liquid, which runs coolant in pipes beside the processor and leaves the server in a conventional rack, is the liquid method projected to grow.

The money is why the tank loses the bid. Data Center Knowledge cites a study putting immersion at about $1 million per megawatt of IT load, against $650,000 per megawatt for direct-to-chip. That is $350,000 more capital for every megawatt before a watt of IT is turned on. Operators trying to energize capacity quickly feel that gap on day one. The operating side looks better, and the article gives a worked cluster rather than a slogan. Adam Silva Consulting estimates that cooling a 64-rack AI cluster over 10 years costs $42 million with advanced air, $31 million with direct-to-chip liquid, and $28 million with single-phase immersion. Air to immersion is a $14 million difference across that decade. Direct-to-chip to immersion is a $3 million difference. The tank wins the 10-year cooling bill, and it wins by less against the liquid method operators can adopt without throwing out the rack. A $350,000-per-megawatt premium has to be earned back from that $3 million gap, plus whatever footprint and energy the bath saves. The article does not convert the 64 racks into megawatts, so the payback cannot be stated as a number of years; the comparison that is in the source is already enough to see the problem. Three million dollars of operating advantage against a capital premium measured per megawatt is a hard sell when the alternative keeps the cabinet, the lift, and the technician’s existing procedure.

The rest of the hesitation is how the building is run. Airflow stops being the constraint that sets row pitch and white-space layout, so the architecture can change, and the article argues it should. Servers come out of a tank, not a cabinet. Monitoring, service workflows and technician training all move with that. Direct-to-chip also changes design, but it preserves more of the server-and-rack habit, which is why incremental adoption is easier. The point at which the $1 million-per-megawatt tank stops losing to the $650,000 direct-to-chip install is the point at which heat density and water limits make a $3 million, 10-year edge on a 64-rack cluster, and a $14 million edge against advanced air, worth redrawing the hall and retraining the floor. Data Center Knowledge’s condition is concrete: if power-hungry training and inference keep expanding, and if pressure to cut water and energy rises with them, the bath gets more attractive. If that boom flattens before operators make the capital and the cultural commit, immersion stays where it is in 2026, a pilot beside a production hall still built for racks. The Teardown: The Needle Count on the Probe Card

A probe card is the last removable interconnect a wafer sees before dice are committed to a package. It holds a bed of probes that land on pads or microbumps, feed power and test patterns, and build the map of which dice are worth stacking. A larger logic die, a taller high-bandwidth memory stack, or a 2.5D or 3D assembly adds pads, contact force, and pattern length at that step, so the card gets more expensive and the touchdown takes longer even when the number of wafers does not. Yahoo Finance, tracing where investors might look once a single GPU vendor no longer anchors the hardware story, puts FormFactor on that test chokepoint and ACM Research on the plating tools that build the package the card has to qualify.

FormFactor's reported split is the worked example. Probe cards account for about 801.9 million dollars. Systems, the testers and handlers around those cards, account for about 178.2 million dollars. The interface that wears out against the wafer is the larger line. The analyst-consensus narrative in that piece says FormFactor's high-bandwidth-memory probe-card revenue guidance for the first half of 2026 sits more than 50 percent above the first half of 2025, and reads that gap as higher average selling prices and a larger test footprint, not merely as more units. On the tool side, ACM Research is credited with about 1.14 billion dollars of semiconductor equipment and services revenue, virtually all of it from mainland China, with production and evaluation orders already booked for panel-level electroplating and with advanced-packaging revenue growth described as triple-digit. The wafer engine those cards and tools serve is Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing's foundry segment, about 4.94 trillion New Taiwan dollars. An investor quoted in the piece, under the name steingar, frames the load as advanced logic demand that scales super-linearly with compute complexity rather than with unit volumes. Set against the FormFactor split, that is a test-time claim: if each package adds bumps and stacks, touchdowns and card prices rise faster than wafer starts, which is why a half-on-half jump of more than 50 percent in high-bandwidth-memory card guidance can outrun a matching jump in starts.

The same electrical idea explains why the card, not the handler frame, is where the money sits. Each high-bandwidth memory stack is a separate electrical citizen until a probe has touched it and the tester has decided it can be bonded. If probes cannot resolve contacts one from another, the card can still power the die and still lose the map of which contact is in trouble. Yahoo Finance does not publish needle counts or touchdown force. The dollars it does publish rank the steps. Probe cards at 801.9 million dollars against systems at 178.2 million dollars say the consumable interface is FormFactor's centre of gravity. A guidance line more than 50 percent higher for one half of high-bandwidth-memory cards says the mix inside that interface is shifting toward the harder wafers. Triple-digit advanced-packaging growth at ACM, on a 1.14 billion dollar base that is virtually all mainland China, says plating and panel work are scaling alongside the test step. The 4.94 trillion New Taiwan dollar foundry figure is the wafer volume both steps are trying to qualify and to build.

That choice — treating the probe card as the binding gate on an AI wafer — stops holding when the first-half 2026 card guidance, more than 50 percent above the year-earlier half, fails to print because packages stop adding bumps and stacks. Then the 801.9 million dollar probe-card line is no longer the scarce step, and the limit moves to the plating book, ACM's 1.14 billion dollars almost entirely in mainland China, or to the 4.94 trillion New Taiwan dollar foundry engine those cards were only qualifying. The Teardown: The Thread Count Left in the Weights

A model’s first numbers are not a smooth random field. On NVIDIA GPUs, PyTorch’s CUDA path fills those numbers with Philox, a counter-based generator, and it does the fill in parallel. Each thread owns a stream of values and writes them into fixed slots in the weight matrix. XenoSpectrum, reporting Will DePue’s Unemployment Labs write-up of November 12, 2026, walks through why the slot pattern is a hardware fingerprint: even with the same seed, a different total thread count stores the same random stream in different places.

For the large tensors in this check, PyTorch caps the launch at 2,048 threads per streaming multiprocessor. A Hopper GPU with 132 streaming multiprocessors therefore launches 270,336 threads. A B200 or B300, at 148 streaming multiprocessors, launches 303,104. A GB200 or GB300, at 152, launches 311,296. The stride between elements one thread handles is that total. Change the streaming-multiprocessor count and the arrangement inside the matrix changes, even though the generator and the seed did not.

Training then overwrites most of those values. Rows that never see a gradient can keep the initial bits. Rare tokens in an embedding layer are the usual survivors. Once a seed and a thread layout are inferred, the initial matrix can be regenerated and compared with the published weights, bit for bit when the match is exact. That is a test of the initialization configuration, not of every GPU that later ran gradient steps, and not of where the machine sat.

The worked case in the XenoSpectrum account is DeepSeek-V4-Flash against a 148-streaming-multiprocessor Blackwell layout. DePue reproduced 461 of 469 weight rows at a statistical significance of 1,385 sigma. Bojie Li, reimplementing the method without DePue’s code, reproduced 461 of 468 rows at 1,384 sigma. On a B300, 461 of 469 rows correlated at 0.99 or higher, and 445 of them matched bit for bit. On an H100, the same style of check reproduced 57,344 unupdated values of DeepSeek-V3 exactly. On a rented GB200, all 13 unupdated rows of Kimi K3 reproduced from the seed 20256586; shifting that seed by one found no matching rows. Across a search of about 6 trillion seed-and-layout combinations, chance matches were estimated to top out near 8 sigma, against detections of 49 to 1,385 sigma on the six reported models. Li’s GPU-type call matched DePue’s on all 65 models both examined.

The spacing test stops being the right instrument when the published file has no unupdated rows left to read, when initialization is not this Philox layout at 2,048 threads per streaming multiprocessor, or when the weights are closed. A later checkpoint that shares Kimi K3’s seed, as Kimi K3.1-Pro does, can also look like a fresh run when it is only more post-training on the same start. Source: xenospectrum.com

The Teardown: The Copy After Every Layer

A training step is arithmetic plus a copy, and the copy is what infra.bea.ai times when it asks whether a consumer GPU can stand in for a data-center accelerator. The guide puts an RTX 4090 at 82.6 teraflops of BF16, then draws three gaps compute does not close: 24 gigabytes of memory against 80, an interconnect of 32 gigabytes per second against 900, and no error-correcting memory where training expects it. Parameters, optimizer state and activations all have to live in that 24 gigabytes. The link has to move a slice of those numbers after every layer if the model is split across cards. PCI Express is the only path on a consumer board. There is no NVLink, so tensor-parallel all-reduce cannot hide inside a dedicated GPU fabric.

Take the guide’s 7-billion-parameter case, sharded across eight 4090s. With a micro-batch of one, a sequence length of 2048 and full checkpointing, activations are about 2 gigabytes and the running total is 18 gigabytes. Headroom is 6 gigabytes. That remainder is the budget for a longer sequence. At 4096 tokens the same card needs gradient checkpointing and a micro-batch of one. Extra sequence length is not a software preference. It is the memory that is left after weights and optimizer state have taken their share.

The interconnect numbers show why adding cards does not refill that budget. Tensor-parallel all-reduce has to finish after every layer. infra.bea.ai times it at 640 milliseconds on PCI Express and 22 milliseconds on NVLink. If the forward and backward pass takes 500 milliseconds, the PCI Express copy more than doubles the step. Fully sharded data parallel does not dodge the wire: an all-gather at the start of each layer and a reduce-scatter at the end add about 900 milliseconds on PCI Express to a 7-billion-parameter step that would spend about 32 milliseconds on the fast link. Eight cards therefore buy eight small memories and a bus that spends more time synchronizing than the arithmetic spends computing.

That design stops being the right one when either budget is gone. When sequence length consumes the 6 gigabytes of headroom, another 4090 does not create one larger address space. When the 640-millisecond all-reduce exceeds the 500-millisecond compute step, another card adds a participant to the copy, not a shorter step. The 82.6 teraflops still sit on the board. They wait on 32 gigabytes per second, and on 24 gigabytes that cannot see the other 24. Source: infra.bea.ai

The Teardown: The Voltage Where Delay Bends

Near threshold, a transistor sits so close to the voltage where it just begins to conduct that a small sag in the supply stretches delay much more than the same sag would on a normal rail. Semiconductor Engineering's November 12 account of edge-AI system-on-chip design places that regime at about 0.4 to 0.5 volts in advanced FinFET processes. Energy per operation falls as the rail falls. Dynamic energy tracks the square of voltage, so the same switching activity on a lower rail spends sharply less energy. What arrives with that saving is delay that grows rapidly, device-to-device variation that widens, and noise margins that shrink. Timing paths become sensitive to small voltage changes. IR drop and local voltage droop become first-order effects. A guard band drawn from nominal-voltage behaviour stops predicting the silicon, and models taken at the nominal voltage no longer describe the part.

Memory is often the floor under that rail. A single-rail memory with silicon-validated assist features can support operation down to about 600 millivolts, which lets the designer take the V-squared energy cut without splitting the power grid. A dual-rail memory separates the logic supply from the memory supply so compute can drop further while the array stays where it still holds state. Semiconductor Engineering points to an ISSCC 2025 result: a dual-rail SRAM at 38 megabits per square millimetre, logic at 380 millivolts and the memory rail at 540 millivolts, built in a 3-nanometre FinFET. Ultra-low-power register files hold local data next to compute. The split of jobs follows the block. Multiply-accumulate tiles want small, distributed SRAM. Activation units want those register files with wide data paths. Shared system buffers want a high-bandwidth AXI interface.

Logic libraries have to be built for the bend in the curve, not borrowed from a nominal-voltage deck. The account calls for moment-based LVF timing and noise models across a wide process-voltage-temperature range, delay and slew models that capture the nonlinear sensitivity near threshold, and extra checks for MIS effects, pulse-width integrity, skew interactions and mixed-device behaviour, because noise margins are thinner. Silicon validation, including extended timing and functional sweeps, is what keeps those models honest. I/O is the quiet veto on the whole plan. Control interfaces such as GPIO, I3C, I2C and SWI3S, high-speed links such as LVDS and RGMII, clock and reference inputs, SD and eMMC, test I/O, and 2.5D or 3D integration I/O for chiplets each have to run consistently across the supplies the core wants. If the periphery cannot, it sets the system minimum voltage, and the core's near-threshold work never appears in the product.

A closed loop among foundation IP, EDA tools and silicon feedback is what the account says makes operation around 0.4 to 0.5 volts predictable. Memories, cell libraries and I/Os have to be chosen and validated early. The near-threshold rail stops being the right choice when the memory or the I/O cannot follow the compute supply down, or when the extra delay and variation force guard bands that hand back the energy the lower voltage was meant to buy. In that case a dual-rail split, or a rail held above the sharp part of the delay curve, is what keeps a real-time edge workload inside its timing budget. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: When the Next Hall Leaves the Hub

Two-thirds of new US data center projects are targeting rural locations, Data Center Knowledge reports, while nearly nine-tenths of the halls already standing remain in developed areas. The installed base and the pipeline are no longer the same map.

Site selection used to stack three tests on top of each other: access to power, proximity to end users, and a high-performance network already in reach. Those tests explain the old concentration in or near cities, and in hubs such as Northern Virginia, Texas, and California. A rural parcel fails the old tests on paper. It sits farther from users, so latency rises, and both power and network gear may be less developed or simply farther away.

Power is the test that moved. Public grids, including grids in wealthy, highly developed regions, cannot always meet what a new data center asks for. Operators are exploring power they generate themselves — dedicated generation assets or an on-site plant. From that angle, a thin local grid stops being a veto. Distance from existing electrical infrastructure becomes something you build around, not a reason to discard the parcel. Land works the same direction: undeveloped urban parcels large enough for a hall are limited and expensive, while many rural markets are less tight. Fewer neighbors can also mean simpler permitting and less organized opposition.

Fiber does not get that waiver. A data center still needs high-performance network infrastructure, which in most cases means fiber-optic cable, and many rural areas have little of it. The cost comparison is what makes the split workable. Extending fiber is typically less expensive and less complex than building long-distance power infrastructure or developing major on-site generation. If fiber already exists within a few hundred miles of the proposed site, connecting to it can be economically viable. On-site generation covers the electrons; a few hundred miles of fiber covers the packets. Neither substitute covers the other.

The two-thirds figure is a pipeline count, not a census of what is already energized. Nearly nine-tenths of existing data centers are still in developed areas, so the industry is not moving the old halls. It is siting the next wave somewhere else. The idea itself is not new. Amazon explored data centers in Chile and Argentina more than eight years ago, and rural siting as a discussion goes back at least a decade. Hyperscalers are again looking at remote parts of South America. What changed is execution: over the past few years, operators have begun breaking ground on rural projects at a greater scale, pulled by power sourcing, land, and community response.

That design choice stops being the right one when the job cannot spend the distance. Halls that need ultra-low latency stay near users. Builds whose scale does not justify the infrastructure spend a remote site requires stay near a grid and a fiber route that already exist. If power, land, and local opposition stay tight, a larger share of the next large projects lands outside the old metro hubs. A workload whose round trip cannot spare those miles never makes that shortlist. Source: datacenterknowledge.com

The Teardown: The Insertion That Cannot See the Next Model

A test insertion used to be a cut the process and the physics already knew how to make. Semiconductor Engineering's account of where AI silicon is now starts from the opposite problem: the workload can change in the time it takes to get the chip out of the building, so the question is no longer only whether a contact is good. It is which slice of the build is still worth a tester hour once the algorithm that justified the die has already moved on.

The path is a chain of partial views. Wafer sort and singulated-die probe can sit at the fab, with that site's test program and its own record of failure mechanisms. The next assembly — a compute die, a stack of high-bandwidth memory, other chiplets, an interposer — is likely an OSAT job, on a different program, and the two data sets do not have to meet. Advantest's Scott Segura describes the physical reason the chain is getting longer: more active parts in the substrate, parts moved closer together, functional test creeping into what used to be a contact check, and performance probe at die level that now has to hold thermals. Known-good die, in that account, has to handle 3 kilowatts while it is still a singulated die. Silicon photonics, already, is five insertions. The substrate those dies land on is leaving the round wafer for a 310 by 310 millimetre panel, chosen so the tools can hold more dies, and that panel test is the step Teradyne's Jeorge Hutarte puts at the OSAT.

Walk the clock against that chain. ChatGPT arrived in November 2022. Widespread commercial use of agentic systems took slightly more than two years — about the time to design, verify, validate, ride a shuttle, package the part, and drop it into a working system. The design cycle those customers are trying to shrink is still 18 to 24 months, and some large chipmakers have told their EDA suppliers they want six. Even if the drawing board hit six months, the fab, the package, and the board would still be staring at a model generation that turned over inside the same window. Segura's customers already tape out, run the model or look at the end application, and then do not ship that chip. They ship a derivative, or a different version. Every insertion written for the first tapeout — the 3-kilowatt singulated probe, the five photonics stops, the panel-level functional test — was aimed at a part the lab may already have walked away from.

The cut stops being the right cut when the failure you still need to catch only exists after the compute die, the memory stack, the interconnect, the firmware, and the software are in one box, and the company holding that box cannot see the wafer-sort mechanisms from the company that probed the die. More insertions then buy contact confidence on a workload that has already left, while the combined behavior the field will actually run sits in a data set the previous insertion never receives. The Teardown: The Capacitor in the Wiring

A one-transistor, one-capacitor cell can sit in the metal stack above a processor instead of taking a die of its own. BigGo Finance describes that cell as back-end-of-line memory: the capacitor and its access transistor are built in the interconnect layers, not in the silicon where the logic transistors are drawn. The channel is an amorphous oxide semiconductor, deposited cold enough that the copper wiring underneath is not put through a process above 400 degrees Celsius. Conventional silicon transistors leak when they are off. The oxide channel's off-current is low enough, in the account BigGo Finance carries from the foundry's own presentation, to wipe out gate-induced drain leakage, which is what lets a capacitor hold charge without a six-transistor latch watching it.

That six-transistor latch is the SRAM bit cell. Six devices per bit is why cache eats area that could have been arithmetic. A capacitor cell uses one transistor and one capacitor. Spread across the wiring above a large processor, the same footprint holds more bits because the memory is no longer competing for the transistor layer. It is also not monolithic 3D DRAM in the usual sense, where memory cells are stacked vertically on a memory die. The array uses the fact that a processor die is typically much larger than a memory die, so the bits can spread out instead of climbing.

The test chip BigGo Finance cites, presented at the 2025 Symposium on VLSI Technology and Circuits, combines memory and logic on a single die and ran at 0.75 volts. It retained data beyond 128 milliseconds, finished random read and write cycles in under 8 nanoseconds, and withstood more than 10^14 program-and-erase cycles. Walk those in order. At 0.75 volts the array sits on a low logic-like rail, so the reported test did not need a separate high-voltage domain just to store a bit. Retention beyond 128 milliseconds means the cell held data for longer than a tenth of a second in that test; a designer still refreshes, but the leakage that forced SRAM's six transistors is the thing the oxide channel is there to cut. Under 8 nanoseconds is the random-cycle time the test chip showed, an access time, not a bandwidth for a stack of DRAM dies. More than 10^14 program-and-erase cycles is the endurance the presentation reported for that test chip.

Frederick Chen, quoted by BigGo Finance, puts the area argument in one comparison. Because the array can use the processor's larger footprint instead of a DRAM die's usually much smaller one, "It could be >10x," so the embedded array would not need as many layers as standalone monolithic 3D DRAM.

The same article records the foundry's public line. A spokesperson said TSMC focuses on its core business, dedicated IC foundry, and that its mission is "to be the trusted technology and capacity provider of the global logic IC industry for years to come." The statement never used the word DRAM. Chao Yu-chun, chair of Winbond Electronics, said a direct entry into conventional DRAM is unlikely, pointing to proprietary process technology and a tangled patent landscape, and noted that TSMC already works with memory makers including Samsung and Winbond on advanced packaging. An embedded layer, in the article's distinction, extends the foundry business. A full DRAM push would put the foundry in the merchant memory market beside Samsung Electronics and SK hynix.

This wiring-layer cell stops being the right substitute when the customer needs a merchant memory die with its own stack, sold beside the logic chip, rather than bits spread through the interconnect of a processor that is already large. If the logic die is not the bigger footprint Chen describes, the greater-than-ten-times area does not show up, and the layer count of a standalone 3D DRAM is back in the problem. An embedded capacitor also does not, on the figures reported, replace a high-bandwidth stack: the test chip's numbers are 0.75 volts, retention beyond 128 milliseconds, access under 8 nanoseconds, and endurance past 10^14 cycles, not a per-stack bandwidth. When the workload is that stack, the foundry's logic-capacity business and the memory makers' dies stay on opposite sides of the package. Source: finance.biggo.com

The Teardown: Ten Kilovolts, Then the Rack

A solid-state transformer takes medium-voltage alternating current and turns it into direct current for the rack in one box. In a 5-megawatt hall on the Galaxy campus, SemiAnalysis saw that box fed with 10-kilovolt alternating current and delivering 800-volt direct current. The stages it replaces are the ones a conventional hall stacks in a row: a low-voltage transformer, an uninterruptible power supply, and a rectifier. Each stage exists to change voltage or to clean it. Each stage also turns a slice of the incoming power into heat, and each one takes floor area, switchgear, and a maintenance window. Cutting them out shortens the path from the feeder to the server busbar and takes a whole electrical room out of the building programme.

Chinese data centres have been on some form of direct-current distribution for almost two decades, which is why an 800-volt solid-state step reads as a continuation rather than a first experiment. China Telecom ran 240-volt direct-current pilots in 2007. Alibaba's Panama power system came later on that same path. SemiAnalysis puts roughly a quarter of Chinese data centres built in the past decade on direct current, against about 97 percent of US data centres still on alternating-current uninterruptible supplies. The visitors note that this 800-volt architecture is still on the US roadmap, not in widespread American halls. An operator that already runs direct current at rack scale can treat the solid-state transformer as the next voltage step. An operator whose plant is still an AC UPS chain has to change the distribution practice, the protection scheme, and the spare-parts bench at the same time.

Walk the 5-megawatt hall as a power budget. Five megawatts of IT load has to cross from a 10-kilovolt feeder to an 800-volt bus. In the conventional chain that power would be stepped down, passed through or around a UPS, then rectified before it reached a rack power shelf. Three conversions, three sets of losses, three places a fault can open the path. The solid-state transformer does the voltage change and the AC-to-DC conversion together, so the hall's electrical plant is that transformer, the battery storage used to smooth the load, and the rack. The battery still matters because a wind-fed line does not hold a flat megawatt hour after hour. What disappears is the low-voltage transformer room and the UPS row that would otherwise sit between 10 kilovolts and the servers. Yield on that path is not a packaging yield. It is whether the medium-voltage gear holds its setpoint while the IT load swings, and whether a fault on one bus can be cleared without dropping the hall.

Cooling on the same campus uses one water loop for liquid-cooled and air-cooled servers, and it does so with no central chiller plant. That only works if the water the air-cooled gear can accept is water the climate can make. Nvidia has said Vera Rubin can run on 45-degree water without chillers. A US hall often keeps a chiller anyway, for air-cooled equipment and for the option to move load between the two cooling types. Galaxy drops the central plant and, SemiAnalysis writes, keeps much of that flexibility, in a design northern China has already run at scale. One loop means one water temperature for both kinds of server. The liquid-cooled machines can take warm water. The air-cooled machines set the ceiling, because their inlet has to stay inside what their fans and heat sinks can reject.

The shared loop fails its job the day air-cooled machines need inlet water colder than towers can make in that weather, because then either those machines run hot or a chiller has to pull the entire loop down. The 10-kilovolt solid-state step loses its reason when the utility handoff is already at low voltage, or when the site cannot maintain a medium-voltage direct-current bus with the practice that has been normal in Chinese halls since the 240-volt pilots of 2007. The Teardown: When Attention Is Sixty Percent of the Step

On a single Nvidia B200, the NVIDIA Technical Blog puts attention at about 60 percent of diffusion-transformer GPU kernel time for FLUX 3 Image Klein at 2048 by 2048 under NVFP4, with vanilla BF16 attention, for both the Base checkpoint and the distilled one. Generating the image is a loop. A text encoder, here Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, conditions the job. A diffusion transformer then refines a noisy latent, step after step, and an autoencoder turns the latent into pixels, or encodes a reference image when the job is an edit. The loop, not the final decode, is where the clock goes. Higher resolution makes the attention matrices larger, so the same step count costs more. Blackwell’s NVFP4 format cuts the weight and activation traffic of the matrix multiplies that feed that attention. TensorRT LLM VisualGen then fuses those multiplies with their epilogues, including SwiGLU and the query-key-value projections that also apply normalization and rotary embeddings, through FlashInfer PrimsTS kernels.

The blog’s own ladder shows how the gains stack, and where they stop stacking cleanly. Against uncompiled BF16 on one B200 at 2048 by 2048, the combined optimizations reach up to 3.46 times faster text-to-image and 4.45 times faster image-to-image for the Base model. NVFP4 alone, before those extra layers, is up to 1.62 times faster text-to-image and 1.58 times faster image-to-image than BF16. The latency table that follows uses NVFP4, vanilla attention, and torch.compile, averaged over 10 requests after two warmup requests. The Teardown: The Decoder Beside the Qubit

Error correction is a split job. The quantum chip holds a fragile state; a classical machine has to decide, continuously, which error just happened and which fix to apply before that state drifts. HPCwire’s account of the November 5, 2026 announcement from IBM, the Quantum Science Center at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and AMD describes a bench built for that split. IBM’s piece is the Nighthawk processor: 120 qubits, square-lattice connectivity, a high rate of circuits per second, and pulse control that can act while a circuit is still running. Oak Ridge’s piece is Umbra, powered by AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs and 5th Gen AMD EPYC CPUs. They are co-located so a team can score each error-correction method by qubits, time, samples, and classical cost.

On that quantum hardware a researcher can measure qubits mid-calculation and branch on the result immediately, set the timing of the pulses that guard the qubits, see what the machine actually ran, and work from the raw measurement signal. A code uses many noisy physical qubits to stand in for fewer logical qubits a scientist can trust. The classical decoder reads the syndrome — the pattern of those stabilizer checks — and names the correction. If the name arrives late, it refers to a state that has already moved. Umbra is there so that decoder can be tried on GPU nodes large enough to resemble the classical side a later machine would need, and so competing codes can be ranked by the classical burden they impose. The announcement is explicit that the open question is no longer whether error correction can work on quantum hardware, but how to accelerate and optimize it.

The figures in the HPCwire account separate a modest qubit step from a large operations step. IBM’s recorded experiments have reached up to 70 logical qubits and 438 hard operations. The Department of Energy’s Quantum Genesis mission, through its Q Competition, targets 100 logical qubits running 100,000 hard operations by late 2028. From 70 logical qubits to 100 is 30 more logical qubits, under half again the demonstrated count. From 438 hard operations to 100,000 is the gap the announcement calls roughly 230-fold. Closing that gap, the write-up says, is what the field still has to do. A decoder that keeps up for a few hundred operations has not shown it can keep up when each added operation is another round of measurement and another classical decision. Stretch the circuit by that roughly 230-fold factor and a decoder that was merely fast enough beside Nighthawk becomes the limit, even though the logical-qubit count only had to rise by 30.

This first pairing stops being the right instrument the day the code needs a 121st physical qubit, because 120 is all Nighthawk brings, or the day the question is 100,000 hard operations rather than 438. The announcement says next approaches need those pulse-level controls together on systems large enough to test real codes, and it lists the stack that has to arrive as one piece: concatenated codes, space-time codes, algorithm-aware decoders, hardware-aware compilation, and software orchestration. Until a larger quantum processor shares the bench, Umbra’s job is to price methods in qubits, time, samples, and classical resources, not to deliver the late-2028 operation count. A die-to-die wire inside an advanced package is not a shorter version of a board trace. Semiconductor Engineering, in a system-level signal-integrity piece by Muhammad Hassan, Mahmoud Mahany, and Mohamed Saleh, treats the channel as a stack of discontinuities: microbumps, through-silicon vias, redistribution layers, a silicon interposer or a silicon bridge, then the package transition into the next die. Each of those steps changes impedance and disturbs the return path. At multi-gigahertz rates the loss on those microscopic wires is dominated by skin effect, so insertion loss falls steeply and almost linearly with frequency. The same channel also changes character. At low frequency it behaves like a resistive-capacitive lump. At high frequency it behaves like a transmission line. That shift disperses the edge, spreads symbols into their neighbors, and shows up at the receiver pad as a shorter eye and more jitter.

Crosstalk, not the dielectric, is the louder noise source. Routing gaps of 0.5 micrometres to 2 micrometres let capacitive and inductive coupling inject more noise than the physical dielectric attenuates. The copper itself is on that same scale: traces of 0.5 to 2 micrometres, embedded in deposited silicon dioxide or a low-k film that the foundry has planarized flat, because any waviness misaligns the mask and snaps the wire. A conventional board, by contrast, tolerates thickness variation and uses coarse geometries above 50 micrometres. Checking the die, the interposer, and the package as three separate models misses the coupling that only exists when they are stacked.

Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express is the spec that turns that physics into two different electrical contracts. Semiconductor Engineering walks the split. Advanced packaging, the 2.5D interposer or bridge tier, is specified for an ultra-short reach of 2 millimetres or less, at up to 64 gigatransfers per second per pin under UCIe 3.0, with 64 single-ended mainband data lanes per module. Those signals are left unterminated at the receiver, and the energy target is 0.25 picojoules per bit. Standard packaging, the 2D laminate tier, stretches the channel to 25 millimetres, at 16 to 32 gigatransfers per second per pin, across 16 single-ended mainband lanes per module. The receiver uses 50-ohm parallel ground terminations to kill reflections off the longer trace, and the energy cost is 0.5 picojoules per bit. Physical-layer latency on the open spec is described as up to sub-2 nanoseconds. Compliance is not a lab courtesy: the extracted insertion loss, S21, has to stay strictly above a mandatory voltage-transfer-function mask.

Take the energy numbers as a worked budget, using only those published targets. One bit on the advanced-package path is budgeted at 0.25 picojoules. The same bit on the laminate path is budgeted at 0.5 picojoules, twice the energy, because the termination has to absorb the reflection the short unterminated link never builds up. A module on the advanced path also carries 64 mainband lanes against 16 on the standard path, four times the lane count, at a peak rate of 64 gigatransfers per second against a standard-package band of 16 to 32. The bargain is the reach. Two millimetres is the advanced-package ceiling in that description; 25 millimetres is what the terminated laminate is allowed to span. Stretch the unterminated, 0.25-picojoule link toward that longer run and the reflection the termination was invented to kill comes back, the eye that already lost height to the linear roll-off closes further, and the S21 curve fails the mask even if the arithmetic of the link still “works” on a short channel.

The unterminated advanced-package choice stops being the right electrical design once the physical span leaves that 2-millimetre reach, or once a bump map cannot keep aggressors inside the 0.5-to-2-micrometre gap regime without the coupling exceeding dielectric loss. At that point the designer is on the standard-package contract: fewer lanes, a lower transfer rate, a 50-ohm termination, and 0.5 picojoules per bit. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: The Rack That Boils at the Chip

A two-phase direct-to-chip loop picks heat up at the processor by letting liquid boil against a cold plate, then moves that vapor to a condenser, where it gives the heat up and returns as liquid. The phase change is the point. Sensible cooling has to raise the temperature of a flowing liquid to carry energy away. Boiling spends the same energy on latent heat, so a smaller mass of fluid can move a larger load, and the cold plate can sit closer to the silicon than a room full of air ever will. The condenser is still mandatory. Orbit or a data hall, the heat has not left the building until something on the other side of the loop rejects it. In a rack, that something is a coolant distribution unit tied to the facility water or air the building already has.

HPCwire's account of Accelsius's NeuCool IR150 puts that loop in one frame: a fully integrated IT rack plus CDU, which Accelsius calls the industry's first of that kind, offered as shared two-phase infrastructure rather than a chip demo on a bench. The company is a member of the NVIDIA Inception program. It is giving the rack to Villanova University for the National Science Foundation Center for Energy-Smart Electronic Systems, an industry-university cooperative research center. Villanova is one of three university sites in ES2. Accelsius already supports a separate COOLERCHIPS project at the University of Texas at Arlington, led by Dereje Agonafer. Villanova's Laboratory for Advanced Thermal and Fluid Systems is also on the NVIDIA-led OMNICOOL project under ARPA-E's COOLERCHIPS program, and on work with ES2 industry members. Google, Microsoft and Meta are among the industry partners backing ES2.

The worked target is the center's, not a measured result from this donation. ES2's stated objective is to improve the energy efficiency of U.S. data centers by 20 to 35 percent. By the center's own estimate, that is the equivalent of removing one to two power plants every three years. Read that as a program goal attached to a percent band, not as watts this rack has already saved. The IR150 is the instrument for the percent, not the proof of it. Richard Bonner, Accelsius's chief technology officer, framed the gift as research published with the manufacturers building AI infrastructure, not as a finished efficiency claim. Alfonso Ortega, ES2 site director at Villanova, said the donation gives researchers a rack-level view of two-phase direct-to-chip against rising thermal demand. Visitors are invited to the onsite lab. The article's install expectation, early October 2026, sits before the November 3 announcement date, so the useful fact is the partnership and the hardware class, not a calendar you can schedule against.

The integrated two-phase rack stops being the right install when the hall can hold junction temperature with the air or single-phase loop it already runs. The IR150 exists to put a boiling cold plate and a CDU in the same frame so a lab can see the rack, not the chip, as the thermal object. If the cabinet never approaches the load that forced ES2's 20 to 35 percent problem, that frame is a research tool parked in a room that did not need a phase change. Source: hpcwire.com

The Teardown: The Queue Outruns the Hall

New grid infrastructure takes five to fifteen years to build, while a data centre takes one to three, and that mismatch is now visible in how long a project sits between announcement and groundbreaking. Data Center Knowledge, drawing on GlobalData, reports that projects which broke ground between the third quarter of 2025 and the third quarter of 2026 waited an average of just over two years after they were announced. Projects that started construction between early 2020 and mid-2025 waited about eighteen months. The building did not suddenly take six extra months to pour. The clock that moved is the one that starts when a developer has a site and a customer story and ends when someone will actually energize a connection.

An interconnection is a reserved slice of a substation, a line, and a protection scheme, not a cable the tenant can order with the switchgear. Martina Raveni, senior analyst on GlobalData’s Strategic Intelligence team, told Data Center Knowledge the grid is the hardest constraint because it is structural: the line is a five-to-fifteen-year asset and the hall is a one-to-three-year asset. Luis Fernandes, IDC senior research manager for cloud and data centres, went further from his conversations with developers, designers and large operators. He said grid access is the dominant obstacle by a long shot, to the point that his team had not seen projects delayed for any other reason, because the absence of a connection halts the exercise. Other problems — labour, water, local votes — still exist. They only become the binding delay once a connection is real enough that the rest of the schedule can run.

Walk the published intervals against each other. Eighteen months was the older announcement-to-groundbreaking average. Just over two years is the average for the cohort that broke ground across the latest four reported quarters. That is a lengthening of roughly half a year on a clock that is still shorter than either build time in Raveni’s pair. A one-to-three-year hall that is waiting on a five-to-fifteen-year line is not late concrete. It is a project whose useful start date is the energization date of someone else’s asset. In Europe’s main data-centre hubs, Raveni said developers can wait up to a decade for a connection. Set that decade against a one-to-three-year building and the queue is several construction cycles long before the first rack lands. Skilled-labour shortages sit on top of that: insufficient electricians and other trades are pushing some delivery schedules back by around a year, a delay that only starts once the connection exists.

GlobalData’s demand figure shows why the queue does not clear by itself. Annual data-centre electricity consumption is forecast to rise from 590 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 1,520 terawatt-hours in 2030. That is the load the five-to-fifteen-year system is being asked to absorb while halls are still being promised on a one-to-three-year brochure. Investment does not compress a transmission build into a data-centre build. It bids for the connections that already have a date.

On-site gas is the design that looks like a way around the queue. Raveni said it can bridge a power gap, but redundancy requirements may force generating capacity substantially greater than the data centre’s normal needs. The bridge is then larger than the load it serves. Air pollution and water use from that extra plant feed the same local resistance — bills, water, noise, land — that Ya-Han Brownlee-Chen at DP World described as a growing barrier, and Joe Johnston at Xylem tied to power, water and cooling as one infrastructure problem. GlobalData lists closed-loop cooling, water stewardship and diversified power among operator responses, and calls progress uneven.

The gas bridge stops being the right plant when the temporary machines are still burning after the surrounding grid has moved the other way. Raveni’s line is that a temporary fix becomes permanent and pushes emissions up just as grids are decarbonizing. At that point the developer has not escaped the five-to-fifteen-year system. The developer has built a second power station whose size is set by redundancy, not by the IT load, and has inherited the permitting fight the interconnection queue was supposed to avoid. Fernandes, for his part, said there is still no hard evidence that compute capacity itself is limiting AI development. The dated constraint in this account is the connection, not the accelerator. Source: datacenterknowledge.com
