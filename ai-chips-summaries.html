The Teardown: Ten Kilovolts, Then the Rack

A solid-state transformer takes medium-voltage alternating current and turns it into direct current for the rack in one box. In a 5-megawatt hall on the Galaxy campus, SemiAnalysis saw that box fed with 10-kilovolt alternating current and delivering 800-volt direct current. The stages it replaces are the ones a conventional hall stacks in a row: a low-voltage transformer, an uninterruptible power supply, and a rectifier. Each stage exists to change voltage or to clean it. Each stage also turns a slice of the incoming power into heat, and each one takes floor area, switchgear, and a maintenance window. Cutting them out shortens the path from the feeder to the server busbar and takes a whole electrical room out of the building programme.

Chinese data centres have been on some form of direct-current distribution for almost two decades, which is why an 800-volt solid-state step reads as a continuation rather than a first experiment. China Telecom ran 240-volt direct-current pilots in 2007. Alibaba's Panama power system came later on that same path. SemiAnalysis puts roughly a quarter of Chinese data centres built in the past decade on direct current, against about 97 percent of US data centres still on alternating-current uninterruptible supplies. The visitors note that this 800-volt architecture is still on the US roadmap, not in widespread American halls. An operator that already runs direct current at rack scale can treat the solid-state transformer as the next voltage step. An operator whose plant is still an AC UPS chain has to change the distribution practice, the protection scheme, and the spare-parts bench at the same time.

Walk the 5-megawatt hall as a power budget. Five megawatts of IT load has to cross from a 10-kilovolt feeder to an 800-volt bus. In the conventional chain that power would be stepped down, passed through or around a UPS, then rectified before it reached a rack power shelf. Three conversions, three sets of losses, three places a fault can open the path. The solid-state transformer does the voltage change and the AC-to-DC conversion together, so the hall's electrical plant is that transformer, the battery storage used to smooth the load, and the rack. The battery still matters because a wind-fed line does not hold a flat megawatt hour after hour. What disappears is the low-voltage transformer room and the UPS row that would otherwise sit between 10 kilovolts and the servers. Yield on that path is not a packaging yield. It is whether the medium-voltage gear holds its setpoint while the IT load swings, and whether a fault on one bus can be cleared without dropping the hall.

Cooling on the same campus uses one water loop for liquid-cooled and air-cooled servers, and it does so with no central chiller plant. That only works if the water the air-cooled gear can accept is water the climate can make. Nvidia has said Vera Rubin can run on 45-degree water without chillers. A US hall often keeps a chiller anyway, for air-cooled equipment and for the option to move load between the two cooling types. Galaxy drops the central plant and, SemiAnalysis writes, keeps much of that flexibility, in a design northern China has already run at scale. One loop means one water temperature for both kinds of server. The liquid-cooled machines can take warm water. The air-cooled machines set the ceiling, because their inlet has to stay inside what their fans and heat sinks can reject.

The shared loop fails its job the day air-cooled machines need inlet water colder than towers can make in that weather, because then either those machines run hot or a chiller has to pull the entire loop down. The 10-kilovolt solid-state step loses its reason when the utility handoff is already at low voltage, or when the site cannot maintain a medium-voltage direct-current bus with the practice that has been normal in Chinese halls since the 240-volt pilots of 2007. Source: newsletter.semianalysis.com

The Teardown: When Attention Is Sixty Percent of the Step

On a single Nvidia B200, the NVIDIA Technical Blog puts attention at about 60 percent of diffusion-transformer GPU kernel time for FLUX 3 Image Klein at 2048 by 2048 under NVFP4, with vanilla BF16 attention, for both the Base checkpoint and the distilled one. Generating the image is a loop. A text encoder, here Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, conditions the job. A diffusion transformer then refines a noisy latent, step after step, and an autoencoder turns the latent into pixels, or encodes a reference image when the job is an edit. The loop, not the final decode, is where the clock goes. Higher resolution makes the attention matrices larger, so the same step count costs more. Blackwell’s NVFP4 format cuts the weight and activation traffic of the matrix multiplies that feed that attention. TensorRT LLM VisualGen then fuses those multiplies with their epilogues, including SwiGLU and the query-key-value projections that also apply normalization and rotary embeddings, through FlashInfer PrimsTS kernels.

The blog’s own ladder shows how the gains stack, and where they stop stacking cleanly. Against uncompiled BF16 on one B200 at 2048 by 2048, the combined optimizations reach up to 3.46 times faster text-to-image and 4.45 times faster image-to-image for the Base model. NVFP4 alone, before those extra layers, is up to 1.62 times faster text-to-image and 1.58 times faster image-to-image than BF16. The latency table that follows uses NVFP4, vanilla attention, and torch.compile, averaged over 10 requests after two warmup requests. Base runs 50 denoising steps with a classifier-free guidance scale of 3.5, which means a guided pass and an unguided pass. The distilled Klein runs 16 steps and drops guidance. That is why a format win and a step-count win are different levers: one shrinks each pass, the other deletes passes. Pinned NVFP4 PrimsTS kernels add only 1.02 to 1.10 times on text-to-image versus the autotuned TensorRT LLM NVFP4 GEMM, so kernel fusion is a small slice once the format is already NVFP4. Quantized attention is larger. VC-Attention FP8 with a repair budget of 0.005 reaches 1.18 times on both Base and distilled at 2048 by 2048, with LPIPS of 0.086 on Base and 0.151 on distilled. Sol-Attention at tau of 1.0 with 12 initial dense steps gives 1.30 times faster denoising plus decoding on Base; holding 16 dense steps for fidelity drops that to 1.24 times. Cache-DiT step caching nearly halves Base latency at both 1024 by 1024 and 2048 by 2048, and reaches 1.94 times at the higher resolution with an LPIPS of 0.033. Across eight B200 GPUs, classifier-free guidance parallelism and Ulysses parallelism cut median text-to-image latency from 14.90 seconds to 3.00 seconds on Base and from 2.73 seconds to 1.08 seconds on distilled, with denoising speedups of 6.01 times and 4.44 times. The model itself was trained on a GB200 NVL72.

The 12-step Sol-Attention schedule stops being the right schedule when the job needs the 16 dense steps that pull Base’s denoising-plus-decode gain from 1.30 times down to 1.24 times, or when an LPIPS of 0.151 on the distilled checkpoint, under the same 1.18 times VC-Attention setting that scores 0.086 on Base, is already past what the edit can accept. Source: developer.nvidia.com

The Teardown: The Decoder Beside the Qubit

Error correction is a split job. The quantum chip holds a fragile state; a classical machine has to decide, continuously, which error just happened and which fix to apply before that state drifts. HPCwire’s account of the November 5, 2026 announcement from IBM, the Quantum Science Center at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and AMD describes a bench built for that split. IBM’s piece is the Nighthawk processor: 120 qubits, square-lattice connectivity, a high rate of circuits per second, and pulse control that can act while a circuit is still running. Oak Ridge’s piece is Umbra, powered by AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs and 5th Gen AMD EPYC CPUs. They are co-located so a team can score each error-correction method by qubits, time, samples, and classical cost.

On that quantum hardware a researcher can measure qubits mid-calculation and branch on the result immediately, set the timing of the pulses that guard the qubits, see what the machine actually ran, and work from the raw measurement signal. A code uses many noisy physical qubits to stand in for fewer logical qubits a scientist can trust. The classical decoder reads the syndrome — the pattern of those stabilizer checks — and names the correction. If the name arrives late, it refers to a state that has already moved. Umbra is there so that decoder can be tried on GPU nodes large enough to resemble the classical side a later machine would need, and so competing codes can be ranked by the classical burden they impose. The announcement is explicit that the open question is no longer whether error correction can work on quantum hardware, but how to accelerate and optimize it.

The figures in the HPCwire account separate a modest qubit step from a large operations step. IBM’s recorded experiments have reached up to 70 logical qubits and 438 hard operations. The Department of Energy’s Quantum Genesis mission, through its Q Competition, targets 100 logical qubits running 100,000 hard operations by late 2028. From 70 logical qubits to 100 is 30 more logical qubits, under half again the demonstrated count. From 438 hard operations to 100,000 is the gap the announcement calls roughly 230-fold. Closing that gap, the write-up says, is what the field still has to do. A decoder that keeps up for a few hundred operations has not shown it can keep up when each added operation is another round of measurement and another classical decision. Stretch the circuit by that roughly 230-fold factor and a decoder that was merely fast enough beside Nighthawk becomes the limit, even though the logical-qubit count only had to rise by 30.

This first pairing stops being the right instrument the day the code needs a 121st physical qubit, because 120 is all Nighthawk brings, or the day the question is 100,000 hard operations rather than 438. The announcement says next approaches need those pulse-level controls together on systems large enough to test real codes, and it lists the stack that has to arrive as one piece: concatenated codes, space-time codes, algorithm-aware decoders, hardware-aware compilation, and software orchestration. Until a larger quantum processor shares the bench, Umbra’s job is to price methods in qubits, time, samples, and classical resources, not to deliver the late-2028 operation count. The Teardown: When the Unterminated Link Runs Out of Millimetres

A die-to-die wire inside an advanced package is not a shorter version of a board trace. Semiconductor Engineering, in a system-level signal-integrity piece by Muhammad Hassan, Mahmoud Mahany, and Mohamed Saleh, treats the channel as a stack of discontinuities: microbumps, through-silicon vias, redistribution layers, a silicon interposer or a silicon bridge, then the package transition into the next die. Each of those steps changes impedance and disturbs the return path. At multi-gigahertz rates the loss on those microscopic wires is dominated by skin effect, so insertion loss falls steeply and almost linearly with frequency. The same channel also changes character. At low frequency it behaves like a resistive-capacitive lump. At high frequency it behaves like a transmission line. That shift disperses the edge, spreads symbols into their neighbors, and shows up at the receiver pad as a shorter eye and more jitter.

Crosstalk, not the dielectric, is the louder noise source. Routing gaps of 0.5 micrometres to 2 micrometres let capacitive and inductive coupling inject more noise than the physical dielectric attenuates. The copper itself is on that same scale: traces of 0.5 to 2 micrometres, embedded in deposited silicon dioxide or a low-k film that the foundry has planarized flat, because any waviness misaligns the mask and snaps the wire. A conventional board, by contrast, tolerates thickness variation and uses coarse geometries above 50 micrometres. Checking the die, the interposer, and the package as three separate models misses the coupling that only exists when they are stacked.

Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express is the spec that turns that physics into two different electrical contracts. Semiconductor Engineering walks the split. Advanced packaging, the 2.5D interposer or bridge tier, is specified for an ultra-short reach of 2 millimetres or less, at up to 64 gigatransfers per second per pin under UCIe 3.0, with 64 single-ended mainband data lanes per module. Those signals are left unterminated at the receiver, and the energy target is 0.25 picojoules per bit. Standard packaging, the 2D laminate tier, stretches the channel to 25 millimetres, at 16 to 32 gigatransfers per second per pin, across 16 single-ended mainband lanes per module. The receiver uses 50-ohm parallel ground terminations to kill reflections off the longer trace, and the energy cost is 0.5 picojoules per bit. Physical-layer latency on the open spec is described as up to sub-2 nanoseconds. Compliance is not a lab courtesy: the extracted insertion loss, S21, has to stay strictly above a mandatory voltage-transfer-function mask.

Take the energy numbers as a worked budget, using only those published targets. One bit on the advanced-package path is budgeted at 0.25 picojoules. The same bit on the laminate path is budgeted at 0.5 picojoules, twice the energy, because the termination has to absorb the reflection the short unterminated link never builds up. A module on the advanced path also carries 64 mainband lanes against 16 on the standard path, four times the lane count, at a peak rate of 64 gigatransfers per second against a standard-package band of 16 to 32. The bargain is the reach. Two millimetres is the advanced-package ceiling in that description; 25 millimetres is what the terminated laminate is allowed to span. Stretch the unterminated, 0.25-picojoule link toward that longer run and the reflection the termination was invented to kill comes back, the eye that already lost height to the linear roll-off closes further, and the S21 curve fails the mask even if the arithmetic of the link still “works” on a short channel.

The unterminated advanced-package choice stops being the right electrical design once the physical span leaves that 2-millimetre reach, or once a bump map cannot keep aggressors inside the 0.5-to-2-micrometre gap regime without the coupling exceeding dielectric loss. At that point the designer is on the standard-package contract: fewer lanes, a lower transfer rate, a 50-ohm termination, and 0.5 picojoules per bit. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: The Rack That Boils at the Chip

A two-phase direct-to-chip loop picks heat up at the processor by letting liquid boil against a cold plate, then moves that vapor to a condenser, where it gives the heat up and returns as liquid. The phase change is the point. Sensible cooling has to raise the temperature of a flowing liquid to carry energy away. Boiling spends the same energy on latent heat, so a smaller mass of fluid can move a larger load, and the cold plate can sit closer to the silicon than a room full of air ever will. The condenser is still mandatory. Orbit or a data hall, the heat has not left the building until something on the other side of the loop rejects it. In a rack, that something is a coolant distribution unit tied to the facility water or air the building already has.

HPCwire's account of Accelsius's NeuCool IR150 puts that loop in one frame: a fully integrated IT rack plus CDU, which Accelsius calls the industry's first of that kind, offered as shared two-phase infrastructure rather than a chip demo on a bench. The company is a member of the NVIDIA Inception program. It is giving the rack to Villanova University for the National Science Foundation Center for Energy-Smart Electronic Systems, an industry-university cooperative research center. Villanova is one of three university sites in ES2. Accelsius already supports a separate COOLERCHIPS project at the University of Texas at Arlington, led by Dereje Agonafer. Villanova's Laboratory for Advanced Thermal and Fluid Systems is also on the NVIDIA-led OMNICOOL project under ARPA-E's COOLERCHIPS program, and on work with ES2 industry members. Google, Microsoft and Meta are among the industry partners backing ES2.

The worked target is the center's, not a measured result from this donation. ES2's stated objective is to improve the energy efficiency of U.S. data centers by 20 to 35 percent. By the center's own estimate, that is the equivalent of removing one to two power plants every three years. Read that as a program goal attached to a percent band, not as watts this rack has already saved. The IR150 is the instrument for the percent, not the proof of it. The integrated two-phase rack stops being the right install when the hall can hold junction temperature with the air or single-phase loop it already runs. The IR150 exists to put a boiling cold plate and a CDU in the same frame so a lab can see the rack, not the chip, as the thermal object. The Teardown: The Queue Outruns the Hall

New grid infrastructure takes five to fifteen years to build, while a data centre takes one to three, and that mismatch is now visible in how long a project sits between announcement and groundbreaking. Data Center Knowledge, drawing on GlobalData, reports that projects which broke ground between the third quarter of 2025 and the third quarter of 2026 waited an average of just over two years after they were announced. Projects that started construction between early 2020 and mid-2025 waited about eighteen months. The building did not suddenly take six extra months to pour. The clock that moved is the one that starts when a developer has a site and a customer story and ends when someone will actually energize a connection.

An interconnection is a reserved slice of a substation, a line, and a protection scheme, not a cable the tenant can order with the switchgear. Martina Raveni, senior analyst on GlobalData’s Strategic Intelligence team, told Data Center Knowledge the grid is the hardest constraint because it is structural: the line is a five-to-fifteen-year asset and the hall is a one-to-three-year asset. Luis Fernandes, IDC senior research manager for cloud and data centres, went further from his conversations with developers, designers and large operators. He said grid access is the dominant obstacle by a long shot, to the point that his team had not seen projects delayed for any other reason, because the absence of a connection halts the exercise. Other problems — labour, water, local votes — still exist. They only become the binding delay once a connection is real enough that the rest of the schedule can run.

Walk the published intervals against each other. Eighteen months was the older announcement-to-groundbreaking average. Just over two years is the average for the cohort that broke ground across the latest four reported quarters. That is a lengthening of roughly half a year on a clock that is still shorter than either build time in Raveni’s pair. A one-to-three-year hall that is waiting on a five-to-fifteen-year line is not late concrete. It is a project whose useful start date is the energization date of someone else’s asset. In Europe’s main data-centre hubs, Raveni said developers can wait up to a decade for a connection. Set that decade against a one-to-three-year building and the queue is several construction cycles long before the first rack lands. Skilled-labour shortages sit on top of that: insufficient electricians and other trades are pushing some delivery schedules back by around a year, a delay that only starts once the connection exists.

GlobalData’s demand figure shows why the queue does not clear by itself. Annual data-centre electricity consumption is forecast to rise from 590 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 1,520 terawatt-hours in 2030. That is the load the five-to-fifteen-year system is being asked to absorb while halls are still being promised on a one-to-three-year brochure. Investment does not compress a transmission build into a data-centre build. It bids for the connections that already have a date.

On-site gas is the design that looks like a way around the queue. Raveni said it can bridge a power gap, but redundancy requirements may force generating capacity substantially greater than the data centre’s normal needs. The bridge is then larger than the load it serves. Air pollution and water use from that extra plant feed the same local resistance — bills, water, noise, land — that Ya-Han Brownlee-Chen at DP World described as a growing barrier, and Joe Johnston at Xylem tied to power, water and cooling as one infrastructure problem. GlobalData lists closed-loop cooling, water stewardship and diversified power among operator responses, and calls progress uneven.

The gas bridge stops being the right plant when the temporary machines are still burning after the surrounding grid has moved the other way. Raveni’s line is that a temporary fix becomes permanent and pushes emissions up just as grids are decarbonizing. At that point the developer has not escaped the five-to-fifteen-year system. The developer has built a second power station whose size is set by redundancy, not by the IT load, and has inherited the permitting fight the interconnection queue was supposed to avoid. Fernandes, for his part, said there is still no hard evidence that compute capacity itself is limiting AI development. The dated constraint in this account is the connection, not the accelerator. The Teardown: When the Old Well Still Pumps

A token is the barrel. Each GPU generation is a well. EE Times, walking through a Gartner forecast and an a16z rental chart, treats the cost of that barrel as mostly energy plus whether the chip can do the job at all. Easy wells — newer silicon that finishes more work per watt — stay open when the token price falls. Hard wells shut first. Rent sitting above a depreciation line does not make the well profitable: power, cooling, networking, the building and the cost of borrowing still come out.

Gartner's arithmetic, as EE Times reports it, starts with providers spending close to $1 trillion on AI infrastructure in 2026 and another $6 trillion from 2027 through 2030. To pay for that spend, cover borrowing, and still earn a 12 percent return, the owners of those data centers would need about $8.5 trillion in revenue through 2030. Gartner expects the AI revenue that actually reaches those data centers to be $3.6 trillion at most. The gap is what Gartner calls more than $3.5 trillion of spending that could be exposed to write-downs by 2030. EE Times is careful: that is a risk estimate under Gartner's assumptions, not a prediction that the full amount will be written off. On the same arithmetic, more than 80 percent of neoclouds would no longer exist as independent companies.

Set that against the price of the work. Epoch AI, as cited in the piece, estimates that the price of reaching a given benchmark score has fallen by roughly 13 times a year since 2023, on the benchmarks it tracks. Cheaper tokens can pull in new jobs — the Jevons pattern, where a lower cost per use raises total use — but nothing in the chart guarantees that demand grows faster than the price falls. If use only doubles while the price of a scored result drops thirteenfold, revenue still shrinks.

The rental chart is the field log. a16z, using Ornn data as of October 19, drew a straight line from each Nvidia generation's first observed rent down to zero over six years, about the period hyperscalers use to write the servers off. Every generation was renting above that line. The H200 sat 143 percent above it. EE Times notes that most of the gap opened after April, when token demand took off, and that for most of their lives these chips had rented below the line. A busy well can still lose money once the power bill is paid.

The oil picture breaks where tokens are not the same crude. Interactive and agent work wants low latency and the newest memory. Overnight document work, synthetic data and evaluations mostly want a low cost per token and barely notice delay. A chip whose capital is already written off, with power, cooling and upkeep left as the main costs, can be the cheapest barrel for that second market. There is not one token price. There are at least two.

Keeping every generation online stops being the right call when a newer generation is arriving in volume and the token price has fallen far enough that an unpaid-off older chip's energy cost exceeds what even the latency-blind jobs will pay. Until that crossover, the expensive well still pumps, because the alternative is turning away work the new wells cannot yet absorb. Source: eetimes.com

The Teardown: A Word That Is Two Bytes

A PDP-11 memory transaction is a word, not a byte, and the lowest address bit is a lane select rather than part of the location the memory board is allowed to treat as a full address. Dave Plummer, building a replacement board because a megabyte of period memory for his machine was priced in too many zeros, ran into that rule the hard way. Tom's Hardware reports the project: a Q-bus card, a Lattice MachXO2 FPGA as the memory controller, four ISSI 1 MB chips, a U2S I/O chip, DIP switches, comm ports, and capacitors, tested on a backplane beside an M8190 CPU board.

The bus is 22 bits wide. The M8190 design reserves the top 256 KB for I/O and ROM, so not every address the 22 bits can name is RAM the CPU may use as main memory. Later PDP-11 models could take as much as four megabytes. Four 1 MB chips are four megabytes of physical storage on the card, which matches that ceiling on paper and then has to obey the word rule in the controller.

On a read, the CPU may want one byte, but the controller still has to return a whole word, two bytes. The low bit tells the CPU which lane, low byte or high byte, it will keep. On a write, the same bit has to be decoded before the store. The bus still carries a full word. If the controller writes both bytes, the lane the CPU did not mean to touch gets whatever else was on the bus. Plummer's first controller returned every second word. A commenter on the account joked that he had built 4 MB of memory that turned out to be 2 MB. That is the failure mode in one sentence: the array was sized for four megabytes, and the address decode treated the lane bit as a word address, so half the locations never participated in a legal transaction.

The fix sits in the FPGA, not in a respin of the ISSI chips. Reads always present both bytes. Writes mask to the selected byte and leave the other byte in the word alone. Plummer had not flash-programmed an FPGA before this board; that was a separate bring-up loop, and it is not the same bug. The lane bug is a protocol mismatch between a modern byte-oriented mental model and a 1970 machine whose memory bus never offered a byte strobe the way a later DRAM controller would.

He had already sidestepped the whole class of problem a week earlier with a QBone DEC interface that carries a Beaglebone Black and implements the memory in software on the far side of the bus. That works, and it is a different product: a single-board computer pretending to be Q-bus RAM, not four discrete megabyte chips behind a small FPGA. The discrete board is the right shape when the goal is a period-correct card that the M8190 can cycle as if the chips were of the era. It stops being the right shape when the controller cannot guarantee a byte lane mask on every write. At that point the software path on the QBone is the one that cannot corrupt the untouched half of the word, because the lane select is handled in code before any store, and the four ISSI chips are no longer in the path at all. The Teardown: The Motor That Cannot Just Swap the Magnet

A factory-automation motor stores part of its magnetic field in a permanent magnet, so the drive does not have to create that field from current on every cycle. The way to take it apart is to ask what the current, the steel, and the control loop have to replace if the magnet is no longer allowed to do that job. Asia Business Daily, translating Taiwan’s Economic Daily News, reports that TSMC has asked suppliers whether rare-earth-free motors could be used in factory automation equipment, and is putting components that use rare earths into its equipment supply-chain review.

Those magnets sit in the same class of material China restricted in April 2025: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium — seven elements. The report describes them as key inputs to high-performance permanent magnets used in electric vehicles, robots, and industrial automation, which is why a wafer fab’s handling and positioning motors are now on the same watch list as a robot joint. In October 2025 the controls were widened on paper toward more rare earths and toward permanent-magnet manufacturing technology. Some of those wider measures were postponed. The April 2025 list was not.

Swapping the magnet is not the design. The same report says efficiency, torque, control performance, and reliability all have to meet semiconductor-equipment requirements. A reluctance motor — the approach Shuo Yang is exploring for industrial equipment and for cooling systems in AI data centers — makes torque because a shaped rotor is pulled into the low-reluctance path of a rotating stator field. No rare-earth magnet, so none of those seven elements in the rotor. The stator still needs electrical steel, which is the materials path China Steel is pursuing, and a current waveform precise enough to keep torque smooth, which is why Fukuta’s motor-design work belongs in the same cluster as a drive specialist. In a tool bay, ripple and heat are process risks: a missed step or extra heat in the wrong enclosure is a defect, not a comfort complaint. That is the control-performance bar, and it is why the response is a materials company, a motor company, and a drive approach together, not a single magnet vendor.

The only capacity figures in the report are for a domestic materials line, not for a motor. The Industrial Technology Research Institute runs a rare-earth pilot line and, with government support, is moving a separate demonstration plant targeted for completion by the end of 2028, with annual production of 500 tons of rare earths by the end of 2030. The government plans to use that output to meet about 50 percent of the island’s domestic demand. At the stated 2030 rate, half of demand is still outside the new plant even if the tonnage lands on target. A design that still needs any of the seven April 2025 elements stays inside a licensing regime that is already live. The 500-ton plan does not retire that exposure; it only describes how much of a magnet-based chain Taiwan hopes to feed locally, and the stated share is about half.

The rare-earth-free motor stops being the right substitution when it cannot meet efficiency, torque, control performance, and reliability together on the tool. At that point the actuator stays on a permanent-magnet motor, and the supply plan is inventory plus a license, not a rotor redraw. The demonstration plant does not move that test. It only changes how much magnet material a design that failed the test could hope to source on the island. Source: asiae.co.kr

The Teardown: When the Night Shift Does Not Buy a Node

A process node does not move because the lab stays lit. It moves when someone can still tell a real signal from a measurement artefact, and that judgment does not stack the way wafer starts do. Wccftech’s account of TSMC co-COO and executive vice president Y.P. Chyn, speaking to students at National Cheng Kung University, is a rare on-the-record demolition of the hours theory of research.

The mechanism is simple once you separate the fab clock from the research clock. A production line gains output when a second shift loads more lots onto tools that already have a qualified recipe. Lithography, etch and deposition do not need the same engineer to be awake for every wafer. Development does. A new module is a chain of hypotheses: which film, which dose, which anneal, which defect is the limiter. The next experiment is only useful if the last one was interpreted. If the interpretation is wrong, the night crew does not buy time. It buys a second pile of wafers aimed at the wrong variable. Chyn’s line, as reported, is that research runs on brainpower, not labour, and that longer hours do not by themselves produce the result.

The worked example is the Night Owl program, from a period when TSMC was behind on process technology and trying to catch competitors. The theory inside the company was arithmetic. If TSMC’s research team worked 24 hours a day and US competitors worked only eight, TSMC would hold a time advantage and could overtake them. Twenty-four divided by eight is three. Three times the staffed hours, on that theory, should have been three times the learning rate. TSMC encouraged research engineers to take night development work so the organization would stay open around the clock. The plan was declared a failure within six months, half a year. The multiplier never showed up in the node. The missing term in the arithmetic was decision quality per hour, not hours per day. A tired team running the same mis-specified split does not converge faster. It converges on noise, and the morning team spends the next day unwinding it. That is why a 3x hours claim collapsed inside six months instead of compounding.

Chyn put the organizational repair at the 40-nanometre node. The old sequence was research finishes a process, then hands it to the fab. That handoff hit serious setbacks. The replacement, which TSMC calls One Team, is not a slogan about helping. It is shared ownership of the final result across departments. He gave two examples. First, when a new process moves from research into volume production, a longer overlap, with the fab in the development early, makes the ramp smoother. Second, when a fab hits a process problem, several teams on the root cause resolve it faster than one unit isolating the fault alone. The same rule is what he applies to fabs outside Taiwan: they cannot run as organizations fully cut off from Taiwan, and Taiwan teams have to carry some of the result. He also said getting departments to actually take that shared accountability takes a great deal of time and cannot be done with slogans.

The design choice stops being the right one when the work really is lot-limited rather than judgment-limited. A qualified recipe on a production tool, with the split already decided and the metrology already trusted, does gain from a second shift. Night Owl was the opposite case: an open research problem treated as if it were a loading problem. The moment the next wafer’s value depends on an interpretation that has not been made, adding the other sixteen hours of the day does not finish the node. It only keeps the lights on while the hypothesis stays wrong. Source: wccftech.com

Semiconductor Engineering carries a Siemens account of what that means once agents drive electronic design automation. A self-verifying agent can inspect its own work. A physics-validated workflow is the stricter object: every agent decision is confirmed against a deterministic, physics-based engine before the next step begins. The engine, not the model, is the authority on correctness. Tapeout does not grade on a curve. Silicon works or it does not, and a model that can discuss heat or timing does not relax that binary.

The check cannot wait for the end of the run, because misses compound. A workflow that reasons about physics and does not validate outputs against those engines adds risk at every autonomous step. The longer it runs without validation, the further a design can drift before an error surfaces. Across multiple tools, multiple physical domains, and hours without a person in the loop, unvalidated decisions stack. Continuous validation is what keeps an early miss from becoming the assumption the next tool is built on.

Three-dimensional integrated circuits are the case the account uses. Stacked active dies make heat removal and temperature interactions harder to predict, and the package can add mechanical stress. Electrical, power, thermal, and mechanical effects are tied together, so a change in one moves the others. Doing that by hand, tool by tool, creates engineering handoffs, slows iteration, and caps how many alternatives a team can try. AI factories, the piece notes, are asking for more compute, memory bandwidth, and thermal performance inside a tighter footprint, which is why chiplet 2.5D and 3D packages are carrying the scaling once a monolithic die runs out of room.

The worked example is a nine-stage electro-thermal workflow. MCP servers and skill files for the tools connect to Fuse, which aggregates them, and in this example Claude Code is the coding agent. The flow spans seven Siemens EDA tools and runs 100 to 200 agentic turns over more than 12 hours. Stages run from floor planning, library characterization, and power analysis through thermal signoff, system-level thermal simulation, parasitic extraction, and power- and temperature-aware SPICE simulation. Each stage is validated with signoff-grade physics solvers. The agent executes, reads the result, and keeps refining when a target is missed. It does not bank a stage and hope a final signoff catches it.

The sweep is what those hours are for. Teams can vary heat-transfer-coefficient boundaries, power budgets, and material stack-ups across 200 to 500 or more design points in a cycle. Five hundred divided by two hundred is 2.5, so the top of that stated range is two and a half times the bottom, inside one cycle. The account says the same machinery lets teams configure tools up to 20 times faster, cut engineering effort by up to 95 percent, and evaluate up to 50 times more design options than a conventional manual flow. The 20-times configuration speed is what makes a run of more than 12 hours and 100 to 200 turns able to touch hundreds of points instead of a handful of hand-built decks. The 50-times claim and the 200-to-500 point range are the article’s own measures of that wider search, not two labs reporting the same experiment.

This control stops being the right one when a stage is allowed to advance because the agent is satisfied and the solver speaks only after the twelve hours. At that point a search up to 50 times wider than a manual flow is also up to 50 times more ways for an early, unvalidated miss to propagate through seven tools, and the binary tapeout check arrives after the drift has already been designed in. The Teardown: A Molecule That Emits the Link

A spin that can be addressed one molecule at a time, and that emits a photon at a set optical frequency, is the part HPCwire’s account of NVision’s Nature paper treats as the missing connector. Until that interface existed, molecular spins could be interesting defects and still fail as qubits, because they could not be controlled individually and coherently. The paper, A Single-Molecule Spin-Photon Interface, is described as the first individually addressable organic molecular spin-photon interface. The molecule is a carbene. It is meant to be deposited as a thin film, the way OLED materials are deposited, straight onto a photonic integrated circuit, including HyperLight’s thin-film lithium niobate chiplets. Light, not a wire between neighboring dies, is the link that entangles a stationary spin with another spin on the same chip or on another chip.

That choice changes the floor plan. A monolithic quantum chip packs qubits until crosstalk, control wiring and heat set the spacing. NVision’s photonic integrated quantum circuit uses the optical link so the qubits do not have to sit in that tight neighborhood. The molecules run at 2 to 4 kelvin, not at the millikelvin stack many superconducting parts demand, and they emit at optical frequencies the photonic chip can take without a separate wavelength translation step. Chemistry is the design knob: memory time, gate speed and photon emission are tuned inside one molecule rather than by redrawing a silicon qubit cell. HPCwire reports the screen that produced the carbene: more than 150 candidate molecules evaluated, 40 of them synthesized in the lab.

The loss budget is the worked example, and it is why the foundry claim is not just a slogan. The platform is described as tolerating up to 70 percent photon loss and still operating on standard silicon photonics, instead of demanding an ultra-low-loss optical plant that most CMOS lines do not run. Take a link that throws away seven photons in every ten. If the interface still closes entanglement on the three that arrive, the interconnect can be a photonic chip a foundry already knows how to build, with HyperLight’s electro-optic lithium niobate sitting underneath a deposited molecular film. The alternative path in the same account is decades and multi-billion-dollar proprietary fabs for modalities that only scale inside a custom line. NVision’s commercial sketch puts the first integrated prototype chips in the fourth quarter of 2026, a live on-chip demonstration by the end of 2026, then a climb to 1,000 physical qubits and toward 1,000 logical, fault-tolerant qubits on chip by 2030, in a data-center form factor. Those dates are targets, not delivered systems.

The 70 percent figure is also the exit ramp. If a real photonic link loses more than that share of photons, standard silicon photonics stops being enough and the design is back in the specialized low-loss plant it was written to avoid. The same break appears if the thin-film deposit cannot keep the single-molecule addressability the Nature result showed in the lab, because a film that only works as an ensemble is not a qubit array a foundry can tile. And if the workload needs two-qubit gates that only exist when the spins share a neighborhood, the optical decoupling that removes crosstalk also removes the interaction, and proximity wins again. The Teardown: When the Advance Payment Buys the Slot

Wafer, high-bandwidth memory, a packaging substrate and a test socket can each stop an AI chip, so the delivery date is no longer set by the foundry handoff alone. 36Kr describes the older chain as a defined split: design houses specified the part, wafer foundries manufactured it, and packaging and test plants took the next step, joined by orders and shared standards. That split assumed each link could be booked after the one before it. AI parts broke the sequence because the performance ceiling is set together by the advanced process, the memory and the package. A larger die and a larger package also consume more wafer and more packaging area per chip, so a shortage in one input idles the others.

The wafer side is already spoken for. TrendForce estimated in March 2026 that global foundry revenue would rise 24.8 percent for the full year, to about $218.8 billion, and that TSMC’s 5-nanometer, 4-nanometer and more advanced capacity would stay fully loaded through the end of 2026. 36Kr says that load is Nvidia and AMD GPUs plus custom chips from Google, AWS and Meta. A full advanced-node line does not finish the accelerator if the package, the substrate or the test insertion is late. The scarce object stops being a wafer start and becomes a reserved place in every downstream tool.

The reservation is now paid before the tools exist. In July 2026 Amkor signed a $1.5 billion multi-year advanced-packaging and development agreement with Nvidia, and Nvidia’s advance payment was to support Amkor’s US capacity build. On October 21 the two added a South Korea expansion; 36Kr puts the US and Korea agreements together at $3 billion. Test follows the same pattern. Nvidia provided $1.1 billion to King Yuan Electronics as a capacity-reservation advance, funding a US test factory and the equipment to run it. On substrates, the October 21 arrangements with Ibiden, Samsung Electro-Mechanics and XinXing Electronics are planned to take the relevant substrate capacity to 2.5 times the current level, and Ibiden’s expansion of its Ono plant in Japan is also backed by an Nvidia advance. Cash plus a multi-year volume promise moves a supplier’s build plan earlier than a spot order would. The supplier gets a demand signal it can borrow against; the customer gets a claim on capacity that a later purchase order might not secure.

That choice stops being the right way to buy a chip when the check lands on a link that is not the late one. 36Kr notes that 2.5D packaging of the CoWoS type has been tight since 2023 and that the squeeze has spread to packaging equipment, substrates and key materials, so more wafers, or even a larger packaging house, still leave delivery gated by whichever input did not get the money. It also stops working for anyone but the largest buyers once the line is allocated by who can write the advance. 36Kr’s warning is polarization: firms with capital and the ability to commit long orders take scarce capacity first. A reservation that clears the bottleneck only for those buyers fails the moment the missing part is a tool, a substrate or a material the prepaid supplier cannot pull forward, and it fails the buyer who needs the chip but cannot fund the factory that has to build it. The Teardown: Useful Life in Vacuum

In vacuum, convective cooling does not exist. Heat leaving a processor has to be conducted or pumped to an outer surface, and that surface has to radiate the energy away. EE Times, writing on Google's Project Suncatcher, treats the radiator, the solar arrays, the power distribution and the years the hardware must survive as part of the processor's real performance, because a failed board in orbit is not swapped at the next maintenance window.

On October 1, 2026, a Project Suncatcher prototype reached orbit aboard a SpaceX mission. Developed with Planet, the satellite established contact with the ground. Its task is to test how commercial AI hardware behaves where replacement is not available. Before launch, Google subjected Trillium TPUs to vibration and proton-beam tests and reported encouraging tolerance to accumulated ionizing radiation, while still investigating errors that arise during AI workloads. It also tested a thermal system of heat pipes and radiators in simulated vacuum. Laboratory exposure is an initial indication of resilience. The flight still has to establish the rate of faults, and what those faults do to sustained output, over years.

The plant required to turn that experiment into something like a data center shows up in a model, not in the satellite now on orbit. An April 2026 study by Slava Turyshev, as EE Times recounts it, examined a representative one-megawatt installation and required approximately 5,640 square meters of photovoltaic arrays and 2,500 square meters of radiators. Those are model outputs, dependent on assumptions about solar generation and heat rejection. They are the physical scale of one megawatt, still small beside a terrestrial hyperscale hall, and every square meter is mass that has to be launched. A spacecraft also needs electrical generation and distribution, provision for periods of insufficient sunlight, and data links both between satellites and back to Earth. Each kilogram lands in the launch budget.

The processor sets part of that mass. A faster accelerator that draws more power can require larger solar arrays, more electrical distribution equipment and more radiating surface. Those supporting pieces add mass and can offset the benefit of higher throughput. Software efficiency, error rates, utilization and expected service life all move the comparison between two chips with similar benchmark scores. Take two architectures on the same workload. One completes more operations per second and needs a larger thermal system. The other is slower, draws less power, and allows a smaller spacecraft that may fly a longer mission. The useful comparison is total computation completed over the mission, adjusted for interruptions, degraded operation and the chance of premature failure. A processor that wins the initial benchmark can still deliver fewer productive hours before the spacecraft is retired. For an operator that cannot replace a failed board, that lost lifetime output is a direct cost.

Turyshev's model also examines launch cost, spacecraft construction, utilization and operating life. Under one set of assumptions, the combined cost of launch and spacecraft construction would have to fall below a benchmark based on the published price of a dedicated Falcon 9 launch for the modeled system to compete. That comparison is sensitive to the launch configuration chosen, and it says little about future launch prices. The U.S. Government Accountability Office, in an April assessment, identified cooling, communications and deployment economics as unresolved. Processing observations near the satellites that collect them is the earlier use case, because less raw data might need to be sent to Earth. A general-purpose orbital data center faces the larger infrastructure problem. Communications sit beside power and heat: a distributed workload that moves data often between processors makes the network part of the system cost, and a functioning accelerator does not by itself make the power, thermal and link systems work together at commercial scale.

NASA's High-Performance Spaceflight Computing processor, developed with Microchip Technology, is specified for spacecraft workloads that need autonomy, radiation resilience, power management and fault tolerance. In May 2026, NASA reported preliminary testing that suggested performance roughly 500 times that of radiation-hardened processors then in use. The same lifetime arithmetic applies: throughput only counts for the hours the machine keeps producing.

This design choice stops being the right one when the extra array and radiator a hotter accelerator demands consume the mission mass budget before those extra operations are delivered, or when the job is only local reduction of sensor data and never required a megawatt of general-purpose compute in orbit. The Teardown: Why Backside Power Lets One Exposure Finish the Pitch

High numerical aperture extreme ultraviolet lithography raises the resolution of the light on a wafer until the printable pitch, the space between two features, sits between 16 nanometres and 8 nanometres. Wccftech's account of Intel vice president Chris Auth, speaking on the Dwarkesh podcast, is that a pitch in that window can be printed in a single exposure. One pass is the cost argument: one mask, one alignment, one dose. When a design needs a tighter pitch than one exposure can hold, a foundry prints the pattern in stages. Each stage only has to hit a looser pitch. The exposures together make the dense pattern. That second exposure is double-patterning EUV. It spends a second pass, a second mask, and an overlay budget between the two.

Backside power delivery is how Auth says Intel stays inside the single-pass window. Power wires move off the front of the wafer and onto the back. The front, where the computing transistors sit and where the circuits that move data between them sit, no longer shares that space with the power grid. Auth's claim is that this relaxes the front-side pitch enough to print that level directly with high numerical aperture tools. A looser pitch also relaxes the error tolerance, because features do not have to land as close together. He contrasts that with TSMC, which he says must run tighter pitches because it does not have backside power, cannot print that layer with high numerical aperture in one pass, and has to use double-patterning EUV.

Take those pitch numbers as a gate. If the front side still needs a pitch between 16 nanometres and 8 nanometres, Auth's account says one high numerical aperture exposure can print it. If the pitch has to be tighter than 8 nanometres, that single exposure is outside its window and the flow falls back to two EUV passes. Intel's bet is that moving power to the back keeps the front-side pitch inside the 16-to-8 window, so the second pass never starts. Auth added that 14A is an option in the process design kit today, handled customer by customer. That is narrower than a general release. Earlier reports, which his comments are described as moving past, had discussed backside power on 18A. The scanner choice and the node name are separate: in his telling, high numerical aperture pays only where backside power has already bought the pitch relief.

Chief technology officer Pushkar Ranade, on the same podcast, separates that lithography claim from scale. He said TSMC is a much larger wafer manufacturer today, and that catching up in scale will be slow and gradual. On technology he said Intel is already there, maybe a few percent off on performance or power or area density. A gap of a few percent, he argued, is a different problem from being a whole generation behind. The condition he put first is time and yield. You can be off by a few percent, but you need to be on time, and yields have to be very high to support very large and very rapid ramps. A one-pass exposure that prints inside 8 to 16 nanometres still loses the ramp if yield cannot fill a fast volume curve while the larger foundry is already shipping.

A second failure mode sits in the transistor stack, not in the scanner. Wccftech quotes Intel's Jack Kavalieros on stacking n-type and p-type transistors, the approach IBM has tied to a 0.7-nanometre plan. Connections that used to run on the top, or on the bottom, now have to go vertically between the transistors. The trenches that carry those connections get taller relative to their width. Filling them gets harder. Unfilled trenches leave air gaps and holes. Backside power can free the front side for a single high numerical aperture pass and still be the wrong process once those vertical interconnects, not the in-plane pitch, set the yield. The Teardown: When the Fourth Mask Loses the Exposure

A dense layer does not have to be drawn in a single pass. Break the pattern into simpler pieces, expose each piece on a deep-ultraviolet scanner, and the stack can approximate a feature that one shot of that light cannot print. Tom's Hardware put that workaround to Chris Mack, co-founder and chief technology officer of Fractilia, because export rules have kept ASML’s extreme-ultraviolet tools out of China. Those machines print with 13.5-nanometre light. Without them, the path left is older deep-ultraviolet lithography plus multiple patterning.

What that path has already printed is in shipping phones. A teardown of Huawei’s Mate 60 Pro found the Kirin 9000S made by SMIC on a second-generation 7-nanometre-class process with no EUV. Later analysis of the Kirin 9030 found SMIC’s DUV flow pushed closer to densities associated with 5-nanometre manufacturing, while still lagging the leading processes at TSMC and Samsung. Mack’s point is that a node label hides the cost of getting there. Every added exposure has to land on patterns already etched. More masks mean more overlay error and more process variation. Designers buy a printable layer by leaving extra space around features. The die grows. A larger die means fewer candidates on each wafer, and wafers cost tens of thousands of dollars. The thing sold is the good die, not the wafer.

Mack’s cost line is the worked case. He told Tom's Hardware that industry convention held three exposures on an immersion tool at about the same cost as one EUV exposure. At four masks standing in for that single EUV exposure, EUV becomes the cheaper shot, and the cleaner one, because four overlay stacks accumulate defects one exposure never introduces. Memory shows the same gap in a June Morgan Stanley estimate: CXMT was producing only about one-third as many bits per wafer as Western competitors. Analysts put only about a third of that shortfall on the older process node and called lithography access a hard ceiling. Micron’s 1-alpha DRAM, built without EUV, is the counter-example — competitive chips from deep-ultraviolet light, multiple patterning and tight control, while Samsung and SK hynix used many EUV layers for the same generation. Douglas Fuller of Copenhagen Business School told Tom's Hardware that other routes around EUV for advanced logic are unlikely to be efficient, and that by the end of the decade advanced packaging could still let DUV print the latest DRAM generations.

Four masks against one EUV exposure is where this choice stops paying for a logic layer. Overlay error, a fatter die and fewer good dice per wafer then dominate the scanner that was never delivered. Source: tomshardware.com

The Teardown: When the Panel Gives the Count Back

A large AI package is a rectangle, and the workpiece under it has spent decades as a circle. Semiconductor Engineering’s account of the wafer-to-panel shift, drawing on ASE and Intel Foundry engineers, starts from that mismatch. As the package grows, more of a 300-millimetre wafer’s process time lands outside the circle of usable silicon. A panel gives the corners back. It also exposes how much of “the process” was really a habit of the round part: spin, stepper, single-sided handling, a hot plate sized for a wafer.

Teck Chong Lee of ASE put the practical limit in one flow. Running assembly on a 600-millimetre panel is high risk, and the equipment to do the whole job is not all in place. One option is to finish the interposer at 600 millimetres, then cut it into four pieces before assembly and the C4 bump process. The area win is taken where the tools exist, then partly handed back where they do not.

The same study counts a 5.5-times-reticle interposer. A 300-millimetre wafer yields 9. A 300-millimetre panel yields 16. A 600-millimetre panel divided into four 300-millimetre pieces yields 64. Leaving that 600-millimetre panel intact yields 72. Seventy-two against nine is eight times the wafer, more than the roughly fivefold gap in raw area, because the rectangle wastes almost nothing at the rim. Intel’s Tarik Ibrahim, in a conference presentation covered in the same piece, used a 510-by-515-millimetre panel: a package of roughly four times a reticle field is about 10 units on a 300-millimetre wafer and approximately 48 on the panel. The largest square that fits the wafer is only about 210 by 210 millimetres, so past that outline the wafer is not merely less economical. It cannot hold the part.

What breaks is not the arithmetic. At wafer level the process is single-sided. On a panel it is dual-sided, and uniformity and process capability have to hold across a sheet the tools were not born for. Spin coating needs radial symmetry; on a rectangle the film thins toward the corners and most of the resist is thrown off. In a 600-millimetre flow, photoresist goes down by slit or slot-die coating across an active area of roughly 586 by 586 millimetres. Lithography often leaves the wafer stepper for maskless direct imaging. Fine-line redistribution on glass is being pushed from roughly 5 micrometres line and space toward 2 micrometres and below, which asks for something closer to front-end overlay and temperature control. Front-end tools that already do that precision still assume a round 300-millimetre wafer. Older substrate tools were built for coarser features. The panel sits between those two equipment worlds, and neither hands over a complete line.

Ibrahim’s split is the decision rule. For some form factors the wafer is still the cheaper workpiece, because the panel’s extra area does not yet buy extra good units after yield, handling and tool qualification. Past a size the presentation does not pin to a single number, the panel wins because the unit count jumps — 10 to about 48 in Intel’s four-reticle example, 9 to 72 if a 5.5-reticle interposer can stay intact. The intact panel stops being the right workpiece at the step that has no qualified tool. ASE’s four-way cut is that step: assembly and C4. Seventy-two falls to 64, and the rectangles you spent the interposer module earning are sawn back into pieces the existing line can hold. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: Why the Fiber Array Cannot Touch the Die

Co-packaged optics does not become a volume product when the photonic die lights up on a bench. It becomes a volume product when a factory can attach light and electricity at the same time, on every insertion, without destroying the only connector the module will ever trust. Semiconductor Engineering's panel on scaling co-packaged-optics test past a single die puts the constraint in the handling, not in the laser. Fiber alignment is the bottleneck on the optical engine. The fiber array unit is supposed to couple light without ever contacting the device. Crash it during bring-up and the array is damaged; leave contamination on it and the measurement is junk, so cleaning is part of the test cell, not a side chore.

Dave Armstrong of ISE Labs, an ASE Group subsidiary, draws the power line that keeps many insertions at one die. If the main ASIC is an 8-kilowatt GPU, with multiple connectors around multiple sides, tester resources have to deliver that power and pull the heat back out. That combination is hard to extend past one die. Matt Griffin of Teradyne agrees that most production solutions today are single-site, and that multi-site probe cards would relieve the fiber-alignment bottleneck if they work. Ira Leventhal of Advantest treats the connector as the part that will not scale on optimism. A ball-grid array already teaches the same lesson: socket the device too many times and the balls are at risk. The device-side optical connector is designed to be attached once and, the hope is, never disconnected. A test-side connector that survives 20 to 50 insertions is not a production tool. It needs to last for tens of thousands of insertions, or 100,000. The fix the panel describes is a hardened metal on the tester side. That hardened metal then mates to a receptacle on the device that is not hardened. Careless mating can wear out the device connector before the spec lifetime, which means the test method can scrap the part it was built to qualify.

Kazuo Yamaguchi of Keysight separates the easy simultaneous contact from the one that actually characterizes the engine. DC pads plus an optical probe are a mechanical problem. Modulator and photodetector performance is validated through S-parameters, so optical contact and high-frequency radio-frequency contact have to land together. Probe cards fit only a limited number of RF lines, and their bandwidth is limited, while today's devices are characterized beyond 200 gigahertz. At wafer and die insertions, simultaneous connection is an RF probing problem sitting on top of fiber alignment. Leventhal adds warpage: alignment has to hold in multiple axes, either from the top or from the side, while a dense ball-grid array still has to make reliable electrical contact. Griffin's map of the package makes the geometry worse at module level. Every insertion has an optical interface and an electrical interface. Those interfaces can be on the top, the bottom, or both. At module level they can sit on two to four sides of the package, across some number of optical engines. Optical alignment by itself, he says, is not the large challenge. Doing it on every face, at the same time as power and RF, is.

A worked pass through Armstrong's split shows where single-site is still the rational cell and where it stops paying. Take the 8-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides. Tester power delivery and heat extraction already strain a one-die insertion, so adding a second site does not buy throughput if the tester cannot feed both parts. Photonic-integrated-circuit wafer test, in his experience, is single-site today, with some hope of moving up if probe-card makers—he names Technoprobe—can put more optical sites on one card. Near-packaged optics, by contrast, is typically a single-connector device. High-volume output of that class forces multi-site, because one connector and one alignment per tester hour will not clear the line. He calls insertions two and three the fertile ground: they are the longer, more complex insertions, and they are where multi-site has room to remove time. Griffin's hope is the same from the optical side. Multi-site fiber alignment is what lets the industry ramp faster. It is not here as a production default.

Single-site remains the right choice while three conditions hold at once: the device under test is an 8-kilowatt-class ASIC whose connector count and power exceed what the tester can deliver to a second die; the optical probe must never touch the die, so each site still needs its own clean fiber array; and the measurement that matters is an electro-optic S-parameter past 200 gigahertz, which a probe card cannot fan out across many RF lines. That choice stops being the right one when the product is a single-connector near-packaged engine headed for high volume, insertions two and three dominate the test time, and a hardened test connector can survive on the order of 100,000 matings without chewing through the softer receptacle on the device. Until that connector and a multi-site fiber card both exist, adding sites multiplies crashes and contamination faster than it multiplies good parts. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: When the Checkpoint Outgrows the Sky

Thirteen point four trillion particles stay in the calculation only if every tracer's state can sit in memory while the bytes that merely keep the run alive go somewhere else. HPCwire reports that an Argonne team used the Aurora exascale machine at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility to finish two of the largest cosmological simulations yet run. Each one followed 13.4 trillion dark-matter particles across a region 28.6 billion light-years wide. The code is HACC, the Hardware/Hybrid Accelerated Cosmology Code. Gravity is the only force in these runs. Particles stand in as tracers of dark-matter density and velocity, so the hot state is position and velocity for each tracer, not gas, stars, or radiation.

Aurora is built for that residency. The system has 10,624 nodes and 63,744 GPUs. Each of these runs drew on 8,100 nodes and roughly 50,000 GPUs. The simulations and much of the analysis finished in about four days. High-capacity memory is the constraint the team names: trillions of particles had to stay readily available as the calculation moved. A gravity-only state is what made that volume fit.

The other limit is the restart. A multi-day run on most of the machine cannot assume it finishes in one sitting. Aurora's Distributed Asynchronous Object Storage, DAOS, took the checkpoint stream. The two simulations wrote more than 85 petabytes through DAOS, mostly as restart checkpoints, so an interruption resumes instead of starting over. About 8 petabytes were kept for scientific analysis.

Walk those two write figures. More than 85 petabytes went through DAOS. About 8 petabytes stayed for science. The checkpoint stream is more than ten times the analysis archive. Most of the bytes the runs produced were not the sky. They were the right to continue the sky. Eighty-five against eight is the worked ratio, and it sits on top of the machine split already named: 8,100 nodes out of 10,624, and roughly 50,000 GPUs out of 63,744, for about four days, twice, once for each 13.4-trillion-particle volume.

That cut stops being the right design when the question needs the gas and the stars in the same volume. The same HACC team had already used Frontier for a different cosmology run, a hydrodynamic simulation that added gas, stars, and other galaxy-formation processes, and that effort produced the largest hydrodynamic simulation of the universe to date. Adding those fields multiplies the state per particle. HPCwire is explicit that focusing on gravity alone is what let Aurora hold a larger volume and more particles. When galaxy formation has to live inside the same box as the largest-scale density field, the resident set and the checkpoint both grow, and the gravity-only cut is no longer the run the survey analysis requires. Source: hpcwire.com
