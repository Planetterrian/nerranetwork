The Teardown: The Solver Signs the Stage

Every autonomous step in a chip flow is either inside the physics or it is a risk the next step inherits. Semiconductor Engineering carries a Siemens account of what that means once agents drive electronic design automation. A self-verifying agent can inspect its own work. A physics-validated workflow is the stricter object: every agent decision is confirmed against a deterministic, physics-based engine before the next step begins. The engine, not the model, is the authority on correctness. Tapeout does not grade on a curve. Silicon works or it does not, and a model that can discuss heat or timing does not relax that binary.

The check cannot wait for the end of the run, because misses compound. A workflow that reasons about physics and does not validate outputs against those engines adds risk at every autonomous step. The longer it runs without validation, the further a design can drift before an error surfaces. Across multiple tools, multiple physical domains, and hours without a person in the loop, unvalidated decisions stack. Continuous validation is what keeps an early miss from becoming the assumption the next tool is built on.

Three-dimensional integrated circuits are the case the account uses. Stacked active dies make heat removal and temperature interactions harder to predict, and the package can add mechanical stress. Electrical, power, thermal, and mechanical effects are tied together, so a change in one moves the others. Doing that by hand, tool by tool, creates engineering handoffs, slows iteration, and caps how many alternatives a team can try. AI factories, the piece notes, are asking for more compute, memory bandwidth, and thermal performance inside a tighter footprint, which is why chiplet 2.5D and 3D packages are carrying the scaling once a monolithic die runs out of room.

The worked example is a nine-stage electro-thermal workflow. MCP servers and skill files for the tools connect to Fuse, which aggregates them, and in this example Claude Code is the coding agent. The flow spans seven Siemens EDA tools and runs 100 to 200 agentic turns over more than 12 hours. Stages run from floor planning, library characterization, and power analysis through thermal signoff, system-level thermal simulation, parasitic extraction, and power- and temperature-aware SPICE simulation. Each stage is validated with signoff-grade physics solvers. The agent executes, reads the result, and keeps refining when a target is missed. It does not bank a stage and hope a final signoff catches it.

The sweep is what those hours are for. Teams can vary heat-transfer-coefficient boundaries, power budgets, and material stack-ups across 200 to 500 or more design points in a cycle. Five hundred divided by two hundred is 2.5, so the top of that stated range is two and a half times the bottom, inside one cycle. The account says the same machinery lets teams configure tools up to 20 times faster, cut engineering effort by up to 95 percent, and evaluate up to 50 times more design options than a conventional manual flow. The 20-times configuration speed is what makes a run of more than 12 hours and 100 to 200 turns able to touch hundreds of points instead of a handful of hand-built decks. The 50-times claim and the 200-to-500 point range are the article’s own measures of that wider search, not two labs reporting the same experiment.

This control stops being the right one when a stage is allowed to advance because the agent is satisfied and the solver speaks only after the twelve hours. At that point a search up to 50 times wider than a manual flow is also up to 50 times more ways for an early, unvalidated miss to propagate through seven tools, and the binary tapeout check arrives after the drift has already been designed in. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: A Molecule That Emits the Link

A spin that can be addressed one molecule at a time, and that emits a photon at a set optical frequency, is the part HPCwire’s account of NVision’s Nature paper treats as the missing connector. Until that interface existed, molecular spins could be interesting defects and still fail as qubits, because they could not be controlled individually and coherently. The paper, A Single-Molecule Spin-Photon Interface, is described as the first individually addressable organic molecular spin-photon interface. The molecule is a carbene. It is meant to be deposited as a thin film, the way OLED materials are deposited, straight onto a photonic integrated circuit, including HyperLight’s thin-film lithium niobate chiplets. Light, not a wire between neighboring dies, is the link that entangles a stationary spin with another spin on the same chip or on another chip.

That choice changes the floor plan. A monolithic quantum chip packs qubits until crosstalk, control wiring and heat set the spacing. NVision’s photonic integrated quantum circuit uses the optical link so the qubits do not have to sit in that tight neighborhood. The molecules run at 2 to 4 kelvin, not at the millikelvin stack many superconducting parts demand, and they emit at optical frequencies the photonic chip can take without a separate wavelength translation step. Chemistry is the design knob: memory time, gate speed and photon emission are tuned inside one molecule rather than by redrawing a silicon qubit cell. HPCwire reports the screen that produced the carbene: more than 150 candidate molecules evaluated, 40 of them synthesized in the lab.

The loss budget is the worked example, and it is why the foundry claim is not just a slogan. The platform is described as tolerating up to 70 percent photon loss and still operating on standard silicon photonics, instead of demanding an ultra-low-loss optical plant that most CMOS lines do not run. Take a link that throws away seven photons in every ten. If the interface still closes entanglement on the three that arrive, the interconnect can be a photonic chip a foundry already knows how to build, with HyperLight’s electro-optic lithium niobate sitting underneath a deposited molecular film. The alternative path in the same account is decades and multi-billion-dollar proprietary fabs for modalities that only scale inside a custom line. NVision’s commercial sketch puts the first integrated prototype chips in the fourth quarter of 2026, a live on-chip demonstration by the end of 2026, then a climb to 1,000 physical qubits and toward 1,000 logical, fault-tolerant qubits on chip by 2030, in a data-center form factor. Those dates are targets, not delivered systems.

The 70 percent figure is also the exit ramp. If a real photonic link loses more than that share of photons, standard silicon photonics stops being enough and the design is back in the specialized low-loss plant it was written to avoid. The same break appears if the thin-film deposit cannot keep the single-molecule addressability the Nature result showed in the lab, because a film that only works as an ensemble is not a qubit array a foundry can tile. And if the workload needs two-qubit gates that only exist when the spins share a neighborhood, the optical decoupling that removes crosstalk also removes the interaction, and proximity wins again. The Teardown: When the Advance Payment Buys the Slot

Wafer, high-bandwidth memory, a packaging substrate and a test socket can each stop an AI chip, so the delivery date is no longer set by the foundry handoff alone. 36Kr describes the older chain as a defined split: design houses specified the part, wafer foundries manufactured it, and packaging and test plants took the next step, joined by orders and shared standards. That split assumed each link could be booked after the one before it. AI parts broke the sequence because the performance ceiling is set together by the advanced process, the memory and the package. A larger die and a larger package also consume more wafer and more packaging area per chip, so a shortage in one input idles the others.

The wafer side is already spoken for. TrendForce estimated in March 2026 that global foundry revenue would rise 24.8 percent for the full year, to about $218.8 billion, and that TSMC’s 5-nanometer, 4-nanometer and more advanced capacity would stay fully loaded through the end of 2026. 36Kr says that load is Nvidia and AMD GPUs plus custom chips from Google, AWS and Meta. A full advanced-node line does not finish the accelerator if the package, the substrate or the test insertion is late. The scarce object stops being a wafer start and becomes a reserved place in every downstream tool.

The reservation is now paid before the tools exist. In July 2026 Amkor signed a $1.5 billion multi-year advanced-packaging and development agreement with Nvidia, and Nvidia’s advance payment was to support Amkor’s US capacity build. On October 21 the two added a South Korea expansion; 36Kr puts the US and Korea agreements together at $3 billion. Test follows the same pattern. Nvidia provided $1.1 billion to King Yuan Electronics as a capacity-reservation advance, funding a US test factory and the equipment to run it. On substrates, the October 21 arrangements with Ibiden, Samsung Electro-Mechanics and XinXing Electronics are planned to take the relevant substrate capacity to 2.5 times the current level, and Ibiden’s expansion of its Ono plant in Japan is also backed by an Nvidia advance. Cash plus a multi-year volume promise moves a supplier’s build plan earlier than a spot order would. The supplier gets a demand signal it can borrow against; the customer gets a claim on capacity that a later purchase order might not secure.

That choice stops being the right way to buy a chip when the check lands on a link that is not the late one. 36Kr notes that 2.5D packaging of the CoWoS type has been tight since 2023 and that the squeeze has spread to packaging equipment, substrates and key materials, so more wafers, or even a larger packaging house, still leave delivery gated by whichever input did not get the money. It also stops working for anyone but the largest buyers once the line is allocated by who can write the advance. 36Kr’s warning is polarization: firms with capital and the ability to commit long orders take scarce capacity first. A reservation that clears the bottleneck only for those buyers fails the moment the missing part is a tool, a substrate or a material the prepaid supplier cannot pull forward, and it fails the buyer who needs the chip but cannot fund the factory that has to build it. The Teardown: Useful Life in Vacuum

In vacuum, convective cooling does not exist. Heat leaving a processor has to be conducted or pumped to an outer surface, and that surface has to radiate the energy away. EE Times, writing on Google's Project Suncatcher, treats the radiator, the solar arrays, the power distribution and the years the hardware must survive as part of the processor's real performance, because a failed board in orbit is not swapped at the next maintenance window.

On October 1, 2026, a Project Suncatcher prototype reached orbit aboard a SpaceX mission. Developed with Planet, the satellite established contact with the ground. Its task is to test how commercial AI hardware behaves where replacement is not available. Before launch, Google subjected Trillium TPUs to vibration and proton-beam tests and reported encouraging tolerance to accumulated ionizing radiation, while still investigating errors that arise during AI workloads. It also tested a thermal system of heat pipes and radiators in simulated vacuum. Laboratory exposure is an initial indication of resilience. The flight still has to establish the rate of faults, and what those faults do to sustained output, over years.

The plant required to turn that experiment into something like a data center shows up in a model, not in the satellite now on orbit. An April 2026 study by Slava Turyshev, as EE Times recounts it, examined a representative one-megawatt installation and required approximately 5,640 square meters of photovoltaic arrays and 2,500 square meters of radiators. Those are model outputs, dependent on assumptions about solar generation and heat rejection. They are the physical scale of one megawatt, still small beside a terrestrial hyperscale hall, and every square meter is mass that has to be launched. A spacecraft also needs electrical generation and distribution, provision for periods of insufficient sunlight, and data links both between satellites and back to Earth. Each kilogram lands in the launch budget.

The processor sets part of that mass. A faster accelerator that draws more power can require larger solar arrays, more electrical distribution equipment and more radiating surface. Those supporting pieces add mass and can offset the benefit of higher throughput. Software efficiency, error rates, utilization and expected service life all move the comparison between two chips with similar benchmark scores. Take two architectures on the same workload. One completes more operations per second and needs a larger thermal system. The other is slower, draws less power, and allows a smaller spacecraft that may fly a longer mission. The useful comparison is total computation completed over the mission, adjusted for interruptions, degraded operation and the chance of premature failure. A processor that wins the initial benchmark can still deliver fewer productive hours before the spacecraft is retired. For an operator that cannot replace a failed board, that lost lifetime output is a direct cost.

Turyshev's model also examines launch cost, spacecraft construction, utilization and operating life. Under one set of assumptions, the combined cost of launch and spacecraft construction would have to fall below a benchmark based on the published price of a dedicated Falcon 9 launch for the modeled system to compete. That comparison is sensitive to the launch configuration chosen, and it says little about future launch prices. The U.S. Government Accountability Office, in an April assessment, identified cooling, communications and deployment economics as unresolved. Processing observations near the satellites that collect them is the earlier use case, because less raw data might need to be sent to Earth. A general-purpose orbital data center faces the larger infrastructure problem. Communications sit beside power and heat: a distributed workload that moves data often between processors makes the network part of the system cost, and a functioning accelerator does not by itself make the power, thermal and link systems work together at commercial scale.

NASA's High-Performance Spaceflight Computing processor, developed with Microchip Technology, is specified for spacecraft workloads that need autonomy, radiation resilience, power management and fault tolerance. In May 2026, NASA reported preliminary testing that suggested performance roughly 500 times that of radiation-hardened processors then in use. The same lifetime arithmetic applies: throughput only counts for the hours the machine keeps producing.

This design choice stops being the right one when the extra array and radiator a hotter accelerator demands consume the mission mass budget before those extra operations are delivered, or when the job is only local reduction of sensor data and never required a megawatt of general-purpose compute in orbit. The Teardown: Why Backside Power Lets One Exposure Finish the Pitch

High numerical aperture extreme ultraviolet lithography raises the resolution of the light on a wafer until the printable pitch, the space between two features, sits between 16 nanometres and 8 nanometres. Wccftech's account of Intel vice president Chris Auth, speaking on the Dwarkesh podcast, is that a pitch in that window can be printed in a single exposure. One pass is the cost argument: one mask, one alignment, one dose. When a design needs a tighter pitch than one exposure can hold, a foundry prints the pattern in stages. Each stage only has to hit a looser pitch. The exposures together make the dense pattern. That second exposure is double-patterning EUV. It spends a second pass, a second mask, and an overlay budget between the two.

Backside power delivery is how Auth says Intel stays inside the single-pass window. Power wires move off the front of the wafer and onto the back. The front, where the computing transistors sit and where the circuits that move data between them sit, no longer shares that space with the power grid. Auth's claim is that this relaxes the front-side pitch enough to print that level directly with high numerical aperture tools. A looser pitch also relaxes the error tolerance, because features do not have to land as close together. He contrasts that with TSMC, which he says must run tighter pitches because it does not have backside power, cannot print that layer with high numerical aperture in one pass, and has to use double-patterning EUV.

Take those pitch numbers as a gate. If the front side still needs a pitch between 16 nanometres and 8 nanometres, Auth's account says one high numerical aperture exposure can print it. If the pitch has to be tighter than 8 nanometres, that single exposure is outside its window and the flow falls back to two EUV passes. Intel's bet is that moving power to the back keeps the front-side pitch inside the 16-to-8 window, so the second pass never starts. Auth added that 14A is an option in the process design kit today, handled customer by customer. That is narrower than a general release. Earlier reports, which his comments are described as moving past, had discussed backside power on 18A. The scanner choice and the node name are separate: in his telling, high numerical aperture pays only where backside power has already bought the pitch relief.

Chief technology officer Pushkar Ranade, on the same podcast, separates that lithography claim from scale. He said TSMC is a much larger wafer manufacturer today, and that catching up in scale will be slow and gradual. On technology he said Intel is already there, maybe a few percent off on performance or power or area density. A gap of a few percent, he argued, is a different problem from being a whole generation behind. The condition he put first is time and yield. You can be off by a few percent, but you need to be on time, and yields have to be very high to support very large and very rapid ramps. A one-pass exposure that prints inside 8 to 16 nanometres still loses the ramp if yield cannot fill a fast volume curve while the larger foundry is already shipping.

A second failure mode sits in the transistor stack, not in the scanner. Wccftech quotes Intel's Jack Kavalieros on stacking n-type and p-type transistors, the approach IBM has tied to a 0.7-nanometre plan. Connections that used to run on the top, or on the bottom, now have to go vertically between the transistors. The trenches that carry those connections get taller relative to their width. Filling them gets harder. Unfilled trenches leave air gaps and holes. Backside power can free the front side for a single high numerical aperture pass and still be the wrong process once those vertical interconnects, not the in-plane pitch, set the yield. The Teardown: When the Fourth Mask Loses the Exposure

A dense layer does not have to be drawn in a single pass. Break the pattern into simpler pieces, expose each piece on a deep-ultraviolet scanner, and the stack can approximate a feature that one shot of that light cannot print. Tom's Hardware put that workaround to Chris Mack, co-founder and chief technology officer of Fractilia, because export rules have kept ASML’s extreme-ultraviolet tools out of China. Those machines print with 13.5-nanometre light. Without them, the path left is older deep-ultraviolet lithography plus multiple patterning.

What that path has already printed is in shipping phones. A teardown of Huawei’s Mate 60 Pro found the Kirin 9000S made by SMIC on a second-generation 7-nanometre-class process with no EUV. Later analysis of the Kirin 9030 found SMIC’s DUV flow pushed closer to densities associated with 5-nanometre manufacturing, while still lagging the leading processes at TSMC and Samsung. Mack’s point is that a node label hides the cost of getting there. Every added exposure has to land on patterns already etched. More masks mean more overlay error and more process variation. Designers buy a printable layer by leaving extra space around features. The die grows. A larger die means fewer candidates on each wafer, and wafers cost tens of thousands of dollars. The thing sold is the good die, not the wafer.

Mack’s cost line is the worked case. He told Tom's Hardware that industry convention held three exposures on an immersion tool at about the same cost as one EUV exposure. At four masks standing in for that single EUV exposure, EUV becomes the cheaper shot, and the cleaner one, because four overlay stacks accumulate defects one exposure never introduces. Memory shows the same gap in a June Morgan Stanley estimate: CXMT was producing only about one-third as many bits per wafer as Western competitors. Analysts put only about a third of that shortfall on the older process node and called lithography access a hard ceiling. Micron’s 1-alpha DRAM, built without EUV, is the counter-example — competitive chips from deep-ultraviolet light, multiple patterning and tight control, while Samsung and SK hynix used many EUV layers for the same generation. Douglas Fuller of Copenhagen Business School told Tom's Hardware that other routes around EUV for advanced logic are unlikely to be efficient, and that by the end of the decade advanced packaging could still let DUV print the latest DRAM generations.

Four masks against one EUV exposure is where this choice stops paying for a logic layer. Overlay error, a fatter die and fewer good dice per wafer then dominate the scanner that was never delivered. Source: tomshardware.com

The Teardown: When the Panel Gives the Count Back

A large AI package is a rectangle, and the workpiece under it has spent decades as a circle. Semiconductor Engineering’s account of the wafer-to-panel shift, drawing on ASE and Intel Foundry engineers, starts from that mismatch. As the package grows, more of a 300-millimetre wafer’s process time lands outside the circle of usable silicon. A panel gives the corners back. It also exposes how much of “the process” was really a habit of the round part: spin, stepper, single-sided handling, a hot plate sized for a wafer.

Teck Chong Lee of ASE put the practical limit in one flow. Running assembly on a 600-millimetre panel is high risk, and the equipment to do the whole job is not all in place. One option is to finish the interposer at 600 millimetres, then cut it into four pieces before assembly and the C4 bump process. The area win is taken where the tools exist, then partly handed back where they do not.

The same study counts a 5.5-times-reticle interposer. A 300-millimetre wafer yields 9. A 300-millimetre panel yields 16. A 600-millimetre panel divided into four 300-millimetre pieces yields 64. Leaving that 600-millimetre panel intact yields 72. Seventy-two against nine is eight times the wafer, more than the roughly fivefold gap in raw area, because the rectangle wastes almost nothing at the rim. Intel’s Tarik Ibrahim, in a conference presentation covered in the same piece, used a 510-by-515-millimetre panel: a package of roughly four times a reticle field is about 10 units on a 300-millimetre wafer and approximately 48 on the panel. The largest square that fits the wafer is only about 210 by 210 millimetres, so past that outline the wafer is not merely less economical. It cannot hold the part.

What breaks is not the arithmetic. At wafer level the process is single-sided. On a panel it is dual-sided, and uniformity and process capability have to hold across a sheet the tools were not born for. Spin coating needs radial symmetry; on a rectangle the film thins toward the corners and most of the resist is thrown off. The Teardown: Why the Fiber Array Cannot Touch the Die

Co-packaged optics does not become a volume product when the photonic die lights up on a bench. It becomes a volume product when a factory can attach light and electricity at the same time, on every insertion, without destroying the only connector the module will ever trust. Semiconductor Engineering's panel on scaling co-packaged-optics test past a single die puts the constraint in the handling, not in the laser. Fiber alignment is the bottleneck on the optical engine. The fiber array unit is supposed to couple light without ever contacting the device. Crash it during bring-up and the array is damaged; leave contamination on it and the measurement is junk, so cleaning is part of the test cell, not a side chore.

Dave Armstrong of ISE Labs, an ASE Group subsidiary, draws the power line that keeps many insertions at one die. If the main ASIC is an 8-kilowatt GPU, with multiple connectors around multiple sides, tester resources have to deliver that power and pull the heat back out. That combination is hard to extend past one die. Matt Griffin of Teradyne agrees that most production solutions today are single-site, and that multi-site probe cards would relieve the fiber-alignment bottleneck if they work. Ira Leventhal of Advantest treats the connector as the part that will not scale on optimism. A ball-grid array already teaches the same lesson: socket the device too many times and the balls are at risk. The device-side optical connector is designed to be attached once and, the hope is, never disconnected. A test-side connector that survives 20 to 50 insertions is not a production tool. It needs to last for tens of thousands of insertions, or 100,000. The fix the panel describes is a hardened metal on the tester side. That hardened metal then mates to a receptacle on the device that is not hardened. Careless mating can wear out the device connector before the spec lifetime, which means the test method can scrap the part it was built to qualify.

Kazuo Yamaguchi of Keysight separates the easy simultaneous contact from the one that actually characterizes the engine. DC pads plus an optical probe are a mechanical problem. Modulator and photodetector performance is validated through S-parameters, so optical contact and high-frequency radio-frequency contact have to land together. Probe cards fit only a limited number of RF lines, and their bandwidth is limited, while today's devices are characterized beyond 200 gigahertz. At wafer and die insertions, simultaneous connection is an RF probing problem sitting on top of fiber alignment. Leventhal adds warpage: alignment has to hold in multiple axes, either from the top or from the side, while a dense ball-grid array still has to make reliable electrical contact. Griffin's map of the package makes the geometry worse at module level. Every insertion has an optical interface and an electrical interface. Those interfaces can be on the top, the bottom, or both. At module level they can sit on two to four sides of the package, across some number of optical engines. Optical alignment by itself, he says, is not the large challenge. Doing it on every face, at the same time as power and RF, is.

A worked pass through Armstrong's split shows where single-site is still the rational cell and where it stops paying. Take the 8-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides. Tester power delivery and heat extraction already strain a one-die insertion, so adding a second site does not buy throughput if the tester cannot feed both parts. Photonic-integrated-circuit wafer test, in his experience, is single-site today, with some hope of moving up if probe-card makers—he names Technoprobe—can put more optical sites on one card. Near-packaged optics, by contrast, is typically a single-connector device. High-volume output of that class forces multi-site, because one connector and one alignment per tester hour will not clear the line. He calls insertions two and three the fertile ground: they are the longer, more complex insertions, and they are where multi-site has room to remove time. Griffin's hope is the same from the optical side. Multi-site fiber alignment is what lets the industry ramp faster. It is not here as a production default.

Single-site remains the right choice while three conditions hold at once: the device under test is an 8-kilowatt-class ASIC whose connector count and power exceed what the tester can deliver to a second die; the optical probe must never touch the die, so each site still needs its own clean fiber array; and the measurement that matters is an electro-optic S-parameter past 200 gigahertz, which a probe card cannot fan out across many RF lines. That choice stops being the right one when the product is a single-connector near-packaged engine headed for high volume, insertions two and three dominate the test time, and a hardened test connector can survive on the order of 100,000 matings without chewing through the softer receptacle on the device. Until that connector and a multi-site fiber card both exist, adding sites multiplies crashes and contamination faster than it multiplies good parts. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: When the Checkpoint Outgrows the Sky

Thirteen point four trillion particles stay in the calculation only if every tracer's state can sit in memory while the bytes that merely keep the run alive go somewhere else. HPCwire reports that an Argonne team used the Aurora exascale machine at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility to finish two of the largest cosmological simulations yet run. Each one followed 13.4 trillion dark-matter particles across a region 28.6 billion light-years wide. The code is HACC, the Hardware/Hybrid Accelerated Cosmology Code. Gravity is the only force in these runs. Particles stand in as tracers of dark-matter density and velocity, so the hot state is position and velocity for each tracer, not gas, stars, or radiation.

Aurora is built for that residency. The system has 10,624 nodes and 63,744 GPUs. Each of these runs drew on 8,100 nodes and roughly 50,000 GPUs. The simulations and much of the analysis finished in about four days. High-capacity memory is the constraint the team names: trillions of particles had to stay readily available as the calculation moved. A gravity-only state is what made that volume fit.

The other limit is the restart. A multi-day run on most of the machine cannot assume it finishes in one sitting. Aurora's Distributed Asynchronous Object Storage, DAOS, took the checkpoint stream. The two simulations wrote more than 85 petabytes through DAOS, mostly as restart checkpoints, so an interruption resumes instead of starting over. About 8 petabytes were kept for scientific analysis.

Walk those two write figures. More than 85 petabytes went through DAOS. About 8 petabytes stayed for science. The checkpoint stream is more than ten times the analysis archive. Most of the bytes the runs produced were not the sky. They were the right to continue the sky. Eighty-five against eight is the worked ratio, and it sits on top of the machine split already named: 8,100 nodes out of 10,624, and roughly 50,000 GPUs out of 63,744, for about four days, twice, once for each 13.4-trillion-particle volume.

That cut stops being the right design when the question needs the gas and the stars in the same volume. The same HACC team had already used Frontier for a different cosmology run, a hydrodynamic simulation that added gas, stars, and other galaxy-formation processes, and that effort produced the largest hydrodynamic simulation of the universe to date. Adding those fields multiplies the state per particle. HPCwire is explicit that focusing on gravity alone is what let Aurora hold a larger volume and more particles. When galaxy formation has to live inside the same box as the largest-scale density field, the resident set and the checkpoint both grow, and the gravity-only cut is no longer the run the survey analysis requires. The Teardown: When the Pad Has to Land Inside Forty Nanometres

Hybrid bonding mates a dielectric face and a metal pad in the same step, so the interconnect can sit on a pitch a solder bump or a thermocompression bond has trouble holding. The copper has to land on copper. Alignment is the bond, not a check that happens after the chemistry.

The surface has to arrive flat, clean, and in a known material state. BigGo Finance quotes Applied Materials executive Prabu Raja: “Advanced packaging is no longer just physical assembly in the back end—it is a materials engineering revolution that demands wafer-level precision and cleanliness.” A particle, a recessed copper pad, or an unstable surface from an earlier step can make the bonder’s own positioning accuracy meaningless. That is the reason Applied Materials and Besi, starting from a joint R&D project in Singapore in 2020, built the Kinex die-to-wafer system to pull surface treatment, cleaning, activation and alignment into one tighter flow.

The measured case in that account is a May result from Imec and EV Group. They reached a 200-nanometre copper interconnect pitch in wafer-to-wafer hybrid-bonding research. After bonding, overlay deviation of the copper pads stayed below 40 nanometres across all test chips on 300-millimetre wafers. Forty nanometres is one fifth of a 200-nanometre pitch. If the pad is on the order of that pitch, a 40-nanometre miss already consumes a large share of the contact before corrosion or dishing is counted. Imec, in the same report, says further scaling still has to solve surface topography from chemical mechanical polishing, copper corrosion, dielectric reliability, and metal diffusion along the bond interface.

The lithography tool beside that problem is already shipping, and it is not a bonder. ASML delivered the TWINSCAN XT:260 i-line system for 3D integration and advanced packaging in 2025, specified at 270 wafers an hour. Scanning 270 wafers an hour prints packaging layers. It does not join the pads. An international patent published on 15 May, with ASML Netherlands as applicant, discusses improving bonding-alignment deviation by controlling the surface state of the substrate support. BigGo Finance draws the line in plain terms: ASML has not publicly confirmed a commercial delivery timeline, customer qualification, or mass-production performance for hybrid-bonding equipment. A patent position is not a qualified tool on a high-bandwidth-memory line.

The R&D money shows how early the integrated flow still is. On 1 October, Besi became an innovation partner at Applied Materials’ EPIC centre in Silicon Valley, planned at up to about 5 billion dollars and spent as customer projects advance. The scope moves past die-to-wafer hybrid bonding into thermocompression-bond scaling, die-to-die, die-to-panel, and optical interconnects. Five days later, Applied Materials deepened work with Intel that includes Foveros 3D packaging. Lam Research’s path stays on the hole and the fill: Syndion deep reactive-ion etch for high-aspect-ratio through-silicon vias, and the SABRE 3D copper-plating platform for via fill, wafer-level packaging and redistribution layers. That coverage does not replace the bonder.

Hybrid bonding stops being the right next step on an HBM flow when the added surface-control steps raise the cost of the whole stack faster than the pitch gain pays back, or when polishing topography, copper corrosion, dielectric reliability or interface diffusion cannot be held inside the overlay the pitch requires. BigGo Finance says Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron still have to decide which HBM generation and which stack architecture split traditional thermocompression bonding from hybrid bonding. The Teardown: When One Pool Has to Hold the Scene

A core that cannot see its weights in the same address space has to wait for a copy. iTWire’s account of Nvidia’s RTX Spark laptops describes the machine that tries to delete that wait: one superchip, a Blackwell RTX GPU with up to 6,144 CUDA cores beside a Grace CPU with up to 20 cores, joined by NVLink-C2C, sharing up to 128 gigabytes of unified memory and rated at up to 1 petaflop of FP4. MediaTek’s piece of that CPU, the same report says, was power efficiency and connectivity. The mechanism is the pool, not the logo on the lid. On a split PC the GPU owns a private frame buffer. A tensor that begins in system memory is copied across a peripheral link, used, and often copied back before the CPU can trust it. Unified memory makes the CPU and the GPU clients of one physical pool. A load from either side is an address in that pool. Coherence is the interconnect’s job. If the line is already there, nobody stages a second copy to make the other processor legal. FP4 is the precision tied to the 1-petaflop rating. Four-bit weights are how 128 gigabytes holds a larger network than a wider format would allow in the same bytes. That rating is a peak at FP4. It is not a second number for FP8 or FP16, where the same cores move less work per watt and the same pool holds fewer parameters.

Walk only the figures iTWire prints. Systems from Asus, Dell, HP, Lenovo, Microsoft and MSI start at $2,399, the figure in Nvidia's announcement; iTWire's report put it at $2,399. Set the memory ceiling at 128 gigabytes and set Nvidia’s reported scene claim beside it: 3D scenes of up to 90 gigabytes. That scene is not a side buffer. It is most of the only pool the chip has. The operating system, the tool, and any agent mapped into the same addresses have to live in what the scene does not already occupy. There is no second tier in the description to spill into. An edit and a local model, if both are open, are roommates in that 128 gigabytes, which is the product claim of one PC for creation, gaming and personal agents. The gaming line next to it is AAA titles at 1440p at over 100 frames per second, a shader-and-bandwidth claim on those 6,144 CUDA cores, not evidence of a private graphics memory beside the pool. Adobe is optimising Photoshop, Premiere and Substance 3D for the platform, with up to 2x gains in beta builds at launch. A gain of that size on tools that stream large frames is what shows up when a peripheral copy was a large share of the old timeline. The Grace side, up to 20 cores, is the control plane: scheduling, I/O, the agent loop. It is not a second accelerator hiding in the same package. At a $2,399 start the comparison is not a data-centre GPU tray. It is whether one coherent pool, capped at these figures, replaces a desktop that split CPU DRAM from GPU memory and paid the copy on every handoff.

The single superchip stops being the right box when the live working set no longer fits in 128 gigabytes, or when the result required is not an FP4 peak of 1 petaflop. A 90-gigabyte scene already takes most of the pool. A second scene, a weight that will not quantise to four bits, or a context that has to stay wide, turns the next address into a miss this package cannot cure by adding a card. The partner list and the $2,399 floor do not move that ceiling. The ceiling is the pool. Source: itwire.com

The Teardown: When the Film Has to Survive the Hole

A stacked package only prints if the wafer under the lens is flat enough that the next pattern lands on the last one. That alignment is overlay. Bow is the warp that film stress puts into the wafer: the surface leaves the focal plane, and features shift sideways from the grid the scanner is trying to hit. EE Times, reporting Zeiss Semiconductor Manufacturing Technology at SEMICON West, quotes US sales head Raghu Nayak calling wafer flatness a huge issue, and describes Zeiss DUNE as a tool that measures topography and corrects it wafer by wafer so the next fabrication step can still overlay.

The same account puts a second job beside that correction. Zeiss NLX is specified for 300-millimeter wafers. It is built to make internal structures in a modern package visible without destroying the package, so connections between stacked dies can be checked and a defect caught before the wafer is scrapped. DUNE tries to put the surface back where the scanner expects it. NLX asks whether the bonds inside the stack are actually there. Those are sequential gates, not substitutes: a flat wafer can still hide an open bond, and a bond map does not flatten the wafer the scanner is about to expose.

Panel-level packaging changes the shape of the substrate the film has to cover. EE Times reports that Von Ardenne is extending its deposition portfolio from the 300-millimeter wafer to panel-level packaging, and that US chief executive Stephen Strausser told the magazine the company has a one- to two-year lead in coating and deposition for panel-level glass substrates. The process named is HiPIMS, high-power impulse magnetron sputtering, aimed at high-aspect-ratio barrier and seed layers inside through-glass vias. Those vias are holes through a glass panel that later take metal so a redistribution layer can land on a pad. The claim EE Times relays is dense, highly adherent film in those holes, so a deposition recipe already running on 300-millimeter wafers can move to a larger panel, including through-glass vias and redistribution layers.

The figures in that account are the format and the lead, and they are enough to see where time sits. NLX's inspection field is the 300-millimeter wafer. Von Ardenne's stated lead is one to two years, and the migration it describes is away from that same 300-millimeter format onto a panel. A panel is the larger form factor, so each coating pass addresses more package area than a 300-millimeter disc, but only if the film stays continuous down a hole whose depth is large relative to its width. That continuity is the whole engineering content of the high-aspect-ratio claim here. One to two years is the gap Strausser claims over other coaters on panel-level glass. A packaging line that wants a second source of that barrier-and-seed step is waiting on that interval, not on a lithography node announcement.

The stage is a separate limit, and EE Times puts it on the same show floor. It is the HPE Juniper QFX5252, built as an AMD Helios switch tray, and the layout is the opposite of a chassis switch whose value is a wall of front-panel ports. The fabric connectors are at the back. They blind-mate into the rack’s cable cartridges. The electrical problem is the run from each switch chip to those connectors at the SerDes rate Helios actually uses.

That rate is 224G PAM4. The switch that sounds like the scale-up part is Tomahawk Ultra, and ServeTheHome spells out why it is not in this tray. The current Tomahawk Ultra is a 112G PAM4 SerDes switch: 512 lanes of PAM4-112G. A lane specified at 112G does not carry a 224G PAM4 signal, so those 512 lanes are not a Helios fabric. The tray uses Tomahawk 6 because Helios is on 224G PAM4 SerDes. Two of those 102.6T devices in a 1OU tray are why the assembly is liquid cooled. Internal manifolds feed the cold blocks on the chips.

The connectors do not sit next to the silicon. ServeTheHome notes that the cabled connections to the rear high-density fabric connectors are quite far from the Tomahawk 6 chips, and some are very far. The liquid-cooling blocks in that gap are retimers. A retimer takes a degraded high-speed waveform, samples it, and launches a clean edge so the next stretch of copper can still meet the eye opening the SerDes requires. At 224G PAM4 the channel budget is short enough that a long board-plus-cable run inside one rack fails unless something rebuilds the edge. AMD is using retimers for that reach. NVIDIA has worked to remove them. After the retimer, the tray still needs what ServeTheHome calls an enormous array of copper cables into the cartridges. Retimers buy the reach; copper still carries the lanes. That combination is the picture the site says it will use when explaining why co-packaged optics is the next simplification: move the optical engine next to the switch chip and the long electrical run goes away.

The cooling split matches the power split. The two switch chips and the retimer blocks are on liquid. Fans and airflow guides remain over heatsinks on other components. NVIDIA has been pushing fans out of the AI rack; this Helios tray still air-cools those other bits. The front latch levers are very long because the back panel is long, has to blind-mate, and the insertion force into the Helios rack is significant. ServeTheHome had already covered the QFX5252 at HPE Discover 2026. The show-floor difference is the assembled tray, built to scale up inside the rack rather than out of front-panel connectors.

The copper-plus-retimer choice stops being the right one when the cartridge connectors have to sit so far from the Tomahawk 6 that a 224G PAM4 lane only closes inside the rack if a retimer rebuilds the edge and a full copper array finishes the run. That is the condition already sitting in the open QFX5252: the retimers are there because the connectors are far, and co-packaged optics is the simplification once that distance is what in-rack copper cannot close on its own.

The Teardown: Why the Weight File Sets the Memory Floor

An open-weight release puts the trained parameters where a buyer can load them onto hardware the buyer controls. Semiconductor Engineering's account of that shift starts from the memory those parameters occupy. Ashish Darbari, chief executive of Axiomise, notes in the piece that OpenAI reports gpt-oss-120b runs on a single 80 GB GPU and gpt-oss-20b can run on a device with 16 GB of memory, and that both use MXFP4 quantization. The weights are the file. The GPU or the smaller device is the shelf. If the file does not fit the shelf, that model does not run there, whatever else the chip can do at peak.

Sharad Chole, chief scientist and co-founder at Expedera, tells the same article that publishing the weights cuts vendor lock-in. The user can host the model or pick a service provider, and no single company owns the next token. He also dates the map. As of September 2026, he said, the larger open-weight models come primarily from outside the United States, from Europe or Asia, and the field is moving toward trillion-parameter models. Guillaume Boillet, vice president of strategic marketing at Arteris, draws the line the file does not cross: an open-weight release is not necessarily open source, because training data, code, and usage rights may remain restricted.

The worked example is the pair Darbari cites, and it should be read as a fit check rather than a benchmark. A model at the 120-billion-parameter scale, stored in MXFP4, is reported to fit one 80 GB GPU. A model at the 20-billion-parameter scale, in the same format, is reported to fit 16 GB. The article is explicit that memory fit alone does not establish useful performance. Architects, the piece says, still have to account for memory capacity, sustained performance, changing models, and deployment security. Chole's catalog count makes the churn concrete: about 250 open-weight models from major companies landed on Hugging Face in the last two years, not counting quantized variants or models from smaller firms. He describes an effort to distill a 400-billion-parameter DeepSeek-C model into an 8-billion-parameter Qwen model, and he cautions that the industry is not about to see 100-billion-parameter models breaking every device benchmark. Thomas Andersen, vice president for AI and machine learning at Synopsys, adds the domain limit: general-purpose models are strong on public text and code, and hardware design needs proprietary data those public weights do not contain. The article's own takeaway is the clock mismatch: model architectures can change in weeks, while silicon takes years.

The single-device fit stops being the right deployment when the job needs something the weight file does not carry, or when the memory that merely holds the weights cannot sustain the run. That missing piece is the training set, the source code, or the usage right Boillet flags. It is also the sustained-performance and long-context load listed beside raw capacity, and the proprietary design data Andersen says has to be added before the model is useful in a chip-design flow. At that point the 80 GB shelf that fit gpt-oss-120b is the wrong boundary. The machine has to be sized for the data and the duty cycle, not for the parameter count that cleared the first filter.

The Teardown: Why a Hundred Wafers a Year Is Nobody’s Job

A packaging tool is paid for by the wafers that keep it busy, not by how clever the process is. Europe’s advanced-packaging trouble, as EE Times reports it from Fraunhofer IZM, starts after the prototype works. A company can need fan-out, through-silicon vias or some other nonstandard flow at tens, hundreds or thousands of units. That run is too long for a research institute to carry indefinitely and too short for a high-volume outsourced assembly and test house to staff.

Rolf Aschenbrenner, deputy director of the Fraunhofer Institute for Reliability and Microintegration, sees that gap as an access problem: volume too low for advanced packaging at a commercial OSAT, while the institute can do prototypes and, in some cases, small-volume builds. The intended path is to adapt the process, prove it, and transfer it. The transfer dies when no industrial line will take the book of business.

Fan-out wafer-level packaging makes the arithmetic plain. An RF customer might need around 100 wafers a year. For a large OSAT, Aschenbrenner’s account is that 100 wafers a year does not register, so the job is declined. A medical miniature-camera customer is smaller still: about 20 wafers a year. One wafer can hold tens of thousands of cameras, so those 20 wafers are the entire market, not a pilot that grows. Nobody else’s depreciation schedule is built for that book.

The same reporting covers detector arrays for a novel CT scanner. Sensors have to sit very close together. Conventional wire bonds open gaps that hurt the image, so the design routes connections through the silicon to the backside. The customer wanted that work done in Europe. A research line can hold the process. A production OSAT will not, unless the annual wafer count looks like utilization rather than a favour. The research-fab hold stops being the right home for the flow when an industrial packager will take that yearly wafer count as a real run — 20 wafers, or 100 — instead of a volume that never pays the tool.

The Teardown: Why the Spare Part Prints on the Same Clock as the Wafer

A scanner is bought once. The parts that keep an EUV or DUV tool printing are bought for as long as that tool runs. TradingKey, citing South Korean outlet The Elec, reports that ASML will apply a uniform 10 percent price increase to replacement parts for EUV and DUV lithography equipment supplied to the South Korean market. The list covers regularly purchased consumables and major components replaced because of faults or performance loss. The new prices apply to parts delivered starting January 2027. Procurement departments at Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix have accepted the increase.

That split is the mechanism. The scanner is capital equipment. Wear items are an operating purchase: consumables on a schedule, larger assemblies when they fail or drift. SK Hynix is expanding advanced DRAM and HBM capacity, so more tools on the floor mean more of those parts in the buy. The Elec's account, via TradingKey, ties the maintenance impact to how much equipment is installed, how many components are procured, and how service contracts are written. Depreciation, materials, labor and energy stay in the wafer-cost stack beside that parts line. A flat 10 percent does not replace those other lines. It raises the line that grows when utilization grows.

Second-quarter figures at SK Hynix show the margin this increase has to live inside. Revenue was 79.32 trillion won, up 257 percent year over year. Operating profit was 60.54 trillion won, up 557 percent year over year, for an operating margin of about 76 percent. That margin is what memory pricing cleared against the cost stack before January 2027 deliveries. Adding scanners while taking a uniform increase on every replacement part stops clearing its own cost when extra tool-hours push the parts invoice up faster than the memory price that produced that 76 percent operating margin.

The Teardown: Why the Replay Never Sees the Missing Call

A graphics trace benchmark does not launch the application. It replays a recorded stream of graphics API calls and times how quickly the driver and the GPU finish that fixed script. The result is a playback rate. It is not a training step, and it is not a measure of how a chip moves tensors.

ServeTheHome reports that the Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation has released SPECviewperf 15.0.1 Linux Edition, the first Linux Edition in almost half a decade. SPEC first shipped a Linux build in 2019, as SPECviewperf 13, because many workstation applications are OpenGL-based or are not available on Linux at all. The new Linux suite carries 8 of the Windows edition's 11 benchmarks. The traces are from Blender 3.6, 3DEXPERIENCE CATIA and CATIA V5, Creo 9, Maya 2025, Solidworks 2024, and NX 2406, plus visualizations from OpendTect and the Tuvok library. Against the previous Linux suite, 2020 3.0, Blender is new and the CATIA, Creo, Maya, Solidworks, and NX workloads are updated. There is no DirectX path.

That gap is the mechanism. A replay can time only calls that were captured. If the application never issued the call on the platform SPEC ported, the call is not in the script, and no driver optimization on the machine under test can make it appear. The Linux score therefore describes the OpenGL subset in those eight traces, on the CPU architecture the binary actually runs. This release adds native Arm AArch64 support, the first time the graphics benchmark has been natively available on an Arm-based system, so the timed path need not include an x86 translation layer. The score still says nothing about a collective, a memory stack, or a rack.

The Linux edition stops being the right instrument when the frames that matter live in DirectX, or in one of the three Windows traces this port leaves out. Those calls never enter the script, so they cannot show up in the result. Source: servethehome.com

The Teardown: When the Module Price Is the Allocation

A finished memory module does not record whether its die was bid against an accelerator. The choice is made at wafer start and in the back-end that turns a die into something a board can accept. High-bandwidth parts take advanced DRAM, stacking and test time. Ordinary DRAM and NAND still need cleanroom hours, probe capacity, substrates and module assembly from an industry already steering people and tools toward the higher-margin AI package. When that shared capacity tightens, the line that used to be a small item on a bill of materials becomes the line that sets the shelf price.

TechPowerUp, reporting Circana figures that analyst Mat Piscatella shared on Bluesky, measured the US console market for 2026 through August. Xbox hardware unit sales were down 33 percent from a year earlier. The average price paid for a new Xbox was 529 dollars. A new PlayStation averaged 597 dollars. Total hardware unit sales in August were 559,000, the lowest August total since 2013. Piscatella tied the slump to price hikes that followed the memory and storage shortage.

The mechanism is a queue. A fab books a wafer start well ahead of the shelf. A start aimed at a stacked DRAM die does not later become a console chip. A start that is standard DRAM or NAND still competes for testers, engineers and often the same substrate vendors the AI package is already loading. The device maker then meets two constraints at once: a higher contract price, and a shipment slot that can slip after that price is accepted. Unit sales fall when either constraint binds. The average selling price in the Circana data is what remains after the buyer who would not pay has already left the sample.

That pass-through stops clearing the market when the missing constraint is no longer price. If the module is simply not on the line — testers full, substrates late, NAND held for data-centre drives — a discount does not create a unit. Availability, not the sticker, is what then decides whether a launch weekend ships.
