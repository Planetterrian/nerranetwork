Ocean racing, explained properly — the Offshore North glossary

55 terms across 5 themes: the class and its rules, the boat, racing and navigation, the races on the calendar, and the money behind it all — written for someone who has never touched a rope and wants to follow the sport anyway. Each card ends on the one sentence you could repeat at the dock. The companion to the Offshore North podcast and its campaign dashboard.

The class and its rules

What an IMOCA is, why the boats look different from one another, and how the four-year championship decides who gets to the Vendée Globe start line.

IMOCAalso: IMOCA 60

The 60-foot solo ocean-racing monohull — the Vendée Globe boat.

IMOCA is the International Monohull Open Class Association, the body that writes the rule for the sixty-foot (18.28 m) boats that race the Vendée Globe, the Route du Rhum and The Ocean Race. The rule is a box, not a blueprint: length, draught (4.50 m), mast height (29 m) and a set of safety and stability tests are fixed, and inside those limits a designer can do almost anything. Modern IMOCAs weigh around eight to nine tonnes, carry a canting keel and foils, and can hold thirty knots for hours.

An IMOCA is a sixty-foot carbon prototype built to a box of limits, not to a drawing — which is why no two look alike.

Box rulealso: Open class

A rule that fixes the limits, not the design.

A box rule sets the outside of the box — maximum length, draught, mast height, minimum stability — and leaves the inside to the designer. It is the opposite of a one-design class, where every boat is identical and only the sailors differ. The trade-off is money: an open rule rewards the best-funded design office, so the IMOCA class standardises the most expensive and most breakable parts to keep costs in check.

A box rule says how big the boat may be; it does not say what the boat must be.

One-design partsalso: The shared components

The mast and keel fin every IMOCA must buy from the same supplier.

Since the 2013 rule cycle the class has required the mast and the keel fin — and a short list of rigging parts around them — to be one-design: identical parts from an approved supplier, at a fixed price. The reasoning was reliability and cost, not fairness — dismastings and keel failures had been the class's two most dangerous and most expensive breakages, and a standard part is a tested part. Everything else — hull, foils, cockpit, sails — stays open.

The bits that used to fall off are the bits everyone now shares.

IMOCA Globe Seriesalso: The championship

The four-year points championship whose ranking decides Vendée Globe places.

The Globe Series is the class championship run across the whole cycle between two Vendée Globes. Designated races carry points weighted by grade — the Vendée Globe itself the most, then the big transatlantics, then the shorter events — and the standings are what the Vendée Globe organisers look at when the fleet is oversubscribed. For a campaign that means every race entered is also a qualifying investment.

Every finish banks points, and the points table is the queue for the start line.

Race gradesalso: Grade 1 to Grade 4

How the class weights a race's difficulty and its points.

Designated races are graded. The highest grade is the Vendée Globe itself; solo transatlantics such as the Route du Rhum sit in the next tier; double-handed and crewed events and the shorter regattas below that. A skipper's Vendée Globe qualification hinges on finishing a solo race of a sufficient grade on the boat they will race around the world.

The grade tells you how much a race counts — and whether it counts as a qualifier.

Qualifying for the Vendée Globe 2028also: The published outline

One solo Grade-2 finish on the race boat, then be high enough in the championship.

The organisers' published outline for 2028 has three parts. A skipper must complete at least one Grade 2 solo race from the IMOCA 2025–2028 calendar on the boat they will race in the Vendée Globe, finishing within twice the winner's time. Selection then takes the top 37 of the IMOCA Globe Series, with the remaining places filled under the organisers' own criteria. The fleet is capped at forty boats. The fine mechanics — exact points tables, the designated-race list, wild cards — are published by the organisers and change between editions.

Finish a solo qualifier on your own boat, then be in the top thirty-seven — and there are only forty places.

Wild card

An organiser's discretionary place outside the points ranking.

When the ranking has filled its share of the fleet, the organisers keep a small number of places to award on their own criteria — a notable campaign, a nation not yet represented, a returning champion. A wild card is never something a campaign can plan on, which is why every serious programme races for points first.

A wild card is a door the organisers can open; the points table is the door you can open yourself.

The forty-boat cap

Why there is a queue for the Vendée Globe at all.

The Vendée Globe fleet is limited to forty boats, and the class has more credible campaigns than places. That single number is what turns every 2026 and 2027 race into a selection event: a finish is not just a result, it is a place in the ranking that decides who lines up in Les Sables d'Olonne.

Forty places, more than forty campaigns — that is the whole story of the next two seasons.

Sail numberalso: CAN 80

The national prefix and number on the mainsail — a boat's licence plate.

Every IMOCA carries a nation code and a number: EMIRA IV races as CAN 80. The number follows the hull, not the sponsor name, so a boat that has been renamed three times can still be traced through results by its sail number. That is how you find that the Canadian boat was once Mālama and then Groupe Dubreuil.

Sponsor names change with the money; the sail number follows the hull.

The boat

Foils, keels, wing masts, energy and the capsize test — the machinery that lets one person hold a sixty-footer at highway speed for weeks.

Foils

Wings in the water that lift the hull until drag collapses and speed roughly doubles.

A foil is a curved carbon wing that sticks out of the side of the hull and, as speed builds, generates lift the way an aircraft wing does. Lift takes weight off the hull; less hull in the water means less drag; less drag means more speed, which means more lift. An IMOCA does not fly completely — the class rule bars lifting surfaces on the rudders, so the boats skim rather than fly — but the effect is still dramatic: foiling IMOCAs sustain speeds the previous generation could only touch surfing down a wave.

Foils don't make the boat float better; they make it fly — just enough to leave most of the drag behind.

Foil generations

Why a 2019 foil and a 2024 foil are different animals.

Foils have grown in span and in the amount of lift they carry with each design cycle, and the boats around them have changed to match: stronger hulls, more volume forward to stop the bow burying at speed, and cockpits that protect the skipper from the loads. Talking about a boat's foil generation is shorthand for how much of the modern performance envelope it can reach — and a refit that swaps a foil generation is genuine news for a campaign.

A new foil changes what the boat can do; a new sponsor changes only what it is called.

Why foils break

Loads, collisions and the sea — the class's most common race-changing damage.

A foil carries several tonnes of lift through a slot in the hull, so anything it hits — a whale, a container, a sleeping seal, a floating log — arrives with enormous force. Damage to the foil itself, to its bearings or to the hydraulic ram that moves it is the single most common reason a modern IMOCA finishes a race on one side only. Sébastien Simon's third place in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe on one foil, and Team Malizia's collision in the 2026 Ocean Race Atlantic, are the two cases this show has covered.

The foil is the strongest part of the boat and the easiest to lose.

Canting keel

A keel that swings sideways so the ballast works harder.

A conventional keel hangs straight down. A canting keel pivots at the hull and swings its several-tonne bulb out to the windward side, which pushes the boat upright against the force of the sails and lets it carry far more sail area for the same stability. The keel fin is a one-design part; the hydraulic rams that swing it are one of the systems a solo skipper listens to at night.

Swinging the keel to windward is like a sailor hiking out — except the sailor weighs three tonnes.

Water ballast

Seawater pumped into tanks to add weight where it helps.

Alongside the keel, IMOCAs carry tanks that can be filled with seawater and emptied again. Filling a windward tank adds righting moment without adding permanent weight; filling an aft tank keeps the bow up when the boat is being driven hard downwind. Managing ballast is one of the invisible jobs that separates a fast skipper from a merely brave one.

Ballast is weight you can borrow from the sea and give back.

Wing mastalso: Rotating mast

A mast shaped like a wing that turns to face the wind.

The IMOCA mast is a one-design carbon spar with a wing-shaped section that rotates so its leading edge lines up with the airflow. That makes the front of the mainsail cleaner and the whole rig more efficient than a fixed round tube. Its height is capped by the class rule.

The mast is part of the sail, and it turns to prove it.

The cockpitalso: The cuddy

From an open tiller in the rain to a wheel behind a windscreen.

Early Vendée Globe boats were steered from an open cockpit. The foiling generation slams into waves at speeds that would injure an exposed skipper, so modern IMOCAs enclose the working area under a carbon roof with windows — skippers spend most of a race inside, steering on the autopilot, adjusting sails from winches under cover, and going on deck only when they must.

The faster the boats got, the further inside the skipper moved.

Hydrogenerator

A small propeller on the stern that turns boat speed into electricity.

An IMOCA runs on electricity — autopilot, instruments, communications, the keel and foil hydraulics — and a solo boat cannot burn fuel for weeks. A hydrogenerator is a propeller lowered into the wake that spins a generator; with solar panels on deck and a small engine-driven alternator as backup, it keeps the batteries up for the whole race. Turning it into a reliable system was one of the quiet revolutions of the last two decades.

The boat's own speed charges the batteries that steer the boat.

Sail wardrobealso: J0, J1, J2, J3

Headsails named by size, and why the choice is the whole race.

An IMOCA carries a mainsail plus a set of headsails flown from the bow: the big light-wind sails at the front of the boat, the mid-sized J2 on the structural forestay, and small heavy-weather jibs as the wind builds. Each has a wind range. Race rules limit how many sails may be aboard, so the wardrobe a skipper packs before the start is a bet on the weather for weeks ahead.

Packing the sails is the first routing decision of the race.

The capsize testalso: Self-righting

Every IMOCA must prove it can come back upright on its own.

Before it can race, an IMOCA is pulled over with a crane until it is upside down and must right itself without outside help — using the canting keel, water ballast and the hull's own shape. It is the class's foundational safety rule, and the reason the boats have the volume and deck shapes they do.

An IMOCA has to prove it can get up before it is allowed to fall over.

Autopilot

The crew member that steers most of the race.

A solo skipper cannot stand at the wheel for weeks, so an electronic pilot steers almost all of the time, holding a course or a wind angle from the boat's sensors. Modern pilots are tuned like race cars — anticipating waves, easing off in a gust — and the skipper's real job becomes managing sails, ballast, weather and sleep around it.

The human decides where to go; the pilot does the driving.

Refit

The winter in the shed where races are actually won.

Between seasons a boat comes out of the water for months: structural inspection, new foils or bearings, electronics, a repaint. A refit that changes a boat's performance — a foil generation, a new bow, a lighter deck — is a story; a refit that only repairs is maintenance. The distinction is the difference between a news item and a filler item.

The refit shed is the pit lane: quiet, expensive, and where the season is decided.

24-hour record

Distance covered in one day — the class's favourite speed benchmark.

Because ocean races are decided over weeks, the sport measures pure speed by the greatest distance sailed in twenty-four hours. Modern foiling IMOCAs have pushed the solo mark past six hundred nautical miles — a day's run that would once have needed a full crew on a much larger boat. The hull that became EMIRA IV set a solo record of that kind under Sébastien Simon.

Six hundred miles in a day, alone: that is what the foils bought.

Racing and navigation

Weather routing, the Southern Ocean, sleep, ice gates and what 'unassisted' actually means.

Weather routing

Choosing the fastest road, not the shortest — the same job as flight planning.

Routing software takes a weather forecast and the boat's speed table and works out the track that gets there soonest, which is rarely the straight line: a longer course through stronger wind beats a shorter one through calm. The catch is that the forecast is only a forecast. Skippers, like airline captains, weigh a fast-looking route against how much they trust the model behind it.

The shortest line is almost never the fastest one.

GRIB filesalso: The five-day wall

Compressed forecast data — and why nothing beyond a few days is worth betting on.

A GRIB is the compressed grid of forecast wind and pressure that a boat downloads by satellite. Different models (the American, the European, the French) disagree, and all of them degrade with time: a routing based on day five of a forecast is a guess dressed as a plan. Good skippers route for the next two or three days and keep options open beyond that.

A forecast is a fact for tomorrow, an opinion for the day after, and a rumour for next week.

Polars

The boat's speed table: how fast it goes at every wind angle and strength.

A polar is a chart of the boat's expected speed for every combination of wind speed and wind angle. Routing software multiplies polars by the forecast to find the fastest track, so a skipper who knows their polars are optimistic will route conservatively. Foiling boats have famously spiky polars — very fast in a narrow band, ordinary outside it.

The polar is the boat's honest CV, and the routing software believes every word of it.

Antarctic Exclusion Zonealso: AEZ, ice gate

The virtual fence that keeps the fleet north of the ice.

In the Southern Ocean the fastest route is the shortest one — far south, close to Antarctica — and that is where the icebergs are. Race organisers draw a line of virtual waypoints around the bottom of the world that no boat may cross south of, moved edition by edition with the ice charts. It costs distance and it saves lives; it replaced the older system of a few fixed 'ice gates'.

The exclusion zone is a fence around the bottom of the world, drawn by the ice.

A race with no course

Why the Vendée Globe has a start, a finish, three capes and almost nothing else.

Ocean races are not sailed round buoys. The Vendée Globe's course is 'leave Les Sables d'Olonne, leave the Cape of Good Hope, Cape Leeuwin and Cape Horn to port, come back' — plus the exclusion zone. Everything else is the skipper's choice, which is why two boats can be a thousand miles apart and still be racing each other.

Three capes to port, the ice to starboard, and the whole ocean to choose from.

The doldrumsalso: ITCZ

The belt of calms and squalls near the equator that every transatlantic must cross.

Where the trade winds of the two hemispheres meet, the air rises rather than blows, leaving a band of light winds, thunderstorms and sudden squalls. Crossing it is a lottery every skipper tries to rig: pick the narrowest point, arrive with speed, and hope. Leads built over a week can vanish in an afternoon there.

The doldrums are where the ocean resets the scoreboard.

The Southern Oceanalso: The Roaring Forties

The one ocean with no land to stop the wind going round.

Below forty degrees south the westerly winds circle the planet without hitting a continent, so the low-pressure systems are bigger, the seas longer and the water colder than anywhere else a race goes. It is the reason the Vendée Globe is 'the Everest of the seas' rather than merely a long race, and it is what no Canadian skipper has yet completed solo.

In the Southern Ocean the wind has been blowing since Antarctica, and nothing has told it to stop.

Point Nemo

The remotest point of the course — nearer the space station than to people on land.

In the South Pacific, roughly 2,700 km from the nearest land, lies the oceanic pole of inaccessibility. When a skipper passes it, the closest other humans can be the crew of the International Space Station overhead. It is the emotional bottom of the Vendée Globe: no rescue is quick, and every decision is the skipper's alone.

At Point Nemo the nearest help is in orbit.

Sleepalso: Twenty minutes at a time

How a solo skipper gets through three months of nights.

A solo skipper cannot sleep through the night, so they sleep in fragments — often around twenty minutes at a time, many times a day, timed between sail changes and checks. Sleep management is trained like a physical skill, with specialists and pre-race protocols, because fatigue is what turns a small mistake into a lost race or a lost mast.

The race is won by whoever manages the least sleep best.

Solo, double-handed, crewed

The three formats, and why the same boat races all three.

IMOCAs race solo (the Vendée Globe, the Route du Rhum), double-handed (the Transat Café L'OR, formerly Transat Jacques Vabre) and fully crewed (The Ocean Race and its Atlantic and European events). The boat is the same; the tempo is not. Crewed racing drives the boat harder and reveals what it can do; solo racing reveals what one person can sustain.

Same boat, three sports — the difference is how many people are awake.

On Board Reporteralso: OBR

The fifth person aboard in The Ocean Race, who films but may not sail.

The Ocean Race requires each crewed IMOCA to carry a media crew member who shoots, edits and sends the story home by satellite but is forbidden from helping to sail the boat. The OBR is why the sport has become a daily video diary — and why the crewed races feel closer than the solo ones, where the skipper is their own reporter.

The OBR can film the sail change but not touch the rope.

Jury rig

The improvised mast that gets a dismasted boat home.

When a mast comes down, the skipper cuts the wreckage free before it holes the hull, then builds something to hang a sail from — the boom, a spinnaker pole, whatever survived. A jury rig might make five knots instead of twenty-five, but it can cover thousands of miles, and finishing a Vendée Globe under one is a legend in its own right.

A jury rig is a mast made of what is left.

Unassistedalso: Non-stop, without assistance

What the Vendée Globe's three defining words actually permit.

Non-stop means the boat cannot put into port. Unassisted means the skipper may not receive outside help — no parts, no crew, no shore routing advice — and must make every repair alone with what is aboard. A skipper may anchor to make a repair, but any assistance from another person ends the race. The rule is why a Vendée Globe finish is an engineering feat as much as a sailing one.

You may stop the boat; you may never let anyone touch it.

Elapsed time

First home wins — there is no handicap in IMOCA racing.

Many yacht races correct each boat's time for its rating, so a slower design can win on paper. IMOCA racing is elapsed time only: the first boat across the line wins, full stop. That is why design matters so much, why the newest foilers are favourites, and why an older boat's honest goal is often a strong finish rather than a win.

In this class the clock is the only judge.
▶ Heard on Ep 5 at 3:47

Virtual waypoints

Marks that exist only on the chart-plotter — how races are shaped at sea.

Race committees increasingly define courses with waypoints that are just coordinates: a virtual start line offshore, a mark to leave to port in the middle of the Bay of Biscay, an exclusion zone. The Défi Azimut's 48-hour race is built from them each year to fit the forecast, and The Ocean Race Atlantic started from a virtual line 145 miles off New York.

The mark is a number on a screen, and rounding it still counts.

The races

The calendar the campaign lives by: the Vendée Globe, the Route du Rhum and the events in between.

Vendée Globe

Solo, non-stop, unassisted around the world, every four years from Les Sables d'Olonne.

First sailed in 1989, the Vendée Globe is the only solo non-stop round-the-world race, roughly 24,000 nautical miles from Les Sables d'Olonne in western France, south around the three great capes and home. The 2024–25 edition was won by Charlie Dalin in a record 64 days 19 hours. The next start is 12 November 2028 — the date this show counts down to every week.

One person, one boat, around the world without stopping — and no Canadian has yet finished it.

Route du Rhumalso: Route du Rhum – Destination Guadeloupe

Solo across the Atlantic from Saint-Malo to Guadeloupe, all classes on one start line, every four years.

Created in 1978 and won that first year by a Canadian, Mike Birch, by 98 seconds, the Route du Rhum starts every four years from Saint-Malo in Brittany and finishes about 3,540 nautical miles later in Pointe-à-Pitre. The 2026 edition starts on 1 November at 13:02 with 118 solo skippers across six classes — ULTIM, Ocean Fifty, IMOCA, Class40, Vintage Multi and Vintage Mono — and includes Scott Shawyer's first solo transatlantic on a foiling IMOCA.

Every class, one start line, one ocean — and a Canadian won the first one.

Défi Azimutalso: Défi Azimut-Lorient Agglomération

The September test bench in Lorient: speed runs, a 48-hour solo race and a crewed lap of Groix.

Each September the IMOCA fleet gathers in Lorient for three events in a week: timed speed runs over a nautical mile, the 48H Azimut solo race around waypoints in the Bay of Biscay, and the crewed Tour de Groix. It is the last look at the fleet before the autumn's big race and the place where new boats and new foils are measured against each other. In 2026 Sam Goodchild won the 48H and set a Runs record at 32.1 knots.

Lorient in September is where the fleet finds out who did their homework.
Source ↗

Vendée Arctique

The solo race north from Les Sables d'Olonne toward the Arctic Circle.

A solo IMOCA race that sends the fleet north from Les Sables d'Olonne into the cold, light and often windless waters below the Arctic Circle and back. It is a qualifier-grade test of a skipper alone on a big boat, and in 2026 it produced the season's breakout result: Ambrogio Beccaria's win on Allagrande Mapei in his first solo race on the boat.

The Arctique is the Vendée Globe's cold, short rehearsal.
Source ↗

The Ocean Racealso: The Ocean Race Atlantic, The Ocean Race Europe

The crewed round-the-world race, and its new Atlantic and European events.

The Ocean Race — once the Whitbread, then the Volvo — is the crewed round-the-world race in legs, sailed by IMOCAs since 2023, when the boat now called EMIRA IV won it as Mālama. Its shorter formats, The Ocean Race Europe and the inaugural Ocean Race Atlantic of September 2026 (New York to Lorient, won by United by the Ocean by under nine minutes after eight days), fill the years between editions with crewed racing on the same boats.

Same boats, full crews, and the finish can come down to minutes after a week at sea.
▶ Heard on Ep 4 at 3:51▶ Heard on Ep 3 at 3:49 Source ↗

Transat Café L'ORalso: Transat Jacques Vabre

The double-handed transatlantic from Le Havre, sailed in odd years.

The autumn double-handed transatlantic, sailed every two years from Le Havre to the Caribbean or Brazil depending on the edition, was the Transat Jacques Vabre for three decades and now carries the Café L'OR name. It is the class's big two-up race and a frequent first ocean race for a new boat or a new skipper pairing.

Two people, one boat, the Atlantic in November.

New York Vendéealso: New York Vendée – Les Sables d'Olonne

The solo transatlantic from New York to Les Sables d'Olonne — Scott Shawyer's first solo IMOCA race, in 2024.

Sailed in the spring before a Vendée Globe, the New York Vendée is a solo race eastward across the Atlantic to the Vendée Globe's home port and a late qualifying opportunity for the fleet. Scott Shawyer finished fourth in his first solo IMOCA race there in 2024, on his previous boat.

Eastbound, alone, into the port the whole sport is aimed at.

Rolex Fastnet Race

The biennial classic from the Solent round the Fastnet Rock — where IMOCAs race the rest of the world.

A roughly 695-mile race from the English south coast to the Fastnet Rock off Ireland and on to Cherbourg, sailed every two years by everything from club cruisers to Ultims. IMOCAs use it as a summer training race and a shakedown; EMIRA IV retired from the 2025 edition under Canada Ocean Racing.

The Fastnet is the one weekend the whole of sailing races the same course.

The ladderalso: Mini 6.50 → Figaro → Class40 / IMOCA → Ultim

How French skippers come up, and why a Canadian from industry is an outlier.

The sport's professional path runs through the Mini 6.50 (a 6.5-metre solo transatlantic class), the one-design Figaro circuit, then Class40 or a first IMOCA, and for a few, the Ultim trimarans. Most of the fleet came that way. A skipper who arrives from business and engineering, buys a podium boat and learns it system by system — as Scott Shawyer has — is taking a different road to the same start line.

Most skippers climb the ladder; some build their own.

Class40

The affordable forty-footer — the biggest fleet on the Route du Rhum start line.

A twelve-metre monohull built to a cost-capped rule, Class40 is where many professional careers and most self-funded ones happen. Forty-nine of them are entered in the 2026 Route du Rhum, more than any other class, which is why the class is called the sport's proving ground.

Class40 is where the sport is widest; IMOCA is where it is tallest.
▶ Heard on Ep 5 at 3:47

Ocean Fifty

Fifty-foot trimarans — one class, whatever an old article calls it.

The Ocean Fifty is the class of 50-foot (15.24 m) offshore trimarans, formerly the Multi50. It is one of the six classes on the 2026 Route du Rhum start line with eleven entries. There is no separate 'Class 50': that name and Ocean Fifty refer to the same boats.

Ocean Fifty and 'Class 50' are the same eleven boats.
▶ Heard on Ep 5 at 3:47 Source ↗

Ultimalso: ULTIM

The 32-metre flying trimarans — a different sport at three times the speed.

The Ultims are the largest and fastest ocean racers: 32-metre foiling trimarans that fly fully clear of the water and cross the Atlantic in days rather than weeks. Six of them start the 2026 Route du Rhum and will reach Guadeloupe long before the monohulls. Moving 'up to Ultim' is the ambition Jérémie Beyou announced in September 2026.

An Ultim is what happens when a trimaran stops touching the sea.
▶ Heard on Ep 5 at 3:47

Vintage Multi and Vintage Monoalso: formerly Rhum Multi / Rhum Mono

The Route du Rhum's two categories for historic and out-of-class boats.

Alongside the four professional classes, the Route du Rhum keeps two categories for boats that do not fit a modern class rule — older multihulls and monohulls with their own histories, some of them veterans of earlier editions. The organisers renamed them Vintage Multi and Vintage Mono for 2026; older coverage says Rhum Multi and Rhum Mono. Thirteen boats are entered in each.

Six classes on the line, and two of them are the race's own history sailing again.

The business of the sport

Who pays, why boats are named after companies, and how a podium boat ends up with a new flag on its transom.

Title sponsorship

Why the boat is called after a company — and why that is a media buy, not charity.

In France offshore racing is a mainstream sport, so a company that pays for a campaign puts its name on the boat and gets months of prime-time coverage: the Vendée Globe start, the tracker, the daily video. An IMOCA campaign costs millions of euros a year, and it is bought as advertising that has to out-perform the alternatives. That is why the fleet is French-funded, why the calendar bends around French ports, and why an English-language audience has to work to follow it.

The boat's name is an invoice, and the race is the campaign.
▶ Heard on Ep 6 at 4:02

Entry fees and the cost of a start

Why even entering a race is a decision.

Major races charge substantial entry fees, and behind each start sit the delivery to the port, the shore crew, insurance, the safety kit and the race village presence sponsors expect. A campaign choosing which events to sail is choosing where to spend a fixed budget for the most points, the most learning and the most exposure.

Every start line has a price, and a campaign's calendar is its budget in disguise.

The second-hand marketalso: How a podium boat changes hands

Why last cycle's winners become next cycle's newcomers' boats.

A new IMOCA is a multi-year, multi-million-euro build, so most campaigns start with a proven boat sold on by a team that has ordered a new one. A podium hull from the last Vendée Globe is the classic purchase: fast, debugged, and available the year after the race. EMIRA IV — Ocean Race winner as Mālama, third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe as Groupe Dubreuil — is exactly that kind of boat.

In this class the newcomer's boat is often the champion's last one.

The new-build boom

A record number of new boats being built while sponsorship tightens.

The cycle after a Vendée Globe is when new boats are commissioned, and the 2028 cycle has produced an unusually long order book for the French yards — at the same time as several established skippers have publicly gone looking for new partners. More boats and less money is the class's current tension, and it decides who can afford to be on the start line in 2028.

The yards are full and the sponsors are careful — that is the class's whole business story right now.
▶ Heard on Ep 6 at 4:02

Youth pathwayalso: Pathway Programme

How a campaign grows the next skippers as well as the current one.

Several campaigns run programmes that put young sailors through the offshore ladder — coaching, deliveries, a first race. Canada Ocean Racing's Pathway Programme does that for young Canadians alongside the Vendée Globe campaign, which is the reason the team talks about a first Canadian finish as a beginning rather than an end.

A pathway programme is a campaign investing in its own successors.
