# Offshore North — Field Guide

**Purpose:** Injected into every episode prompt as domain background, alongside the standing facts. ## The sport's shape

Offshore ocean racing's professional ladder runs Mini 6.50 → Figaro → Class40 / IMOCA → Ultim. The IMOCA 60 is the Vendée Globe class and this show's centre of gravity. Around it: Class40 (the affordable proving ground, ~12 metres), Ocean Fifty (50-foot trimarans), and the Ultims (30-metre flying trimarans — a different sport at triple the speed). The Vendée Globe — solo, non-stop, unassisted around the world — is the sport's Everest; the Route du Rhum — solo across the Atlantic, all classes at once — is its great festival.

Nearly all of it is bankrolled, raced, and reported in French. That asymmetry is this show's reason to exist.

## Vendée Globe 2028 — the published qualification outline

The outline of the 2028 rules is published on both imoca.org and vendeeglobe.org (this SOURCES the standing facts' open item — the system may now be described in these terms, and no further):

- A skipper must **complete at least one Grade 2 solo race** from the IMOCA 2025–2028 calendar **on the boat they will race in the Vendée Globe**, finishing within **twice the winner's time**.
- Entry selection: be among the **top 37 in the IMOCA Globe Series** championship (plus the remaining places under the organisers' criteria).
- The fleet is **capped at 40 boats**.

Finer mechanics (points tables, exact designated-race list, wild cards) still need in-week sourcing before airing. The strategic consequence for the Canadian campaign is airable: every solo race finished on EMIRA IV both banks Globe Series points and can tick the solo-qualifier box — which is what makes races like the Route du Rhum count double.

## The people who matter in this cycle

- **Charlie Dalin** won the 2024–25 Vendée Globe in a record 64 days 19 hours — nine days inside the old record — while being treated for a gastrointestinal cancer he had raced with for over two years. **He died on 11 June 2026, aged 42.** This is the sport's defining loss of the cycle. Handle with dignity: facts, no melodrama, and never as a segue device. When the 2028 race is discussed in France, it is discussed in his shadow.
- **Yoann Richomme** — 2nd in that Vendée (65d 18h), a double Route du Rhum Class40 winner before moving up; front-rank favourite class-wide.
- **Sébastien Simon** — 3rd, on the boat that is now EMIRA IV, sailing much of the course on one foil. Launched his new **Groupe Dubreuil II** in August 2026. He is the show's connective tissue: his old boat carries the Canadian campaign, his new one shows where the class is going.
- Others whose names recur: **Boris Herrmann** (Malizia — Germany's star), **Sam Goodchild** and **Pip Hare** (the British contingent), **Justine Mettraux** (top Swiss finisher), **Violette Dorange** (the 2024–25 race's youngest finisher and its breakout star), **Jean Le Cam** (the fleet's elder and folk hero), **Conrad Colman** (NZ/US, finished a Vendée under jury rig). ## The Canadian lineage — this show's home ground

Four chapters, and the show should know all of them cold:

1. **Mike Birch won the very first Route du Rhum, 1978** — a Canadian, 47 years old, in the little yellow 12-metre trimaran *Olympus Photo*, run down and passed Michel Malinovsky's much larger monohull *Kriter V* in sight of the Guadeloupe finish to win by **98 seconds** after 23 days at sea. The closest great finish in ocean racing history; it began the multihull era; the French never forgot him. He died in 2022 at 90. **The card to play for Route du Rhum coverage:** a Canadian won the first edition of the race a Canadian now sails toward — use it as the frame for the RdR arc, deployed properly once, not sprinkled weekly.
2. **Gerry Roufs, Montreal — the sport took him.** Lying second in the 1996–97 Vendée Globe, his last message from the deep South Pacific described 15-metre seas and winds above 80 knots; he was never heard from again (January 1997). Isabelle Autissier diverted to search in vain; wreckage of *Groupe LG 2* reached the Chilean coast months later. No Canadian angle on the Vendée is honest without him.
3. **Derek Hatfield carried the flag next** — Canadian solo racer who campaigned *Spirit of Canada* through the round-the-world races of the 2000s and started a Vendée Globe; his attempts fell short of a Vendée finish (exact races and retirement details: confirm before use). He died in 2016.
4. **Scott Shawyer is chapter four.** No Canadian has ever completed the Vendée Globe. That is the sentence the whole show hangs from.

## Why France owns this sport (the business background)

Offshore racing is a mainstream professional sport in France and a niche one everywhere else: Vendée Globe starts draw crowds in the hundreds of thousands to Les Sables d'Olonne (exact figures: confirm before quoting), skippers are household names, and title sponsorship of an IMOCA campaign — typically several million euros a year, with top campaigns well beyond that (band figures only; confirm specifics) — is bought as prime-time brand media, not charity. This is why boats carry sponsor names as their names, why the calendar bends around French ports and French school holidays, and why an English-language show has a gap to fill. The business of the sport — who can afford to race, what a foil costs, why teams share designs — is genuinely dramatic and chronically under-covered in English.

## The storyteller's physics bank (one-liners to build from — expand any of these properly in Plain Sailing, never all at once)

- Foils don't make the boat float better; they make it **fly** — lifting hull weight out of the water until drag collapses and speed doubles.
- A solo skipper sleeps in **20-minute fragments** while the autopilot — which steers far more of the race than the human does — holds a 60-foot boat at highway speed through the night.
- Everything on board is rationed energy: **5,000+ calories a day** in freeze-dried bags, drinking water made from the sea, electricity from hydrogenerators and solar.
- Weather routing is the same job as airline flight planning — pressure systems, trade-offs between the short road and the fast road, decisions on incomplete information (this is Dan's native frame; the field guide licenses the parallel, the one-reference budget still applies).
- Modern connectivity (high-bandwidth satellite links) turned the loneliest sport on earth into a daily video diary — the storytelling revolution is recent and worth explaining.
- The sea still wins sometimes: the class's self-righting requirement, the rescue culture where rivals divert for each other (Roufs's search; the famous 2020-race rescue of a skipper from a life raft by Jean Le Cam — details: confirm before use), and why "everyone finishes the rescue conversation before the racing one."

## The three audiences — and the bridges between them

The show is built for three rings at once, and the outer ring pays for the inner two:

1. **Offshore fans** — they want the depth the English press doesn't give them.
2. **Casual and recreational sailors** — they know a boom from a bow but not a Globe Series point; they are the natural core audience and the easiest to lose with insider stakes.
3. **Curious general listeners** — the people who watch mountaineering documentaries, follow Formula 1, listen to adventure and endurance stories. They have never sailed and never will, and the show must work for them anyway.

The evidence that ring 3 exists is overwhelming: mainstream outlets cover this sport as HUMAN QUEST, never as sailing results (a national newspaper put the Canadian campaign on its front sports page framed entirely around life, death, and a dream); the Vendée Globe's virtual race game draws players in the hundreds of thousands to millions per edition (exact figure: confirm before use); and a solo sailor's death led national news in France. The audience is there when the story is told as a story.

**The bridges — how sport-internal stakes translate into universal ones. Use these as thinking tools; never as stock phrases, and never more than one or two per episode:**
- The Vendée Globe ↔ Everest: the hardest thing in the discipline, where finishing at all is the achievement.
- An IMOCA ↔ a Formula 1 car: a carbon prototype at the edge of the rules, sailed by one person, at highway speed, for weeks.
- Weather routing ↔ flight planning (Dan's native frame): the same decisions, the same maps, no engines.
- The refit shed ↔ the pit lane: races are won in the work between them.
- Point Nemo ↔ deep isolation: at the remotest point of the course the nearest humans can be the crew of the space station overhead.
- Sleep, fatigue, fear, self-reliance ↔ everyone's life: the skipper's 20-minute sleep and 3 a.m. decisions are extreme versions of universal experience — the sport is a laboratory for questions any listener has.
- Sponsorship ↔ media buying: a campaign is a business decision that has to out-perform advertising, which is why the money story is a story.

**The cast principle:** casual audiences follow PEOPLE. Introduce recurring skippers the way a serial drama introduces characters — each gets one vivid, factual identifier when they appear, so a returning listener recognises them and a new listener places them instantly. Build the recurring cast over weeks; never assume a name is known.

**The door rule:** every episode must work for someone who has never sailed and has no tie to Canada. Canada is the doorway into a universal quest — one person, alone, trying to sail around the world without stopping — not a nationality requirement. If a segment's stakes only land for someone who already knows the class politics, reframe the stakes first in ring-3 terms, then add the depth for ring 1.
