Produce a written essay-style brief covering all six segments below. The downstream podcast prompt will turn this into flowing narration — keep the tone narrative, not bullet-pointed. Each segment should read like prose paragraphs, not lists.

### Segment 1 — The Cold Open
2–3 sentences. Open with a specific detail taken from the research sources — a dated decision, a measured result, a named place or person — that immediately reveals the irony. Never a scene, a moment or a person the sources do not describe: a sourced fact told plainly beats an invented scene. State the core tension: "This was designed to [X]. Instead, it caused [Y]." No generic intros. This is the spoken opening of the episode — separate from the ``**HOOK:**`` title line above.

### Segment 2 — The Good Intention
7–9 sentences. Open on the person the sources name: who created or championed this, what they had seen, and the dated decision they made. What problem were they trying to solve? What was the historical context — what made this seem like a great idea at the time? Establish empathy: rational people, reasonable decisions, the information they had.

### Segment 3 — The Implementation
7–9 sentences. How was it rolled out or adopted? What early signs of success were there (if any)? What did proponents say at the time? What did skeptics warn about, if anything? Specific dates and places.

### Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences
14–18 sentences. The core of the episode. What actually happened? Tell it through the people the sources name — who first noticed, who measured it, who lived with it — and give each finding with whose finding it is and when. Explain the causal chain — WHY did the good intention produce this outcome? — one link at a time, in the order it happened. Cover second and third-order effects. A researcher or an institution is introduced by full name and what they did, never by initials alone.

### Segment 5 — The Aftermath
7–9 sentences. What was the response once the consequences became clear? Was it reversed, recalled, redesigned? Did the fix itself create new unintended consequences (the meta-irony case)? What is the current state?

### Segment 6 — The Lesson
4–6 sentences. Extract one to three actionable principles listeners can apply to their own decisions. Each principle names THIS case's mechanism — the actor, the measure or rule, and what it rewarded — so it could not be pasted into a different episode unchanged; never open a principle with an abstract subject such as "incentive structures" or "complex systems", and never state a principle as a timeless law with "always". Close with a forward-looking question or connection to today.

CRITICAL CONSTRAINTS:
- Do not fabricate statistics, dates, or quotes. When uncertain, hedge naturally ("by most accounts…", "estimates suggest…").
- Do not include segment headers in the spoken output — the downstream prompt will weave them into natural transitions. Keep the segment markers in THIS brief so the podcast writer can see the structure.
- Do not narrate hindsight smugly. The decision-makers were doing their best. Avoid "of course" / "obviously" / "anyone could see".
- Where a specific source genuinely supports a fact, name it in the prose and record it in the claims ledger; never invent a venue, year, or document, and never give a general fact a citation shape it cannot carry.
- Length follows the sources: about 1,000–1,300 words when they are rich, less when they are thin. Every sentence carries a fact, a named person's action or a link in the causal chain the brief has not given yet; a sentence that only rephrases, sums up or reflects is cut. Never pad, and never tell a finding twice.
- A cause the sources do not establish is not asserted. When a figure describes a whole country or a whole period, the hook and the body say so, and do not hand all of it to the one policy the episode is about.
