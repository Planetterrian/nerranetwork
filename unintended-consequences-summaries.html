Ep 149: Wolves entering 29 Wisconsin counties cut deer-vehicle collisions by 24 percent each year.
Download Read transcript

Wolves entering 29 Wisconsin counties cut deer-vehicle collisions by 24 percent each year.

Segment 1 — The Cold Open

In the winter of 2009, a radio-collared wolf crossed a county road in northern Wisconsin and paused to scent-mark a stretch of pavement that also served as a deer travel corridor. Within two years, local deer began shifting their movement patterns away from that same stretch. The change registered not in wolf howls but in the county sheriff’s reduced stack of collision reports.

Segment 2 — The Good Intention

Jennifer Raynor, then a graduate student, began studying wolves because state and federal agencies had spent decades trying to bring the animals back under the Endangered Species Act. The goal was straightforward: restore an apex predator whose removal had let white-tailed deer numbers climb unchecked across the Great Lakes states. Managers and biologists viewed recovery as an ecological correction that would also reduce chronic deer damage to farms and forests. At the time, the most visible costs of wolves—livestock losses—were easy to tally and compensate, while any wider benefits remained speculative. Raynor later recalled that the costs of predators are salient and simple to measure, whereas the benefits often operate through indirect ecological pathways that are harder to quantify. Wisconsin’s wolf population had grown from a handful of animals recolonizing from Minnesota and Michigan in the 1970s, and by the early 2000s the question was no longer whether wolves could persist but how their presence would ripple outward into human systems. The Endangered Species Act listings and subsequent delisting attempts focused almost entirely on pack counts and livestock compensation formulas, leaving road-safety effects outside the formal accounting.

Segment 3 — The Implementation

Gray wolves recolonized Wisconsin naturally from Minnesota and Michigan starting in the mid-1970s, with steady growth after federal protection strengthened in the 1990s. By 2010, wolves occupied 29 of the state’s 63 counties tracked in long-term records. The state briefly delisted wolves in 2012, allowing limited hunting, then saw them relisted after legal challenges. Throughout, wildlife agencies focused on monitoring pack territories and paying verified livestock claims rather than measuring effects on roads. Department of Natural Resources maps recorded the year each county first held a resident pack, creating a natural experiment that later researchers could align with collision statistics. Early monitoring emphasized minimum wolf counts and compensation payments, which averaged roughly $3 million statewide over thirty-five years. No one at the time collected systematic before-and-after data on deer movement along the specific linear features—paved roads—that wolves began using as travel corridors.

Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences

Raynor, working with Corbett Grainger and Dominic Parker, matched wolf maps to deer-vehicle collision data from 1988 to 2010 across those 63 counties. In the 29 counties wolves entered, collisions fell 24 percent on average. Only about one-quarter of the drop traced to fewer deer overall; the larger share came from deer altering their behavior near roads that wolves used as travel corridors. The study, published in PNAS in 2021, calculated that the average county saved roughly $375,000 annually in collision costs. Across Wisconsin the annual total reached about $10.9 million—roughly 63 times the verified livestock losses the state had paid out over the preceding decades. Drivers on treated roads experienced fewer nighttime impacts without any change in speed limits, fencing, or traffic volume. The mechanism worked because wolves treat roads as efficient hunting paths, moving along them at dusk and dawn when deer are also active; the resulting “landscape of fear” keeps deer farther from the pavement edge even when overall deer density declines only modestly. Of the 24 percent reduction, roughly 6 percentage points came from lower deer numbers while the remaining 18 points came from behavioral avoidance, a distinction the researchers isolated by comparing counties with similar deer harvests but different wolf occupancy. This split matters because recreational hunters remove deer yet do not patrol the same road corridors at the same times or with the same consistency, so their activity does not generate the same localized avoidance response. The data covered 1988–2010, a period when wolf packs established territories gradually, allowing the team to treat each county’s first documented pack year as the start of the treatment effect. No corresponding drop appeared in the 34 counties that remained wolf-free, and the pattern held after controlling for changes in traffic volume and reporting practices. The economic figure of $10.9 million statewide therefore reflects both fewer crashes and the avoided medical, vehicle-repair, and insurance costs attached to each incident.

Segment 5 — The Aftermath

Wildlife managers in Wisconsin and neighboring states began citing the collision data when weighing wolf-hunting seasons and delisting petitions. The finding did not reverse any policy outright, but it supplied a concrete dollar figure that had been missing from earlier debates. Raynor noted that recreational hunting removes deer yet does not recreate the same “landscape of fear” along roads, because hunters do not patrol the linear corridors wolves favor. Similar analyses are now underway in other states where wolf range overlaps dense road networks. The PNAS paper itself became part of public comment records during later delisting discussions, giving agencies a quantified co-benefit to place beside livestock compensation totals. No new fencing or signage programs were altered as a direct result, yet the study shifted the terms of debate from purely ecological or agricultural frames to one that included measurable road-safety returns.

Segment 6 — The Lesson

When Jennifer Raynor’s team compared counties that gained wolves with those that did not, the largest safety gain appeared where wolves used roads as hunting paths rather than where they simply reduced total deer numbers. That pattern shows how a single management choice—maintaining wolf presence in a county—can shift prey movement at specific locations without requiring separate road-safety spending. The same data set also revealed that the benefit scaled with wolf occupancy rather than with hunter harvest rates, suggesting managers cannot assume human hunting substitutes for every effect predators produce. States now weighing wolf policy can plug their own collision and livestock-compensation numbers into the Wisconsin framework to see whether the same ratio holds. The study therefore supplies a template for measuring indirect benefits that appear only after the predator has altered fine-scale prey behavior along human infrastructure.

In 1988, the Philippine law meant to redistribute 4,900 hectares of Hacienda Luisita instead let owners keep control through shares.

Segment 1 — The Cold Open

In June 1988, Congress passed the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Law to transfer large estates to the families who tilled them. On the Cojuangco family’s Hacienda Luisita in Tarlac, a stock-distribution plan approved the next year gave farmworkers shares instead of title to the land. The family retained majority ownership and later converted portions of the estate to industrial and commercial use.

Segment 2 — The Good Intention

The law grew out of decades of rural unrest and repeated attempts at land reform that had left tenancy rates high. President Corazon Aquino, whose family owned Hacienda Luisita, signed Republic Act 6657 on 10 June 1988 after the 1986 People Power revolt had raised expectations that concentrated landholdings would finally be broken up. Lawmakers included the stock-distribution option in Section 31 because they believed corporate farms could remain efficient while still giving workers an ownership stake and a share of profits. At the time, officials argued that compulsory subdivision of integrated plantations would disrupt production and leave new owners without credit or equipment. The Department of Agrarian Reform and the Presidential Agrarian Reform Council therefore treated the option as one legitimate route to the law’s stated goal of “land to the tiller.” The arithmetic they worked with was straightforward: a single large corporation could issue shares representing the land’s value, spread those shares across thousands of workers over thirty years, and still meet production targets that small plots might not sustain. Officials also noted that many estates already operated as integrated sugar or rice operations with shared irrigation and milling infrastructure; breaking those links risked lowering output at a moment when the post-Marcos economy needed steady agricultural exports. By allowing the stock route, the framers hoped to avoid both the political backlash from landowners and the administrative burden of surveying and titling every hectare immediately.

Segment 3 — The Implementation

Hacienda Luisita, Inc. submitted its stock-distribution plan in 1989. A referendum held that year recorded 92.9 percent approval among farmworker beneficiaries, according to Department of Agrarian Reform records. Workers received 33.296 percent of the shares, distributed over thirty years, plus a three-percent production share. The Presidential Agrarian Reform Council approved the plan, and similar arrangements were later adopted on other corporate estates. Government reports at the time highlighted the rapid enrollment of large holdings under the new mechanism. The 33.296 percent figure was calculated from the ratio of the land’s declared value to the corporation’s total equity, so that farmworkers technically owned a slice of the company rather than individual parcels. Because the remaining equity stayed with the original shareholders, the Cojuangco family continued to elect the board and set land-use policy. The three-percent production share was an annual cash distribution tied to gross sales, meant to give workers immediate income while the stock vested. Early government tallies counted these plans as successful coverage under the law, since the paperwork showed workers listed as beneficiaries on corporate books.

Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences

Because the stock plan left the Cojuangco family with majority voting control, day-to-day management and land-use decisions remained with the original owners. Philippine Institute for Development Studies researchers Marife Ballesteros and colleagues later documented how the same mechanism, together with conversion permits and registration of excess land to relatives, allowed several estates to avoid physical redistribution. On Luisita itself, portions were reclassified for industrial parks and sold; additional hectares were taken for the Subic-Clark-Tarlac Expressway. Farmworkers who held minority shares received dividends that fluctuated with corporate performance rather than secure title they could mortgage or pass to heirs. A 2004 strike by the Alyansa ng mga Manggagawang Bukid ng Hacienda Luisita brought renewed attention to the gap between promised benefits and actual control. The Presidential Agrarian Reform Council’s 2005 revocation order and the Supreme Court’s 2011 and 2012 rulings confirmed that the stock plan had not transferred effective ownership. The causal chain ran through the voting arithmetic: with only one-third of the shares, workers could never outvote the family on whether to sell or rezone land. When the corporation later sought conversion permits for non-agricultural use, the minority stake gave workers no formal seat at the table. The Subic-Clark-Tarlac Expressway alone removed several hundred hectares from agricultural production, and the compensation paid to the corporation flowed to the majority shareholders. Workers who had expected eventual land titles found themselves holding paper whose value depended on corporate profitability rather than on the rising price of farmland itself. Over time, some families sold their vested shares for immediate cash, further concentrating ownership back inside the original corporate structure. The pattern repeated on other estates where stock plans were approved: the law’s alternative compliance path rewarded owners who maintained corporate form while the physical land stayed under unified management.

Segment 5 — The Aftermath

After the Supreme Court decisions, compulsory acquisition proceeded on the remaining agricultural land. The last Certificates of Land Ownership Award for Hacienda Luisita were issued in 2018. The Ballesteros study for the Philippine Institute for Development Studies found that the overall program had distributed roughly 4.7 million hectares by 2016, yet many large estates remained intact through the very options the 1988 law had permitted. Subsequent administrations tightened conversion rules and removed the stock-distribution provision from later extensions of the program. The final distribution on Luisita occurred under compulsory acquisition orders that valued the land as of the 1989 taking date, so compensation reflected earlier prices rather than current market rates. Even after the shares were set aside, some converted parcels had already been sold or leased for industrial use, leaving the redistributed agricultural remainder smaller than the original estate. The Ballesteros review noted that while aggregate hectares distributed looked substantial, the persistence of large contiguous holdings showed that evasion routes built into the original statute had limited the program’s structural impact.

Segment 6 — The Lesson

When Congress wrote the stock-distribution clause into the 1988 law, it rewarded landowners who could keep corporate control while satisfying the letter of redistribution. Later regulators responded by revoking plans that left voting power unchanged, showing that ownership rules must be measured by who actually decides land use. The same pattern appears whenever reform statutes create an alternative compliance path without requiring transfer of decision rights. Policymakers today could test a proposed ownership rule by asking whether the new arrangement changes the identity of the person or board that signs the next land-use permit. How might today’s policymakers test whether a new ownership rule truly shifts control before it is written into law?

Thirty-eight Dutch Large Coppers released at Woodwalton Fen in 1927 required annual captive releases for over sixty years.

Segment 1 — The Cold Open

In the summer of 1928 more than a thousand adult Large Coppers flew across the sedge and pools of Woodwalton Fen. Capt. E.B. Purefoy had brought thirty-eight Dutch butterflies to the site the year before and planted their foodplant, great water dock, in advance. The colony survived only because wardens continued to breed and release fresh adults every season while the surrounding farmland kept draining the fen drier.

Segment 2 — The Good Intention

The English race of the Large Copper, Lycaena dispar dispar, had disappeared by 1851 after centuries of fen drainage for agriculture. By the early twentieth century only fragments of wetland remained in eastern England. Capt. E.B. Purefoy, an experienced lepidopterist, decided that the newly discovered Dutch race Lycaena dispar batavus offered a chance to return the butterfly to one of those fragments. Woodwalton Fen, a small National Trust reserve in Huntingdonshire, still held pockets of suitable vegetation. Purefoy and the reserve managers believed that preparing the site with its larval foodplant would let a self-sustaining colony establish itself inside the protected boundary. The plan reflected the best available knowledge: the butterfly’s extinction had been caused by habitat loss, so targeted habitat restoration plus fresh stock should reverse it.

The fenland drainage that removed the original English population had begun in the seventeenth century with the cutting of large straight channels and the installation of wind-powered pumps. By the nineteenth century steam engines accelerated the lowering of water levels across hundreds of square miles, converting peat soils into arable fields. Purefoy therefore worked with a landscape whose hydrology had already been altered for more than two hundred years. He could see that the remaining fragments were small, yet he also knew that the Dutch race had evolved in similar, if not identical, wetland conditions. The decision to proceed rested on the assumption that a carefully chosen reserve could be made sufficient if the missing plant was restored.

Segment 3 — The Implementation

In 1927 Purefoy released twenty-five males and thirteen females of the Dutch race at Woodwalton Fen after the reserve had been planted with great water dock. The following year more than one thousand adults were counted on the wing, a result that appeared to confirm the approach. Learn Butterflies records that the early numbers encouraged everyone involved. The reserve staff continued modest management, keeping the foodplant abundant inside the fen’s existing ditches and pools. No one at the time expected that the colony would require repeated human intervention simply to persist.

The planting itself involved setting out hundreds of young Rumex hydrolapathum plants along the wetter margins of the reserve’s internal waterways. Purefoy chose locations where the peat remained saturated for most of the year, matching the conditions he had observed in Dutch colonies. The 1928 count of over one thousand adults therefore represented roughly twenty-seven times the number of founders, an apparent multiplication that suggested the restored foodplant and the protected status of the fen were together sufficient. Wardens recorded the presence of eggs and larvae on the dock leaves that same season, reinforcing the sense that the cycle had closed.

Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences

The land beyond Woodwalton Fen’s boundaries had been progressively drained for arable farming since the seventeenth century. Each new pump and ditch lowered the regional water table, pulling moisture out of the reserve through the peat. Great water dock, which needs consistently wet conditions, became harder to maintain in sufficient density. Duffey’s studies and the records kept at the reserve show that natural recruitment soon fell below replacement levels. Wardens responded by rearing butterflies in a greenhouse and releasing batches of adults each summer, a practice that continued for decades. Larvae were also caged on selected plants to protect them from flooding or drought. These measures kept a visible population alive, yet the colony never became independent of the annual supplements. By the late 1960s a major flood further damaged the foodplant stands, prompting another round of captive rearing after 1969. The surrounding agricultural drainage continued unchanged, so the hydrological deficit grew rather than shrank. The second- and third-order effects were cumulative: each year of lowered water table reduced the area of suitable dock, which in turn increased the proportion of the population that had to be artificially supported. Without that support the numbers would have collapsed far earlier than the early 1990s, when the last supplemented adults were seen.

The peat itself compounded the difficulty. Once drained from the outside, the organic soil began to oxidise and subside, lowering the surface of the reserve relative to the surrounding fields and making it even harder for water to remain at the level the dock required. Every additional pump installed on neighbouring farms increased the gradient, drawing water through the peat more rapidly. The wardens’ greenhouse rearing therefore addressed only the symptom—the shortage of adult butterflies—while the underlying shortage of suitable larval habitat continued to widen. Caging larvae protected individuals from immediate weather extremes, yet it could not expand the total area of dock that stayed wet long enough for the caterpillars to complete their development. The arithmetic of the intervention thus shifted year by year: more adults had to be reared to compensate for fewer naturally occurring plants.

Segment 5 — The Aftermath

After the final sightings in 1994 and 1995 the formal reintroduction programme at Woodwalton Fen ended. The site remained a National Nature Reserve, but managers accepted that a viable Large Copper population could not be sustained without restoring the wider fen hydrology. The Great Fen project, now under way, aims to re-wet large areas of former farmland between Woodwalton and Holme Fen, using clay bunds and controlled water levels to recreate the conditions the butterfly once required. No new releases have occurred while the landscape-scale work continues. The original 1927 effort therefore stands as a sixty-year demonstration that protecting a single fragment is not enough when the surrounding drainage regime remains unaltered.

The project’s current phase involves purchasing arable land and installing impermeable barriers so that winter rainfall can be retained rather than pumped away. These measures address the same water-table gradient that had steadily reduced the area of great water dock inside the original reserve. Until the restored blocks reach a sufficient size and wetness, however, any reintroduced butterflies would face the same constraints that forced the earlier programme into perpetual supplementation.

Segment 6 — The Lesson

Purefoy and the Woodwalton wardens treated the reserve boundary as the decisive limit of their intervention; the result was a population that existed only inside annual budgets for captive rearing. Any plan that sets a fixed geographical perimeter must also secure the water regime across that perimeter, or the foodplant itself will disappear. When the next reintroduction is considered, the test will be whether the Great Fen’s restored water table can support great water dock without constant human rescue. The same question faces every recovery project that begins inside a remnant habitat ringed by intensified land use.

The 2007 Renewable Fuel Standard expanded U.S. corn area by 2.8 million hectares, raising nitrate flows into the Gulf.

Segment 1 — The Cold Open

In the spring of 2008, farmers across Iowa and Illinois prepared fields for record corn plantings after Congress passed the Energy Independence and Security Act the previous December. Policymakers had intended the expanded Renewable Fuel Standard to cut oil imports and lower tailpipe emissions by blending more corn ethanol into gasoline. Instead, the same policy increased synthetic nitrogen applications on Midwest cropland, sending additional nitrate loads down the Mississippi River toward the Gulf of Mexico.

Segment 2 — The Good Intention

President George W. Bush signed the Energy Independence and Security Act on December 19, 2007, after both houses of Congress approved it with bipartisan support. The law’s Title II expanded the Renewable Fuel Standard originally created in 2005, setting rising annual volume requirements that reached 36 billion gallons of renewable fuel by 2022. At the time, high and volatile crude-oil prices, dependence on imported petroleum, and concerns over greenhouse-gas emissions from transportation made greater domestic biofuel production appear a practical step. Lawmakers and agency staff at the Environmental Protection Agency viewed corn-starch ethanol as a fuel already in commercial use that could scale quickly without new engine technology. The policy therefore rewarded increased corn production as the most direct route to meeting the statutory targets. In the broader context of 2005–2007, when oil repeatedly exceeded $70 per barrel and supply disruptions in the Middle East remained fresh, substituting a portion of gasoline demand with a domestically grown liquid fuel aligned with both security and environmental goals that many legislators had already endorsed in the earlier Energy Policy Act.

Segment 3 — The Implementation

The statute took effect immediately, and the Environmental Protection Agency began issuing annual renewable-volume obligations that obligated refiners to blend specified quantities of ethanol. Corn prices rose sharply through 2012 as demand for ethanol feedstock grew, then fell; by 2016 the season-average price was below its 2008 level. In the first years after enactment, ethanol production capacity expanded rapidly in the Midwest, and by 2010 the industry was on track to meet the law’s intermediate targets. Early reports from the Department of Agriculture noted higher farm incomes and greater domestic fuel output. A few hydrologists, including those later cited in Donner and Kucharik’s 2008 PNAS paper, warned that meeting the full ethanol volumes would require more corn acreage and therefore more fertilizer, but those cautions received limited attention during the rollout. Refiners met their obligations by purchasing Renewable Identification Numbers tied to each gallon of ethanol, creating a liquid market that transmitted the statutory demand directly into the corn futures price; because the volume schedule increased each year, the price signal remained consistent rather than temporary.

Segment 4 — The Unintended Consequences

Lark et al. (PNAS, 2022) combined econometric land-use models with biophysical data and found that the Renewable Fuel Standard increased U.S. corn acreage by 2.8 million hectares and total cropland by 2.1 million hectares between 2008 and 2016. The same study estimated that nationwide synthetic nitrogen fertilizer use rose 7.5 percent and nitrate leaching from agricultural soils increased 5.3 percent. Because most of the additional corn was grown in the Mississippi River basin, a larger share of that reactive nitrogen moved downstream. Donner and Kucharik’s earlier PNAS modeling had projected that satisfying the ethanol mandates would raise dissolved inorganic nitrogen export by the Mississippi-Atchafalaya system between 10 and 34 percent. The extra nitrate reached the northern Gulf of Mexico each summer, where it fueled algal blooms whose subsequent decay depleted oxygen in bottom waters. The seasonal hypoxic area, already large before 2007, therefore faced additional nutrient pressure that made reduction targets harder to reach. Farmers responded to higher corn prices by planting continuous corn rather than rotating with soybeans, a shift that further raised nitrogen requirements. Equipment dealers and fertilizer suppliers adjusted inventories accordingly, locking in the higher application rates for multiple seasons. The policy’s price signal operated nationwide, so even regions outside the traditional Corn Belt saw expanded corn plantings and associated fertilizer use. One consequence of the continuous-corn shift is that soybean nitrogen credits disappear; each additional hectare therefore receives a full recommended nitrogen rate rather than a reduced rate that would have applied after a legume, amplifying the modeled 7.5 percent national increase in reactive nitrogen. Because the Mississippi basin drains roughly 40 percent of U.S. cropland, even modest percentage rises in application translate into thousands of additional tons of nitrogen reaching the river each year.

Segment 5 — The Aftermath

By the mid-2010s the modeled water-quality effects had entered regulatory discussions, yet the statutory volume mandates remained in place. The Environmental Protection Agency continued to adjust annual standards but did not alter the underlying corn-ethanol pathway. Industry groups disputed the magnitude of the nitrogen increases reported by Lark et al., arguing that yield improvements and improved nutrient management had offset much of the expansion. No comprehensive federal program has since reversed the land-use changes attributed to the Renewable Fuel Standard. Some states have tightened nutrient-management rules for watersheds draining to the Gulf, yet the federal biofuel requirements continue to shape planting decisions each spring. Estimates of current nitrate loads still reflect the elevated baseline established after 2007. The persistence of the mandates reflects the statutory structure: once the volume schedule is written into law, only new legislation or a successful lawsuit can change the core requirement, leaving later environmental programs to operate around an agricultural baseline that has already shifted.

Segment 6 — The Lesson

When Congress tied renewable-fuel volumes directly to corn-starch ethanol in the 2007 Act, the price premium on corn rewarded any farmer who could plant more of it, regardless of downstream nitrogen consequences. The Environmental Protection Agency’s annual blending obligations then transmitted that price signal year after year without an automatic adjustment for water-quality outcomes. Later attempts to meet hypoxia-reduction goals therefore had to work against an agricultural system already reoriented by the earlier mandate. Policymakers today weighing new fuel or feedstock targets can examine whether the compliance metric itself creates measurable pressure on land use or nutrient cycles before the volumes are locked into statute.
