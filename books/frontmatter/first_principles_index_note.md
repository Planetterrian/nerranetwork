Every chapter in this series uses the same measuring stick, so it is
worth stating the rule once, plainly.

The Idiot Index is the ratio between what a finished thing sells for
and what its raw material inputs cost as commodities — both sides in
dollars, both sides at the same moment in time, both sides drawn around
the same boundary. The numerator is the delivered price a real buyer
pays. The denominator is the market price of the constituent materials
(and, where a chapter says so explicitly, the thermodynamic minimum
energy) needed to make one unit — never the cost of the machines,
labor, or organization, because the entire point of the ratio is to
expose how much of the price those things account for.

Three consequences of the rule. First, an index near one is not a
target: real processes need equipment, people, and margins, so a
healthy mature industry often sits between two and five. Second, the
index is an order-of-magnitude instrument, not an accounting figure —
chapters round aggressively and say so. Third, the boundary must match
on both sides: a bored tunnel compared against a whole transit line, or
a hospital list price compared against a reagent floor, produces a
number that measures the mismatch instead of the engineering. Where an
earlier telling of one of these stories mixed boundaries, this edition
redraws the comparison like-for-like and says what changed.

When a chapter cannot express its floor in dollars — because the
binding input is energy, or time, or a material with no liquid market —
it names the unit it is using in place of dollars and does not call the
result comparable across chapters. A ratio is only as honest as its
denominator.
