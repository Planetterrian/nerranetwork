The truth behind Donald Trump’s ‘biggest oil deal in world history’: The Guardian

The Guardian reports that an August plan for the Pentagon’s office of strategic capital to hold a third of North American Blue Energy Partners — a deal President Donald Trump called “the biggest oil deal in world history” — is almost certainly doomed on legal and logistical grounds. US forces seized Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in January, the paper reports, moved him to a federal holding facility in the United States, and left Delcy Rodríguez in his place. Experts and officials who spoke with The Guardian said virtually everything Trump and his cabinet have said about the deal is false or misleading: Venezuelan law does not allow oil deals longer than 25 years, and the office is not authorized in US law to own company shares. They said the structure will not bring new capital into Venezuela’s oil sector and, contrary to Trump, will not refill the Strategic Petroleum Reserve any time soon; The Guardian reports the reserve fell to a longtime low after the US invaded Iran.

Both Sides: Ontario's property assessment freeze

Premier Doug Ford’s government still has to decide whether to restart Ontario property assessments or leave municipal tax bills tied to January 2016 values. In 2020, at the height of COVID-19, the province ordered the Municipal Property Assessment Corporation to pause. The Globe and Mail reports the system was never switched back on.

Paul Sullivan, a British Columbia property-tax consultant and national tax leader at Ryan ULC, wants the freeze lifted. His best reason is quiet overpayment. Owners whose property rose less than the local average, or fell, have paid more than they otherwise would, sometimes thousands of dollars a year, because the base never moved. He told The Globe and Mail the biggest problem is education and transparency: people do not know, and if they did there would be outrage. He has launched a campaign to end the pause.

Critics of lifting it warn, as The Globe and Mail reports, of explosive political cost. Owners whose property rose faster than their community’s average could face higher bills. The freeze did not stop municipalities from raising rates; it froze the values those rates use, normally reset every four years and phased in over four. MPAC was about to issue a 2020 reassessment when the pandemic hit.

Both sides share one roll: six years of bills on the fully phased-in 2016 value, now a decade old, after other pandemic rules ended. Sullivan’s campaign is the live push, and Ford’s government has left the 2020 order in place. MPAC, in that same account, says almost half of Ontario condo units are worth less than $500,000 as prices plunge, a change the frozen roll does not capture. Source: [theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-ontarios-taxing-problem-with-property-owners/)

Ipsos Public Affairs senior vice-president Gregory Jack said the stay-or-go numbers have not really changed through the campaign, and that turnout matters because the side that wants to leave is more motivated than the side that wants to stay. Ipsos found 76 percent of Albertans know the ballot has several questions, and 62 percent plan to vote on all 10. Premier Danielle Smith added the separatism question on May 21 to a referendum announced February 19 that began with nine questions on immigration and the Constitution. Both Sides: Impeaching a judge over her rulings

Kentucky’s Supreme Court has left in place an order that stops lawmakers from continuing the impeachment of Fayette Circuit Judge Julie Muth Goodman. The fight is whether the General Assembly may remove a judge for her rulings, or whether those disputes belong in appeals and judicial discipline.

The House says Goodman abused judicial discretion and authority, and it passed an impeachment resolution on March 20. After Franklin Circuit Judge Phillip J. Shepherd ruled on March 31 that the proceedings were null and void, lawmakers told the Lexington Herald-Leader he has no constitutional authority to void an impeachment and that a Senate trial should still happen. Their best reason is institutional: impeachment, they argue, is a legislative power a trial court cannot cancel.

The Supreme Court says this petition was not a lawful impeachment because it was never sworn or verified by affidavit, as state law requires. The justices said the allegations were disagreements over rulings in six cases, not impeachable offenses, and that such complaints must go through appeals and the judicial conduct commission. They warned that impeaching judges over controversial decisions would violate the separation of powers, threaten judicial independence, and could lead to what the court called tyrannical control of the courts. They also said the process denied Goodman due process, because five of the six cases were still pending and ethics rules barred her from publicly defending her decisions.

Both sides are contesting the same March 20 resolution, and neither disputes that a separate judicial conduct investigation into Goodman can still move forward. Thursday’s order records an even split among the justices taking part, so the April 6 opinion stands and the injunction against further impeachment proceedings remains.

What This Show Covers

If you want one region, every day, told straight, this is that brief: what was decided, what happened, what it changes and for whom. Each edition also sets out one contested question, with the strongest case on each side, and one measurable sign of progress when the day’s reporting contains one.

Both Sides: A ban on artificial superintelligence

The decision before Congress is whether the United States should ban artificial superintelligence, pause development of advanced artificial-intelligence systems, and create a Department of Artificial Intelligence. Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar are introducing legislation that would do all three, the Associated Press reports. It is a proposal being introduced, not a law that has been passed.

Sanders’s case, as the AP reports it, starts with risk. He argues that the potential dangers demand immediate action and international cooperation. The AP says the proposal comes as prominent artificial-intelligence executives and researchers increasingly warn about potentially catastrophic risks from rapidly advancing systems, and that Congress has struggled to agree on safeguards. The reason he puts first is that those dangers justify a ban, a pause, a new department, and cooperation beyond one country.

The case against that approach, as the AP attributes it to President Donald Trump, is competitive position. He has pushed back on new regulations and emphasized maintaining an advantage over China. The reason in that reported position is the edge over China, and resistance to new rules, rather than a pause on advanced systems or a ban on superintelligence.

The fact neither account disputes is that Sanders and Casar are introducing a bill, and that it is not yet law. What happens next is that introduction. The AP frames the open choice as Sanders’s call for a ban and international cooperation against Trump’s reported resistance to new regulation in the name of an advantage over China.
