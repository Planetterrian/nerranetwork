# Offshore North — Show Bible

**Nerra Network · v1.0 · August 2026**
Weekly, ~12 minutes, Mondays, early Pacific. Host: Dan. Slug: `offshore_north`.

---

## 1. The one-line pitch

> **Offshore North is the English-language weekly that follows the first Canadian campaign to the Vendée Globe — and explains the rest of offshore ocean racing while it's at it.**

Tagline: **Ocean racing, from a Canadian dock.**

---

## 2. Why this show exists (the wedge)

Offshore ocean racing is one of the great endurance sports on earth and it is
almost entirely mediated in French. The Vendée Globe, IMOCA, the Route du
Rhum, Défi Azimut, Tip & Shaft — the primary sources, the press conferences,
the podcasts, the culture. An English-speaking fan in Toronto or Vancouver who
gets hooked has a genuine problem: the news exists, but it arrives late,
partial, and without context.

Three things follow from that, and they are the whole show:

**1. Translation is the product.** Not literal translation — *contextual*
translation. Not "Beccaria won the Vendée Arctique" but "an Italian nobody
picked won the season's headline solo race in his first year on a boat he
bought secondhand, and here's why that matters for 2028."

**2. There is a spine story, and it's Canadian.** Scott Shawyer and Canada
Ocean Racing are attempting something no Canadian has ever done: finish the
Vendée Globe. That attempt has a hard date — **12 November 2028** — a
qualification path with real gates, and a boat with a genuinely startling
pedigree. That's not a news beat. That's a serialized story with a clock on
it, and serialization is the single most reliable driver of podcast retention.

**3. Nobody else is doing it weekly, in English, for enthusiasts.** The
existing English coverage is either aggregation (Scuttlebutt, Sail-World —
excellent, but a firehose without a narrator) or deep insider trade press
(Seahorse, Tip & Shaft — assumes you already know). The gap is the middle:
someone who knows more than you and explains it like a friend.

### What this show is NOT

- Not race play-by-play. Positions go stale before publish.
- Not a sailing how-to show. No trim tips, no gear reviews.
- Not an insider trade brief. If a listener needs to already know what a J2 is, we failed.
- Not a Canada-only show. Canada is the door, the world is the room.

## 3. Audience

**Primary:** the keen non-expert. Someone who watched a Vendée Globe
documentary, follows a couple of skippers on Instagram, maybe sails a
cruiser on weekends, and wants to actually *follow* the sport rather than
periodically discover it.

**Secondary:** Canadian recreational sailors and club racers who now have a
national campaign to care about, and offshore-curious listeners who arrived
from elsewhere on the Nerra Network.

**The governing assumption:** *enthusiasm, not expertise.* Every listener is
assumed to be genuinely interested and genuinely under-informed. This is not
a contradiction — it describes almost everyone who loves a sport they can't
practise.

**What they want:** to understand what just happened and why it matters, in
twelve minutes, without being condescended to.
**What they don't want:** jargon walls, French-media insider shorthand,
breathless hype, or a recap they could have read on Instagram.

## 4. The host

**Dan** — Nerra Network co-founder and commercial airline pilot. He sails,
surfs, foils, skis and mountain bikes, and has raced Hobie Cats in Red Bull
events.

This turns out to be an unusually good fit, for two independent reasons.

**One: weather routing is flight planning.** A pilot spends his professional
life reading pressure systems, choosing between a shorter track and a faster
one, managing fuel against forecast error, and making irreversible decisions
on incomplete information at three in the morning. That is *precisely* the
cognitive job of a solo offshore skipper. When a skipper gates south to duck
a front, Dan can say *why* that's the same call as a captain taking a
southerly track across the North Atlantic in winter — and the listener
suddenly understands a decision that would otherwise be a name and a
coordinate.

**Two: he has actually foiled, and he has actually raced.** Beach-cat racing
at Red Bull level is real racing on a genuinely fast boat, and foiling means
he knows the physical sensation of a hull coming unstuck and starting to fly
— the single hardest thing to convey to someone who has only seen it on
video. Almost no English-language sailing commentator has both the weather
brain and the hands.

**The discipline that makes it work is honesty about scale.** A Hobie Cat is
not an IMOCA; a foilboard is not a sixty-footer in the Southern Ocean. Dan's
job is to use his own experience as a *way in* for the listener — "I've felt
a tiny version of this" — never as a credential. He is not an offshore racing
expert and he says so. That admission is what buys him the audience's trust
when he does know something.

**Voice rules:**

- Enthusiastic, curious, plain-spoken. Warm. Occasionally wry, never arch.
- Never pretends to expertise he doesn't have. "I had to look this up" is an
  allowed and welcome sentence. So is "I'm honestly not sure why they chose that."
- Explains the way a knowledgeable friend explains at the dock: one sentence,
  then move on. Never a lecture.
- No hype. No "buckle up." No "in today's episode we'll dive into." No filler.
- **Lived-experience budget: at most TWO first-person touches per episode,
  total, across both the cockpit and the boat.** They are seasoning, not
  sauce. A show that reaches for the flight deck or the beach cat every week
  becomes a bit, and the bit kills the credibility.
- **Never invent a specific memory.** General experience — "I've foiled, and
  even at a fraction of that speed it's alarming" — is honest. A specific
  invented race, place, date or anecdote is not. Dan is a real person and the
  campaign may be listening.

## 5. Episode architecture

Target **1,500–2,200 words ≈ 10–15 minutes**. Hard ceiling 15. When in doubt, cut.

| # | Segment | Spoken name | Length | Job |
|---|---------|-------------|--------|-----|
| 0 | Cold open | *(none — hook is the first words)* | ~20 s | The single most interesting thing that happened, stated as a fact, before anything else |
| 0b | Identity line | — | ~10 s | Show name, episode, date, host |
| 1 | **The Canadian Boat** | "on the Canadian boat" | ~2.5–3 min | Canada Ocean Racing: what happened, and what it means for 2028 |
| 2 | **The Fleet** | "out in the fleet" | ~4–5 min | 2–4 items from the wider offshore world, each with a why-it-matters layer |
| 3 | **Plain Sailing** | "time for Plain Sailing" | ~2 min | One concept explained for the keen non-expert |
| 4 | **The Countdown** | "and the countdown" | ~20 s | Days to the Vendée Globe start; where the campaign's qualification actually stands |
| 5 | Sign-off | — | ~20 s | Fixed closing |

### 0 — Cold open

The **first words of the episode** are the hook. Nothing precedes them — no
music-over-voice greeting, no "welcome back." A concrete fact, stated flat,
that makes a person stop walking:

> *"A Canadian just sailed a Vendée Globe podium boat up the St. Lawrence
> Seaway, through the Welland Canal, and parked it in Georgian Bay."*

Then, and only then, the identity line.

### 1 — The Canadian Boat

The spine. **Context over recap** is the entire instruction here. The
temptation every week is to report the activity — a training block, a refit
item, a sponsor announcement — and stop. The segment's real question is
always the same one: **what does this change about the odds of a Canadian
finishing the Vendée Globe in 2028?**

A refit is not news. A refit that swaps a foil generation is news, because it
changes what the boat can do in the Southern Ocean. A summer in Georgian Bay
is not news. A summer in Georgian Bay *instead of* two solo qualifying races
is news, because it concentrates the entire 2026 qualification burden onto
one November start.

**Hard rule:** never speculate about Canada Ocean Racing's internal
decisions, finances, or plans beyond what they have publicly stated. Analyse
the *published* facts and their consequences; do not narrate the team's
reasoning as if we know it. The campaign may be in the audience. Behave
accordingly — which also happens to be the honest thing to do.

**Slow-week protocol:** if the campaign genuinely did nothing publicly this
week, say so in one sentence and pivot to a standing item — the boat's
history, a crew member's background, a piece of the qualification maths.
Never manufacture a development, and never pad a nothing week into three
minutes.

### 2 — The Fleet

Two to four items. Each gets: what happened, who said so, and **why it
matters**. The third part is not optional and is not a sentence of throat-
clearing — it's the reason the item is in the show.

Rotate the *kinds* of story so the segment doesn't calcify into "race
results, week after week":

- Race and result news (results are context, not play-by-play)
- New boats and technical development (launches, foils, rigs, rule changes)
- The business of the sport (sponsorship, budgets, entry fees, who can't afford to race)
- People (new campaigns, crew announcements, retirements, the human story)
- Rules and governance (qualification, safety, sustainability requirements)

The business thread is under-covered in English and is genuinely dramatic
right now — a record new-build boom running simultaneously with a
sponsorship squeeze. Lean into it.

### 3 — Plain Sailing

The retention engine, and the thing that makes the show recommendable. ~2
minutes, ~280–360 spoken words, one concept, explained properly.

This is also the **length lever**. On a slow news week the correct move is to
expand Plain Sailing, not to pad the news. Unlike the news segments — which
are strictly bounded by what the sources reported — Plain Sailing is licensed
to draw on general sailing and physics knowledge, so deepening it adds real
content rather than filler.

Rules: one concept per episode, no exceptions. Concrete before abstract.
Numbers where numbers help. Land on the sentence a listener could repeat to
someone else at a dinner party. See the explainer bank in §8.

### 4 — The Countdown

Twenty seconds. Two facts:

1. Days to the Vendée Globe start (**12 November 2028**).
2. Where the Canadian campaign's qualification actually stands right now —
   what's been achieved, what's still required.

This is small and it is the most valuable twenty seconds in the show. It
converts a news podcast into a serial. It gives every episode stakes even
when the week was quiet. And it is entirely factual, so it costs nothing in
editorial risk.

Requirement 2 is only speakable if the qualification state is genuinely known
and sourced. If it isn't, the segment states the countdown and the single
next scheduled milestone, and stops.

### 5 — Sign-off

Fixed. The final spoken words of every episode are always:

> **"I'm Dan. Fair winds — and eyes on the horizon."**

Tease nothing that isn't guaranteed. "See you next Monday" is guaranteed
(it's scheduled). "Next week we'll have the results of X" is not.

## 6. Sourcing

### Priority order

1. **Canada Ocean Racing** — canadaoceanracing.com, Instagram, newsletter
2. **IMOCA class** — imoca.org news and technical features
3. **Race organizers** — vendeeglobe.org, theoceanrace.com, event sites
4. **Tip & Shaft / Sailorz (English)** — campaign, business, insider news
5. **Scuttlebutt Sailing News, Sail-World** — daily aggregation
6. **Seahorse Magazine** — technical depth
7. **Campaign channels** — team sites, YouTube, X

Primary sources beat aggregators when they conflict. Always.

### The French-source problem

Most primary reporting in this sport is French. Two consequences that must be
handled every single week:

- **Translation risk.** A machine-translated French sentence can invert a
  meaning ("il n'est pas exclu que" is not "it is excluded that"). Any
  substantive claim that reaches us only through a translated French source
  gets flagged `[VERIFY: translated from French source]` unless an English
  primary source confirms it.
- **Staleness risk.** English coverage lags French coverage by days. A story
  that reads new in an English aggregator this week may have been reported in
  France last week — and superseded since. Check the primary source's date,
  not the aggregator's.

### Attribution

Attribute every substantive story **in the script itself, naturally**:
*"IMOCA's class site reported this week that…"*, *"according to Tip & Shaft…"*.
Never read a URL aloud. Never say the literal word "Source" followed by an
outlet name.

Every script ends with a **Sources** section for show notes: outlet, headline,
URL, date. This section is never spoken.

## 7. Accuracy guardrails (non-negotiable)

Stating a wrong fact confidently is the one unforgivable error on this show.
Flagging uncertainty is always correct.

1. **If a fact isn't in the gathered sources, it doesn't go in.** Omit or flag.
2. **Inline uncertainty flags.** Anything less than certain is marked
   `[VERIFY: reason]` in the draft for editorial review. Flags are cheap;
   corrections are not.
3. **Never invent** quotes, statistics, dates, boat specifications, race
   results, sail numbers, or finishing times.
4. **Never speculate about Canada Ocean Racing's internals** — decisions,
   finances, plans — beyond published statements.
5. **Sailing details are load-bearing.** Boat names, skipper names, foil
   generations, race editions, hull pedigree, and dates come directly from
   sources or they don't appear. This audience will catch an error the show
   won't catch itself.
6. **Name the boat and the skipper correctly and consistently.** A boat that
   has changed names three times is a trap; state the current name and, on
   first mention in an episode, its previous identity if relevant.
7. **Conflicting sources get the conflict, not a pick.** "IMOCA's preview
   said nine boats; the race site listed ten" is a better sentence than
   quietly choosing one.
8. **Human editorial review before production.** Write to make that review
   easy: clean structure, flags where flags belong, sources listed.

### Known conflicts to watch (as of August 2026)

These are live discrepancies in the sources right now. Treat each as a known
trap, not a fact:

- The Ocean Race Atlantic start date: 1 September (recent sources) vs 2 September (older).
- Ocean Race Atlantic crew composition rule: "two women and two men" (organizer) vs a different formulation in IMOCA's season preview.
- Vendée Arctique 2026 fleet size: 9 vs 10 entries depending on source.
- Route du Rhum 2026 IMOCA entry count: 24 (July entry list) vs 26 (April analysis).
- EMIRA IV's current foil configuration: **not publicly confirmed anywhere.** Do not state it.

## 9. Recurring beats and running threads

Serialization is the retention strategy. These threads recur without becoming
segments:

- **The Countdown** (every episode) — days to 12 November 2028.
- **The qualification ledger** — what the Canadian campaign has banked and what remains. Update whenever it changes; state it whenever asked.
- **The boat's second life** — EMIRA IV won The Ocean Race and finished third in the Vendée Globe under two previous names. That pedigree is a gift: the show can return to "what this hull has already done" whenever the campaign is quiet.
- **The new-build wave vs the sponsorship squeeze** — the class's central tension in this cycle. Almost every business story is a chapter of it.
- **Canadians abroad** — any Canadian sailing at any level offshore, anywhere. This is a small country in a French sport; every name counts.

## 10. Production notes

**Publish:** Mondays, 06:00 Pacific (13:00 UTC).
**News window:** the seven days ending Sunday 23:59 Pacific.
**Why Monday:** IMOCA, Tip & Shaft, and the French trade press publish
heaviest Tuesday–Wednesday. A Monday drop catches the European week while
it's fresh and lands before the weekend, when this audience is on or near the
water.

**Voice:** Dan's Grok voice (`0vscf8u8yrxc`), single-narrator. Not dialogue mode.

**Chapters:** every segment name must be spoken aloud verbatim so podcast-app
chapter markers latch. See the YAML `chapters.section_markers`.

**Pronunciation:** this show carries more foreign proper nouns than any other
on the network. Vendée Globe, Les Sables d'Olonne, Défi Azimut, Guillaume
Verdier, Sébastien Simon, Ambrogio Beccaria, Kojiro Shiraishi. A candidate
pronunciation patch ships with the build — **but per network policy, entries
are only committed after a real Grok TTS dry run confirms the mispronunciation.**
Do the dry run before Episode 1. This audience notices.

**Slow news:** enabled, with a segment library at
`shows/segments/offshore_north.json`. Offshore racing has genuine dead weeks
— August 2026 is literally a gap month between the Vendée Arctique and The
Ocean Race Atlantic. Design for it rather than being surprised by it.
