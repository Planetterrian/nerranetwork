## This Week in Space & Science

The week belonged equally to robots that roll and to light that has been traveling since the universe was young. On Mars, Perseverance added another 91 meters in a single day and, with help from AutoNav, its hazard-detection driving system, claimed the record for the longest distance traveled on another world. Distance is not a vanity metric: every extra meter is another outcrop, another workspace, another page of Martian geology. Back on Earth, ispace-EUROPE and ALTEC signed an agreement at the International Astronautical Congress in Antalya to put lunar rovers through functional tests, mission simulations, and operator training inside analogue facilities in Turin, including a Lunar Simulation Centre funded by the Italian Space Agency. ALTEC already supports ESA's ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover from its Rover Operations Control Centre; the new memorandum extends that culture of rehearsal to commercial lunar mobility.

While hardware practiced for the Moon, the Moon itself offered quieter news. Detailed maps of young tectonic scarps now give the clearest measure yet of how much the satellite has contracted as its interior has cooled, and they show that the shrinking did not stop in deep time. In parallel, astronomers checking the oldest gas clouds found primordial helium still in line with Big Bang nucleosynthesis — roughly three parts hydrogen to one part helium by mass, with later stellar helium carefully subtracted. Farther out, a supercluster some seven billion light-years away, holding more than two quadrillion solar masses, is being read as a test of whether dark matter particles interact with one another. Closer to familiar stars, the hot Jupiter WASP-189b revealed a transmission spectrum whose elemental mix mirrors its host, evidence that planet and star drew from the same disk. ALMA's COMPASS program added a different inventory item: the first detection of a rare isotopic form of methanol in a star-forming region, a tracer for the cold chemistry that feeds planet-forming disks.

Crewed spaceflight supplied the human scale. NASA scheduled an October 20 briefing on the last two spacewalks that will finish the station's roll-out solar array upgrades and lift power generation by as much as 30 percent. SpaceX's Crew-12 commander, home from the ISS, talked about the thinness of the biosphere from orbit. At Starmus VIII in Tenerife, the Artemis II crew accepted the Stephen Hawking Medal for making a lunar flight something people could follow live. In the lab, NYU researchers used a Random Positioning Machine to watch how microgravity-like conditions reshape cell nuclei — a careful step toward understanding what long missions do to human cells.

## Top Stories

### Perseverance claims the off-world distance record
NASA's Perseverance rover now holds the record for the longest distance traveled on another world, covering more ground on Mars than any rover has managed on Mars or the Moon. AutoNav, its onboard hazard-detection system, lets the rover pick safe paths without waiting on Earth for every turn, which is how a 91-meter day becomes possible. More kilometers mean more science stops, so the record is a win for the instruments as much as for the wheels. ### Primordial helium still matches the Big Bang
Helium in the cosmos comes in two batches: the primordial fraction forged in the first minutes after the Big Bang, and the helium later produced in stars. Spectral lines in the oldest gas clouds preserve the original mix — about three parts hydrogen to one part helium by mass — and new measurements continue to align with standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis. Distinguishing the two sources takes careful accounting of stellar enrichment over cosmic time, which is why each clean measurement still matters. ### Young lunar faults measure a shrinking Moon
A survey of recent tectonic landforms, rather than ancient degraded ones, offers the most detailed estimate yet of how much the Moon has contracted while cooling. High-resolution orbital images, latitude comparisons, and crater counts on the scarps themselves place the youngest faults in geologically recent epochs and show that shortening has continued at a modest rate into the present. The tighter contraction number feeds models of the lunar interior's heat budget and lithospheric strength. ### A hot Jupiter that chemically matches its star
Astronomers captured the transmission spectrum of WASP-189b as it passed in front of its host and found elemental abundances that closely track those in the star. The match implies that planet and star accreted from the same reservoir of disk material, a useful benchmark for how giant planets gather gas and solids. It also opens a path to compare chemistry across more hot Jupiters and their hosts. [▶ Episode 222 · 2026-10-14](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/fascinating_frontiers/ep222.html)

### Artemis II crew awarded the Stephen Hawking Medal
Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, and Jeremy Hansen accepted the medal on stage at Starmus VIII in Tenerife; Christina Koch was recognized even though she could not attend. The honor cited live communications, interviews, and photographs that let audiences follow their lunar flight in real time. Wiseman described seeing Earth the size of a thumb from 400,000 kilometers and feeling only love, peace, hope, and unity — an echo of honors once given to Apollo crews. ## Research Spotlight

### How much has the Moon shrunk?

The Moon is not geologically frozen. As its interior has slowly lost heat, the crust has adjusted, wrinkling into scarps and related tectonic landforms. Earlier global surveys mixed ancient, degraded features with younger ones, which blurred the total amount of radial contraction. This week's work isolates the young faults.

The method is straightforward in concept and painstaking in practice. Researchers used high-resolution orbital imagery to separate fresh scarps from older terrain, compared structures across latitudes to constrain when the most recent shortening occurred, and counted craters superimposed on the scarps to assign relative ages. Those ages place the youngest faults in geologically recent epochs. The resulting inventory yields tighter bounds on total contraction than previous maps, and it shows that the Moon has kept shortening at a modest rate into the present rather than finishing the job in its youth.

That number is not trivia. Thermal-evolution models have to match both how much the Moon has cooled and when that cooling produced visible tectonics. A cleaner contraction estimate feeds the heat budget of the lunar interior, the thickness and strength of the lithosphere, and the question of whether modest seismicity should still be expected. For future surface missions, young scarps are also practical geology: places where the crust has recently broken, and where landing-site planners may want either a closer look or a wider berth.
