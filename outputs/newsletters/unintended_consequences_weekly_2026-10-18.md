## By the numbers

- **20 acres** — converted to crops for every 100 CRP acres idled
- **One-third** — the coal of a Newcomen engine, after Watt’s condenser
- **72** — mongooses released on Hawaiʻi Island in 1883
- **20 million** — flex-fuel vehicles earning CAFE credits by 2014
- **2.8 million ha** — extra U.S. corn after the 2007 fuel standard

In the late 1980s a farmer in the central United States signed a federal contract to stop planting one field so the soil could rest. On a neighboring parcel he still owned, new ground was cleared and planted instead. The land taken out of production was measured and celebrated. The land brought into production was not.

That shape ran through every episode this week. Someone drew a box around the thing they could see and treated everything outside it as fixed. The rest of the system did not stay still.

The Conservation Reserve Program entered the 1985 Food Security Act after years of visible soil loss across the Great Plains and Midwest. Lawmakers wanted less erosion, smaller grain surpluses, and a rental payment for retiring highly erodible acres for ten years. Bids selected the worst fields first. Idle 100 acres shedding eight tons of soil apiece, and you save 800 tons a year—on paper.

No one at the drafting table treated the rest of each farm as a moving piece. [▶ Episode 140 · 2026-10-12](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep140.html) For every 100 acres idled, farmers converted about 20 acres of other land to crops. The contracted fields did rest. The cultivation frontier simply shifted.

A century earlier the same error had a different fuel. In 1769 James Watt patented a separate condenser that let a steam engine do the same work on roughly one-third the coal a Newcomen engine required. Mine owners watched fuel bills drop. William Stanley Jevons, writing *The Coal Question* in 1865, asked what would happen once that cheaper power reached every mill, every pit, and every project that had previously been uneconomic. Watt had been fighting a ruinous cost—often more than twenty-five pounds of coal per horsepower-hour. [▶ Episode 141 · 2026-10-13](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep141.html) The improved engine used one-third the coal. Britain burned far more coal than before. Lower cost did not stretch a fixed pile. It invited more work.

On the Hāmākua Coast of Hawaiʻi Island the intervention was alive. In the early 1880s rats stripped cane until whole sections became unharvestable. Planters had seen small Indian mongooses work in Jamaica and believed an introduction would be simple and self-sustaining. W. H. Purvis brought the first animals in 1883; Joseph Marsden, later “Mongoose Joe,” arranged a shipment of seventy-two from Jamaica the same year. No one tested the broader diet. [▶ Episode 142 · 2026-10-14](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep142.html) Within decades the same mongooses were pulling eggs and goslings from nēnē and other ground-nesting birds across four islands. Seventy-two animals, released with a clear job, rewrote a food web the importers had not mapped.

Congress later tried to rewrite a fuel market with a credit. The Alternative Motor Fuels Act of 1988 followed the oil shocks and the farm crisis. Automakers could count each flex-fuel vehicle as far more efficient than it was by applying a 0.15 divisor to the alternative-fuel portion of its rating. The credit was capped at 1.2 miles per gallon per fleet—enough to matter when standards tightened by tenths. Policymakers expected ethanol use to follow the vehicles onto the road. [▶ Episode 143 · 2026-10-15](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep143.html) By 2014 more than twenty million flex-fuel vehicles sat in American driveways. Almost none used E85, because pumps stayed scarce outside the Midwest. Detroit collected the CAFE credit. The fuel did not have to flow for the credit to count.

Hatcheries make the substitution problem plainer. Canada’s Department of Fisheries and Oceans launched the Salmonid Enhancement Program in 1977 after Pacific returns fell. Officials believed hatcheries could lift freshwater survival from the natural 2–25 percent into the 70–80 percent range. The ocean looked like a vast, underused feeding ground. By 2001, biologists sampling coho in the Strait of Georgia found that seventy percent of the catch carried a hatchery mark. [▶ Episode 144 · 2026-10-16](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep144.html) Those marked fish survived the marine phase worse than unmarked wild fish, and the wild share of the population had fallen. The program produced the numbers it was built for, in part by replacing the fish it was meant to add.

Water engineering repeated the pattern at the scale of a floodplain. After World War II, Turkish planners launched the Southeastern Anatolia Project—GAP—to lift a poor region with hydropower and irrigation. Syria and Iraq followed. Thirty-two major dams rose on the Tigris and Euphrates. Models treated the rivers as a linear supply chain: store the flood, release it when useful. Downstream, the Mesopotamian marshes had lived for millennia on the pulse of high water, not on a steadier average. [▶ Episode 145 · 2026-10-17](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep145.html) By the early 2000s, Landsat showed reed beds reduced to cracked salt flats. Timing of delivery had mattered as much as volume. The dams served factories and cotton. They removed the flood that had kept the marshes alive.

The week closed on another biofuel ledger. President George W. Bush signed the Energy Independence and Security Act on December 19, 2007, expanding the Renewable Fuel Standard toward 36 billion gallons by 2022. Corn-starch ethanol was already commercial; it could scale without new engines. In the spring of 2008, farmers across Iowa and Illinois prepared for record plantings. [▶ Episode 146 · 2026-10-18](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/unintended_consequences/ep146.html) The 2007 standard expanded U.S. corn area by about 2.8 million hectares and raised nitrate flows down the Mississippi toward the Gulf of Mexico. Energy security and tailpipe carbon were the boxes being measured. The dead zone sat outside them.

None of these people were cartoon villains. USDA officials could see gullies. Watt could see wasted coal. Planters could see ruined cane. Legislators could see oil shocks. Biologists could see empty nets. Engineers could see drought. Each intervention was locally rational given the data on the table.

What they shared was a boundary. The CRP contract measured the enrolled field. The Watt patent measured coal per horsepower-hour. The mongoose shipment measured rats in cane. The CAFE divisor measured a vehicle that *could* burn E85. The hatchery measured smolts released. The dam measured stored acre-feet. The RFS measured gallons blended. In every case the rest of the system moved.

Two principles kept repeating. First, a gain measured inside a box is not a net gain until you check the edges. Leakage, rebound, and slippage are names for treating everything outside the intervention as fixed. Second, a proxy that can be collected without the underlying behavior will be collected without it. Flex-fuel credits, hatchery marks, and idled-acre contracts all paid for a representation of the good.

The original aims were decent. The week’s cases leave a question worth asking before the next contract is signed: if this works exactly as designed, what else will people, animals, and markets do next?
