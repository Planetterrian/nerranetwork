## Top Stories

### 1. A possible first moon beyond the Solar System
Observations with ESO's Very Large Telescope uncovered evidence of a Jupiter-mass body orbiting the brown dwarf in the CD-35 2722 system. If the companion is a true exosatellite rather than a binary partner, it would be the first moon detected outside our Solar System — a long-sought missing piece in how planets and their attendants form around objects that never quite became stars. Confirmation would add a new category to the exoplanet census and a new test of formation models at the boundary between planets, moons, and brown dwarfs. ### 2. A two-million-light-year treble clef, reawakened
Chandra and eROSITA show that the radio relic VLSS J0318.9+5755, first examined in 2004, is fossil plasma from a supermassive black hole, later re-energized by a shock from colliding galaxy clusters. Composite images paint radio in red and newly detected X-rays in blue; the feature remains hidden in visible light by the Milky Way's disk. Structures like this are rare laboratories for how clusters interact and how old particles can be made to radiate again. ### 3. Magnetars born in a millisecond
High-resolution simulations of low-mass neutron-star binaries found that Kelvin-Helmholtz mixing at contact can amplify magnetic fields more than 300-fold in roughly one millisecond, producing a magnetar remnant rather than a black hole. The run began just before collision and captured the full amplification before the remnant had time to settle. The result offers a concrete channel for the universe's strongest magnets and for the transients they power. ### 4. Europe's lunar service station and cargo lander
OASIS is designed to supply at least 75 watts by day and 15 watts through the lunar night, plus communications and data handling, so payloads need not carry heavy night-survival systems. ### 5. White dwarfs that are not white
Researchers at the University of Warwick modeled 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs of Earth and found that five of them — about 3.3 percent — take on pink, purple, cyan, or green tints when carbon, calcium, sodium, or planetary debris sits in their dense atmospheres. A true blackbody can only appear red, orange, white, or pale blue; these remnants break that rule. Among them is WD J1330+6435, a star that would look green. One in thirty is a small fraction, yet across the Galaxy it is a lot of oddly colored stars. ## Research Spotlight

**How a magnetar is born in one millisecond**

Most neutron stars sit above the Chandrasekhar limit of white dwarfs yet below the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit near 2.6 solar masses. Merge two of the lighter ones and you can, in principle, get a single neutron star instead of a black hole. The open question has been whether that remnant can also become a magnetar — an object whose magnetic field reaches 10^14 gauss and higher, powering giant flares and some of the sky's most extreme transients.

A team running on Japan's Fugaku supercomputer followed low-mass binaries whose initial fields were already strong, around 3 trillion gauss, at the upper end of ordinary neutron-star values. The simulation started just before contact and ended shortly after. In that window the neutron fluid developed Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities along the shear layer where the two stars touched. Material mixed so thoroughly that the merged star's field grew more than 300 times stronger in roughly one millisecond — fast enough to influence the remnant's structure before it had time to settle.

That timescale matters. If amplification is this rapid, a newborn magnetar can tap its field for early electromagnetic counterparts, including short gamma-ray burst afterglows and peculiarities in kilonova light, without waiting for a slower dynamo. The work, posted on the arXiv as *A magnetar formation in binary neutron star merger*, does not claim that every merger makes a magnetar. It shows that when the masses allow a neutron-star remnant, hydrodynamics alone can do the magnetic heavy lifting. The observational test will be future gravitational-wave events whose remnants show prompt, magnetar-like X-ray plateaus. Until then, the millisecond remains one of the more astonishing intervals in computational astrophysics: long enough to rewrite a star's magnetism, short enough to miss if you blink.
