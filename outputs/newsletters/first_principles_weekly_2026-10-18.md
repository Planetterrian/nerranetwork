The bill is almost never the materials. This week’s seven episodes start from that fact: finished prices that follow the last successful shipment, not the kilograms, the joules, or the copper pair.

## The week in first principles

Every price on this week’s tape begins as an analogy. Movers bill the hour because crews have always been quoted that way. Packers shipped the whole steer because animals had always gone to market that way. Tire makers, cabinet shops, telephone companies, and radio factories each inherited a finished form—and then charged for the form. First-principles work starts one step earlier: what mass actually has to move, what energy the process consumes, what the raw sheet or the copper pair can already do if you stop copying the last successful shipment.

[▶ Episode 128 · 2026-10-12](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep128.html) opened on a household move across town. Service Anchor’s 2026 book shows $90–150 per hour for a two-person crew with truck and $130–200 for three people, with two- or three-hour minimums. The clock starts when the crew arrives and stops only after the last item sits inside the new residence. Stairs, a narrow hall, a sofa that will not rotate around a corner: each of those is more billed presence, not more physics. The truck itself burns diesel on the order of 48 cents a mile and carries ownership costs near 40 cents more—roughly $0.90 a mile all-in. When two people spend an extra forty minutes fighting that sofa, the customer pays forty minutes at the full crew rate while the engine idles. The final bill tracks coordination and risk—insurance, idle summer capacity, dispatch—more tightly than the incremental cost of rolling the truck another mile.

[▶ Episode 129 · 2026-10-13](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep129.html) is the week’s cleanest ratio. Before Swift, packers loaded live cattle onto railcars and paid freight on the entire animal. Roughly half of every steer was bone, hide, and offal. Encyclopedia.com’s split still holds: a 1,000-pound animal yielded about 600 pounds of usable beef. Shippers paid to haul the discarded 400, plus feed and water en route, plus animals that weakened or died. Gustavus Swift, after moving his packing operation to Chicago in 1875, refused that baseline. He looked at the physical requirements of keeping meat cold and at the actual mass that needed to move. He slaughtered in the Midwest and sent only the cuts in ice-cooled, insulated cars. He eliminated freight on half the animal. The old unit of shipment was a walking cow. The new unit was cold, dressed mass over distance.

[▶ Episode 130 · 2026-10-14](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep130.html) put perhaps fifteen dollars of natural and synthetic rubber, carbon black, steel wire, and textile cord against a retail price of one hundred to two hundred dollars—an Idiot Index on the order of seven to thirteen, depending on the aisle. Physics does not require the spread. The green tire is assembled on a drum and cured at roughly 150 degrees Celsius for about twenty minutes. After the press come brand distribution, wholesale, retail inventory, mounting, warranty, and marketing that separates two similar carcasses. The magic-wand number is the commodity floor. Everything above it is coordination, capital recovery, and story.

[▶ Episode 131 · 2026-10-15](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep131.html) is what happens when the analogy is simply more copper. Before inductive loading, a long circuit lost so much signal to capacitance that engineers had to accept short range or thick, expensive runs. New York–Chicago opened in 1892 on that scaling logic and stayed costly and unreliable for decades. Companies priced the call to recover the metal and the operators who patched every circuit by hand. Twenty dollars bought only three minutes from New York to San Francisco. Michael Pupin’s loading coils, patented in December 1899, and George Campbell’s parallel work at AT&T inserted inductance at regular intervals. The same pair carried intelligible speech hundreds of miles farther without a proportional pile of extra copper. Vacuum-tube repeaters from John J. Carty’s team in 1915 pushed the principle across the continent. The first-principles move was not a thicker gauge. It was a different electrical element.

[▶ Episode 132 · 2026-10-16](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep132.html) makes the tire story architectural. A finished kitchen of twenty to thirty cabinets sells for several thousand dollars and contains only a few hundred in sheet goods and hardware—an Idiot Index on the order of ten. Panels come from plywood, particleboard, or MDF, usually faced with melamine or a thin veneer, and must meet EPA TSCA Title VI formaldehyde limits in force since 2019. A designer measures, a shop cuts, the boxes sit for weeks, and installers shim and scribe to walls that are never square. Custom work redraws the same basic box for finish options and drawer counts. The index lives in the handling, the storage, the scheduling, and the scribe.

Then the factories that closed their gaps. [▶ Episode 133 · 2026-10-17](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep133.html): A. Atwater Kent’s 35-acre plant on Wissahickon Avenue in Philadelphia replaced wooden breadboards, point-to-point wiring, and separate batteries with stamped metal chassis, pre-formed tube sockets, and conveyor soldering. Functions that had belonged to one skilled assembler walking a bench were split across stations. He built nearly one million radios in 1929.

[▶ Episode 134 · 2026-10-18](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep134.html) rhymes with it. RCA’s Model 630TS listed at $352 in October 1946: thirty tubes, a wooden cabinet, a thirteen-channel tuner. Pre-war television had been laboratory gear. After 1946 the furniture habit held—fresh chassis, custom wood, dealer margins—because each stage was designed for isolated performance rather than shared tooling. Within a few years the same basic chassis, produced in volume by RCA and its licensees, helped pull average factory prices down to $189 by 1949. Three hundred fifty-two dollars was not a law of electrons. It was a starting point that standardization could attack.

Thread the seven together and the method does not change. Do not ask what the last successful firm charged. Ask what has to move, what has to stay cold, what inductance the pair already needs, what the press does in twenty minutes, what the sheet of MDF actually is. Analogy ships the bone. First principles ship the beef. The opportunity is still sitting in plain sight wherever a quote tracks crew hours, cabinet scribes, brand stories, or furniture finish more tightly than it tracks mass and energy. Swift, Pupin, Kent, and RCA are not nostalgia. They are existence proofs that the index comes down when someone rebuilds the unit of shipment around the physics instead of the habit.

**P.S.** Someone is going to do to the local move what Swift did to the live steer—price the mile and the mass instead of the crew—and the first firm that does will not need a summer surcharge to explain itself.
