## By the numbers

- **few cents → 5¢** — roughly, steel to a Gillette blade after progressive dies
- **on the order of 10×** — office-chair price over steel, foam, and fabric
- **under $2 vs. over $20** — early pneumatic-tire materials versus selling price
- **10× or more** — short-haul ticket over jet fuel per passenger
- **~$5,000 vs. a few hundred dollars** — 1886 combine versus its steel and canvas

If you only remember one move from this week, remember this one: write down the atoms first. Every episode from Monday through Sunday ran the same experiment. Take a finished thing people treat as expensive because it has always been expensive. Strip it to steel, rubber, foam, fuel, canvas. Call that the magic-wand number — the raw-material floor. Then divide the sticker by that floor. The Idiot Index that falls out is not an insult. It is a map of every step we kept because the last generation kept it.

Gillette is the cleanest illustration we have, which is why [▶ Episode 121 · 2026-10-05](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep121.html) started there. Before 1904 a man shaved with a straight razor that cost several dollars and went back to a barber or a hone when the edge died. Afterward he paid five dollars for a holder and five cents for a blade stamped from a few cents of steel. Nothing about carbon content had to change. What changed was the category. A razor had been a durable capital good, forged from a bar, heated to roughly 800 degrees Celsius, then ground for hours to a 15-to-20-degree bevel. Each piece was essentially unique. Progressive dies treated the edge as a consumable thin strip. Per-shave cost could fall toward the price of the metal because the process finally matched the physics: you only need a sharp film of steel, not a lifetime tool. That is reasoning from materials. The old cutlery trade was reasoning by analogy — copy the forged blade, because that is what a razor is.

Office chairs never took the equivalent step. [▶ Episode 122 · 2026-10-06](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep122.html) put a standard adjustable chair next to its bill of materials and found several hundred dollars of price sitting on tens of dollars of steel, foam, and fabric. The gap is specification, not metallurgy. Catalogs accrete levers and lumbar mechanisms because an earlier model lacked them and a competitor matched the feature. Certification to standards such as BIFMA repeats load and durability tests on every variant, then spreads that cost across modest volume. The base, gas cylinder, and foam arrive from separate shops; final assembly often happens in small batches for custom colors. Each handoff takes a margin. No one is forced to ask whether most users need every adjustment. The current price feels like a property of the chair instead of a property of a fragmented, over-specified process.

Rubber went through both halves of the pattern in a single generation. [▶ Episode 123 · 2026-10-07](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep123.html) followed John Dunlop's 1888 patent from solid band to thin envelope. Solid bicycle tires had been copied since the 1860s because they were simple to mold and consumed a large volume of expensive gum. Dunlop swapped that mass for cotton canvas, sulfur, and a little natural rubber inflated with air. Raw ingredients for one tire fell to under two dollars. Early commercial versions still sold for over twenty, because every step remained hand work behind patents. The industry's first reaction was that the new tire was fragile, not that the old tire had been wasteful. For more than a decade the pneumatic product carried a finished price on the order of ten times its commodities. Analogy plus royalties can hold a price up long after the atoms have gotten cheaper.

Steam power is the other historical collapse, and [▶ Episode 125 · 2026-10-09](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep125.html) treated Parsons as Gillette's counterpart in energy. In 1884 a compact machine turned high-pressure steam into steady rotary power for a dynamo at 18,000 revolutions per minute. Reciprocating engines of the 1880s were scaled factory and ship designs — pistons, cranks, flywheels, packing that had to be replaced — and each added cylinder increased weight and leak paths. Parsons let steam expand through successive rows of precisely angled blades. Hundreds of moving parts became a balanced rotor whose material content was measured in hundreds of pounds, while early finished units still cost thousands. By 1900 the Idiot Index on electricity from steam had dropped sharply. The working fluid was still steam. The conversion path had stopped copying the piston.

Harvesting grain had the same fork in the road. [▶ Episode 127 · 2026-10-11](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep127.html) went back to Benjamin Holt's first commercial combine in California in 1886. The machine cut, threshed, cleaned, and bagged in one pass. It sold for five thousand dollars and held only a few hundred dollars of steel and canvas. Before that, harvest was a copied sequence from smaller eastern farms: reapers in the field, wagons to a stationary thresher, a belt from a steam engine or a horse team. Early wheeled machines still pulled that old sequence with twenty to forty horses and were built to order. Farmers accepted the cost because no one had asked what minimum parts physics required to move grain from stalk to sack. Combining the jobs was the first-principles cut. The steel was never the expensive part.

Two live categories this week still look like the pre-Gillette razor.

[▶ Episode 124 · 2026-10-08](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep124.html) placed a typical domestic short-haul ticket next to the jet fuel required to move one passenger through that leg. The fare often exceeds that fuel cost by a factor of ten or more. Fuel is the physics. Around it sit airport slots allocated by long-standing rules, distribution and agency fees that can reach double-digit percentages of the fare, yield algorithms that sell the same seat at widely different prices, crew costs fixed once the flight is scheduled, and airport charges with minimum guarantees. The visible price bundles costs that do not scale with one extra passenger. Inside the industry the bundle feels normal because every competitor faces the same constraints. The magic-wand number is sitting in the wing tanks. The sticker is the institution.

[▶ Episode 126 · 2026-10-10](https://nerranetwork.com/blog/first_principles/ep126.html) did the inventory on a gym. A typical club charges around fifty dollars a month. The steel, motors, and basic electronics that actually move the weights and belts are a few dollars of commodity. Opening a Planet Fitness club, when equipment is financed, runs between roughly 1.28 and 3.77 million dollars, with the bulk in leasehold improvements and site work rather than machines. Equipment packages for a mid-size facility range from about 150,000 to 1.8 million dollars and already include distribution, installation, and warranties. Industry figures put membership churn around one-third per year, so marketing and staffing to replace those members are inside the dues. Each club is still largely assembled on site, with little of the repetition that drives factory unit cost down. The member is not paying for a motor. The member is paying for a retail lease, a custom build-out, and a revolving door.

Put the seven next to each other and the method stops being a metaphor. Name the function. Name the atoms. Separate the physics from the analog. Where someone actually did that — stamping a blade, inflating a tire, expanding steam through a rotor, combining reaper and thresher — finished cost moved toward the floor, even when patents and hand assembly delayed the landing. Where we have not — the over-specified chair, the short-haul ticket, the gym in a strip mall — the Idiot Index is a polite name for process we have agreed to treat as natural. The raw-material floor is already public. The work is refusing to reason from last year's catalog.
