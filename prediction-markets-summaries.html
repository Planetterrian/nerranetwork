How It Works: Hedging With Event Contracts

An event contract pays if a stated outcome occurs and pays nothing if it does not. Using that payoff as a hedge means the gain is meant to offset a loss on something the holder already has, so the contract is doing a job closer to insurance than to a standalone wager. The price is what the other side will accept to take the remaining risk.

Good Authority, citing Marieke de Goede’s Virtue, Fortune, Faith: A Genealogy of Finance, says nineteenth-century regulators separated bucket-shop gambling from legitimate futures by whether losses could be quantified and modeled. In that account, a gambler faces the house. A hedger shifts risk toward someone with a different appetite for it.

A 2020 paper in the journal Risks works the arithmetic of a second trade: after the price moves, take the opposite position to the first. The result it derives is that a profit can be locked in without a forecast of the final outcome. Two frictions break that result in practice. An exchange charges a fee. Some markets have no lay option, so there is no way to take the against side at all.

A 2026 paper on arXiv uses Bitcoin as a proof of concept and treats Kalshi contracts on the coin’s future price as option-like hedges, in bullish, bearish, and flat regimes. The authors report that a dynamic mix of those contracts and the spot coin beat simpler portfolios across all three regimes. They also say fees can erode the result. That is a research claim about those constructions, not a plan to copy.

Regulators still have to decide which use is the real one. Good Authority reports that roughly 90 percent of Kalshi’s trading volume is sports contests; that Nevada, Minnesota, New York, and Washington have tried to ban those bets; and that the CFTC has filed suits asserting that it alone regulates futures markets. The Third Circuit sided with the CFTC in April. The Sixth and Ninth Circuits have ruled for the states on at least some of these markets. The Supreme Court may eventually hear some of the cases on appeal. A price is easier to read as insurance, or harder, once the fee, the right to take the other side, and the court that claims the contract are all in view.

How It Works: Sports Event Contracts, Sportsbooks and Gambling Harm

A sportsbook posts a number and stands on the other side of it. A sports event contract on an exchange does not. Two people agree a price; if the listed event occurs, the contract pays a fixed sum to the buyer, and if it does not, the seller keeps what was paid. The venue does not have to hold a view. The last trade is the price. When the other side is a pool rather than a person, an automated market maker sets that price from a formula and a pile of collateral.

A 2021 paper in the Journal of Finance treats liquid sports-betting contracts as a laboratory for pricing anomalies. Two features do the work: there is no systematic risk of the kind equities carry, and the contract's terminal value is set outside the betting itself. That design gets around the joint-hypothesis problem, so a gap between the traded price and the later result can be read as mispricing rather than as compensation for risk. The author finds strong momentum, a pattern consistent with delayed overreaction and not with underreaction or with rational pricing. The returns are a fraction of returns in financial markets and fail to overcome transaction costs, so arbitrage does not clear the pattern. An observation from that betting record also predicts value and momentum returns in U.S. equities. None of that is a method to copy, and it is not a claim that any live contract on Kalshi or Polymarket is cheap or rich.

A 2023 paper on arXiv looks at the pool version of the same problem. It argues that an automated market maker built like Uniswap is not lined up with fair odds, which leaves liquidity providers holding a risk the formula did not price. The authors describe UBET's UAMM, smart contracts meant to set sports prices inside collateral pools, and they report experiments with positive outcomes. That is a design result, not a published accuracy record.

Read the number on a sports contract as a trade, or as a formula's output, not as a house line and not as a settled chance. The Journal of Finance result says those trades can carry a momentum streak that costs make hard to trade away. A thin book is a weaker version of the same object. The papers measure prices and liquidity. They do not measure gambling harm, which is a separate question from whether the last trade was informative.

How It Works: Superforecasters and Forecasting Tournaments

The method that won a U.S. Intelligence Community geopolitical forecasting tournament was to cull top performers each year and assign them to elite teams. A 2015 paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science calls those teams' members superforecasters. They maintained high accuracy across hundreds of questions and a wide array of topics, defying expectations of regression toward the mean two years in a row. The authors find support for four mutually reinforcing explanations: cognitive abilities and styles, task-specific skills, motivation and commitment, and enriched environments. Superforecasters, they write, are partly discovered and partly created. The high-performance incentives of tournaments, the paper says, highlight aspects of human judgment that would not come to light in laboratory paradigms focused on typical performance.

A 2014 paper in Psychological Science describes the setting. Five university-based research groups competed to recruit forecasters, elicit predictions, and aggregate those predictions into probabilities for events in a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament. One group found support for three psychological drivers: training, teaming, and tracking. Probability training corrected cognitive biases, encouraged the use of reference classes, and supplied heuristics, such as averaging when multiple estimates were available. Teaming let forecasters share information and discuss the rationales behind their beliefs. Tracking placed the highest performers — the top 2 percent from year one — on elite teams that worked together. Training, team collaboration, and tracking improved both calibration and resolution. Statistical algorithms, reported elsewhere, improved the accuracy of the aggregation. Putting statistics and psychology to work produced the best forecasts two years in a row.

Those abstracts do not show that an event-contract price automatically inherits that record. They do show that accuracy moved when selection, training, teams, and a statistical aggregation rule changed. A price is easier to read as a forecast when the pool and the combination rule are visible, and harder when they are not. How It Works: The Wisdom of Crowds, and When It Fails

A crowd forecast is an average. Each person brings a piece of information, and the average cancels some of the individual error if those pieces are not the same piece repeated.

Da, Diebold, Göbel, Gouveia and Li, in a 2025 arXiv paper, treat macroeconomic survey averages as portfolios of forecasts. They compare the U.S. Survey of Professional Forecasters with a model in which forecast errors are equicorrelated, and they plot accuracy against the number of forecasters in the average. The equicorrelation model fits growth and inflation almost perfectly. Gains from adding forecasters are larger for inflation than for growth, and they are largely exhausted once five to ten representative forecasters are in the average.

Social influence can wreck that cancellation or improve it, depending on the setup. A 2020 Management Science paper on Estimize found that the more public information a user viewed before an earnings forecast, the less weight that user put on private information. Individual forecasts got more accurate. The consensus got worse, because useful private information never entered the average. Experiments that restricted what randomly selected users could see confirmed that more independent forecasts produced a more accurate consensus. Estimize switched to a blind platform in November 2015.

A 2017 PNAS paper argues the opposite danger is overstated in decentralized networks: there, social influence can create learning that improves the group, so influence rather than independence can be the accurate path. A 2020 PNAS paper adds plasticity. When people can rewire who they listen to, and they get feedback, the network can beat its best member. One path changes the links so stronger forecasters count more. The other leaves the links alone and has accurate people resist influence, so their first estimate keeps more weight.

For a market, the price is that kind of average with money on it. It works when traders are not all staring at the same public number. It fails when they copy one another, drop their private information, or share one wrong story. A blind or independent read can protect the average. A network that can drop bad links can sometimes do better than independence. Neither result is a reason to take a side. How It Works: How an Automated Market Maker Sets a Price

An automated market maker quotes a price even when no second trader is waiting. In a logarithmic market scoring rule, that quote comes from a price function, and the liquidity parameter inside it — often written as b — sets how far one trade moves the price. Raise b and the same trade nudges the quote less. Lower b and the quote jumps farther. The sponsor stands as counterparty, so the parameter is also a bound on how much the sponsor can lose while the market stays open. The abstract of a 2018 paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management calls logarithmic market scoring rules one of the most simple and widely used of these mechanisms, and says market makers have to set b in the price functions because it has significant effects on market performance.

That paper, “Optimizing the liquidity parameter of logarithmic market scoring rules prediction markets,” used artificial prediction markets — simulations built to mimic real ones — to measure forecast standard error and the time for the price to converge to the true value. Forecast standard error fell as the liquidity parameter rose. For any given number of traders, the simulations found a parameter value that kept price adaptability appropriate and produced the fastest price convergence. The authors treat standard error, time to convergence, and cost as the tradeoff the market maker has to set.

A quote that barely moves and a quote that leaps can come from the same price function at different settings of b. In those simulations, a higher liquidity parameter came with a smaller forecast standard error, while the fastest convergence, for a given crowd size, sat at one value that preserved adaptability. Cost belongs in that same choice, because the sponsor is paying to keep the quote up. Someone reading a market price is also reading that setting: how far one trade is allowed to move the number, and who stands on the other side. The paper is not a guide to which side of any contract to take.

How It Works: Proper Scoring Rules

How a forecast is scored decides whether honesty is the profitable report. When a principal can pay after a random variable resolves, a strictly proper scoring rule is incentive compatible: the strategic agent’s best move is to reveal the private probability, and more accurate forecasts receive higher scores in expectation. That mechanism is the baseline in a 2018 arXiv paper on what to do when the outcome never arrives.

Surrogate scoring rules cover the case where the principal sees only agents’ reports. They combine a bias-correction step with an error-rate estimate for a reference answer defined from those reports. With a single bit of information about the prior distribution, and in a multi-task setting, the paper shows these rules recover the strictly proper score in expectation, as if ground truth were in hand. They still quantify information quality without that ground truth. They also induce dominant truthfulness in reporting. The method was verified theoretically and empirically on data from real human forecasters.

A 2015 Monthly Weather Review paper takes up multivariate forecasts, where few proper scores exist and many of them require a full density. The energy score, a multivariate generalization of the continuous ranked probability score, is the only commonly used score that applies to ensemble forecasts, a finite sample. Its ability to detect incorrectly specified correlations is limited. Variogram-based proper scores, built from a geostatistical idea, were more sensitive in simulations to wrong means, variances and correlations, and more discriminative on correlation structure. A case study of postprocessed wind-speed forecasts at five Colorado wind-park sites confirmed that pattern.

Reading a market price is reading a forecast that will be scored when the contract resolves. There, a proper rule can make honesty pay. Where there is no ground truth, or where the question is several correlated pieces at once, these papers say the choice of score decides which mistakes stay invisible.

How It Works: Insiders and Event Contracts

A bookmaker who thinks someone in the crowd already knows the result will not post the price a fully public crowd would justify. H. S. Shin's 1993 model says the bookie builds a cushion against that insider, and the cushion is not spread evenly. Longshots get the worse price. That gap is the favourite-longshot bias.

A 2003 paper in the Bulletin of Economic Research checked the pattern in a variety of sports betting markets where bookmakers set the odds. The bias was there. The precise pattern was not the same market to market. The paper also found evidence for a central prediction of the Shin model: margins increase with the number of competitors. A larger field gives an insider more places to hide a bet, and gives the bookmaker more reason to widen the take.

A 2002 paper in Economica asked a different question: when odds shorten sharply, is that an insider, or a crowd chasing a crowd? N. F. R. Crafts had suggested in 1985 that a pronounced shortening could signal insider trading. The later authors applied the Shin measure of insider incidence at opening prices and again at starting prices. When odds shortened and the Shin measure rose, betting returns were significantly positive. When odds shortened and the Shin measure fell, returns were negative. The authors read the second case as herd behaviour, not information.

That split is the caution. A move can be someone who knows something, which pulls the price toward the outcome and away from a fair deal for everyone else. Or it can be someone following the last trade, which adds noise and still punishes whoever joined late. These studies are about bookmakers posting odds, not peer-to-peer event contracts, so the mechanism does not transfer one for one. On an exchange there is no bookie forced to shade every contract; the cost shows up in the spread and the fee. What does transfer is the tension. A venue that attracts people trading on non-public information can print a price closer to the eventual result and, at the same time, a price that is less fair to everyone without that information. The direction of the last trade does not, by itself, say which of those two things just happened.

How It Works: How Markets Aggregate Information

A market price pools scattered information when the trade is the message. Someone holding a private signal buys or sells, the quote moves, and the next person meets a number that already contains that signal. An automated market maker can post the quote without a human taking the other side.

Information Aggregation in Exponential Family Markets, a 2014 paper on arXiv, shows those makers can be designed from exponential family distributions, a standard template in statistics. It connects price aggregation to the way learning agents pool beliefs when they use the same distributions. Under exponential utility, a form of risk aversion, the paper analyzes market behavior and the price equilibrium. It also considers traders who face a budget constraint rather than that utility alone.

Final prices are a weaker record than they look. Are Final Market Prices Sufficient for Information Aggregation?, a 2025 paper on arXiv, studies parimutuel betting on horse races, where a wager cannot be made contingent on the final odds. Interim odds show expected returns depend on the path, not only the finish: horses whose odds declined in the final five minutes earned higher realized returns than horses with similar final odds. A two-period extension of the Ottaviani and Sørensen information model accounts for the pattern, with late declines coming from informed bettors wagering on private signals. The paper adds that final-odds and return patterns alone may not separate information aggregation from probability distortions.

A reader cannot treat the last price as a complete file of what was known. The path into the price matters, and so does whether anyone could contract on the price that eventually printed. The racing measurement describes those markets. It is not a method for any contract.

How It Works: Arbitrage: When Two Venues Disagree

A winner-take-all set is built to be exhaustive and mutually exclusive, so related prices should add to one dollar. Falling short of that dollar, or running past it, is a bound that follows from the payoff at settlement. It is not automatically a position a trader can close while the market is still open.

A 2025 arXiv paper on Polymarket names two forms of that gap. Market-rebalancing arbitrage sits inside one market. Combinatorial arbitrage runs across related markets. From on-chain order-book history, the authors estimate about 40 million dollars of profit extracted. A full comparison of every linked condition does not scale, so they narrowed the search by timing, topic, and combinatorial relationship.

A 2026 arXiv paper splits the bound again. Payoff-space no-arbitrage follows from terminal payoffs. Protocol-executable no-arbitrage depends on the conversions a trader can actually make before settlement. On Polymarket's negative-risk markets, linked binaries stand for mutually exclusive outcomes, but the NegRisk Adapter operationalizes only the NO-to-YES direction before settlement. Reconstructing depth-aware portfolios and on-chain conversion traces, the authors estimate 1.12 million dollars of arbitrage profit: 1.086 million from converter-enabled strategies and 32 thousand from baskets held to settlement. In the order-book sample, positive violations concentrated on the unsupported YES side, while adapter-supported NO-side violations were less frequent and shorter-lived. The authors' reading is that a pre-settlement conversion strengthens enforcement by reducing capital lock-up and letting inventory recycle. They also built a prototype that would make the reverse path executable before settlement.

When two prices disagree, the same constraint applies. A gap is enforceable only if a trader can turn one position into the other before resolution. Where that conversion is missing, the bound can sit until the contract pays.

An event contract is a promise with a price. The buyer pays now. If the stated event happens, the contract pays a fixed sum. If it does not, the contract pays nothing. How It Works: Who Decides the Answer? Resolution, Rules and Oracles

A crypto-settled contract does not pay when traders converge. Collateral stays locked until an oracle delivers a result and winning claims can be redeemed. That handoff is the oracle problem, as an ACM Computing Surveys paper frames it: a smart contract can read on-chain data, but the event the question is about lives off-chain, so some mechanism has to move the outside result onto the chain.

The survey splits oracle implementations into two groups. Voting-based designs finalize an outcome mainly from participants' stakes. Reputation-based designs combine reputation and performance metrics with authenticity-proof mechanisms aimed at data correctness and integrity. Both are described as ways to give a smart contract final feedback. The paper closes by noting remaining challenges and future research directions, without treating either family as settled.

A separate arXiv paper measures what the wait does to the price. When collateral remains locked until oracle settlement, a near-certain dollar is a delayed dollar, so the price embeds a maturity-dependent settlement discount in addition to beliefs about the outcome. The authors recover an implied settlement-discount term structure from persistent near-certain contracts, using realized settlement times, and summarize it as an annualized settlement wedge. The recovered wedges are positive, maturity-dependent, and time-varying. Adjusting prices by those curves reduces the near-certainty horizon gradient by roughly 48 to 88 percent, which the paper treats as evidence that much of the raw maturity pattern is priced settlement friction rather than forecast error alone.

Design changes the wedge. negRisk conversion compresses discounts by recycling part of the position into synthetic collateral. Yield-bearing collateral flattens the term structure by reducing the opportunity cost of lock-up. On this account, pricing quality is endogenous to settlement mechanics, collateral productivity, and capital-recycling design. A price near certainty is a belief plus a claim on money that cannot move until the named resolution process — an oracle report, a stake-weighted vote, or a reputation-weighted check — says the question is closed.

How It Works: Can a Prediction Market Be Manipulated?

A contract can settle on a number its own holders are free to trade. That is settlement manipulation in plain form. A paper posted to arXiv on June 30, 2026, models contracts that pay on an asset price holders can move by trading the underlying. The model says those contracts transfer wealth from prediction-market liquidity traders to manipulators. It also says they harm price discovery in the underlying, even as that market becomes more liquid. After Polymarket launched a five-minute Bitcoin contract, settlement-time spot order flow spiked. The spikes caused large price reversals after settlement. Manipulators captured a large amount of profit, mostly from retail. Manipulation was largely absent in the fifteen-minute contracts. Lengthening the horizon removed it, and the authors call that the market-design remedy their model and evidence support.

A second paper, posted to arXiv on January 28, 2026, pairs agent-based simulation with an analytic account of price dynamics. Bettors differ in expertise, noisy private information, learning rates and budgets, and they watch public opinion on the outcome before they bet. The simulated market is stable across a broad parameter space. Complex behavior and price interactions produce self-regulatory price discovery. A highly resourced minority — a whale — with a biased valuation can still shift the price, and the shift is temporary. The move is larger and lasts longer when other bettors herd and learn slowly. Theory in the paper says whales shift prices in proportion to their share of market capital. How long the distortion lasts depends on non-whale learning rates and on how intensely those bettors herd.

A short contract on a tradable price is the case where the defense fails in this evidence. A longer horizon is the fix that paper measured. A binary book can be shoved by capital share and herding, then pull back if the rest of the book keeps learning.

How It Works: Markets Against Polls

In a continuous double auction, traders buy and sell contracts and can be ranked by what they earn. A prediction poll, in the test that follows, asks each person for a probability instead, alone or in a team, and ranks those judgments with a Brier score.

Management Science published that head-to-head test in 2017. The paper reports the first large-scale, long-term experimental comparison of the two methods. More than 2,400 people forecast 261 events across two seasons of a geopolitical prediction tournament, and they were randomly assigned to markets or to polls. Uri Gneezy accepted the paper for the journal’s behavioral economics area.

Market prices were more accurate than the simple average of poll forecasts in both seasons. Team polls then beat the markets, but only after the forecasts were combined with temporal decay, more weight for forecasters who had done well before, and recalibration. The polls’ largest edge appeared at the start of questions that still had a long time to run.

That pattern is the useful limit. A raw average of probability judgments lost to the market price. A statistically aggregated team poll did not. The gap favored the polls earliest, on the longest questions. Proper scoring feedback and collaboration were part of the poll design that produced the stronger aggregated result. A price is one way to compress a crowd. It is not the only way, and in this tournament it was not always the most accurate way. None of these comparisons is a suggestion to take either side of a contract.

How It Works: The Favourite-Longshot Bias

In a parimutuel market, the favorite-longshot bias is a return pattern: the expected return on an outcome tends to rise with the share of bets placed on that outcome. A 2010 paper in the American Economic Journal: Microeconomics treats that pattern as something to test, not as a fixed law. It ties the direction and the size of the bias to the ratio of private information to noise in the market. That ratio, the paper says, should show up in observables: how many people are betting, how many outcomes are on offer, how much private information is around, how much participation comes from recreational interest, whether bets can be divided finely, whether noise arrives after the fact, and whether outcomes were already unequal before anyone bet.

A September 2026 paper looks for the same pattern on Polymarket, using 588 million trades by 2.48 million accounts. In the trades as they happened, purchases below 10 cents lost 19.3 cents per dollar. Purchases at or above 90 cents earned 0.83 cents per dollar. Grouping changes the picture. When each contract is weighted equally, longshots lost 6.3 cents per dollar. When related contracts are first grouped by parent event, those longshots gained 4.1 cents per dollar. The two-sided pattern — cheap contracts losing, expensive ones gaining a little — held in crypto and politics and was absent in sports. Accounts with the strongest past habit of buying longshots, the top tenth, made 26.6 percent of the next month's low-price purchases and earned returns similar to everyone else. The top tenth of accounts that had been buying favorites made 15.1 percent of the next month's high-price purchases and earned less than others. The paper's claim is an aggregate favorite-longshot bias on Polymarket whose size depends on how contracts are grouped. A price near zero or near one is not, on this evidence, a clean read of probability; the grouping of the contract, and whether the market is sports, politics, or crypto, changes the return that price has delivered. Those figures describe past trades. They are not a reason to take either side of any contract.

How It Works: Calibration: What It Means for a Forecast to Be Right

Calibration means the stated probability matches how often the outcome arrives, across many events rather than one. A sixty percent forecast is calibrated, in that class of calls, if the event happens about sixty percent of the time. Sharpness is separate: probability mass near zero or one, rather than near fifty percent. Sharp and calibrated are not the same thing. One surprising result does not make the forecast wrong, because the score is taken over the set, after the events resolve.

Monthly Weather Review in 2013 describes an improvement to ensemble kernel density model output statistics, called EKDMOS. Model output statistics equations are multiple linear regressions relating observations to ensemble-mean predictors. Spread-skill relationships are one-term linear regressions that predict the ensemble mean’s expected accuracy from the ensemble spread. The method applies the equations to each member, fits a probability density by kernel density fitting, and adjusts that density’s spread to the spread predicted by the spread-skill relationship. On North American Ensemble Forecast System two-meter temperature forecasts from October 1, 2007, through March 31, 2010, the improved EKDMOS forecasts were more reliable than an earlier spread adjustment and than rank-sorting bias-corrected members, with a better calibrated spread-error relationship and more day-to-day spread variability.

Weather and Forecasting in 2010 compares bias correction with spatial disaggregation and an analog method on daily precipitation forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Ensemble Prediction System, over a ten-day period, for the Ohio River basin from 2002 through 2006, against Tropical Rainfall Monitoring Mission observations. Neither beat a simple spatial-interpolation benchmark on forecast skill. Both improved reliability, the analog more so. Bias correction improved the bias for all forecast amounts, less so for large amounts, but the downscaled patterns were unrealistic. The analog reduced biases over a wider range of amounts and produced more realistic patterns.

Reading a market, reliability is whether the price’s implied probability matches later frequency, and sharpness is how far that price sits from fifty percent. Those tests show a fix can improve reliability without improving skill against a simple benchmark. A single resolution does not settle calibration, including when a market is thin or the stake is play money.

How It Works: Liquidity, Spreads and Thin Markets

Automated market makers in prediction markets recruit third-party liquidity providers who deposit assets and earn fees from trades. The arXiv paper Bootstrapping Liquidity in BTC-Denominated Prediction Markets shows cross-market making against stablecoin venues supplies the most user-friendly risk profile yet requires active professional makers or platform subsidies. The same paper finds DeFi redirection of user trades enables rapid bootstrapping but exposes participants to liquidation thresholds and exchange-rate volatility. A General Theory of Liquidity Provisioning for Prediction Markets demonstrates that protocols allowing arbitrary cost functions can run multiple market makers in parallel while recovering existing DeFi approaches as special cases. Liquidity expressed as a matrix-valued function becomes necessary once three or more securities trade because single-dimensional parameters cannot cover the full price space. Thin markets move sharply on single trades because limited depth leaves wide bid-ask spreads that one order can cross. Listeners can identify thin markets by low 24-hour volume relative to open interest and by observing large price jumps after modest trading activity. The papers note that automated market making remains simple to deploy but carries capital inefficiency and permanent loss risk for providers. Cross-market making trades stability for the need for ongoing professional oversight.

How It Works: Why a Price Can Be Read as a Probability

A contract that pays a fixed sum if an event happens, and nothing if it does not, trades at a price between those two payoffs. Buyers are taking the view that the event is more likely than that price; sellers, that it is less likely. Where both sides can trade, the price is where that disagreement has cleared for the moment, which is why it can be read as a probability — the terms on which the market last found a counterparty, not a verdict issued by the venue.

A paper in Management Science compared that reading with another way of pooling forecasts. More than 2,400 participants forecast 261 events over two seasons of a geopolitical tournament. They were randomly assigned to prediction markets — continuous double auctions, ranked on earnings — or to prediction polls, submitting probabilities alone or in teams and ranked on Brier scores. In both seasons, market prices were more accurate than the simple mean of the poll forecasts. Team polls outperformed the markets once forecasts were aggregated using temporal decay, weights based on past performance, and recalibration. The polls’ biggest advantage was at the beginning of long-duration questions.

A paper on cost-function market makers notes that trading in a duality-based design collectively implements Follow-The-Regularized-Leader. Modifying that pricing rule, the authors propose a Smooth Quadratic Prediction Market so that trading implements general steepest gradient descent, with a better worst-case monetary loss for AD securities and with no-arbitrage and an instantaneous price preserved. They also look at bounded budgets and buy-only securities, cases where a clean probability reading leaves something out.

The same research flags a limit: independent judgments, properly aggregated, beat market prices at the start of long questions. This show will not say which contract to buy or sell. Every probability here carries its venue, its volume and the time it was read.
