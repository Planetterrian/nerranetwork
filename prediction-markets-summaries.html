How It Works: Reading a Wallet: How a Large Trade Gets Traced

A trade on a public blockchain is a record of an address sending value at a time, which is the whole of what "reading a wallet" starts from. The address is a string the chain already publishes. It is not a name, a passport, or a brokerage account. Anyone with the transaction hash can see the size, the timestamp, and the address that signed it. Reporters and researchers start there because the record does not depend on the venue handing over a customer file.

The next step is clustering, and it is weaker than a name. Analysts group addresses that fund one another, that move funds in a tight sequence, or that show up together around the same market. A cluster is a hypothesis that those addresses are operated together. It is not proof that one person sits behind them. An exchange deposit address, a shared treasury, or two people using one signer will look, on the chain, like a single actor. The inference stops where the funding path hits a service that pools customer funds, because the chain then shows the pool, not the customers.

The concrete case in today's material is not a named whale. It is a count of wallets. RG.org, citing the Anti-Corruption Data Collective's Gambling on Democracy update with data current to September thirtieth, reported that ten wallets control seventeen percent of all congressional volume on Polymarket. Read that sentence as a concentration statistic, not an identification. Ten addresses, or ten clusters the group treated as wallets, account for seventeen cents of every dollar in that congressional set on that venue. The finding does not say those ten wallets belong to ten people, or to one person, or to anyone with information the rest of the market lacks. It says the volume is not spread across thousands of equal accounts.

A second finding in the same update measures how little money it takes to push a displayed price. In markets with live order books as of September thirtieth, the group found that fifty-five point one percent could be moved five cents off their mid-price for less than one dollar, and that fewer than one percent required ten thousand dollars or more. Walk the first number. The mid-price is the middle of the quoted book. Five cents off that middle is a visible jump on a contract that pays at most one dollar. If less than one dollar of orders can produce that jump, the book is shallow: the price a headline quotes can be a thin layer of resting orders, not a deep disagreement among many traders. The same update found that seventy-six percent of the congressional markets hold less than one thousand dollars. A large headline total and a shallow book can be true in the same dataset, because the total is the sum and the book is the slice available at the current price.

Both companies say they guard against misconduct, the same RG.org account reported. That is the dispute on offer: a wallet concentration and a shallow book are measurements of the market's shape, and the venues' answer is that they police the conduct those measurements might suggest. The measurements do not themselves prove manipulation. They also do not prove the opposite. A listener who has followed a trade to a wallet has learned an address, a size, and a time. Treating that address as a person, or that size as informed money, is a step the chain does not take. How It Works: Fees, Spreads and the House Take

A quoted price is the terms of a trade, not the full cost of holding a view. On Choice Prediction Markets, a user sets a number from 0 to 100. A price of 60 means that user is putting the chance at 60 percent. The order executes only when another participant takes the opposite side at that price. Pending orders can be amended or cancelled before they match. When the market resolves, the winning side settles at 100 and the losing side at zero. The gross gap between a price of 60 and a settlement of 100 is 40 points if that side wins; the 60 is lost if it does not. That is the contract's own arithmetic. It does not make 60 the correct probability, and it is not a coin flip a user can enter for free, because nothing trades until someone accepts the other side.

Research on transaction costs in other markets shows why the gap between a quote and a completed trade changes what the quote is worth.

A 2007 paper in The Journal of Finance used a complete record of U.S. over-the-counter secondary trades in corporate bonds and estimated average transaction costs by trade size for every bond that traded more than nine times between January 2003 and January 2005. Costs fell significantly as trade size rose. Highly rated bonds, recently issued bonds, and bonds close to maturity were cheaper to trade than other bonds. Bonds with transparent trade prices had lower costs, and costs dropped when the TRACE system began publicly disseminating those prices. The paper's sequence is the worked example: restrict the sample to bonds with more than nine trades, estimate cost against size, compare bonds whose prices are public with bonds whose prices are not, then look at the same bonds after TRACE publication starts. The result in the paper is that public traders benefit from price transparency. An opaque book is a more expensive book.

A 2020 paper in The Journal of Finance found that the index credit-default-swap market stayed two-tiered after Dodd-Frank, despite regulatory efforts to promote all-to-all trading. Transaction costs were higher for dealer-to-client trades than for interdealer trades, and the paper says the difference is explained by the higher, largely permanent price impact of client trades. Most interdealer trades were liquidity-motivated and used low-cost, low-immediacy protocols. Dealer-to-client trades were not anonymous; they almost always improved on contemporaneous executable interdealer quotes, and dealers appeared to price-discriminate based on the perceived price impact of the trade. The paper suggests the structure follows from client trades being relatively infrequent, large, and differentially informed. What looks like a house take can be the cost of moving the price, not only a posted commission.

A 2023 paper in The Journal of Finance is the dispute over how those costs should be scored. Ignoring transaction costs, the Hou, Xue, and Zhang q-factor model and the Barillas and Shanken six-factor model have high maximum squared Sharpe ratios and small alphas across 205 anomalies. They do not come close to spanning the mean-variance frontier a trader can actually reach. Accounting for transaction costs, the Fama and French five-factor model has a significantly higher squared Sharpe ratio than either alternative, and variations that use cash profitability perform better still. The paper's claim is that tests which skip costs are biased toward models that employ high-cost factors.

A listener who has that sequence reads a market price as a number someone else must be willing to take, then asks what it costs to get in and to get out. A quote near 50 on a yes-or-no contract is still a price that has to clear a book, with settlement at 100 or zero, and the research says the cost of clearing can be large enough to change which model looks right. None of these papers is a reason to prefer one side of a live contract. How It Works: Can AI Forecast?

A mention market is a contract that resolves on whether a specified keyword is spoken during a future public event. The paper "Forecasting Future Language: Context Design for Mention Markets" asks how the input to a large language model should be built when the forecast target is that kind of contract, including whether the live contract price is handed to the model as a prior.

The experiments use earnings-call mention markets: will a company say a specified keyword on its upcoming call. The authors vary two things. One is which text the model sees, news, prior earnings-call transcripts, or both. The other is how market probability, meaning the prediction-market contract price, is used. Market-Conditioned Prompting, or MCP, explicitly treats that price as a prior and instructs the model to update the prior with textual evidence, rather than re-predicting the base rate from scratch.

Walk the design without a dollar figure, because the abstract does not publish one. The contract already carries an implied probability that the keyword will be said. The model is given prior transcripts, news, or both. Under MCP it revises the market prior in light of that text instead of throwing the price out and starting a fresh base rate. MixMCP then mixes the market probability with that MCP forecast, dampening the model's posterior update with the market prior so the final number is not the model's unconstrained revision.

The paper reports three results. Richer context consistently improves forecasting performance. MCP, which updates the market probability rather than discarding it, yields better-calibrated forecasts. A mixture of the market probability and MCP outperforms the market baseline, and by dampening the model's update with the market prior it yields more robust predictions than either the market or the language model alone. The abstract also notes that recent work shows large language models can generate forecasts competitive with human forecasters. This paper does not re-run that comparison. Its claim is about context design on mention markets.

A second paper, "Making Alternative Data Work: Context-Augmented LLMs for Financial Forecasting," uses prediction markets in a different role. They are one channel of alternative data, with consumer transactions and web traffic, brought in to forecast a firm's future financial performance. The authors propose a two-agent framework. The first agent identifies the firms for which each alternative-data channel is likely to be informative. The second predicts revenue using firm- and channel-specific context. They evaluate the framework across four commercial alternative data channels. In those experiments, adding alternative data in context alongside other financial information improves the language model's forecasting relative to either source alone, and those forecasts are more accurate than those of standard forecasting baselines.

The limits are stated in the papers themselves. Firm-level alternative data often have limited historical coverage, are relevant only to specific prediction targets or subsets of firms, and arrive through heterogeneous channels, which is why they are hard to drop into a conventional forecast. On mention markets, the weaker design the authors test is a model that ignores the contract price and re-predicts the base rate. MixMCP exists because an undamped model update was less robust, in their tests, than a forecast that kept the market prior in the mix. No named outside critic is quoted in either abstract.

Read the contract price, after that, as a prior with a job, not as a verdict and not as a poll. In the mention-market design, the price is the starting probability, new text is evidence that may move it, and the more accurate setup in their tests was the one that refused to throw the price away. That is a description of a research design, not a reason to buy, sell, or trade any contract.

How It Works: Are Betting Markets Efficient?

A betting market is efficient, in the sense researchers use, when the price already contains what a simple trading rule would need, so that rule cannot be expected to make money once the bookmaker's margin is paid. The test is not whether the price looks wise. The test is whether a rule written down in advance, applied to old prices, still shows a profit large enough that chance is a poor explanation.

Spread betting, also called handicap betting, makes the test easy to see. The book sets a point spread. A bet wins if the side beats that number, not merely if the side wins the game. If the spread already summarizes what bettors know, backing favourites, backing underdogs, or chasing teams that just covered should not pay after the vig. A 2022 review in the Journal of Sports Economics gathered more than six hundred implementations of such strategies from the long literature on this microstructure. The finding is two-sided. Predictable glitches show up: prices are not a perfect photograph of true chances. Those glitches are too small to exploit for a profit, which is what an efficient market is supposed to look like once costs are counted. Misperception and efficiency are not opposites in that result. People can be systematically a little wrong, and the wrongness can still be smaller than the fee for acting on it.

The same review changes how a listener should read a paper that claims an edge. Finance has started to treat a result as worth attention only when the test statistic clears a high bar, a z-score whose absolute value is greater than three, because researchers try so many cuts of the data that some will look special by luck. That control, the authors write, is becoming mainstream in finance and has not yet become mainstream in betting-market research. Six hundred strategy tests are exactly the setting where a lucky winner should be expected. A single pretty backtest, without that hurdle, is not evidence that the market left money on the table.

English football bookmakers, studied in the Journal of Prediction Markets for the seasons from 2002 to 2006, show a related pattern with a different shape. Home odds and away odds carried a positive favourite-longshot bias. Draw odds went the other way, a negative longshot bias. Separately, the authors identify a draw bias: betting the draw returned more than betting home or away. They also investigated rules that use the disagreement among bookmakers' prices.

Those two papers do not license a bet. One says apparent errors in spread markets failed the profit test across hundreds of tries. The other says football prices in those four seasons were not symmetric: draws behaved differently from home and away, and the favourite-longshot pattern was not the same for every outcome. A listener who knows that reads a contract price as a clearing price among whoever chose to trade, not as a poll and not as a finished forecast. A published bias is a description of old prices. It is not a rule that survives the next fee, the next sample, or the next six hundred attempts.

How It Works: A Short History of Election Betting

Between 1868 and 1940, large and often well-organized markets took bets on U.S. presidential elections. A person who thought a candidate would win could buy a claim that paid if that candidate won, and a person who disagreed could sell it. The price of that claim was the forecast. It was not a poll. It was the level at which people in the market were willing to risk money.

A 2004 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives studies those markets and sets out four findings. The market did a remarkable job forecasting elections in an era before scientific polling. It was fairly efficient, despite the limited information of participants and active attempts to manipulate the odds. The markets disappeared largely because scientific polls rose and because other forms of gambling became more available. The paper also discusses lessons that history holds for the present. The abstract does not itemize those lessons.

The limit is already in the second finding. The paper calls the market fairly efficient even while recording attempts to manipulate the odds. A price can be leaned on and still land near the result if other traders take the other side. It can also miss when the people trading share a blind spot. The abstract does not measure how hard a price had to be pushed before it broke. For a reader now, the useful distinction is simple. A market price is a forecast only in the sense that people risked money at that level, under rules for who gets paid. Those presidential markets show that such a price can track an election before scientific polls exist, and that the markets themselves can disappear once polls and other gambling are easier to find. They do not turn any single price today into a verdict. Source: [doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1257/0895330041371277)

How It Works: Why Economists Care: Prices as Public Information

A binary event contract pays a fixed amount if a stated event occurs and nothing if it does not. In a market where traders can buy and sell that contract, the price is the point at which the people willing to take the other side have stopped arguing with their money. Anyone can read it. They do not have to pay the researchers, open the filings or sit in the room where the information was gathered. That is the public-information case: the forecast is a byproduct of trade, posted continuously, and free to the next reader.

The cost of producing that byproduct is the part the slogan skips. A 2020 paper, Timely Information from Prediction Markets, treats prediction markets as tools that elicit and aggregate beliefs from strategic agents, then points at a waste channel: agents may exhaust the social welfare by competing to be first to update the market. The design problem it sets is a trade-off between how quickly information is aggregated and how much that information costs. To maximise social welfare, the market has to pay people to acquire information quickly enough to be useful, but not faster, and more expensively, than necessary, and it has to get them to report truthfully and on time. The paper studies two settings — information that is valuable only before a deadline, and information whose value falls as time passes — and says it uses theorems and simulations to demonstrate the mechanisms. It does not hand a reader a live accuracy score.

A separate research design asks what changes when institutional liquidity shows up. How It Works: Futarchy and Decision Markets

Futarchy picks a policy by running markets that are conditional on the choice. A group first agrees on a measurable result it cares about. Traders then price that result under each option. The option whose conditional price looks better is the one the rule selects.

A study in Frontiers in Blockchain asks whether that arrangement can govern decentralized science. The authors examined governance data from 13 DeSci DAOs and replayed proposals from VitaDAO. Under a deterministic model, every historical decision pointed the same way as the futarchy-preferred outcome. They call that latent compatibility, and they say a real version still needs measurable indicators and a diverse set of traders.

Decision markets are the narrower tool inside that idea. They pull private information out of people who have their own interests and fold it into a forecast that depends on the action. A 2023 paper on arXiv says the standard design pays for honesty with a random decision rule. Sometimes the principal must do the thing the forecast has already marked as worse.

That paper sketches three mechanisms that keep a fixed decision instead. If the principal already holds a signal, she can score everyone else against it, then choose using the combined forecasts plus her own signal. If she does not, she can offer one agent a share of the profit and take that agent's signal as the stand-in, or she can score two agents against each other with a proper peer-prediction rule and use those signals the same way. A test of the first design, in a multi-agent bandit system, trained agents to a level similar to a Bayesian model that could see every signal at once.

For a reader, a conditional price is not a plain forecast. It prices an outcome only if a choice is made, and the papers tie its usefulness to the score, the stand-in, and the indicator the group picked.

How It Works: Prediction Markets Inside Companies

A corporate prediction market is a market a company runs among its employees so their trades can produce a price on a question inside the firm. A 2017 paper in MIS Quarterly builds an analytical model of two inputs to that price: the precision of public information, and social interaction among the people trading.

Raising the precision of public information is not always beneficial to accuracy. The authors trace that to what they call the hidden-profiles effect. The information-aggregation mechanism places a larger-than-efficient weight on existing public information. Information that is not already public is underweighted. That is the hidden profile: evidence some participants hold and the price does not fully pick up.

A socially embedded prediction market, with information sharing among participants, may help correct that inefficiency and improve performance. The model also identifies conditions under which increased precision of public information is detrimental in a nonnetworked prediction market and, separately, detrimental in a socially embedded one. More precise public information can hurt either design. The paper's managerial implication is the detrimental effect of public information, and the role of social networking among employees, in a corporate prediction market.

Read against that model, a price is not neutral aggregation. It can lean too hard on the number everyone already shares. How It Works: Hedging With Event Contracts

An event contract pays if a stated outcome occurs and pays nothing if it does not. Using that payoff as a hedge means the gain is meant to offset a loss on something the holder already has, so the contract is doing a job closer to insurance than to a standalone wager. The price is what the other side will accept to take the remaining risk.

Good Authority, citing Marieke de Goede’s Virtue, Fortune, Faith: A Genealogy of Finance, says nineteenth-century regulators separated bucket-shop gambling from legitimate futures by whether losses could be quantified and modeled. In that account, a gambler faces the house. A hedger shifts risk toward someone with a different appetite for it.

A 2020 paper in the journal Risks works the arithmetic of a second trade: after the price moves, take the opposite position to the first. The result it derives is that a profit can be locked in without a forecast of the final outcome. Two frictions break that result in practice. An exchange charges a fee. Some markets have no lay option, so there is no way to take the against side at all.

A 2026 paper on arXiv uses Bitcoin as a proof of concept and treats Kalshi contracts on the coin’s future price as option-like hedges, in bullish, bearish, and flat regimes. The authors report that a dynamic mix of those contracts and the spot coin beat simpler portfolios across all three regimes. They also say fees can erode the result. That is a research claim about those constructions, not a plan to copy.

How It Works: Sports Event Contracts, Sportsbooks and Gambling Harm

A sportsbook posts a number and stands on the other side of it. A sports event contract on an exchange does not. Two people agree a price; if the listed event occurs, the contract pays a fixed sum to the buyer, and if it does not, the seller keeps what was paid. The venue does not have to hold a view. The last trade is the price. When the other side is a pool rather than a person, an automated market maker sets that price from a formula and a pile of collateral.

A 2021 paper in the Journal of Finance treats liquid sports-betting contracts as a laboratory for pricing anomalies. Two features do the work: there is no systematic risk of the kind equities carry, and the contract's terminal value is set outside the betting itself. That design gets around the joint-hypothesis problem, so a gap between the traded price and the later result can be read as mispricing rather than as compensation for risk. The author finds strong momentum, a pattern consistent with delayed overreaction and not with underreaction or with rational pricing. The returns are a fraction of returns in financial markets and fail to overcome transaction costs, so arbitrage does not clear the pattern. An observation from that betting record also predicts value and momentum returns in U.S. equities. None of that is a method to copy, and it is not a claim that any live contract on Kalshi or Polymarket is cheap or rich.

A 2023 paper on arXiv looks at the pool version of the same problem. It argues that an automated market maker built like Uniswap is not lined up with fair odds, which leaves liquidity providers holding a risk the formula did not price. The authors describe UBET's UAMM, smart contracts meant to set sports prices inside collateral pools, and they report experiments with positive outcomes. That is a design result, not a published accuracy record.

The Journal of Finance result says those trades can carry a momentum streak that costs make hard to trade away. A thin book is a weaker version of the same object. How It Works: Superforecasters and Forecasting Tournaments

The method that won a U.S. Intelligence Community geopolitical forecasting tournament was to cull top performers each year and assign them to elite teams. A 2015 paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science calls those teams' members superforecasters. They maintained high accuracy across hundreds of questions and a wide array of topics, defying expectations of regression toward the mean two years in a row. The authors find support for four mutually reinforcing explanations: cognitive abilities and styles, task-specific skills, motivation and commitment, and enriched environments. Superforecasters, they write, are partly discovered and partly created. The high-performance incentives of tournaments, the paper says, highlight aspects of human judgment that would not come to light in laboratory paradigms focused on typical performance.

A 2014 paper in Psychological Science describes the setting. Five university-based research groups competed to recruit forecasters, elicit predictions, and aggregate those predictions into probabilities for events in a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament. One group found support for three psychological drivers: training, teaming, and tracking. Probability training corrected cognitive biases, encouraged the use of reference classes, and supplied heuristics, such as averaging when multiple estimates were available. Teaming let forecasters share information and discuss the rationales behind their beliefs. Tracking placed the highest performers — the top 2 percent from year one — on elite teams that worked together. Training, team collaboration, and tracking improved both calibration and resolution. Statistical algorithms, reported elsewhere, improved the accuracy of the aggregation. Putting statistics and psychology to work produced the best forecasts two years in a row.

Those abstracts do not show that an event-contract price automatically inherits that record. They do show that accuracy moved when selection, training, teams, and a statistical aggregation rule changed. How It Works: The Wisdom of Crowds, and When It Fails

A crowd forecast is an average. Each person brings a piece of information, and the average cancels some of the individual error if those pieces are not the same piece repeated.

Da, Diebold, Göbel, Gouveia and Li, in a 2025 arXiv paper, treat macroeconomic survey averages as portfolios of forecasts. They compare the U.S. Survey of Professional Forecasters with a model in which forecast errors are equicorrelated, and they plot accuracy against the number of forecasters in the average. The equicorrelation model fits growth and inflation almost perfectly. Gains from adding forecasters are larger for inflation than for growth, and they are largely exhausted once five to ten representative forecasters are in the average.

Social influence can wreck that cancellation or improve it, depending on the setup. A 2020 Management Science paper on Estimize found that the more public information a user viewed before an earnings forecast, the less weight that user put on private information. Individual forecasts got more accurate. The consensus got worse, because useful private information never entered the average. Experiments that restricted what randomly selected users could see confirmed that more independent forecasts produced a more accurate consensus. Estimize switched to a blind platform in November 2015.

A 2017 PNAS paper argues the opposite danger is overstated in decentralized networks: there, social influence can create learning that improves the group, so influence rather than independence can be the accurate path. A 2020 PNAS paper adds plasticity. When people can rewire who they listen to, and they get feedback, the network can beat its best member. One path changes the links so stronger forecasters count more. The other leaves the links alone and has accurate people resist influence, so their first estimate keeps more weight.

For a market, the price is that kind of average with money on it. It works when traders are not all staring at the same public number. It fails when they copy one another, drop their private information, or share one wrong story. A blind or independent read can protect the average. A network that can drop bad links can sometimes do better than independence. Neither result is a reason to take a side. How It Works: How an Automated Market Maker Sets a Price

An automated market maker quotes a price even when no second trader is waiting. In a logarithmic market scoring rule, that quote comes from a price function, and the liquidity parameter inside it — often written as b — sets how far one trade moves the price. Raise b and the same trade nudges the quote less. Lower b and the quote jumps farther. The sponsor stands as counterparty, so the parameter is also a bound on how much the sponsor can lose while the market stays open. The abstract of a 2018 paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management calls logarithmic market scoring rules one of the most simple and widely used of these mechanisms, and says market makers have to set b in the price functions because it has significant effects on market performance.

That paper, “Optimizing the liquidity parameter of logarithmic market scoring rules prediction markets,” used artificial prediction markets — simulations built to mimic real ones — to measure forecast standard error and the time for the price to converge to the true value. Forecast standard error fell as the liquidity parameter rose. For any given number of traders, the simulations found a parameter value that kept price adaptability appropriate and produced the fastest price convergence. The authors treat standard error, time to convergence, and cost as the tradeoff the market maker has to set.

A quote that barely moves and a quote that leaps can come from the same price function at different settings of b. In those simulations, a higher liquidity parameter came with a smaller forecast standard error, while the fastest convergence, for a given crowd size, sat at one value that preserved adaptability. Cost belongs in that same choice, because the sponsor is paying to keep the quote up. Someone reading a market price is also reading that setting: how far one trade is allowed to move the number, and who stands on the other side. The paper is not a guide to which side of any contract to take.

How It Works: Proper Scoring Rules

How a forecast is scored decides whether honesty is the profitable report. When a principal can pay after a random variable resolves, a strictly proper scoring rule is incentive compatible: the strategic agent’s best move is to reveal the private probability, and more accurate forecasts receive higher scores in expectation. That mechanism is the baseline in a 2018 arXiv paper on what to do when the outcome never arrives.

Surrogate scoring rules cover the case where the principal sees only agents’ reports. They combine a bias-correction step with an error-rate estimate for a reference answer defined from those reports. With a single bit of information about the prior distribution, and in a multi-task setting, the paper shows these rules recover the strictly proper score in expectation, as if ground truth were in hand. They still quantify information quality without that ground truth. They also induce dominant truthfulness in reporting. The method was verified theoretically and empirically on data from real human forecasters.

A 2015 Monthly Weather Review paper takes up multivariate forecasts, where few proper scores exist and many of them require a full density. The energy score, a multivariate generalization of the continuous ranked probability score, is the only commonly used score that applies to ensemble forecasts, a finite sample. Its ability to detect incorrectly specified correlations is limited. Variogram-based proper scores, built from a geostatistical idea, were more sensitive in simulations to wrong means, variances and correlations, and more discriminative on correlation structure. A case study of postprocessed wind-speed forecasts at five Colorado wind-park sites confirmed that pattern.

Reading a market price is reading a forecast that will be scored when the contract resolves. There, a proper rule can make honesty pay. Where there is no ground truth, or where the question is several correlated pieces at once, these papers say the choice of score decides which mistakes stay invisible.

How It Works: Insiders and Event Contracts

A bookmaker who thinks someone in the crowd already knows the result will not post the price a fully public crowd would justify. H. S. Shin's 1993 model says the bookie builds a cushion against that insider, and the cushion is not spread evenly. Longshots get the worse price. That gap is the favourite-longshot bias.

A 2003 paper in the Bulletin of Economic Research checked the pattern in a variety of sports betting markets where bookmakers set the odds. The bias was there. The precise pattern was not the same market to market. The paper also found evidence for a central prediction of the Shin model: margins increase with the number of competitors. A larger field gives an insider more places to hide a bet, and gives the bookmaker more reason to widen the take.

A 2002 paper in Economica asked a different question: when odds shorten sharply, is that an insider, or a crowd chasing a crowd? N. F. R. Crafts had suggested in 1985 that a pronounced shortening could signal insider trading. The later authors applied the Shin measure of insider incidence at opening prices and again at starting prices. When odds shortened and the Shin measure rose, betting returns were significantly positive. When odds shortened and the Shin measure fell, returns were negative. The authors read the second case as herd behaviour, not information.

That split is the caution. A move can be someone who knows something, which pulls the price toward the outcome and away from a fair deal for everyone else. Or it can be someone following the last trade, which adds noise and still punishes whoever joined late. These studies are about bookmakers posting odds, not peer-to-peer event contracts, so the mechanism does not transfer one for one. On an exchange there is no bookie forced to shade every contract; the cost shows up in the spread and the fee. What does transfer is the tension. A venue that attracts people trading on non-public information can print a price closer to the eventual result and, at the same time, a price that is less fair to everyone without that information. The direction of the last trade does not, by itself, say which of those two things just happened.

How It Works: How Markets Aggregate Information

A market price pools scattered information when the trade is the message. Someone holding a private signal buys or sells, the quote moves, and the next person meets a number that already contains that signal. An automated market maker can post the quote without a human taking the other side.

Information Aggregation in Exponential Family Markets, a 2014 paper on arXiv, shows those makers can be designed from exponential family distributions, a standard template in statistics. It connects price aggregation to the way learning agents pool beliefs when they use the same distributions. Under exponential utility, a form of risk aversion, the paper analyzes market behavior and the price equilibrium. It also considers traders who face a budget constraint rather than that utility alone.

Final prices are a weaker record than they look. Are Final Market Prices Sufficient for Information Aggregation?, a 2025 paper on arXiv, studies parimutuel betting on horse races, where a wager cannot be made contingent on the final odds. Interim odds show expected returns depend on the path, not only the finish: horses whose odds declined in the final five minutes earned higher realized returns than horses with similar final odds. A two-period extension of the Ottaviani and Sørensen information model accounts for the pattern, with late declines coming from informed bettors wagering on private signals. The paper adds that final-odds and return patterns alone may not separate information aggregation from probability distortions.

A reader cannot treat the last price as a complete file of what was known. The path into the price matters, and so does whether anyone could contract on the price that eventually printed. The racing measurement describes those markets. It is not a method for any contract.

How It Works: Arbitrage: When Two Venues Disagree

A winner-take-all set is built to be exhaustive and mutually exclusive, so related prices should add to one dollar. Falling short of that dollar, or running past it, is a bound that follows from the payoff at settlement. It is not automatically a position a trader can close while the market is still open.

A 2025 arXiv paper on Polymarket names two forms of that gap. Market-rebalancing arbitrage sits inside one market. Combinatorial arbitrage runs across related markets. From on-chain order-book history, the authors estimate about 40 million dollars of profit extracted. A full comparison of every linked condition does not scale, so they narrowed the search by timing, topic, and combinatorial relationship.

A 2026 arXiv paper splits the bound again. Payoff-space no-arbitrage follows from terminal payoffs. Protocol-executable no-arbitrage depends on the conversions a trader can actually make before settlement. On Polymarket's negative-risk markets, linked binaries stand for mutually exclusive outcomes, but the NegRisk Adapter operationalizes only the NO-to-YES direction before settlement. Reconstructing depth-aware portfolios and on-chain conversion traces, the authors estimate 1.12 million dollars of arbitrage profit: 1.086 million from converter-enabled strategies and 32 thousand from baskets held to settlement. In the order-book sample, positive violations concentrated on the unsupported YES side, while adapter-supported NO-side violations were less frequent and shorter-lived. The authors' reading is that a pre-settlement conversion strengthens enforcement by reducing capital lock-up and letting inventory recycle. They also built a prototype that would make the reverse path executable before settlement.

When two prices disagree, the same constraint applies. A gap is enforceable only if a trader can turn one position into the other before resolution. Where that conversion is missing, the bound can sit until the contract pays.

How It Works: The Law: Event Contracts, Regulators and the Courts

An event contract is a promise with a price. The buyer pays now. If the stated event happens, the contract pays a fixed sum. If it does not, the contract pays nothing. In the United States, that shape is what pulls the instrument toward the Commodity Exchange Act and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. State authorities have treated the same instrument as gambling. Which label wins is not settled.

A 2026 Note in the Boston College Law Review says political prediction markets have existed in limited formats since 1988 and returned to public view in the 2024 elections, when platforms such as Kalshi offered trades on United States presidential and congressional races. The Note asks whether those trades are legal. It records that the CFTC said no and a federal district court said yes. Examining the statutory source of the agency's power to review trades based on the outcome of events, sometimes called event contracts, the Note concludes that the law is contradictory and unclear. It also concludes that political prediction markets benefit the public, and it recommends new Commodity Exchange Act language that would narrow the CFTC's authority to review event contracts and clarify the public-interest standard used in that review. That recommendation is the Note's. It is not an enacted amendment.

The same definitional problem appears under other statutes. A 2015 article in the Victoria University of Wellington Law Review examines New Zealand's Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, which it calls the most substantial overhaul of that country's securities law in recent history, and asks how the Act treats the prediction market iPredict. Its conclusion is narrow: iPredict can operate under the Act only if the Financial Markets Authority declares that its contracts are derivatives and grants substantial exemptions from regulatory compliance. The author then recommends a more coherent approach by analogy with the Act's prescribed intermediary service licences.

For someone reading a market, the label decides the pipe the contract travels through: who may list it, who may be shut out, and which public-interest test a regulator applies. The Boston College Note's finding is that, on political event contracts in the United States, that test still sits in contradictory statute. What is unsettled should be read as unsettled.

How It Works: Who Decides the Answer? Resolution, Rules and Oracles

A crypto-settled contract does not pay when traders converge. Collateral stays locked until an oracle delivers a result and winning claims can be redeemed. That handoff is the oracle problem, as an ACM Computing Surveys paper frames it: a smart contract can read on-chain data, but the event the question is about lives off-chain, so some mechanism has to move the outside result onto the chain.

The survey splits oracle implementations into two groups. Voting-based designs finalize an outcome mainly from participants' stakes. Reputation-based designs combine reputation and performance metrics with authenticity-proof mechanisms aimed at data correctness and integrity. Both are described as ways to give a smart contract final feedback. The paper closes by noting remaining challenges and future research directions, without treating either family as settled.

A separate arXiv paper measures what the wait does to the price. When collateral remains locked until oracle settlement, a near-certain dollar is a delayed dollar, so the price embeds a maturity-dependent settlement discount in addition to beliefs about the outcome. The authors recover an implied settlement-discount term structure from persistent near-certain contracts, using realized settlement times, and summarize it as an annualized settlement wedge. The recovered wedges are positive, maturity-dependent, and time-varying. Adjusting prices by those curves reduces the near-certainty horizon gradient by roughly 48 to 88 percent, which the paper treats as evidence that much of the raw maturity pattern is priced settlement friction rather than forecast error alone.

Design changes the wedge. negRisk conversion compresses discounts by recycling part of the position into synthetic collateral. Yield-bearing collateral flattens the term structure by reducing the opportunity cost of lock-up. On this account, pricing quality is endogenous to settlement mechanics, collateral productivity, and capital-recycling design. A price near certainty is a belief plus a claim on money that cannot move until the named resolution process — an oracle report, a stake-weighted vote, or a reputation-weighted check — says the question is closed.

How It Works: Can a Prediction Market Be Manipulated?

A contract can settle on a number its own holders are free to trade. That is settlement manipulation in plain form. A paper posted to arXiv on June 30, 2026, models contracts that pay on an asset price holders can move by trading the underlying. The model says those contracts transfer wealth from prediction-market liquidity traders to manipulators. It also says they harm price discovery in the underlying, even as that market becomes more liquid. After Polymarket launched a five-minute Bitcoin contract, settlement-time spot order flow spiked. The spikes caused large price reversals after settlement. Manipulators captured a large amount of profit, mostly from retail. Manipulation was largely absent in the fifteen-minute contracts. Lengthening the horizon removed it, and the authors call that the market-design remedy their model and evidence support.

A second paper, posted to arXiv on January 28, 2026, pairs agent-based simulation with an analytic account of price dynamics. Bettors differ in expertise, noisy private information, learning rates and budgets, and they watch public opinion on the outcome before they bet. The simulated market is stable across a broad parameter space. Complex behavior and price interactions produce self-regulatory price discovery. A highly resourced minority — a whale — with a biased valuation can still shift the price, and the shift is temporary. The move is larger and lasts longer when other bettors herd and learn slowly. Theory in the paper says whales shift prices in proportion to their share of market capital. How long the distortion lasts depends on non-whale learning rates and on how intensely those bettors herd.

A short contract on a tradable price is the case where the defense fails in this evidence. A longer horizon is the fix that paper measured. A binary book can be shoved by capital share and herding, then pull back if the rest of the book keeps learning.
