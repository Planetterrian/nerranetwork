How It Works: The Winners You Hear About: Survivorship Bias

Survivorship bias is the error of scoring a strategy, a fund, or a forecaster only on the records that are still in the sample. The failures drop out — delisted, shut, demoted, or simply no longer posting — and the average of what remains looks better than the average of everyone who tried.

A 2026 study of India’s NIFTY Smallcap 250 makes the arithmetic concrete. The author rebuilt who was actually in the index, using market-capitalization ranking from price-and-volume data, including delisted names in the exchange’s bhavcopy files, across 1,437 stocks and nine years, 2016 through 2025. An equal-weight portfolio of only the stocks that are members now was then set beside an equal-weight portfolio of everyone who had been a member. Survivor-only backtesting overstated annual returns by 4.94 percentage points, which the paper calls a 23.3 percent overstatement, and overstated Sharpe ratios by 0.097, a 9.1 percent overstatement. Turnover over the window was 82.5 percent. Of that churn, 16.1 percent were delisted, 33.1 percent graduated out of the small-cap index, and 33.2 percent were demoted. All three groups contributed to the bias, so the distortion is not only bankruptcies. The reconstruction matched current members with 100 percent accuracy and was estimated at 85 to 90 percent accuracy for historical membership. That gap is the method’s own limit: a survivor-free test is only as complete as the list of who left.

A 2010 Review of Finance paper approached the same error from mutual funds, and it is described there as the first to calculate, test, and explain different definitions of survivorship bias in fund performance rather than a single headline gap. The survival-and-performance link was stronger for small funds. Non-survivors significantly underperformed. New funds did not significantly outperform, which cuts against the idea that the bias is just a parade of brilliant newcomers replacing the old book. The paper’s practical point is that the problem does not vanish because one famous database was cleaned: it remains where survivor-free data is unavailable, and some methods still require truncated samples. Which definition you use changes the measured bias, so two analysts can quote two different “survivorship penalties” from the same graveyard.

Read a public track record the way those studies read an index. A screenshot of forecasts that are still online, or a strategy page that shows only accounts still trading, is the current-constituent portfolio. The 4.94-point gap appeared because the leavers were put back in, not because the survivors’ returns were fake. Until the disappeared names are counted — shut funds, deleted accounts, markets a forecaster stopped publishing after a miss — the average is a description of who remained, not of the method. Nothing here is a verdict on any contract, venue, or trade. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Combining Forecasts: Why an Average Often Wins

A combined forecast is a new probability built from several old ones. The usual construction is a weighted average: each forecaster, poll or model gets a weight, and the weights are chosen so that past errors shrink. The long record in this literature is that the optimized mix often loses to the plain one. An equal-weight average of the candidate forecasts is repeatedly more robust than methods that try to beat it. Researchers call that the forecast combination puzzle.

A 2015 paper on arXiv sets out why the puzzle appears. The explanations it examines are estimation error, weighting formulas that do not fit the data, and screening that drops models before the combination is formed. It adds a split the earlier account left out. Combining for adaptation means you do not know which single forecast will win, so you want a mix that stays close to whichever one does. Combining for improvement means you believe a blend can beat every candidate, including the best. Using an improvement method on an adaptation problem, or the reverse, can itself create the puzzle. The paper's proposal is a multi-level AFTER strategy, a sequential weighting scheme that treats the simple average as one more candidate. The procedure can fall back on equal weights when estimated weights are too noisy to help, and it can still up-weight a strong forecast when the data support that.

A 2023 paper, also on arXiv, argues that the puzzle is partly a testing problem, not only a forecasting problem. Combinations are usually built in a way that leaves tests of predictive accuracy with low power and without proper size control. Under the null of no inferior accuracy, the test statistic does not follow the standard normal distribution that researchers typically assume. Its asymptotic distribution is non-standard. A test that is too ready to accept the simple average will keep crowning it. The same paper says that low power can be avoided, when it is feasible, by estimating the combinations more efficiently. It shows the point both for a functional of interest and for predictive densities, and it uses a short example on daily financial returns to show how a researcher can sidestep the puzzle in practice.

Probability forecasts fail in a further way, set out in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society in 2010. Linear pooling, a weighted average of probabilities, is the popular method. Take two or more distinct probability forecasts that are each calibrated, so that events occur at the rate the forecast assigns. Any non-trivial weighted average of those forecasts is necessarily uncalibrated, and it lacks sharpness. The average is pulled inward relative to the sharper input. Recalibration is required even in that ideal case. The authors' tool is a beta-transformed linear opinion pool: a beta curve, fit together with the weights, that can push the pooled probability outward or pull it inward after the linear pool is formed. They illustrate it in a simulation and in a case study of statistical forecasts and National Weather Service probability-of-precipitation forecasts.

A 2021 paper on arXiv takes the practical objection. Dynamic combinations, which change weights as a series moves, can forecast well and still never be used, because they cost too much to run and because a stack of models is hard to explain. Model compression trains one model to imitate the ensemble. In the experiments, the compressed model had comparable predictive performance and a drastic cut in computational cost. The compressed individual model with the best average rank was a rule-based regression model, so the step also made the forecast easier to read. The paper notes that time series make compression harder than the neural-network cases usually studied, because the data keep evolving.

Read a market price as one already-combined forecast, not as a raw observation. Line that price up with a poll, a model and another venue, and an equal-weight average is the combination this research treats as the robust default when the job is adaptation and the sample for estimating weights is thin. Estimated weights earn a place when the job is improvement and the weights can be estimated without spending the gain on estimation error. A straight average of calibrated probabilities is not itself a calibrated probability. It needs a recalibration step, which is what the beta transform is for, before the blend is treated as sharp. That is a way to see what a published probability is — a pool, with a pooling rule, and the rule changes the number — not a method for choosing a contract.

How It Works: Trading Against Someone Who Knows More: Adverse Selection

A market maker quotes a bid and an ask without knowing if the next order belongs to someone who has seen information the maker has not. That trader buys what is cheap and sells what is rich. The maker loses on that fill. The loss is adverse selection. The maker's reply, in these studies, is a wider spread, so uninformed flow pays for informed losses, and a quote that steps away after an informative order, because the order itself is information.

The arXiv paper Market Informedness and Market-Maker Profitability builds a market in which makers differ in what they know and in how much they dislike holding inventory. Prices form inside the market. A fundamental value moves on its own. Taker orders follow a state-dependent self-exciting process, so a burst of trading makes another burst more likely. The authors show that process is stable over a finite horizon, then train the makers with multi-agent reinforcement learning under centralized training and decentralized execution.

At low aggregate informedness, most of the market has not seen the fundamental. An informed taker can trade against a quote that has not moved. The maker cannot separate that order from noise. The paper finds informed market-order flow especially harmful then, and the adverse-selection risk severe. Raise informedness. More orders already reflect the fundamental, and endogenous prices move with them. An informed trade can still hit a late quote, but the quote is late by less, and the maker's other fills are closer to what is known. Maker profitability then trends upward overall, with local reversals from complex dynamics and stochastic learning. The authors treat that pattern as price discovery offsetting the cost of adverse selection.

A second arXiv paper, When AI Trading Agents Compete, trains a high-frequency maker by reinforcement learning inside a Hawkes limit-order-book model, so price impact comes from the book rather than from an assumed formula. The maker does not reprice on every event. The problem is impulse control, trained with proximal policy optimisation and self-imitation learning. Against a medium-frequency trader working a meta-order, the maker learns, after that training, to use the drift the meta-order creates. The quote moves because of the order. That move is the book learning from the order. Higher profits for the learning maker did not necessarily mean significantly larger slippage for the slower trader.

Informed Trading Intensity, in the Journal of Finance, trains a machine-learning measure on a class of informed trades. It rises before earnings, before mergers and acquisitions, and before news announcements. The authors link it to return reversal and asset pricing, and say it works by capturing nonlinearities and interactions among informed trading, volume, and volatility, including impatient informed trading and commonality in informed trading.

How It Works: Betting on the Weather: Forecasts, Contracts and Hedging

A weather derivative pays on a measured weather index, not on a farmer showing that one field failed. The index can be cumulative average temperature over a season, or growing degree-days, a running total of how far temperature sits above a base. Two gaps sit between that payout and a crop loss. Product-design basis risk is the gap between the index and yield. Geographical basis risk is the gap between the weather station named in the contract and the field.

A 2019 paper on arXiv, "Hedging crop yields against weather uncertainties -- a weather derivative perspective," tries to close the first gap for maize. The authors use a machine-learning ensemble to measure which weather variables move maize yield. They report that the most significant variable was average temperature. They then propose a mean-reverting model for that temperature: the speed of mean reversion can change through time, the level it reverts toward is seasonal, and local volatility depends on the local average temperature. The same structure is extended to a multi-dimensional model for different but correlated locations. From those temperature models they present pricing models for futures, for options on futures, and for basket futures, on cumulative average temperature and on growing degree-days.

Walk that maize setup in the order the paper builds it. First the ensemble selects the driver: average temperature, the variable it found most tied to maize yield. Second, the temperature path is not treated as a flat number. It is pulled toward a seasonal mean, at a speed that itself varies, with volatility that depends on the local average. Third, a futures price is the price of a claim on the cumulative average of that path, or on growing degree-days built from it. Fourth, a buyer who does not want a single station can use a basket future and put weight on the stations that fit the crop. The paper says pricing futures on baskets reduces geographical basis risk, because buyers can select stations and set the weights. An option on the future is a right, not an obligation, on that same index. The authors say the ensemble step is there to eliminate product-design basis risk. They present the pricing models; this abstract does not publish a worked premium.

A 2011 paper on arXiv, "Pricing Weather Derivatives for Extreme Events," prices protection against extremes rather than against a seasonal average. It models weather extremes as a max-stable process, using spatial statistics and extreme-value theory, then simulates payments for a general collection of weather derivatives. The simulations are built to capture the spatial dependence of payments, so a bad outcome in one place is not treated as independent of a bad outcome nearby. The authors bring in catastrophe-ratemaking results to compute risk loads and premiums for weather derivatives that are renewal-additive.

The refusal the 2019 paper names is commercial. It says most smallholder farmers and agricultural stakeholders are unwilling to pay the price of weather derivatives because product-design and geographical basis risks are present in the pricing models. The ensemble-plus-basket design is the authors' answer to that refusal. The text does not claim the refusal then ends. The 2011 premiums still rest on the max-stable model of extremes and on the simulated, spatially dependent payments. If that description of the tail is wrong, the risk loads built from it move with the error.

Read a temperature contract as a claim on an index, and ask which index. Cumulative average temperature and growing degree-days transfer different accumulations of heat and cold. A basket transfers a weighted mix of places, which the 2019 paper treats as a way to cut geographical basis risk, not as a match to every field. An extremes contract, in the 2011 setup, transfers the tail, and its premium includes a risk load from catastrophe ratemaking, not only an expected payout. That is a way to see what risk the price is for, and what basis risk remains if the crop, the place, or the tail does not match the index. It is not a view on whether any listed contract is cheap or rich, and it is not a hedge to copy. Source: arxiv.org Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Probability Weighting: Why Small Chances Feel Bigger

In rank-dependent utility theories, including prospect theory, the psychological weight attached to an outcome need not equal the probability of that outcome. A probability weighting function lets stated probabilities be weighted nonlinearly, so a small stated chance can carry more decision weight than its face value and a chance near certainty can carry less. The shape repeatedly estimated in that literature is an inverse S: concave at low probabilities, then convex.

Wu and Gonzalez, in Management Science in 1996, treated that shape as a claim to test rather than a form to assume. Earlier empirical studies had suggested an inverse-S weighting function, first concave and then convex, but estimation had required assumptions about the functional form of the value function, the weighting function, or both. They proposed two preference conditions that are necessary and sufficient for concavity and for convexity of the weighting function, and that can be tested without assuming the form of the value function. The instrument was a preference ladder: a series of questions that differ only by a common consequence. Those concavity-convexity ladders validated an S-shaped weighting function, concave up to a probability below 0.40 and convex beyond that probability. The tests also showed significant nonlinearity away from the boundaries, 0 and 1, not only at the edges. The ladder data were fit to weighting functions proposed by Tversky and Kahneman in 1992 and by Prelec in 1995.

Abdellaoui, in Management Science in 2000, separated the elicitation into two steps and kept it parameter-free, so the whole individual preference functional could be drawn out without a prior restriction on its formula. The method elicits utility first and decision weights second, under rank-dependent expected utility and under cumulative prospect theory. In an experimental study of monetary outcomes, utility functions were concave for gains and convex for losses. The elicited weighting functions satisfied upper and lower subadditivity and were consistent with previous parametric estimations. In that data, the probability weighting function for losses was more elevated than the function for gains.

The dispute is as much about what the theory has been said to predict as about the bend in the curve. A 2010 paper in Strategic Management Journal argued that prospect theory's predictions differ sharply from what strategy scholars had inferred. Its value function, the paper said, predicts negative risk-return associations for high performers and positive associations for low performers, directly contrary to the strategy literature. The isolation assumption means most firm choices should appear as mixed gambles, while prior prospect-theory theorizing in strategy had implicitly assumed unmixed gambles. The paper's further claim is that prospect theory does not make the general predictions most strategy researchers had assumed; the predictions depend on a full range of parametric and choice characteristics that work had ignored. A 2005 review in the Annual Review of Political Science described a different resistance: international-relations scholars who study security had used prospect theory extensively, while Americanists, comparativists, and political economists had shown little interest. That review located the tepid response in aversion to behavioral assumptions, not in problems unique to the theory, and treated the difficulty of extending a laboratory account to political decisions in the field as an open theoretical problem rather than a settled finding.

A listener who keeps that distinction reads a market price as a traded price, not as a felt weight. A contract whose price implies a small probability sits in the region where the 1996 ladders found overweighting of stated chances; a price near certainty sits where those weights turned convex and came in below the stated probability. Abdellaoui's elicitations add that the bend need not be the same in gains and in losses. None of that is a reason to buy or sell a contract, and none of it says a venue's price has already applied a laboratory weighting function. It says the number on the screen and the weight in a choice experiment are different objects, measured in different ways.

How It Works: Overconfidence: Forecasts That Are Too Sure

Overprecision is an excessive confidence that one knows the truth, and a 2013 paper in Psychological Science measured it without taking a spoken confidence interval at face value. The authors inferred how narrow a person's uncertainty was from how little that person moved a point estimate when the cost of guessing too high differed from the cost of guessing too low.

That cost gap is the worked example, and the paper supplies the direction rather than a numeric table. If overshooting the true value is the more expensive mistake, someone who is genuinely unsure should slide the guess toward the cheaper error. A larger slide means a wider uncertainty. The precision implied by the slide can then be set against how much the person actually knew. People adjusted less than that knowledge justified. Subjective confidence drove the shortfall: they acted as if the right answer sat in a tighter band than their record supported.

A second kind of overconfidence appears when the forecast is a probability piled onto models. The 2020 paper "When are Bayesian model probabilities overconfident?" finds that a posterior over models often concentrates on a single model even when other measures of fit, or of forecasting ability, show no strong preference. A moderate change in the sample can shift that concentration onto another model. The authors derive the sampling variance of the Bayes factor in univariate and multivariate linear regression.

Pooling several forecasts does not, by itself, erase the first problem. A 2012 paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management tested Cooke's classical model, equal-weight linear pooling, and the best-expert approach on a large database of real-world expert judgments, using leave-one-out cross-validation. Both linear-pooling methods significantly outperformed the best expert, so more than one voice helped. Linear pooling still did not effectively counteract expert overconfidence. Dependence cut the other way by method: Cooke's classical model was more sensitive to expert dependence than equal weights, and high dependence generally produced much poorer aggregated results, while equal weights were more robust across dependence levels. The paper also says that broadening subjective intervals or distributions may occasionally mitigate overconfidence, and that the number of experts and the number of seed questions also affect calibration of the aggregated distribution.

Whether "overconfidence" is one trait is disputed in this literature. A 2011 paper in the Journal of Behavioral Decision Making found that miscalibration on an interval-production task correlated across two sets of general-knowledge questions of varying difficulty, given at different times, which is the pattern of a trait. The hard-easy effect split by measure: the more difficult the task, the greater the overprecision, and also the greater the underplacement of one's performance relative to others. Dispositional optimism and self-efficacy showed no relation to that interval miscalibration. Measures of a disposition to positive illusions correlated with one another, did not correlate with the interval-production task, and correlated negatively with optimism about societal risks. In a final study, that same disposition did predict miscalibration when the same questions were scored by a probability-evaluation technique. The authors' conclusion is that overconfidence is not a unitary construct but a series of overlapping ones.

A calibration chart is the picture of the gap those papers are measuring. Stated probability is on one axis; the share of those statements that later came true is on the other. If contracts priced near the top of the scale resolved yes about as often as that price implied, the points would lie near the diagonal. Overprecision is the bend away from that line: high stated chances come true less often than the price suggests, and low stated chances come true more often. None of these papers tests a named prediction-market venue, and none of them identifies a contract as too high or too low. What changes for a listener is how to read a price that has rushed toward certainty. That price is a claim about frequency. It still has to be checked against how often similarly sure prices, on that kind of question, were right — and against whether the people behind the price were independent, or were all updating on the same story.

How It Works: Ask the Experts or Ask the Market? The Delphi Method

A Delphi forecast is a managed conversation with a panel, not a price that updates whenever someone trades. Experts are asked, usually for a medium- or long-range estimate, to judge how some phenomenon will evolve. Their answers are subjective. A facilitator turns those answers into something closer to a shared number by aggregating them — descriptive statistics are the example given in the forecasting-software literature — and sends that aggregate back. The experts see the group result, revise, and the next round starts. Rounds continue until the estimates converge on stable points. The point of the loop, as a 2014 account in International Letters of Social and Humanistic Sciences puts it, is to shrink disagreement without directly steering the panel: expert feedback drives the study to a conclusion.

The RAND Corporation developed the method at Santa Monica, California, for long-range forecasting in management science. A later variation, described in Advances in Fuzzy Systems in 2021, keeps that same back-and-forth with the experts and changes only the estimation step, replacing ordinary summaries with triangular fuzzy numbers. The paper illustrates the variation with a numerical example; the account available here does not reprint that example's inputs, so the mechanism is the useful part: communication stays Delphi, the math that folds the answers together does not.

A tourism study published in Sustainability shows what a finished Delphi looks like when the subject is a direction, not a tradable event. Researchers collected judgments from experts in tourism and hospitality about Thailand. The stabilized view was that Thailand could keep strengths that matter to visitors across income groups, and could keep the cultural distinctiveness the authors call "Thai-ness," in a region where tourism development is growing more important. The same panel pointed at repairs: better training programs for people in tourism organizations, a less strict immigration policy, adoption of new information technology, and tourism information that is more current. There is no price path in that result. There is a consensus a ministry or a business can read as a planning brief.

Two further designs show how the panel can be mixed with a machine without becoming a market. A 2020 paper in Information notes that experts often already have time-series software and often do not use it. The authors' fix is a "virtual expert": add, into the same aggregate the humans see, a forecast from the Prophet library, a Facebook tool usable in Python or R. The stated gain is time and cost — fewer hours from data analysts — not a claim that the software outranks the room. A 2013 paper in the Journal of Business Economics and Management goes further into finance. It pairs Evolino recurrent neural networks, fed orthogonal inputs, with a Delphi expert evaluation, and uses that combination in a portfolio decision. A statistical study in the paper is described as demonstrating reliability and describing accuracy, and a trading simulation is used to show what the model can do. Those pages do not hand this show a scoreboard against a prediction market, so no such score is stated here.

That is the practical split. Delphi pays in reputation inside a closed round: you revise because you saw the group, and the facilitator decides when the number is stable enough to publish. A market pays, when it pays in money, for being on the right side of a defined contract, and the number moves whenever a trade prints. Manifold's own description of its mana markets is the near case: prices there are forecasts from traders with reputation at stake, not dollars. An organization that needs a long-range qualitative brief — which strengths to protect, which practices to fix — is doing what the Thailand tourism panel was built to do, and a Delphi loop fits that job because the output is a revised expert consensus rather than a contract that must resolve. An organization that needs a number someone can trade against a dated, checkable event is doing a different job. Neither design, on the evidence in these papers, replaces the other. A listener who has watched a Delphi round should read a market price as a live bid, not as a panel that has been asked twice and then closed. How It Works: Probabilities Hidden in Options Prices

Option prices are claims on slices of a future price. Read as a set, calls and puts sketch how the market is charging for each range the underlying might land in. The probabilities backed out of those prices are called risk-neutral. That label does not mean the trader feels no risk. It means the probabilities are the ones that line the option prices up with the underlying and, in the index case, a weighted basket, once payouts are discounted — as if the price equaled a discounted expected payoff and did not carry a separate surcharge for bearing risk. A probability someone would write down for planning can differ, because people pay to avoid bad states and will accept a lower expected payoff to shed them. The risk-neutral figure folds that payment into the probability. A fat risk-neutral crash chance can mean traders think a crash is likely, or that they dislike crashes enough to bid up the insurance, or both. The option price alone does not split those stories.

A 2011 paper in the Review of Finance uses a related cut. Option-implied volatility was already known as a strong predictor of future realized volatility. The authors find that option-implied volatility and skewness also predict future realized beta, the measure of how a stock moves with the market. Under assumptions they set out, a beta can be built from option-implied return moments using equity options and index options, and it can be computed from option data on a single day. A historical beta needs a stretch of past returns, so it cannot, by construction, register a break that happened this morning. The option-implied beta can, because that morning’s option surface is the only input.

Two results sit beside that mechanism. In the Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis in 2023, researchers built a model-free joint risk-neutral distribution for the nine industry sectors in the S&P 500, consistent with options on those sectors and on their weighted index. Option-implied dependence was highly asymmetric and it moved through time. They then split correlation into two conditionals: when the market moves down, and when it moves up. The risk premium on the down correlation was strongly negative. The risk premium on the up correlation was positive. Their reading is that investors dislike the loss of diversification when markets fall, and that they prefer high correlation when markets rally. That is a priced preference, not a coin-flip chance that two sectors move together.

A 2018 comparison posted on arXiv ran fifteen predictive schemes across more than twenty-one years and scored the densities on statistical consistency, local accuracy and forecasting errors. With a composite they call the Integrated Forecast Score, risk-neutral densities beat historical-based predictions on information content. The Variance Gamma model produced the highest out-of-sample likelihood of observed prices and the lowest predictive errors. An ARCH-based method, GJR-FHS, produced the most consistent forecasts across the whole density. Lognormal densities, the Heston model and the Breeden-Litzenberger formula produced biased predictions and were rejected in statistical tests. There is no single “the” implied distribution. The extraction method changes the forecast, and some standard recipes fail the tests.

The break shows up inside the day as well as across models. A 2020 paper in Management Science studied intrahorizon value at risk in a jump-diffusion setting and proposed a displaced mixed-exponential model that can closely approximate a wide class of Lévy processes. Using the S&P 100 and American options, and separating jump from diffusion in a consistent way, the authors found that jumps accounted for about 90 percent of intrahorizon value at risk on average. Option-implied estimates responded to market changes much more than historical estimates, which performed poorly in back-tests.

A listener who treats a risk-neutral probability as a plain forecast is reading a price that has already mixed belief, aversion to bad states, and, inside the day, a large jump piece. An event contract pays a fixed amount if a named event occurs, so its price is closer to a probability of that named event under whatever mix of belief and preference the traders brought. The option surface answers a different question — where a price lands, and how the tails are priced — and these papers say the risk-neutral version often carries more information about later prices than a backward-looking density, without being the same object as a forecast with the price of risk removed.

How It Works: Do Prices Overreact to News?

A price underreacts when news is still spreading across traders who cannot see one another’s private information in the quote, and it overreacts when the traders who act on that lag can only chase the last move. A 1999 paper in the Journal of Finance builds that sequence with two groups of boundedly rational agents: newswatchers and momentum traders. Each newswatcher observes some private information, but fails to extract other newswatchers’ information from prices. Information that diffuses gradually across the population leaves prices underreacting in the short run. That underreaction is what lets momentum traders profit by trend-chasing. If those momentum traders can only implement simple, univariate strategies, their attempts at arbitrage lead to overreaction at long horizons. The paper’s point is a unified account: one market can show both mistakes, in that order, without a separate theory for each, and the same setup generates several other distinctive implications beyond the short-run lag and the long-horizon overshoot.

The measurement with a stated size is the 2006 Journal of Finance paper on the disposition effect, the tendency of investors to ride losses and realize gains. The author uses data on mutual-fund holdings to construct a measure of reference purchasing prices for individual stocks, then tests whether that tendency induces underreaction to news and, through it, return predictability. Walk the test in the order the abstract gives it. First, holders have a reference purchase price, so on a news date some of them sit on a capital gain and some sit on a capital loss. Second, post-announcement price drift is most severe whenever those capital gains and the news event have the same sign — good news in the hands of people already ahead, or bad news in the hands of people already behind. Third, the magnitude of the drift depends on the capital gains or losses experienced by the stock holders on the event date, not on the headline alone. Fourth, an event-driven strategy based on this effect yields monthly alphas of over 200 basis points. That figure is what the paper measured in stocks and fund holdings. It is not a method for any contract, and it is not a reason to take a side.

A 2014 paper in Management Science finds an overshoot rather than a lag, and it locates the overshoot in who is trading together. Mutual funds herd — trade together — into stocks with consensus sell-side analyst upgrades, and herd out of stocks with consensus downgrades. The influence of those recommendation changes on fund herding is stronger for downgrades, and among managers with greater career concerns. The paper reads career-concerned managers as incentivized to follow analyst information, and as having a greater tendency to herd on negative stock information, given the greater reputational and litigation risk of holding losing stocks. The timing matters for whether the herd moves the price too far. Starting in the mid-1990s, when aggregate mutual-fund equity ownership is significantly higher, stocks traded by career-concerned herds of fund managers in response to analyst recommendation changes experience a significant same-quarter price impact, followed by a sharp subsequent price reversal. The reversal is the paper’s evidence that the first move went too far. The paper was accepted by Wei Jiang. Its suggestion is that analyst recommendation revisions induce herding by career-concerned fund managers, and that this type of trading has become price destabilizing with the increasing level of mutual fund ownership of stocks.

The 1992 Journal of Finance paper is the dispute inside this set, and it cuts the analyst channel down to size. Analysts’ forecasts underreact to recent earnings. That feature of the forecasts is consistent with certain properties of the naive seasonal random walk forecast that Bernard and Thomas (1990) hypothesize underlie the well-known anomalous post-earnings-announcement drift. The underreactions in analysts’ forecasts are at most only about half as large as necessary to explain the magnitude of the drift. Half is not zero, and it is not the whole drift. The same paper documents that the extreme analysts’ forecasts studied by DeBondt and Thaler (1990) cannot be viewed as overreactions to earnings, and are not clearly linked to the stock-price overreactions discussed in DeBondt and Thaler (1985, 1987) and Chopra, Lakonishok, and Ritter. The conclusion is that security analysts’ behavior is at best only a partial explanation for stock-price underreaction to earnings, and may be unrelated to stock-price overreactions. A listener who hears “analysts underreact, therefore prices underreact” is hearing a claim this paper says is, at most, half an account of the earnings drift, and possibly no account at all of the longer overreactions.

Options split the same question by the shape of the news, not by a single bias that always lags or always overshoots. A 2001 paper in the Journal of Finance investigates options-market reaction to changes in the instantaneous variance of the underlying asset. There are three findings. First, options-market investors underreact to individual daily changes in instantaneous variance. Second, these same investors overreact to periods of mostly increasing or mostly decreasing daily changes in instantaneous variance. Third, they tend to underreact to current daily changes in instantaneous variance that are preceded mostly by daily changes of the opposite sign, and to overreact when the preceding daily changes mostly have the same sign. The third finding can reconcile the first two, and the paper says it is also consistent with well-established cognitive biases. A single day’s variance surprise is not the same object as a streak of them. The sign of what came before changes which mistake shows up today.

These tests were run on stocks, mutual funds, and options, not on a named prediction-market venue, so they do not certify any contract listed above. A listener can still use the test they share. Set the first print after a piece of news next to what the price does afterward. Ask whether information is still diffusing across people who cannot read one another’s signals in the quote, whether holders are sitting on gains or losses that match the sign of the news, whether a herd is following a recommendation into a quarter that later reverses, or whether the latest trades are chasing a streak rather than a single surprise. None of those questions is a pick. Each one is a way to tell a lag from an overshoot without treating the latest price as the last word.

How It Works: Can a Price Change the Outcome?

A forecast can move the world it is scoring. Performative prediction, as a 2026 arXiv survey describes the setup introduced by Perdomo and coauthors in 2020, is the case where deploying a predictive model shifts the environment, so the distribution the model expected and the distribution it then meets no longer match. The survey calls that shift a distribution map. It separates two targets. Performative stability is a fixed point: the model is consistent with the distribution its own deployment produces. Performative optimality is the stronger aim, a model that minimizes risk after the shift has happened. The survey's sorting rule is how much is known about the map. With a fully known map, the forecaster can plan for the reaction. With a poorly known map, the same optimization can chase a reaction that will not arrive. In the half-decade after 2020, the survey says, the literature added solution concepts, extended the setup, offered new theoretical analyses, and examined intersections with other fields.

They add that newer information — published probabilities derived from prediction market contract prices, and aggregated polling summaries — may have similar effects, so a majority for a candidate or a policy could grow in a cascade. Their test did not trade a contract. They randomly assigned people in a diverse national sample to receive information about different levels of support for three public policies. The thing that differed across groups was the reported level of support, not a new argument about the policy. They found that public opinion as expressed through polls affects individual-level attitudes, and that the size of the effect depends on issue characteristics. Read as a worked path: assign the message, hold the policy fixed, compare attitudes. If the groups diverge, the published support figure was an input to opinion, not only a photograph of it. The paper treats a prediction-market price as a channel that may work the same way. The measurement it reports is about poll information and attitudes, and the effect is issue-specific rather than a single shift that applies to every question.

A true forecast can still be a bad one to have published. A 2023 paper in Ethical Theory and Moral Practice defines a self-fulfilling prophecy as a prediction that brings about its own truth, and it sets out four elements that define the case. The critique is that typical self-fulfilling prophecies arise from mistakes about the relationship between a prediction and its object. Because the prediction comes true, those mistakes send no error signal, to humans or to machines. Truth shrouds the mistake. The authors argue that this blocks accountability for the outcome, and that failures of accountability, and the missed corrections that follow, connect self-fulfilling prophecies to feedback loops. They pay special attention to how automated prediction can make those problems worse.

The method dispute is about the map, not about whether prices exist. A 2024 arXiv paper on distributionally robust performative prediction says that misspecifying the distribution map can produce a poor approximation of the true performative optimum. It offers a distributionally robust performative optimum as a hedge when the nominal map is not the actual one, and it says that version can be rewritten as an augmented performative-prediction problem so it can still be optimized. The authors say they show guarantees that this robust point approximates the true optimum when the nominal map differs from the actual one, and that experiments suggest an advantage over the ordinary approach when the map is misspecified at a micro or a macro level. If the reaction to a published number is the thing you modeled wrong, chasing the ordinary optimum does not get you the outcome you thought you were optimizing.

A 2023 arXiv paper takes the same family of problems into inequality constraints, noting that earlier work had stayed with unconstrained cases even though transportation, finance, public policy, and recommendation systems often face limits. It aims at optimal solutions rather than only performatively stable points, and it proposes an adaptive primal-dual algorithm for location families. The analysis it reports is regret and constraint violations on the order of the square root of the time horizon, using the square root of T plus 2T samples, where T is that horizon. The point for a market reader is narrower than the algorithm: a published probability can be an input to the next decision, and the next decision can be boxed in by a rule the forecast did not choose.

On a question where seeing the figure could change a vote, a gift, or a decision, the price is partly a description and partly a cause. A play-money book is reputation, not dollars. A deep real-money book is still not a reason to take a side. It is evidence about what traders paid, under rules that can themselves be part of what happens next. How It Works: Anchoring: How the First Number Sticks

Anchoring is the pull of a first number on a later estimate. In the version decision researchers call anchoring and adjustment, a person starts at that initial figure and revises, and the revision often stops short of where a calculation made from scratch would land. A contract price is that kind of first number: it is on the screen before the reader has finished forming an estimate of their own.

A journal experiment makes the sequence concrete. Revista Finanzas y Política Económica describes work with 670 students in Concepción, Chile, Cali, Colombia, and Lima, Peru. Participants saw graphics of the S&P MILA Pacific Alliance Select index for a year, a month, a week, and the last closing value, then made a forecast judgment of the index. Least squares and a data panel were the analysis. The authors report that the anchoring and adjustment heuristic influenced those forecasts. From the same judgments they infer optimism bias: the issued forecast leaned optimistic or pessimistic rather than sitting neutral to the charts they had just been shown. The design isolates the starting display. Everyone saw the same index, arranged from a long window down to the last close, and the last close is the number closest to the moment of the judgment. If later estimates cluster toward that close, or toward whichever window the person treated as the reference, the forecast is not independent of the first figure on the page. The authors’ least-squares and panel results say that influence was present for this index. The optimism finding is separate and directional: the cognitive process of the forecast was biased toward an optimistic or pessimistic position, which the paper treats as an error of expectation sitting alongside the anchor.

Financial Management carries a second finding, from survey forecasts of macroeconomic statistics. Those forecasts systematically underpredict the statistics. Past macroeconomic announcements predict the forecast errors, which is the signature of an anchor in the history rather than a one-off miss. The stock market does not see through the bias. The authors find statistically significant stock-price effects of the “anticipated” components of macroeconomic announcements. Their account is that investors overweight historical information and do not make sufficient adjustments after new information arrives. The anticipated piece is the part a rational reader would already have in the price. A detectable price effect on that piece means the market treated stale forecast content as news.

An online prediction-market study disputes the idea that the pull is automatically an error. The arXiv paper on interval elicitation of forecasts split forecasters by what they could see. People who could not see the current state of the market until they had made their own separate estimates moved those estimates closer to the market forecast once the market became known. Their first edits to the market forecast were very similar to the first edits of forecasters who could always see the market. Accuracy was similar in both conditions. The authors conclude that the more elaborate elicitation method might not improve forecasts, and that any anchoring on the state of the market does not constitute an error in judgment. On their reading, stepping toward the visible price can be an update, not a failure to think.

Read a contract with that split in mind. The student charts and the macro surveys say a first number can leave the next estimate under-revised, and that a market of investors can fail to strip the stale part out of a forecast. The prediction-market experiment says moving toward the visible price can be what informed forecasters do, with no accuracy gap in that test relative to people who saw the price the whole time. The practical distinction is which number arrived first and what question it was answering. A last close on a chart, a prior macro announcement, and a contract price are different anchors. Noticing which one you saw before you had an estimate of your own is the use of the research. None of it is a reason to take a side of any contract. A play-money price, such as a Manifold reading in mana, is still only the number those traders produced with reputation at stake.

How It Works: Base Rates: Where a Good Forecast Starts

A base rate is the frequency of an outcome in a class of similar past cases, counted before anyone argues about the case now on the table. Using it means starting with that frequency and then adjusting for what is specific. Skipping it — treating the story in front of you as if the class had no history — is base rate neglect.

An AAPG Bulletin paper walks through the miss with a set of real wells. Lundin Petroleum drilled 25 exploration wells on the Norwegian Continental Shelf from 2011 through 2015. The relevant base rates were the historical exploration success rate in that area and the sizes of fields already found there. Against those frequencies, the paper says Lundin explorers consistently and significantly underestimated geological probability of success and overestimated success-case volumes. The mechanism is sequential. First, write down how often similar wells hit and how large similar finds were. Second, write the prospect-specific geology beside that, not instead of it. Third, notice that a portfolio of case-only estimates will not match the historical distribution even if each write-up sounds careful. The author argues explorers ignore those frequencies because the frequencies do not feel like they are about this prospect, and that the miss shrinks when the historical rates are integrated into the prospect evaluation rather than left in a separate file.

A 2024 paper on robust forecast aggregation, posted on arXiv, asks what happens when the people you are combining are themselves neglecting the base rate. Two experts each see a private signal and each state a probability for a binary world state. A parameter lambda, from zero to one, measures how much base-rate information they fold in: zero is complete ignorance of it, one is perfect Bayesian updating. The score is worst-case regret against an omniscient benchmark, the standard taken from Arieli, Babichenko and Smorodinsky in 2018. The result is a V-shape. Regret does not fall in a straight line as experts become better Bayesians. An intermediate lambda, short of one, can produce lower worst-case regret than perfect posteriors. The reason is about the aggregator, not about the world. The aggregator does not know the experts' information structure. Extreme, fully updated probabilities can be harder to combine safely across the worst case the aggregator has to fear. The authors also propose an aggregator meant to keep regret low when lambda itself is unknown, and they say they ran an empirical test of the neglect model. The abstract does not report that test's measurements, so the finding to keep is the V-shape, not a claimed improvement on live markets.

A Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience study separates who resists the miss. On the lawyer-engineer problem, the Cognitive Reflection Test predicted performance on conflict items, where the base rate and the stereotype point opposite ways. Short-term memory predicted the nonconflict items. In the scanner, conflict items brought greater activation in the anterior cingulate cortex even when the person still answered with the stereotype. Higher reflection scores went with greater posterior cingulate activation on those conflict items, a region the authors tie to shifting attention between outside cues and an internal check. That is one classic task, not a trading log.

Reference class forecasting is the project-management version of the same start. A Project Management Journal paper compared it with forecasts built by tracing the future path of the specific project, including methods based on Monte Carlo simulation and earned value management. On real project data, forecasts taken from the actual outcomes of similar past projects performed best for both cost and time.

The dispute is inside that literature, not a claim that base rates never help. The AAPG paper treats neglect as a correctable error. The arXiv result says that, for an aggregator who does not know how the experts are informed, a perfect Bayesian posterior is not automatically the input that minimizes worst-case regret. A listener reading a market price can hold both. The price is a case-level number from whoever is trading. The base rate is the hit rate of the class that case belongs to. If those two are far apart, the useful question is which class the contract actually resolves into, and whether the traders started from that class or from the story. Neither comparison is a reason to take a side. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Extremizing: Pushing an Average Away From the Middle

An average of probabilities is a biased instrument in a specific direction, toward one half, according to the 2014 paper in Decision Analysis titled Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More Extreme. The authors describe the common method as a simple weighted average of many individuals' estimates of an uncertain event. They argue those aggregated probabilities correspond more closely to the real world if they are transformed by being pushed closer to zero or to one.

The first distorting factor is compression of the probability scale at the two ends. Random error tends to push the average probability toward one half, because error that would have fallen beyond the end of the scale cannot, and so it accumulates on the inside. That effect does not occur for the median forecast. Arguably, the authors add, it does not occur for the mean of the log odds of the individual forecasts either.

The second factor affects the mean, the median, and the mean of the log odds. Forecasters take into account their individual ignorance of the total body of information available. Individual confidence in the direction of a probability judgment, high or low, thus fails to take into account the wisdom of crowds that results from combining different evidence available to different judges. Each person shades inward for a gap that the group, taken together, may already have filled.

The paper's first finding is that the same transformation function can approximately eliminate both distorting effects, with different parameters for the mean and for the median. The second finding is that use of the median can help distinguish the two effects, because the scale-compression problem is identified with the average and not the median, while the ignorance problem remains in the median. The abstract does not report the parameter values, so the size of either push is not a figure available from the source.

A weighted average of individual probabilities is pulled toward one half by error that bunches inside the ends of the scale. The median of those same forecasts drops that pull. Both still include each forecaster's discount for information they personally lacked. One transformation then pushes the aggregate toward zero or one, with a different parameter for the mean than for the median. The extra push the mean requires is how the authors separate compression from ignorance, and the median's remaining push is the ignorance effect. They call the fix approximate.

The paper itself qualifies the log-odds escape hatch with the word arguably, and it qualifies the cure as approximate rather than exact. The second factor is stated only for the case in which different judges hold different evidence. A 2019 Decision Analysis paper on probability forecasts and their combination takes up miscalibration, dependence among forecasters, and the choice of an evaluation measure, and it connects aggregation to decision making. It does not measure the 2014 transformation, so it neither confirms the parameters nor disputes them. Dependence is the neighboring problem: if the forecasts are not drawing on different evidence, the wisdom-of-crowds premise in the 2014 account is not met.

A listener should not treat a contract price as the average this paper repairs. A price is not a weighted mean of independent forecasts, and it is not their median. Nothing in the paper says to push a live price closer to zero or one, and that would be a trading rule, which this is not. Near one half, a forecast average may be compressed or privately cautious; a traded price may simply be unresolved. Near an extreme, a traded price is not thereby a corrected poll. The paper's claim stops at aggregated forecasts. Source: doi.org Source: doi.org

How It Works: Reading the Market's Rate Expectations

A federal funds futures price is a traded rate, not a ready-made chance that the Federal Reserve will move at a named meeting. The quote is the market’s price for the average federal funds rate over a future month. Turning that price into the probability of a decision means peeling off a term premium and then mapping what remains onto the discrete choices the committee actually faces.

A 2016 note in the Federal Reserve’s FEDS Notes series looks at term premiums at the very front of the curve. It uses federal funds futures together with the Desk’s Survey of Primary Dealers and its Survey of Market Participants, and it treats the premium that gets subtracted as an assumption, not as a number the futures price announces by itself. The arithmetic runs like this, and it only yields a probability if each input is the right one. Start with the futures-implied rate for the month that contains the meeting. Subtract the rate that would prevail if the committee stood still. What remains mixes compensation for bearing rate risk with the chance of a different policy setting. Subtract the premium being assumed. Divide what is left by the size of the step the committee is choosing among. Change the premium and the probability moves even though the futures price did not.

Two findings show why that last step is not bookkeeping. A 2009 paper in the Journal of Money, Credit and Banking says many studies estimate the risk premium on federal funds futures by assuming that average forecast errors are zero, or that survey forecasts are good proxies for expectations. Those assumptions can fail: an unanticipated declining trend in the funds rate breaks the zero-average-error claim, and survey respondents can behave strategically, so a survey is not a clean stand-in for the market’s expectation. Premiums estimated under those assumptions can be biased. With a different method, the authors find that the premiums have often been negative since 2000, a pattern they describe as generally consistent with the negative betas observed in the 2000s. A negative premium means the futures rate can sit below the expected future rate, so reading the raw futures price as the expected rate would understate the chance of higher policy settings.

A 2005 paper in the Journal of Futures Markets shows a different object once options, not just futures, are available. Options on federal funds futures began trading in March 2003. Because the Federal Open Market Committee chooses among discrete outcomes, the implied probability density can be recovered with ordinary least squares, rather than with the heavier methods used when an underlying price is treated as continuous, as with equities, foreign exchange, or commodity futures. The same estimation can recover a density for one meeting or a joint density for several meetings, and it can impose restrictions on the probabilities within a meeting and across meetings. The authors use those recovered probabilities to assess how data releases and Federal Reserve communication change the perceived likelihood of actual policy outcomes.

The dispute sits in the premium, not in the futures quote. The 2009 paper objects to two shortcuts — zero average forecast errors, and surveys as proxies — used when the premium is not observed directly. The 2016 note does not crown one premium assumption as the true one. It asks which assumptions are plausible given futures prices and the two Desk surveys.

An event contract on the same meeting is a different instrument. It pays a fixed amount if a stated decision occurs and nothing otherwise, so its price is already scaled as a probability of that statement, before anyone subtracts a term premium. Fees, capital, and a thin book can still move that price. A futures-implied chance of a hike exists only after someone names a premium and a menu of rate steps. Hearing both numbers on the same morning, a listener can ask which object was priced: a contract that pays if the committee does a named thing, or a rate future that still needs a premium assumption before it becomes a probability.

In that model, governance improves the liquidity of a company's shares only when the market also contains noise traders the authors describe as semi-informed. How strong the effect is depends on how much those traders take part in transactions. Governance and that participation have what the model calls a synergistic effect on liquidity. Take the semi-informed flow away, and the liquidity channel the authors are tracking does not appear.

The step that matters is the counterparty. A better-informed trader can act on a signal only if someone else will take the other side. If everyone is waiting on the same information, there is no trade, and the governance improvement never shows up as liquidity. The 2020 paper is a model of listed stocks, not a measurement of a prediction-market contract. What it isolates is still readable on a contract: money that is uninformed, or only partly informed, is not only a source of error. In this account it is a condition for anyone else to trade at all.

A 2015 field study in the International Journal of Financial Management looks for that mix in Indian equities. The authors use daily trading data plus intra-day prices, volumes, and trade times at one-day and five-day intervals for the Nifty-Fifty firms listed on both the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange, and they take market returns from the NSE Nifty and the BSE Sensex. They ask whether variation in returns includes noise-trader risk, and whether informed traders' short-run arbitrage pushes them into long-short positions that hedge it. The paper argues that noise has a systematic component and a firm-specific component, and that both vary over time. Those components include what the authors separate as idiosyncratic shocks and noise shocks. At lag periods, a long-short position across these markets can hedge the fundamental systematic shock and the fundamental firm-specific shock, and may detach the noise shock from them. The study's closing claim is that, during the trading day, prices impound information and noise together.

A 2016 paper in Qualitative Research in Financial Markets pushes on the habit of treating every individual trader as a dumb noise trader. Experienced researchers and practitioners rated the mathematical and verbal reasoning demands of investment tasks used in earlier studies. On that assessment, individual investors can perform a number of complex cognitive actions, especially those that demand higher-order verbal reasoning, and they look more limited on tasks that demand greater mathematical reasoning. The authors add that the math-heavy tasks are the ones considered more relevant to performance. They also limit their own result. The measurement is indirect, so they will not claim a definite score for retail competence; a direct test would be a laboratory experiment, and they say that data was not available for the retail investors in view. They could analyze ten tasks, which limits statistical power and blocks a deeper deductive test. Their practical notes stay on disclosure: illustrate key investor documents mostly in words, tell people what studies of this kind found, and offer online math practice built for individual investors. That is not a trading instruction.

The three papers do not draw one portrait, and forcing one would misread them. The 2020 piece is a model, and its noise trader is semi-informed, not empty of information. The 2016 authors dispute the undifferentiated "dumb noise trader" label, then mark the narrow base under their own dispute: ten tasks, no direct performance test. A listener can still read a contract price differently. A move can be information, or it can be the other side of an order that was never about the fact on the screen. In the 2016 assessment, the people in that flow are uneven — more capable on verbal reasoning than on the math that performance is said to require — not uniformly lost. How It Works: Who Wins and Who Loses: What Studies Find on Trader Returns

A price can line up with what traders already believed, and the people who held the belief that later paid can still be the ones who made the money. That is the mechanism in a Journal of Prediction Markets study of private information, overconfidence, and trader returns. The setting was four real-money prediction markets whose contracts paid on a movie's box office. Before any market opened, every trader submitted a detailed forecast of that movie's performance. The private information was self-generated. No one randomly handed a group a perfect tip, which is how many lab experiments create "insiders." Accuracy could be scored only after the box office result existed.

The authors treated overconfidence as the thing to look for in behavior, not as a slogan. Volume and the timing of trading were consistent with overconfidence: people who had done the analytical work traded as if that work were more decisive than it was. In three of the four markets, contract prices were consistent with the prior-information equilibrium. The price looked like the information traders had walked in with. In those same three markets, traders whose forecast was associated with the winning contract had significantly higher returns than traders whose forecasts pointed at another contract. In the fourth market, there were no significant differences in returns across those groups.

Those are the only splits the paper's account gives, so the worked example stops there. Four markets. Three prices sat where the prior information said they should, and in those three the traders tied to the winning contract earned significantly more. One market produced no significant return gap at all. A listener looking for a single win rate, a dollar profit, or a share of gains captured by a small group will not find those figures here. The authors' conclusion is narrow, and it is not a strategy: when traders are overconfident and trade accordingly, there can be value to being better informed, if the information is accurate.

A second paper, in the Journal of Futures Markets, splits profit rather than forecasts. It examines local traders on the floor of the Sydney Futures Exchange and decomposes their income into liquidity profits and position-taking profits. Locals made significant position-taking profits. The ability to earn those position-taking profits was positively related to order-flow information, and negatively related to exogenous information, to the locals' own liquidity profits, and to the length of a local's inventory cycle. The authors characterize those locals as active informed traders, not as a desk that only collected the spread for standing in the market.

That "informed" reading of order flow is disputed in a neighboring market. Another Journal of Futures Markets paper uses option order imbalance on Shanghai Stock Exchange 50 ETF options as a proxy for market makers' inventory pressure. Call order imbalances were contemporaneously positively associated with underlying returns, put order imbalances negatively associated, and both were followed by rapid reversals. The authors tie the reversal to temporary price pressure from delta-hedging. Two empirical scenarios, they report, reinforce that the imbalance reflected inventory risk rather than informed trading. Flow that looks like someone who knows the future can be a hedge being laid off.

A listener who treats a price as a forecast can still ask which of these mechanisms made it. In the movie markets, the price often matched beliefs traders already held, and the traders whose belief matched the outcome made more, except in the one market where that gap did not appear. On a futures floor, a meaningful slice of local profit was position-taking tied to order flow, and it shrank when outside news arrived or when inventory sat longer. In the options study, the same-looking imbalance was a market maker's hedge, and it reversed. None of that names a side to take. It is a reason a contract price can say something about an event and still say very little about whether any particular trader is the one who gets paid.

How It Works: Whose Belief Is the Price? The Debate Over What a Price Averages

A binary event contract pays a fixed sum if a stated event happens and pays nothing if it does not. Scale that promise to one dollar and the trading price is a number between zero and one. The argument over what that number averages is an argument about who is allowed to pull it, and with how much force. It is not an argument about which side of any live contract is cheap.

Picture a room of traders, each holding a private chance. Anyone who thinks the event is more likely than the price wants to buy. Anyone who thinks it is less likely wants to sell. Each trade nudges the price toward the side that just acted. Trading can stop when the people still in the market no longer find it worth moving. Under a strict set of assumptions — traders care only about expected payout, they can buy or sell as much as they like, and everyone with a view can reach the book — that stopping point is a weighted average of the beliefs in the room. The weights are not equal votes. A trader with more money behind a view pulls harder. In that narrow case, a contract at 60 cents is not a poll of heads. It is a money-weighted balance. Reading it as a 60 percent chance is a reading of that balance, not of a show of hands.

The critique is that those assumptions do the work, and they often fail. A risk-averse trader will not stake enough to drag the price all the way to their belief, because a wrong bet hurts more than the expected gain feels worth. The price can then sit away from the average belief even when everyone can see the gap. A budget limit does the same job: a trader who is out of money stops pulling, whatever they think. Access does it too. A book closed to people in a given state or country averages only the beliefs of people allowed to trade. Hedging adds another pull. Someone who loses money if the event happens may buy the contract as insurance, not because they think the event is likely. That purchase lifts the price without lifting their stated chance.

Replies to that critique do not deny the gap. They ask when it is large. One line of argument says that if many small traders can take the other side of a hedger, insurance demand gets absorbed and the price moves back toward people trading the event itself. Another says the gap shrinks when the contract is a tiny slice of each trader’s wealth, because risk aversion bites less on a small stake, and widens when the stake is large relative to what a trader can lose. Neither reply restores a simple average of opinions. Both treat the distance between price and average belief as a fact about constraints — risk, budget, access, and why people are in the market.

There is no dollar budget forcing a trader to size a view. The pull comes from a willingness to spend mana and to be seen as wrong. A trader who thinks the chance is far from 44.9 percent can move the print only to the extent the play-money book lets them. The 44.9 percent figure is where that book cleared at the read time. It is not a count of how many of the 4,759 traders would raise a hand for yes, and it is not a dollar-weighted average of beliefs, because no dollars are in the stake.

What the two readings do show is that “the price” on one venue is not one kind of average. A contract with 4,759 traders and a contract with 223 traders are both mana-weighted clearings, and the smaller crowd is a smaller set of beliefs being averaged, even before anyone asks whether those beliefs are careful.

A listener who knows this reads a contract differently. The price is the clearing number: what buyers paid and sellers accepted, on that venue, at that time, in that venue’s unit. On a dollar book it is evidence about the traders who could and did show up, weighted by what they were willing to risk in money. On Manifold it is evidence about traders risking mana and reputation, which the venue itself says is the stake. It is not, by itself, the average opinion of a public, a newsroom, or everyone with a view. A book closed by geography averages a smaller room. A book where only reputation is at stake is a different object from a book where a wrong trade costs dollars. None of that says which side of any contract is cheap. How It Works: Why Traders Hold Losing Positions Too Long

The disposition effect is a split in how a position gets closed: winners are sold early, and losers are held. On an event contract, that split is a trader cashing a contract that has moved in their favor while leaving a contract that has moved against them untouched. The quoted price is the price of contracts people are willing to trade. A loser nobody will sell does not revise that quote.

A second frame sits on top of the single position. A Journal of Finance paper finds that the disposition effect for a stock weakens when the portfolio is at a gain, and is large when the portfolio is at a loss. The authors call that pattern the portfolio-driven disposition effect. They find it in four independent settings: U.S. archival data, Chinese archival data, a U.S. experiment, and a Chinese experiment. It survives controls for extreme returns, portfolio rebalancing, tax considerations, and investor heterogeneity. The evidence they offer is that investors keep one mental frame for the stock and another for the portfolio, and that the two frames together generate the pattern.

When the portfolio is at a gain, the tendency to sell the winner and hold the loser weakens. When the portfolio is at a loss, that tendency is large. The contract most in need of a sale, if new evidence has turned against it, is the loser. That is the contract the pattern holds onto, and the hold is largest when the whole book is down. The winner is the contract the pattern releases, and releasing a winner does less to pull the quote toward bad news the holder has not traded.

A Frontiers in Psychology experiment with 193 professional traders complicates the idea that the pattern is always a mistake. The design was within-subject: the same traders decided on mean-reverting securities and on securities that do not mean-revert. Before any intervention, their disposition-effect trades pointed in the direction that aligned with profit maximization. On a mean-reverting security, selling into strength and holding a loser can be the rational trade, because the price is expected to come back. A simple informational intervention that made the disposition effect salient changed the level of the effect on both kinds of security. That intervention improved trader returns when the decisions were on non-mean-reverting securities. It had a negative impact on returns when traders used it for mean-reverting securities. A nudge can be large, and it helps only when the security behaves the way the nudge assumes.

On Xueqiu, a Chinese social-investment platform, a separate paper matches post data to actual trading data. Social-media information significantly reduces the disposition effect, and the reduction runs through negative information. Shown negative information, individual investors adjust positions in a more rational way, in that paper's account. Investment experience, users followed, region, and gender all change how well the information works. That study is not about event contracts. It is about whether the information in front of a holder can loosen the hold.

Set the three findings next to a price someone is reading as a forecast. The Journal of Finance result says a losing book makes the hold on losers larger, so the contracts that would register bad news are the ones least likely to trade. The Frontiers result says that hold is not automatically an error: if the question mean-reverts, keeping the loser can be the profit-maximizing trade, and a warning that pushes a sale can cut returns. The Xueqiu result says negative information is the channel that gets holders to adjust, so a price can sit still until the bad news is in front of the people holding the losing side, not merely until the news exists. A listener who knows that is reading the trades that cleared, minus the losing positions people would not close. On a question that comes back, stillness can be sense. On a question that does not, stillness can be the missing sell. Source: doi.org Source: doi.org Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Forecasting an Outbreak With a Market

Seasonal influenza forecasting starts from a running count of infections and asks two different questions: when the wave crests, and how big it is. The systems in today's papers do that by fitting a model to past weeks and then updating it as new observations arrive, the way a weather model takes in a fresh reading. They are not exchanges. No abstract here describes a contract, a trader, or a payout.

The worked example with a stated lead time is the New York City study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. For the 2003 through 2008 seasons, researchers assimilated weekly web-based estimates of local infection rates and, after each update, produced a retrospective ensemble of forecasts rather than a single line. They report that skillful real-time predictions of peak timing could be made more than seven weeks before the actual peak. Confidence was not a separate slogan; it was the spread of the ensemble. A narrow spread meant the timing runs agreed. A wide spread meant they did not.

Melbourne shows the split between timing and size. Using Google Flu Trends for 2006 through 2014, a bootstrap particle filter and a mechanistic infection model called the epidemic peak accurately four to six weeks ahead. The magnitude of the peak and the overall burden were much harder to predict. The team chose among hundreds of observation models by treating forecasts like receiver operating characteristic curves. In Fuzhou, SARIMA, Prophet, Holt-Winters, and XGBoost were fit to cases from January 2013 through December 2021 and tested on 2022. XGBoost had the lowest mean squared error, root mean squared error, and mean absolute error on the training set and the test set. The case series was seasonal and cyclical, with an upward trend and large swings. Dante, described in Nature Communications, learns spatial, temporal, and surveillance structure instead of prescribing it, and it issues forecasts that stay coherent across state, regional, and national scales. It beat a Dynamic Bayesian Model for nearly all spatial units, flu seasons, geographic scales, and targets in a retrospective comparison, and it placed first in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's prospective 2018/19 FluSight challenge in the national competition, the regional competition, and the state competition.

Where the work is disputed is in what a good score is allowed to decide. The Melbourne authors say peak size and total burden do not come along for free when timing is skillful. CDC scientists, in a BMC Public Health paper on FluSight challenges hosted since the 2013-14 season, write that federal, state, and local officials need to understand how a forecast was made, how to interpret it, and how it performed in the past before it is built into a response. Heterogeneous surveillance, which Dante's authors flag, means a clean national curve can hide a thin state.

A listener reading a market on an outbreak can borrow that discipline without pretending these papers scored a venue. Name the target: peak week is not peak height, and peak height is not total burden. Name the lead time: more than seven weeks in the New York exercise is a different claim from four to six weeks in Melbourne. And do not treat one percentage as a measurement unless an ensemble, a past error, or both are visible. A contract price collapses a crowd into one number. These studies say that number is usable only for the question it actually answered, on the horizon where it actually had skill.
