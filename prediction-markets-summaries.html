How It Works: Following the Crowd: Herding and Information Cascades

An information cascade is a sequence in which a later decision-maker copies earlier actions and sets aside what they themselves know, because the earlier actions already look like evidence. In a market, that copycat logic can push a price away from the private information traders are sitting on. A flexible price is supposed to interrupt the cascade: once the price can move, the information in earlier trades is already in the number, so copying the last trade adds nothing new.

A 2005 internet experiment in the American Economic Review tested that idea, the theory of informational cascades in financial markets set out by Christopher Avery and Peter Zemsky in 1998. More than 6,400 subjects took part, including a subsample of 267 consultants from an international consulting firm. Walk the design the way the paper reports it. Subjects make decisions in a market whose price is free to move, rather than in a setting where only the previous person's action is visible. The flexible price prevented herding: people did not simply pile onto the last trade and ignore their own signal. Contrarian behavior still distorted prices. Even after 20 decisions, convergence to the fundamental value was rare. The consultants did not behave in a significantly different way from the other subjects. So a price that can move blocks one failure mode, blind copying, and still leaves another: trades against the crowd that pull the price off the value the signals would support, with convergence still uncommon after a long string of decisions.

A 2007 paper in the Journal of Finance put the same kind of question to people who do this for a living. Market professionals from the Chicago Board of Trade were observed in a controlled setting and compared with a student control group. Analysis of over 1,500 individual decisions found that the professionals were better able to discern the quality of public signals, and that their decisions were not affected by the domain of earnings. The student group did not show that same pattern. The authors treat the gap as a point about market efficiency: who is in the market changes how public signals get used.

A 2012 paper in Management Science looked for herding where the "price" is a funding tally, on Prosper.com, then the largest microloan market in the United States. Well-funded borrower listings tended to attract more funding after the researchers controlled for unobserved listing heterogeneity and payoff externalities. The lenders were not only mimicking. They inferred creditworthiness from peer lending decisions and used publicly observable borrower characteristics to moderate those inferences — active observational learning, which the paper calls rational herding, rather than passive copying, which it calls irrational herding. Obvious defects amplified the herd: poor credit grades increased a listing's herding momentum, because lenders inferred that the borrower must be better than the grade or the crowd would not have funded it. Favorable traits did the opposite. Friend endorsements weakened the herding effect, because lenders could attribute the crowd to a fact they could already see. Follow-up analysis found that rational herding beat irrational herding in predicting loan performance.

A 2018 paper in the Annals of Financial Economics tested herding in crypto-currency markets with the CSAD method of Chang et al. (2000). It used daily returns of six major crypto-currencies and the market index CCI30 from 07-08-2015 to 18-01-2018, and it checked up markets against down markets and high volatility against low volatility. Herding showed up in both up and down markets, which the paper reads as over-enthusiasm and over-reaction. Market volatility did not have a significant impact. Herding depended on market activity rather than on volatility.

A 2013 review in the Review of Behavioral Finance is the dispute on the shelf. More than two decades of work have produced real insight into investor herding, and the evidence is still inconclusive in places. The review says relatively little is known for markets other than equity markets, and that empirical methods still need to address current limitations. A crypto result, a loan-listing result and a trading-floor result do not automatically transfer to an event-contract book.

Read a fast price with that split in mind. A crowd all hitting the same side can be people ignoring what they know, or a price that has already absorbed what earlier traders knew, so the next copy is empty. It can also be contrarians shoving the number away from the signals, the failure the 2005 experiment still found after 20 decisions. And a visible fact can explain the herd without any hidden insight, the way friend endorsements did on Prosper: once the obvious merit is public, the pile-on says less. None of that says which side of any contract is cheap. It says a price is a record of decisions, and those decisions are not all information.

How It Works: Where the Money Sits: Collateral, Custody and Payout

On a collateralized prediction market, the dollar committed to a contract does not come back the moment the outcome is known. It sits until an oracle settles the claim and the winning position can be redeemed. A paper on settlement discounting puts the mechanism in one line: when collateral remains locked until oracle settlement, a near-certain dollar is a delayed dollar. The price therefore carries two things at once. One is a belief about the outcome. The other is a maturity-dependent discount for the wait between certainty and cash.

The authors recover that second piece from contracts that stay near certain, using the settlement times those contracts actually realized. They summarize the recovered curve as an annualized settlement wedge. The wedges they recover are positive, they change with maturity, and they change over time. That is the worked path through their own figures. Start with the raw prices of near-certain claims across horizons. The horizon gradient is how much those prices differ as the wait until settlement lengthens. Adjust the prices by the recovered wedge curves. The paper finds that this adjustment reduces the near-certainty horizon gradient by roughly 48 to 88 percent. Most of the raw pattern across maturities, on that measure, is a priced settlement friction, not forecast error alone. What remains after the adjustment is the part the wedge does not explain.

Two design findings sit next to that measurement. Neg-risk conversion compresses the discounts by recycling part of the position into synthetic collateral, so less capital has to sit idle for the full wait. Yield-bearing collateral flattens the term structure by cutting the opportunity cost of the lock-up: the dollar that cannot be withdrawn can still earn something while it waits. In both cases the price moves because the funding terms moved, not because the underlying event became more or less likely.

The paper does not claim the wedge removes the gradient entirely. A reduction of 48 to 88 percent leaves a residual. The result is also tied to a particular architecture: collateral locked until an oracle redeems the claim. A venue that pays from a different custody setup, or that lets capital turn over sooner, is outside what these curves measure. The limit is the one the measurement states.

A listener who knows this reads a price near certainty differently depending on when the dollar can actually leave. A contract that is already sure, but whose collateral cannot move until a later oracle date, is not the same object as a contract that settles at once. Part of the gap between those prices can be the wait, the productivity of the collateral, and whether the market recycles capital, rather than a different belief about the event. The paper's closing claim is that prediction-market prices aggregate information through a financial infrastructure whose funding conditions are measurable. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Why a Contract That Settles Next Year Trades Below Its Odds

A contract that pays if an event happens, and pays nothing if it does not, is a claim on cash that arrives only when the contract settles. If that date is a year away, the payout is a year away even for a trader who is already sure of the result. The money posted to hold the position sits as collateral until then. Cash that is locked cannot be spent, lent, or posted somewhere else in the meantime. Whatever that cash could have earned over the wait is a cost of holding the contract. A trader who has to borrow the collateral, or who simply has another use for it, will not hand over today the full amount they think the contract is likely to pay later. The price can sit below the probability that trader actually holds. The gap is a charge for time, not a quieter forecast.

Suppose a trader's own belief is that the event is more likely than not, and the contract cannot pay until next year. The most they will post today is that belief, reduced by what they give up while the collateral is stuck. The reduction tracks the return available on that cash over the life of the contract, plus any fee the venue takes for keeping the position open. A contract that settles next week has almost none of that wait. The same belief, on the same event, can therefore show a higher price on the near contract than on the far one. Reading the far price as if it were the near price treats two different claims as one forecast.

Venues that pay interest on collateral change the arithmetic. If the cash posted earns a return while it waits, the trader is no longer giving up the whole alternative yield. The time charge shrinks, and the price can sit closer to the probability the trader holds. A venue that pays nothing on margin leaves the full cost of the wait inside the price. Comparing a long-dated price on a venue that pays interest with one on a venue that does not, without knowing which is which, mixes a funding term into what looks like a probability.

The adjustment fails when capital is not what binds. A position limit, a fee that does not scale with time, or a resolution rule that can void or rewrite the contract can move the price for reasons that have nothing to do with the calendar. A thin market can print a number that no one was willing to defend with size. Play money, where the stake is reputation rather than cash that could have earned a return, does not carry the same time charge, so its long-dated price is not the same object as a cash price on a distant contract.

Once that is clear, a listener reads a far-dated price with the settlement date in view, and asks whether the collateral earns anything while it sits. A price below a stated belief is not, by itself, a claim that the event is less likely. It may be the cost of waiting to be paid.

A winner-takes-all contract pays if one side receives more votes than the other, and pays nothing otherwise, so the price is a chance of finishing ahead. A vote-share contract pays according to the share of the popular vote, so the price is a forecast of the margin, not of the winner.

A paper in PS: Political Science & Politics walks through both designs for the 2024 presidential popular vote, read on September 29. The vote-share market was thinly traded, and it priced a 9-percentage-point Democratic popular-vote margin. The winner-takes-all market was thickly traded, and it priced an 85.7 percent chance that the Democrat would receive more votes than the Republican. The authors then built one distribution from both markets. That combined reading was a 6- to 7-percentage-point Democratic margin, and an 87.0 percent chance the Democrat would finish ahead in the popular vote. The paper says significant uncertainty remained.

The arithmetic is the lesson. Start with the thin vote-share price: a 9-point Democratic margin. That number is not a probability of winning. It is a priced margin. Move to the thick winner-takes-all price: 85.7 percent that the Democrat receives more votes. That number is not a margin. Combine the two, as the paper does, and the margin shrinks to 6 to 7 points while the chance of finishing ahead rises slightly, to 87.0 percent. How It Works: How Fast a Price Moves: Markets Against the Clock

A prediction-market price is a running yes-or-no answer, and the clock starts the moment public information changes the chance of that answer. A print that jumps in the first minute can look finished. It often is not. A paper posted to arXiv, “When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets,” separates a fast twitch from a full update. The setting combines binary payoffs, public signals the authors can time exactly, and high-frequency prices, so a change in the traded price can be set next to a change in a benchmark probability built only from information that is already public.

Prices in that setting are informative, and they become more accurate as resolution approaches. During the event, prices respond rapidly to public signals and move in the expected direction. Directional responsiveness is not the same as efficient updating. Relative to an out-of-sample benchmark probability model, a one-minute change in the benchmark probability is associated with only about a 0.64-for-one contemporaneous change in the market price. Walk that coefficient as the paper states it, without adding a second result. Take a one-minute move in the benchmark as one unit. About 0.64 of that unit shows up in the price in the same minute. The rest of that one-minute benchmark move is not in the price yet. The missing adjustment predicts future price drift over the following several minutes. That later drift is still there net of subsequent changes in the benchmark probability, so it is not only the market chasing a benchmark that kept moving after the first minute.

The authors then look at why the gap opens. Salient public signals are incorporated relatively quickly in liquid markets. The same signals generate substantially greater underreaction when liquidity is low. Underreaction gaps associated with salient states also predict stronger subsequent drift. The pattern they describe is gradual price discovery shaped by attention and trading frictions together. A market can see the public signal, move the right way, and still leave part of the benchmark’s update for the next several minutes. Liquidity changes how large that leftover is. Salience changes how hard the leftover pulls on later prices.

Nothing in the paper is a halt rule, a trading instruction, or a claim that one side of any live contract is the right side. The useful reading is narrower. A price that moves in the expected direction in the first minute is evidence that traders responded to public information. It is not evidence that the implied probability has absorbed the full benchmark update. In a liquid book, more of a salient signal lands quickly. In a book with low liquidity, the same kind of signal leaves a larger underreaction, and the paper finds that those gaps predict more drift afterward. Market-implied probabilities in this study are often directionally correct, yet the adjustment stays incomplete and depends, in a predictable way, on liquidity and salience. A listener who keeps that split in mind treats the first print as a start, then asks whether the book was liquid enough for that start to be close to the full public update. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Why the Prices in a Race Do Not Add Up to One

A multi-candidate race on a prediction venue is usually not one contract. It is a row of separate yes-or-no contracts, one name each. Each contract is written to pay a fixed unit if that name is the winner and to pay nothing if another name is. When the outcomes shut one another out, and when one of them has to happen, a complete set of fair yes prices should be worth that same unit. The numbers on the screen often add up to more than the unit, or to less. The gap is about how the contracts are quoted and cleared. It is not a second race hiding inside the first.

Each leg has a bid, the price at which a trader can sell, and an ask, the price at which a trader can buy. The figure a venue prints may be the last trade, the midpoint between bid and ask, or the price a buyer would actually have to pay. Adding the asks adds the expensive side of every book. Adding the bids adds the cheap side. Neither total is the cost of a sure payout, and neither has to equal the unit the contracts pay. A last-trade print on a quiet name can sit away from the live bid and ask, so a column of last prices is not a portfolio a trader could lift in one go.

Fees open the same gap from another direction. If the venue takes a cut from the premium, or from the payout, buying every yes is not a free conversion even when the printed prices add up to exactly the unit. The buyer pays the sum of the asks, plus any fee, and receives one unit only if the written rules really do pay exactly one winner. A total above the payout unit can be what is left after the spread and the venue's cut. A total below the unit can mean the books are stale, one outcome is missing from the menu, or sellers have not posted size on the names that look expensive.

A thin book does the rest of the work. On a contract with little size at the inside price, one small order moves that name and leaves the other names where they were. The sum jumps even though nothing about the race changed. Until someone else posts the other side, the total can sit away from the payout unit. A listener reads that sum next to the size on each leg. The sum alone is not a verdict on the field.

Some market designs try to pull the legs back together. A combined or basket order lets a trader buy yes on one name and no on the others in a single action, or sell a complete set, so the venue can match the package against one pool of collateral instead of five unrelated wagers. Negative risk, in that generic sense, is the idea that holding no on every candidate but one is the same economic position as yes on the remaining name, so the margin system should not charge as if those bets were strangers. When that netting works, a trader who sees yes prices adding past the payout can sell the expensive set, or buy the cheap set, and that trade is what drags the sum back toward the unit. When the venue does not net the positions, or when the book is too thin for the package to fill, the gap can sit there. None of that says which name is cheap. It says the column is not a pie chart.

Two ways the sum misleads should stay separate. The contracts may not cover every possible winner. And the resolution rules may pay more than one winner, or none, if the written criteria overlap or if the event is cancelled or voided. In that case a sum of one is the wrong benchmark. The rule comes before the arithmetic.

A listener who has this stops treating a total above or below a dollar as a forecast. The useful comparison is leg against leg, on the same side of the book, after asking whether the outcomes exclude one another, whether a fee sits between the printed price and the payout, and whether each contract has enough size that the printed price is a price someone can trade. The gap is a statement about market structure. It is not, by itself, a statement about who wins.

How It Works: One Question Inside Another: Correlated and Combinatorial Markets

A combinatorial prediction market prices not only standalone events but events defined as conditions and as Boolean combinations of a base set. The joint distribution is the hard part. Explicitly writing down every combination is infeasible once there are more than a few base events, and computing or updating prices in a logarithmic market scoring rule book is #P-hard. That is why these markets are difficult to run even when the questions look ordinary.

The scoring rule decides whether nested questions stay consistent. In the account in the Journal of Prediction Markets, scoring rules elicit probability estimates from individuals, while betting markets elicit consensus estimates from groups. A market scoring rule does both, and eliciting from a group costs no more than eliciting from an individual. For a bet on one event given another event, only the logarithmic versions preserve the probability of the given event. Logarithmic versions also preserve the conditional probabilities of other events, and so preserve conditional independence. Given logarithmic rules that already elicit relative probabilities of base event pairs, it costs no more to elicit estimates on all combinations of those base events.

That preservation is the difference between two linked questions and one joint book. A contract on a policy-rate cut and a separate contract on an inflation print can each trade briskly and still disagree about the conditional, because nothing in two standalone books forces the price of "cut, given a high print" to match the two marginal prices. A logarithmic market scoring rule is the version that, on a bet on one event given another, leaves the probability of the conditioned event unchanged. A reader who sees only the two standalone prices cannot check that conditional. The prices can all be locally active and still be jointly impossible.

Correlation adds an information problem on top of the arithmetic. A 2017 paper in the American Economic Review studies information spillovers when privately informed sellers hold assets with correlated values. A trade in one asset can provide information about the others. The information content of that trading behavior is endogenous: it is not a fixed leak, and it depends on the equilibrium being played. When assets are sufficiently correlated, that endogeneity leads to multiple equilibria. The equilibria are ranked by trade volume and by efficiency. Introducing post-trade transparency can increase or decrease both welfare and trading volume, depending on the asset correlation, the equilibrium being played, and the composition of market participants. Publishing the last trade is not automatically an improvement.

Two computational lines try to make the joint book runnable. How It Works: The Order Book: How Two Traders Find a Price

A market maker provides liquidity by quoting prices to buy and to sell an asset, earning the spread while managing inventory risk. On an order book, those quotes are offers waiting for someone to take the other side. A trade is the moment a buy interest and a sell interest meet. Until that match, the person who posted the quote is the one carrying the position. That is the whole job the research below studies on Polymarket: post prices, earn the gap between them, and survive the inventory.

Polymarket's books are described as thin, and thin books change that job. If few other orders are resting, the maker's own quote is what a later trader can hit. The paper names three problems that follow. Adverse selection may cause a policy trained on real data to stop quoting, because the traders who take the quote can know more than the quote does. The consequences of a quote can arrive many steps after the action, so a learning system struggles to connect a later gain or loss to the earlier post. Prediction-market level-two data does not identify the individual orders a simulator needs.

The authors' path through those problems is reconstruction and training, not a hypothetical match at a made-up price. They reconstructed order-level LOBSTER-format data from Polymarket and trained agents in JaxMARL-HFT on 417 real markets. They enriched the baseline maker's observation space with the book and recent flow, and they redesigned its actions and inventory controls. They also proposed a cooperative split into a quoter and a risk manager, trained with recurrent Independent Proximal Policy Optimisation and recurrent policies. Reward design is how they tried to fix the delayed consequence. They investigated reward blending and a post-fill markout, a score of how price moves after a fill, so the two agents could be credited for what each one did.

Two findings come from that comparison, and they do not say the same thing. The redesigned single-agent market maker quotes more frequently and incurs smaller held-out losses than the retrained JaxMARL-HFT baseline. The contributions of the jointly changed observations, actions, and risk constraints are not separately identified, so a reader cannot tell which change produced the smaller loss. Decomposition alone performs poorly. Careful reward design substantially improves the desk relative to the unshaped split and yields the authors' best result.

The limit is in the same abstract. Models are evaluated on realised end-of-episode portfolio value on held-out markets. None establishes positive average portfolio value in periodic held-out evaluation. Each configuration was trained with one seed. The authors say the environment, the data, and the protocol lay a foundation for makers that quote many linked markets at once. They do not report that this foundation already keeps a positive average value once the markets are ones the agents did not train on.

A listener who has that picture reads a screen price as a quote that still has to be held, not as a crowd that has already agreed. On a thin book, the number can be one maker's offer. A completed trade and a quote that is still resting are different objects: one has found a counterparty, and the other has not. How It Works: What Volume Does and Does Not Tell You

A price is a number. Volume is the trail of how that number got there, and the trail is not the same thing as the information behind it. A paper posted on arXiv treats a prediction market as a Bayesian inverse problem: the unknown is a yes-or-no outcome, and the data are a history of market-implied probabilities plus the volumes traded along the way. The observation model sits in log-odds space. Conditional on volume, each price increment is drawn from a latent mixture of trader types. Informed flow, uninformed flow, heavy-tailed microstructure noise, and adversarial or manipulative flow can all produce an increment. The only observables the model requires are price and volume. Volume, in that setup, is a conditioner, not a proof. It changes which mixture you think generated the move. It does not, by itself, say the move was informed.

Start with a prior on the outcome. Observe a path: a sequence of implied probabilities and the volume attached to each step. Convert the probability path into log-odds so increments are additive. At each step, condition on that step’s volume and ask which type-mixture is likeliest to have produced an increment of that size. Update the posterior on the outcome only to the extent the outcome-conditional laws of those increments are separable. The paper’s identifiability criterion is Kullback–Leibler separation between those laws. If the law of increments when the event happens is hard to tell apart from the law when it does not, the history is not informative, however long the path and however large the volume. The paper also states posterior concentration and finite-sample error bounds under regularity assumptions, and it defines stability as how much posterior odds move when the observed price–volume path is perturbed. Realized information gain is the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the posterior from the prior; expected information gain is mutual information between the outcome and the history. Synthetic experiments in the paper are reported as validating the concentration rates and the identifiability thresholds. They are not a field test of any live venue.

A second result, from a Mathematical Finance paper on dynamic trading volume, says why a large volume number can be a property of the market’s plumbing rather than of anyone’s forecast. In a market with finite depth and constant investment opportunities, a large investor with a long horizon and constant relative risk aversion trades a safe asset and a risky asset. Trading volume in that model approximately follows a Gaussian, mean-reverting diffusion, and it increases with depth, with volatility, and with risk aversion. Finite depth also rules out leverage and short sales, because those positions may not stay solvent even if the investor can trade continuously. Read that against the inverse-problem paper and the split is clear. One paper says volume scales with how deep the book is, how much the price jumps around, and how risk-averse the large trader is. The other says that once volume is observed, you still have to separate informed types from uninformed types, noise, and adversarial flow, and that separation can fail.

The failure mode the arXiv paper names is type-composition confounding, or an outcome–nuisance symmetry: the same price–volume path can be produced by different mixes of trader types, so the inverse problem is ill-posed. In that regime, adding more of the same history does not tighten the posterior in a way you can trust. A listener who knows this reads a busy market and a quiet one differently, and does not treat the busy one as automatically better. A large volume says many contracts changed hands, or, in the finite-depth model, that depth, volatility, or risk aversion were high enough to generate a lot of trading. It does not say the traders were informed, that the types were identifiable, or that a small perturbation of the path would have left the implied probability where it is. A thin history can be uninformative because there is little to condition on. A thick history can be uninformative because the increment laws do not separate. The useful question is whether the path distinguishes the outcome, not whether the volume figure is large.

How It Works: Who Provides Liquidity and Why

A prediction-market contract settles to one of two payoffs. The person who quotes both sides is not only marking a position that moves with the last trade. Any size still open when the question resolves jumps to the winning payoff or to zero. That binary settlement is why the quoting problem is different from the one studied for a stock or a future that can be rolled forward. The screen price is a probability, and the job of the quote is to turn an incoming order into an update of that probability without leaving the quoter holding a settlement bet they did not mean to keep.

In the framework posted to arXiv as Optimal Market Making in Prediction Markets, that price is modeled as a conditional probability of the outcome, produced by transforming a latent belief that diffuses over time. The market maker chooses a bid and an ask. The objective is expected terminal wealth. Two risks sit inside the same control problem: mark-to-market inventory risk while the market is still open, and settlement risk on whatever position remains at resolution. The authors derive the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and characterize the unique optimal bid and the unique optimal ask. They transform the equation into the latent-belief space and use a fixed-point argument to prove that a classical solution exists and is unique, and they verify that the quoting strategy from that solution is optimal.

Their comparison is the worked example, and the abstract does not attach dollar figures, contract counts, or a named venue to it. Put a myopic quoter next to the optimal one. The myopic rule maximizes only the instantaneous expected mark-to-market profit — the edge on the next trade, marked at the new probability. The optimal rule also has to care how inventory behaves if the latent belief keeps moving, and how that inventory pays if the event resolves one way or the other. Walk the inputs the numerical analysis actually names, in the order the abstract gives them. Inventory changes the quote because a maker who is already long the yes side is less willing to buy more of it, and more willing to sell it, so the bid and the ask shift to shed that exposure. Market beliefs change the quote because the latent probability is the object being marked; a quote that ignores the current belief is quoting a different contract than the one on the screen. Time to resolution changes the quote because settlement risk dominates when the binary payoff is close, while mark-to-market risk has longer to compound when resolution is far. Risk aversion changes how hard those penalties bite. A more risk-averse maker posts a wider quote, or a more aggressive inventory skew, for the same book. Against the myopic benchmark, the paper finds that the optimal quoting strategy substantially improves downside protection while preserving most of its expected profit.

That result is about how a quote is chosen once a liquidity setting exists. It is not a finding that one side of a live contract is cheap. The abstract’s numerical analysis shows dependence on inventory, market beliefs, time to resolution, and risk aversion. It does not report a live-exchange test, a fee schedule, or a reward in dollars or points.

A second paper, Adaptive Liquidity in Prediction Markets via Online Learning, pulls a different lever: how much liquidity the mechanism itself supplies, rather than where one maker posts a bid and an ask. Prediction markets rely on liquidity to convert trades into informative prices. Existing mechanisms fix that liquidity before trading starts. The freeze locks a tradeoff between price responsiveness and worst-case loss, even though trading conditions are not stationary. A thick setting means one trade barely moves the probability, so the price looks calm for a mechanical reason. A thin setting means one trade swings it, and the mechanism’s worst-case loss is the other side of the same design choice. Order flow can change regime in the middle of a contract’s life. A liquidity number chosen on day one cannot follow that change.

Their mechanism does not lock one cost function. It mixes a family of cost-function markets through learnable weights, and the mix is still a single market. The abstract says the construction preserves no-arbitrage, a bounded worst-case loss, expressiveness, and a positive upside. Each round, a hybrid structural risk signal scores the tradeoff between price impact and inventory risk. That signal is the per-round objective. Standard online-learning algorithms then get switching-regret guarantees relative to the best sequence of liquidity regimes in hindsight, not merely the single best fixed regime. The guarantee is about regret against a changing benchmark. It is not a claim that the adaptive book beats every trader. Simulations in the paper show the weights shifting when order flow changes and when inventory changes. The mechanism moves liquidity across regimes rather than leaving the opening setting in place until resolution.

Neither abstract names a venue’s liquidity-rewards program, a points schedule, or a dollar subsidy paid to quoters. A live reward that pays traders to post is outside what these papers measure. What they do establish is why a quote has to be compensated, in the spread or in some other form. With nobody posting a bid and an ask, a trade has nowhere to land and the probability does not update. Liquidity set too high dulls that update. Liquidity set too low lets one order rewrite the probability and raises the mechanism’s worst-case loss. A listener who has that distinction reads a tight spread as a quote someone was willing, or paid, to post — not as a second, independent vote that the probability is right. Volume that is a maker trading against incoming orders is flow through a quote, not a pile of separate forecasts. A price near resolution is not the same object as a price months out: the first paper puts time to resolution inside the quoting decision, and the second puts a regime that can switch as order flow and inventory change. Neither result tells a listener which side of a contract to take. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Why a 95-Cent Contract Is Not Free Money

A prediction-market contract pays one dollar if a stated outcome occurs and nothing if it does not. The Globe and Mail describes the usual purchase price as somewhere between one cent and ninety-nine cents. That price, plus any fee, stays locked in the contract until it resolves. The most a correct call can return is the gap between what was paid and one dollar. A miss takes the whole amount paid. A contract priced at ninety-five cents leaves five cents as the entire possible gain on ninety-five cents that cannot be used elsewhere until resolution, and a wrong call loses the ninety-five cents. Those five cents are not interest. They are the remainder on an all-or-nothing claim.

The Globe's worked figures are Canadian access fees on U.S. event contracts, and they show how a flat charge eats the gap. The Canadian Investment Regulatory Organization permits authorized investment dealers to offer a limited selection of event contracts traded and cleared through certain exchanges and clearing houses regulated by the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Currently only Wealthsimple and Interactive Brokers Canada have that permission. Wealthsimple Predict gives clients a subset of contracts listed on Kalshi. Interactive Brokers Canada offers contracts on Kalshi and on its own exchange, ForecastEx.

Wealthsimple charges a fixed two U.S. cents per contract, up front. A contract priced at 42 U.S. cents therefore costs 44 U.S. cents. A correct call pays one U.S. dollar. A wrong call loses the 44 cents. Interactive Brokers Canada charges two U.S. cents per contract on Kalshi and one U.S. cent per contract on ForecastEx. U.S. investors trading directly on Kalshi pay a fee that varies with price and with the number of contracts, not a flat amount per contract.

Buying 100 contracts priced at one U.S. cent each would cost 63 U.S. cents in trading fees directly on Kalshi. The same 100 contracts through Wealthsimple Predict would cost two U.S. dollars in trading fees, more than three times as much. At 50 U.S. cents the gap narrows: 100 contracts would cost $1.75 in fees on Kalshi, against two U.S. dollars through Wealthsimple Predict. Two U.S. cents is 20 percent of a contract priced at 10 U.S. cents, and 4 percent of a contract priced at 50 U.S. cents. Benjamin Felix, chief investment officer and portfolio manager at PWL Capital, told the Globe that on a low-price contract the percentage fees get significant quickly.

Currency is a second charge, because the contracts are denominated in U.S. dollars. How It Works: Reading a 3% Contract

A contract here is a claim that pays one dollar if a stated event resolves yes and nothing if it resolves no. The price is what a buyer pays for that claim. A price of three cents is the market’s number for the yes side: three cents per dollar of payoff, which is a three percent implied probability. It is not a statement, in this show’s voice, that the event will or will not happen, and it is not a tip about which side to hold.

Read the two sides together. Yes at three cents means the complementary no claim is priced near ninety-seven cents, because the two payoffs add to one dollar if the market is quoting a single mutually exclusive question. The cheap side and the expensive side are the same disagreement seen from opposite chairs. Someone who buys yes ties up three cents until the venue resolves the contract. Someone who buys no ties up about ninety-seven cents for the same wait. Time is not free on either side, but it is not the same burden. Capital locked in the favorite cannot be used on another question until resolution, whenever the rules say that date is. A three-cent yes position immobilizes little cash and can sit for a long time; a ninety-seven-cent no position immobilizes almost the whole dollar for that same calendar.

Fees change the arithmetic more at the tail than in the middle, even when the fee schedule is the same. A charge taken from a three-cent price, or from the one-dollar settlement, is a large slice of what the long-shot buyer put in. The same charge is a smaller slice of a fifty-cent contract. So the number on the screen and the return a trader actually keeps are different objects, and the gap is wider when the displayed price is tiny. A three percent price is therefore not “a three percent forecast” in the way a fifty percent price can be read as a near-even split. At fifty cents, yes and no lock similar capital, and a small fee is a similar fraction of either side. At three cents, the displayed probability, the cash tied up, and the fee’s share of that cash point in different directions.

A low price also says less about calibration than a middle price does, for a structural reason rather than a slogan. Many rare events can each trade at a few cents. If buyers treat a cheap contract as a lottery ticket — a small stake for a dollar payoff — the price can sit above the frequency with which those events actually resolve yes, across a basket of long shots, without any one contract looking obviously wrong on its own. The reverse can happen if sellers of the cheap side demand extra compensation for the rare, total loss of the favorite’s stake. Either pattern means a three-cent print is a price under those pressures, not a clean frequency. A listener who wants the price as a forecast has to ask what else is in the number: how long capital is stuck, how large a fee is relative to three cents, and whether the question is one of many long shots that traders buy because the stake is small.

Nothing in that reading is a recommendation to buy the three-cent side, sell it, or treat either side as mispriced. The useful habit is narrower. When a board shows three percent, say the venue, say whether the money is real or play money, and remember that three cents buys a dollar only if yes resolves — and that ninety-seven cents is the other way of expressing the same quote.

How It Works: Reading a Wallet: How a Large Trade Gets Traced

A trade on a public blockchain is a record of an address sending value at a time, which is the whole of what "reading a wallet" starts from. The address is a string the chain already publishes. It is not a name, a passport, or a brokerage account. Anyone with the transaction hash can see the size, the timestamp, and the address that signed it. Reporters and researchers start there because the record does not depend on the venue handing over a customer file.

The next step is clustering, and it is weaker than a name. Analysts group addresses that fund one another, that move funds in a tight sequence, or that show up together around the same market. A cluster is a hypothesis that those addresses are operated together. It is not proof that one person sits behind them. An exchange deposit address, a shared treasury, or two people using one signer will look, on the chain, like a single actor. The inference stops where the funding path hits a service that pools customer funds, because the chain then shows the pool, not the customers.

The concrete case in today's material is not a named whale. It is a count of wallets. RG.org, citing the Anti-Corruption Data Collective's Gambling on Democracy update with data current to September thirtieth, reported that ten wallets control seventeen percent of all congressional volume on Polymarket. Read that sentence as a concentration statistic, not an identification. Ten addresses, or ten clusters the group treated as wallets, account for seventeen cents of every dollar in that congressional set on that venue. The finding does not say those ten wallets belong to ten people, or to one person, or to anyone with information the rest of the market lacks. It says the volume is not spread across thousands of equal accounts.

A second finding in the same update measures how little money it takes to push a displayed price. In markets with live order books as of September thirtieth, the group found that fifty-five point one percent could be moved five cents off their mid-price for less than one dollar, and that fewer than one percent required ten thousand dollars or more. How It Works: Fees, Spreads and the House Take

A quoted price is the terms of a trade, not the full cost of holding a view. On Choice Prediction Markets, a user sets a number from 0 to 100. A price of 60 means that user is putting the chance at 60 percent. The order executes only when another participant takes the opposite side at that price. Pending orders can be amended or cancelled before they match. When the market resolves, the winning side settles at 100 and the losing side at zero. The gross gap between a price of 60 and a settlement of 100 is 40 points if that side wins; the 60 is lost if it does not. That is the contract's own arithmetic. It does not make 60 the correct probability, and it is not a coin flip a user can enter for free, because nothing trades until someone accepts the other side.

Research on transaction costs in other markets shows why the gap between a quote and a completed trade changes what the quote is worth.

A 2007 paper in The Journal of Finance used a complete record of U.S. over-the-counter secondary trades in corporate bonds and estimated average transaction costs by trade size for every bond that traded more than nine times between January 2003 and January 2005. Costs fell significantly as trade size rose. Highly rated bonds, recently issued bonds, and bonds close to maturity were cheaper to trade than other bonds. Bonds with transparent trade prices had lower costs, and costs dropped when the TRACE system began publicly disseminating those prices. The paper's sequence is the worked example: restrict the sample to bonds with more than nine trades, estimate cost against size, compare bonds whose prices are public with bonds whose prices are not, then look at the same bonds after TRACE publication starts. The result in the paper is that public traders benefit from price transparency. An opaque book is a more expensive book.

A 2020 paper in The Journal of Finance found that the index credit-default-swap market stayed two-tiered after Dodd-Frank, despite regulatory efforts to promote all-to-all trading. Transaction costs were higher for dealer-to-client trades than for interdealer trades, and the paper says the difference is explained by the higher, largely permanent price impact of client trades. Most interdealer trades were liquidity-motivated and used low-cost, low-immediacy protocols. Dealer-to-client trades were not anonymous; they almost always improved on contemporaneous executable interdealer quotes, and dealers appeared to price-discriminate based on the perceived price impact of the trade. The paper suggests the structure follows from client trades being relatively infrequent, large, and differentially informed. What looks like a house take can be the cost of moving the price, not only a posted commission.

A 2023 paper in The Journal of Finance is the dispute over how those costs should be scored. Ignoring transaction costs, the Hou, Xue, and Zhang q-factor model and the Barillas and Shanken six-factor model have high maximum squared Sharpe ratios and small alphas across 205 anomalies. They do not come close to spanning the mean-variance frontier a trader can actually reach. Accounting for transaction costs, the Fama and French five-factor model has a significantly higher squared Sharpe ratio than either alternative, and variations that use cash profitability perform better still. The paper's claim is that tests which skip costs are biased toward models that employ high-cost factors.

A quote near 50 on a yes-or-no contract is still a price that has to clear a book, with settlement at 100 or zero, and the research says the cost of clearing can be large enough to change which model looks right. How It Works: Can AI Forecast?

A mention market is a contract that resolves on whether a specified keyword is spoken during a future public event. The paper "Forecasting Future Language: Context Design for Mention Markets" asks how the input to a large language model should be built when the forecast target is that kind of contract, including whether the live contract price is handed to the model as a prior.

The experiments use earnings-call mention markets: will a company say a specified keyword on its upcoming call. The authors vary two things. One is which text the model sees, news, prior earnings-call transcripts, or both. The other is how market probability, meaning the prediction-market contract price, is used. Market-Conditioned Prompting, or MCP, explicitly treats that price as a prior and instructs the model to update the prior with textual evidence, rather than re-predicting the base rate from scratch.

Walk the design without a dollar figure, because the abstract does not publish one. The contract already carries an implied probability that the keyword will be said. The model is given prior transcripts, news, or both. Under MCP it revises the market prior in light of that text instead of throwing the price out and starting a fresh base rate. MixMCP then mixes the market probability with that MCP forecast, dampening the model's posterior update with the market prior so the final number is not the model's unconstrained revision.

The paper reports three results. Richer context consistently improves forecasting performance. MCP, which updates the market probability rather than discarding it, yields better-calibrated forecasts. A mixture of the market probability and MCP outperforms the market baseline, and by dampening the model's update with the market prior it yields more robust predictions than either the market or the language model alone. The abstract also notes that recent work shows large language models can generate forecasts competitive with human forecasters. This paper does not re-run that comparison. Its claim is about context design on mention markets.

A second paper, "Making Alternative Data Work: Context-Augmented LLMs for Financial Forecasting," uses prediction markets in a different role. They are one channel of alternative data, with consumer transactions and web traffic, brought in to forecast a firm's future financial performance. The authors propose a two-agent framework. The first agent identifies the firms for which each alternative-data channel is likely to be informative. The second predicts revenue using firm- and channel-specific context. They evaluate the framework across four commercial alternative data channels. In those experiments, adding alternative data in context alongside other financial information improves the language model's forecasting relative to either source alone, and those forecasts are more accurate than those of standard forecasting baselines.

The limits are stated in the papers themselves. Firm-level alternative data often have limited historical coverage, are relevant only to specific prediction targets or subsets of firms, and arrive through heterogeneous channels, which is why they are hard to drop into a conventional forecast. On mention markets, the weaker design the authors test is a model that ignores the contract price and re-predicts the base rate. MixMCP exists because an undamped model update was less robust, in their tests, than a forecast that kept the market prior in the mix. No named outside critic is quoted in either abstract.

Read the contract price, after that, as a prior with a job, not as a verdict and not as a poll. In the mention-market design, the price is the starting probability, new text is evidence that may move it, and the more accurate setup in their tests was the one that refused to throw the price away. That is a description of a research design, not a reason to buy, sell, or trade any contract.

How It Works: Are Betting Markets Efficient?

A betting market is efficient, in the sense researchers use, when the price already contains what a simple trading rule would need, so that rule cannot be expected to make money once the bookmaker's margin is paid. The test is not whether the price looks wise. The test is whether a rule written down in advance, applied to old prices, still shows a profit large enough that chance is a poor explanation.

Spread betting, also called handicap betting, makes the test easy to see. The book sets a point spread. A bet wins if the side beats that number, not merely if the side wins the game. If the spread already summarizes what bettors know, backing favourites, backing underdogs, or chasing teams that just covered should not pay after the vig. A 2022 review in the Journal of Sports Economics gathered more than six hundred implementations of such strategies from the long literature on this microstructure. The finding is two-sided. Predictable glitches show up: prices are not a perfect photograph of true chances. Those glitches are too small to exploit for a profit, which is what an efficient market is supposed to look like once costs are counted. Misperception and efficiency are not opposites in that result. People can be systematically a little wrong, and the wrongness can still be smaller than the fee for acting on it.

The same review changes how a listener should read a paper that claims an edge. Finance has started to treat a result as worth attention only when the test statistic clears a high bar, a z-score whose absolute value is greater than three, because researchers try so many cuts of the data that some will look special by luck. That control, the authors write, is becoming mainstream in finance and has not yet become mainstream in betting-market research. Six hundred strategy tests are exactly the setting where a lucky winner should be expected. A single pretty backtest, without that hurdle, is not evidence that the market left money on the table.

English football bookmakers, studied in the Journal of Prediction Markets for the seasons from 2002 to 2006, show a related pattern with a different shape. Home odds and away odds carried a positive favourite-longshot bias. Draw odds went the other way, a negative longshot bias. Separately, the authors identify a draw bias: betting the draw returned more than betting home or away. They also investigated rules that use the disagreement among bookmakers' prices.

Those two papers do not license a bet. One says apparent errors in spread markets failed the profit test across hundreds of tries. The other says football prices in those four seasons were not symmetric: draws behaved differently from home and away, and the favourite-longshot pattern was not the same for every outcome. A listener who knows that reads a contract price as a clearing price among whoever chose to trade, not as a poll and not as a finished forecast. A published bias is a description of old prices. It is not a rule that survives the next fee, the next sample, or the next six hundred attempts.

How It Works: A Short History of Election Betting

Between 1868 and 1940, large and often well-organized markets took bets on U.S. presidential elections. A person who thought a candidate would win could buy a claim that paid if that candidate won, and a person who disagreed could sell it. The price of that claim was the forecast. It was not a poll. It was the level at which people in the market were willing to risk money.

A 2004 article in the Journal of Economic Perspectives studies those markets and sets out four findings. The market did a remarkable job forecasting elections in an era before scientific polling. It was fairly efficient, despite the limited information of participants and active attempts to manipulate the odds. The markets disappeared largely because scientific polls rose and because other forms of gambling became more available. The paper also discusses lessons that history holds for the present. The abstract does not itemize those lessons.

The limit is already in the second finding. The paper calls the market fairly efficient even while recording attempts to manipulate the odds. A price can be leaned on and still land near the result if other traders take the other side. It can also miss when the people trading share a blind spot. The abstract does not measure how hard a price had to be pushed before it broke. For a reader now, the useful distinction is simple. A market price is a forecast only in the sense that people risked money at that level, under rules for who gets paid. Those presidential markets show that such a price can track an election before scientific polls exist, and that the markets themselves can disappear once polls and other gambling are easier to find. They do not turn any single price today into a verdict. Source: doi.org

How It Works: Why Economists Care: Prices as Public Information

A binary event contract pays a fixed amount if a stated event occurs and nothing if it does not. In a market where traders can buy and sell that contract, the price is the point at which the people willing to take the other side have stopped arguing with their money. Anyone can read it. They do not have to pay the researchers, open the filings or sit in the room where the information was gathered. That is the public-information case: the forecast is a byproduct of trade, posted continuously, and free to the next reader.

The cost of producing that byproduct is the part the slogan skips. A 2020 paper, Timely Information from Prediction Markets, treats prediction markets as tools that elicit and aggregate beliefs from strategic agents, then points at a waste channel: agents may exhaust the social welfare by competing to be first to update the market. The design problem it sets is a trade-off between how quickly information is aggregated and how much that information costs. To maximise social welfare, the market has to pay people to acquire information quickly enough to be useful, but not faster, and more expensively, than necessary, and it has to get them to report truthfully and on time. The paper studies two settings — information that is valuable only before a deadline, and information whose value falls as time passes — and says it uses theorems and simulations to demonstrate the mechanisms. It does not hand a reader a live accuracy score.

A separate research design asks what changes when institutional liquidity shows up. What Happens When Institutional Liquidity Enters Prediction Markets defines a market-quality lens, separates channels through which that liquidity can enter, and maps identification problems in live venue data. Its empirical piece is a synthetic microstructure laboratory, offered as a proof of concept for the measurement pipeline, not as a settlement of the live question. The lesson it draws is narrow on purpose: market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives and automation do not have to work through the same channel; average liquidity gains do not have to become equal gains for every trader; and the sharpest welfare losses are most likely in shock states, when slower takers receive the least pass-through of tighter quoted markets.

Read a posted price, then, as a public number with a private production cost. A tighter quote is not evidence that the slowest reader captured the improvement, and a few minutes of extra speed is not evidence that the race to post it was worth what it burned. Source: arxiv.org Source: arxiv.org
