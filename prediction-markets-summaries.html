How It Works: Base Rates: Where a Good Forecast Starts

A base rate is the frequency of an outcome in a class of similar past cases, counted before anyone argues about the case now on the table. Using it means starting with that frequency and then adjusting for what is specific. Skipping it — treating the story in front of you as if the class had no history — is base rate neglect.

An AAPG Bulletin paper walks through the miss with a set of real wells. Lundin Petroleum drilled 25 exploration wells on the Norwegian Continental Shelf from 2011 through 2015. The relevant base rates were the historical exploration success rate in that area and the sizes of fields already found there. Against those frequencies, the paper says Lundin explorers consistently and significantly underestimated geological probability of success and overestimated success-case volumes. The mechanism is sequential. First, write down how often similar wells hit and how large similar finds were. Second, write the prospect-specific geology beside that, not instead of it. Third, notice that a portfolio of case-only estimates will not match the historical distribution even if each write-up sounds careful. The author argues explorers ignore those frequencies because the frequencies do not feel like they are about this prospect, and that the miss shrinks when the historical rates are integrated into the prospect evaluation rather than left in a separate file.

A 2024 paper on robust forecast aggregation, posted on arXiv, asks what happens when the people you are combining are themselves neglecting the base rate. Two experts each see a private signal and each state a probability for a binary world state. A parameter lambda, from zero to one, measures how much base-rate information they fold in: zero is complete ignorance of it, one is perfect Bayesian updating. The score is worst-case regret against an omniscient benchmark, the standard taken from Arieli, Babichenko and Smorodinsky in 2018. The result is a V-shape. Regret does not fall in a straight line as experts become better Bayesians. An intermediate lambda, short of one, can produce lower worst-case regret than perfect posteriors. The reason is about the aggregator, not about the world. The aggregator does not know the experts' information structure. Extreme, fully updated probabilities can be harder to combine safely across the worst case the aggregator has to fear. The authors also propose an aggregator meant to keep regret low when lambda itself is unknown, and they say they ran an empirical test of the neglect model. The abstract does not report that test's measurements, so the finding to keep is the V-shape, not a claimed improvement on live markets.

A Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience study separates who resists the miss. On the lawyer-engineer problem, the Cognitive Reflection Test predicted performance on conflict items, where the base rate and the stereotype point opposite ways. Short-term memory predicted the nonconflict items. In the scanner, conflict items brought greater activation in the anterior cingulate cortex even when the person still answered with the stereotype. Higher reflection scores went with greater posterior cingulate activation on those conflict items, a region the authors tie to shifting attention between outside cues and an internal check. That is one classic task, not a trading log.

Reference class forecasting is the project-management version of the same start. A Project Management Journal paper compared it with forecasts built by tracing the future path of the specific project, including methods based on Monte Carlo simulation and earned value management. On real project data, forecasts taken from the actual outcomes of similar past projects performed best for both cost and time.

The dispute is inside that literature, not a claim that base rates never help. The AAPG paper treats neglect as a correctable error. The arXiv result says that, for an aggregator who does not know how the experts are informed, a perfect Bayesian posterior is not automatically the input that minimizes worst-case regret. A listener reading a market price can hold both. The price is a case-level number from whoever is trading. The base rate is the hit rate of the class that case belongs to. If those two are far apart, the useful question is which class the contract actually resolves into, and whether the traders started from that class or from the story. Neither comparison is a reason to take a side. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Extremizing: Pushing an Average Away From the Middle

An average of probabilities is a biased instrument in a specific direction, toward one half, according to the 2014 paper in Decision Analysis titled Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More Extreme. The authors describe the common method as a simple weighted average of many individuals' estimates of an uncertain event. They argue those aggregated probabilities correspond more closely to the real world if they are transformed by being pushed closer to zero or to one.

The first distorting factor is compression of the probability scale at the two ends. Random error tends to push the average probability toward one half, because error that would have fallen beyond the end of the scale cannot, and so it accumulates on the inside. That effect does not occur for the median forecast. Arguably, the authors add, it does not occur for the mean of the log odds of the individual forecasts either.

The second factor affects the mean, the median, and the mean of the log odds. Forecasters take into account their individual ignorance of the total body of information available. Individual confidence in the direction of a probability judgment, high or low, thus fails to take into account the wisdom of crowds that results from combining different evidence available to different judges. Each person shades inward for a gap that the group, taken together, may already have filled.

The paper's first finding is that the same transformation function can approximately eliminate both distorting effects, with different parameters for the mean and for the median. The second finding is that use of the median can help distinguish the two effects, because the scale-compression problem is identified with the average and not the median, while the ignorance problem remains in the median. The abstract does not report the parameter values, so the size of either push is not a figure available from the source.

A weighted average of individual probabilities is pulled toward one half by error that bunches inside the ends of the scale. The median of those same forecasts drops that pull. Both still include each forecaster's discount for information they personally lacked. One transformation then pushes the aggregate toward zero or one, with a different parameter for the mean than for the median. The extra push the mean requires is how the authors separate compression from ignorance, and the median's remaining push is the ignorance effect. They call the fix approximate.

The paper itself qualifies the log-odds escape hatch with the word arguably, and it qualifies the cure as approximate rather than exact. The second factor is stated only for the case in which different judges hold different evidence. A 2019 Decision Analysis paper on probability forecasts and their combination takes up miscalibration, dependence among forecasters, and the choice of an evaluation measure, and it connects aggregation to decision making. It does not measure the 2014 transformation, so it neither confirms the parameters nor disputes them. Dependence is the neighboring problem: if the forecasts are not drawing on different evidence, the wisdom-of-crowds premise in the 2014 account is not met.

A listener should not treat a contract price as the average this paper repairs. A price is not a weighted mean of independent forecasts, and it is not their median. Nothing in the paper says to push a live price closer to zero or one, and that would be a trading rule, which this is not. Near one half, a forecast average may be compressed or privately cautious; a traded price may simply be unresolved. Near an extreme, a traded price is not thereby a corrected poll. The paper's claim stops at aggregated forecasts. Source: doi.org Source: doi.org

How It Works: Reading the Market's Rate Expectations

A federal funds futures price is a traded rate, not a ready-made chance that the Federal Reserve will move at a named meeting. The quote is the market’s price for the average federal funds rate over a future month. Turning that price into the probability of a decision means peeling off a term premium and then mapping what remains onto the discrete choices the committee actually faces.

A 2016 note in the Federal Reserve’s FEDS Notes series looks at term premiums at the very front of the curve. It uses federal funds futures together with the Desk’s Survey of Primary Dealers and its Survey of Market Participants, and it treats the premium that gets subtracted as an assumption, not as a number the futures price announces by itself. The arithmetic runs like this, and it only yields a probability if each input is the right one. Start with the futures-implied rate for the month that contains the meeting. Subtract the rate that would prevail if the committee stood still. What remains mixes compensation for bearing rate risk with the chance of a different policy setting. Subtract the premium being assumed. Divide what is left by the size of the step the committee is choosing among. Change the premium and the probability moves even though the futures price did not.

Two findings show why that last step is not bookkeeping. A 2009 paper in the Journal of Money, Credit and Banking says many studies estimate the risk premium on federal funds futures by assuming that average forecast errors are zero, or that survey forecasts are good proxies for expectations. Those assumptions can fail: an unanticipated declining trend in the funds rate breaks the zero-average-error claim, and survey respondents can behave strategically, so a survey is not a clean stand-in for the market’s expectation. Premiums estimated under those assumptions can be biased. With a different method, the authors find that the premiums have often been negative since 2000, a pattern they describe as generally consistent with the negative betas observed in the 2000s. A negative premium means the futures rate can sit below the expected future rate, so reading the raw futures price as the expected rate would understate the chance of higher policy settings.

A 2005 paper in the Journal of Futures Markets shows a different object once options, not just futures, are available. Options on federal funds futures began trading in March 2003. Because the Federal Open Market Committee chooses among discrete outcomes, the implied probability density can be recovered with ordinary least squares, rather than with the heavier methods used when an underlying price is treated as continuous, as with equities, foreign exchange, or commodity futures. The same estimation can recover a density for one meeting or a joint density for several meetings, and it can impose restrictions on the probabilities within a meeting and across meetings. The authors use those recovered probabilities to assess how data releases and Federal Reserve communication change the perceived likelihood of actual policy outcomes.

The dispute sits in the premium, not in the futures quote. The 2009 paper objects to two shortcuts — zero average forecast errors, and surveys as proxies — used when the premium is not observed directly. The 2016 note does not crown one premium assumption as the true one. It asks which assumptions are plausible given futures prices and the two Desk surveys.

An event contract on the same meeting is a different instrument. It pays a fixed amount if a stated decision occurs and nothing otherwise, so its price is already scaled as a probability of that statement, before anyone subtracts a term premium. Fees, capital, and a thin book can still move that price. A futures-implied chance of a hike exists only after someone names a premium and a menu of rate steps. Hearing both numbers on the same morning, a listener can ask which object was priced: a contract that pays if the committee does a named thing, or a rate future that still needs a premium assumption before it becomes a probability.

How It Works: Noise Traders: Why Uninformed Money Matters

In that model, governance improves the liquidity of a company's shares only when the market also contains noise traders the authors describe as semi-informed. How strong the effect is depends on how much those traders take part in transactions. Governance and that participation have what the model calls a synergistic effect on liquidity. Take the semi-informed flow away, and the liquidity channel the authors are tracking does not appear.

The step that matters is the counterparty. A better-informed trader can act on a signal only if someone else will take the other side. If everyone is waiting on the same information, there is no trade, and the governance improvement never shows up as liquidity. The 2020 paper is a model of listed stocks, not a measurement of a prediction-market contract. What it isolates is still readable on a contract: money that is uninformed, or only partly informed, is not only a source of error. In this account it is a condition for anyone else to trade at all.

A 2015 field study in the International Journal of Financial Management looks for that mix in Indian equities. The authors use daily trading data plus intra-day prices, volumes, and trade times at one-day and five-day intervals for the Nifty-Fifty firms listed on both the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange, and they take market returns from the NSE Nifty and the BSE Sensex. They ask whether variation in returns includes noise-trader risk, and whether informed traders' short-run arbitrage pushes them into long-short positions that hedge it. The paper argues that noise has a systematic component and a firm-specific component, and that both vary over time. Those components include what the authors separate as idiosyncratic shocks and noise shocks. At lag periods, a long-short position across these markets can hedge the fundamental systematic shock and the fundamental firm-specific shock, and may detach the noise shock from them. Once the same stocks are traded on long-short horizons, the long-short return is what exposes the noise that runs across names. Set next to the same exercise two years earlier, the abstract's reported pattern is directional rather than a published coefficient: intra-day returns from the one-day and five-day data impound significant noise; daily returns show high exposure; weekly returns show moderate exposure. In the GARCH models, the conditional volatility of those long-short returns shows that the time-varying idiosyncratic noise is highly persistent when noise traders are present. The study's closing claim is that, during the trading day, prices impound information and noise together.

A 2016 paper in Qualitative Research in Financial Markets pushes on the habit of treating every individual trader as a dumb noise trader. Experienced researchers and practitioners rated the mathematical and verbal reasoning demands of investment tasks used in earlier studies. On that assessment, individual investors can perform a number of complex cognitive actions, especially those that demand higher-order verbal reasoning, and they look more limited on tasks that demand greater mathematical reasoning. The authors add that the math-heavy tasks are the ones considered more relevant to performance. They also limit their own result. The measurement is indirect, so they will not claim a definite score for retail competence; a direct test would be a laboratory experiment, and they say that data was not available for the retail investors in view. They could analyze ten tasks, which limits statistical power and blocks a deeper deductive test. Their practical notes stay on disclosure: illustrate key investor documents mostly in words, tell people what studies of this kind found, and offer online math practice built for individual investors. That is not a trading instruction.

The three papers do not draw one portrait, and forcing one would misread them. The 2020 piece is a model, and its noise trader is semi-informed, not empty of information. The 2015 study finds noise that stays persistent inside the trading day in one equity market during a recovery; it does not size the effect on an event contract. The 2016 authors dispute the undifferentiated "dumb noise trader" label, then mark the narrow base under their own dispute: ten tasks, no direct performance test. A listener can still read a contract price differently. A move can be information, or it can be the other side of an order that was never about the fact on the screen. In the 2020 account, liquidity is partly supplied by that second group. In the 2015 account, the noise need not be gone by the close. In the 2016 assessment, the people in that flow are uneven — more capable on verbal reasoning than on the math that performance is said to require — not uniformly lost. None of this identifies which side of a live contract is the informed side. It says a price is a trade, and a trade needs a counterparty who may not share the reason for being there.

How It Works: Who Wins and Who Loses: What Studies Find on Trader Returns

A price can line up with what traders already believed, and the people who held the belief that later paid can still be the ones who made the money. That is the mechanism in a Journal of Prediction Markets study of private information, overconfidence, and trader returns. The setting was four real-money prediction markets whose contracts paid on a movie's box office. Before any market opened, every trader submitted a detailed forecast of that movie's performance. The private information was self-generated. No one randomly handed a group a perfect tip, which is how many lab experiments create "insiders." Accuracy could be scored only after the box office result existed.

The authors treated overconfidence as the thing to look for in behavior, not as a slogan. Volume and the timing of trading were consistent with overconfidence: people who had done the analytical work traded as if that work were more decisive than it was. In three of the four markets, contract prices were consistent with the prior-information equilibrium. The price looked like the information traders had walked in with. In those same three markets, traders whose forecast was associated with the winning contract had significantly higher returns than traders whose forecasts pointed at another contract. In the fourth market, there were no significant differences in returns across those groups.

Those are the only splits the paper's account gives, so the worked example stops there. Four markets. Three prices sat where the prior information said they should, and in those three the traders tied to the winning contract earned significantly more. One market produced no significant return gap at all. A listener looking for a single win rate, a dollar profit, or a share of gains captured by a small group will not find those figures here. The authors' conclusion is narrow, and it is not a strategy: when traders are overconfident and trade accordingly, there can be value to being better informed, if the information is accurate.

A second paper, in the Journal of Futures Markets, splits profit rather than forecasts. It examines local traders on the floor of the Sydney Futures Exchange and decomposes their income into liquidity profits and position-taking profits. Locals made significant position-taking profits. The ability to earn those position-taking profits was positively related to order-flow information, and negatively related to exogenous information, to the locals' own liquidity profits, and to the length of a local's inventory cycle. The authors characterize those locals as active informed traders, not as a desk that only collected the spread for standing in the market.

That "informed" reading of order flow is disputed in a neighboring market. Another Journal of Futures Markets paper uses option order imbalance on Shanghai Stock Exchange 50 ETF options as a proxy for market makers' inventory pressure. Call order imbalances were contemporaneously positively associated with underlying returns, put order imbalances negatively associated, and both were followed by rapid reversals. The authors tie the reversal to temporary price pressure from delta-hedging. Two empirical scenarios, they report, reinforce that the imbalance reflected inventory risk rather than informed trading. Flow that looks like someone who knows the future can be a hedge being laid off.

A listener who treats a price as a forecast can still ask which of these mechanisms made it. In the movie markets, the price often matched beliefs traders already held, and the traders whose belief matched the outcome made more, except in the one market where that gap did not appear. On a futures floor, a meaningful slice of local profit was position-taking tied to order flow, and it shrank when outside news arrived or when inventory sat longer. In the options study, the same-looking imbalance was a market maker's hedge, and it reversed. None of that names a side to take. It is a reason a contract price can say something about an event and still say very little about whether any particular trader is the one who gets paid.

How It Works: Whose Belief Is the Price? The Debate Over What a Price Averages

A binary event contract pays a fixed sum if a stated event happens and pays nothing if it does not. Scale that promise to one dollar and the trading price is a number between zero and one. The argument over what that number averages is an argument about who is allowed to pull it, and with how much force. It is not an argument about which side of any live contract is cheap.

Picture a room of traders, each holding a private chance. Anyone who thinks the event is more likely than the price wants to buy. Anyone who thinks it is less likely wants to sell. Each trade nudges the price toward the side that just acted. Trading can stop when the people still in the market no longer find it worth moving. Under a strict set of assumptions — traders care only about expected payout, they can buy or sell as much as they like, and everyone with a view can reach the book — that stopping point is a weighted average of the beliefs in the room. The weights are not equal votes. A trader with more money behind a view pulls harder. In that narrow case, a contract at 60 cents is not a poll of heads. It is a money-weighted balance. Reading it as a 60 percent chance is a reading of that balance, not of a show of hands.

The critique is that those assumptions do the work, and they often fail. A risk-averse trader will not stake enough to drag the price all the way to their belief, because a wrong bet hurts more than the expected gain feels worth. The price can then sit away from the average belief even when everyone can see the gap. A budget limit does the same job: a trader who is out of money stops pulling, whatever they think. Access does it too. A book closed to people in a given state or country averages only the beliefs of people allowed to trade. Hedging adds another pull. Someone who loses money if the event happens may buy the contract as insurance, not because they think the event is likely. That purchase lifts the price without lifting their stated chance.

Replies to that critique do not deny the gap. They ask when it is large. One line of argument says that if many small traders can take the other side of a hedger, insurance demand gets absorbed and the price moves back toward people trading the event itself. Another says the gap shrinks when the contract is a tiny slice of each trader’s wealth, because risk aversion bites less on a small stake, and widens when the stake is large relative to what a trader can lose. Neither reply restores a simple average of opinions. Both treat the distance between price and average belief as a fact about constraints — risk, budget, access, and why people are in the market.

How It Works: Why Traders Hold Losing Positions Too Long

The disposition effect is a split in how a position gets closed: winners are sold early, and losers are held. On an event contract, that split is a trader cashing a contract that has moved in their favor while leaving a contract that has moved against them untouched. The quoted price is the price of contracts people are willing to trade. A loser nobody will sell does not revise that quote.

A second frame sits on top of the single position. A Journal of Finance paper finds that the disposition effect for a stock weakens when the portfolio is at a gain, and is large when the portfolio is at a loss. The authors call that pattern the portfolio-driven disposition effect. They find it in four independent settings: U.S. archival data, Chinese archival data, a U.S. experiment, and a Chinese experiment. It survives controls for extreme returns, portfolio rebalancing, tax considerations, and investor heterogeneity. The evidence they offer is that investors keep one mental frame for the stock and another for the portfolio, and that the two frames together generate the pattern.

When the portfolio is at a gain, the tendency to sell the winner and hold the loser weakens. When the portfolio is at a loss, that tendency is large. The contract most in need of a sale, if new evidence has turned against it, is the loser. That is the contract the pattern holds onto, and the hold is largest when the whole book is down. The winner is the contract the pattern releases, and releasing a winner does less to pull the quote toward bad news the holder has not traded.

A Frontiers in Psychology experiment with 193 professional traders complicates the idea that the pattern is always a mistake. The design was within-subject: the same traders decided on mean-reverting securities and on securities that do not mean-revert. Before any intervention, their disposition-effect trades pointed in the direction that aligned with profit maximization. On a mean-reverting security, selling into strength and holding a loser can be the rational trade, because the price is expected to come back. A simple informational intervention that made the disposition effect salient changed the level of the effect on both kinds of security. That intervention improved trader returns when the decisions were on non-mean-reverting securities. It had a negative impact on returns when traders used it for mean-reverting securities. A nudge can be large, and it helps only when the security behaves the way the nudge assumes.

On Xueqiu, a Chinese social-investment platform, a separate paper matches post data to actual trading data. Social-media information significantly reduces the disposition effect, and the reduction runs through negative information. Shown negative information, individual investors adjust positions in a more rational way, in that paper's account. Investment experience, users followed, region, and gender all change how well the information works. That study is not about event contracts. It is about whether the information in front of a holder can loosen the hold.

Set the three findings next to a price someone is reading as a forecast. The Journal of Finance result says a losing book makes the hold on losers larger, so the contracts that would register bad news are the ones least likely to trade. The Frontiers result says that hold is not automatically an error: if the question mean-reverts, keeping the loser can be the profit-maximizing trade, and a warning that pushes a sale can cut returns. The Xueqiu result says negative information is the channel that gets holders to adjust, so a price can sit still until the bad news is in front of the people holding the losing side, not merely until the news exists. A listener who knows that is reading the trades that cleared, minus the losing positions people would not close. On a question that comes back, stillness can be sense. On a question that does not, stillness can be the missing sell. Source: doi.org Source: doi.org Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Forecasting an Outbreak With a Market

Seasonal influenza forecasting starts from a running count of infections and asks two different questions: when the wave crests, and how big it is. The systems in today's papers do that by fitting a model to past weeks and then updating it as new observations arrive, the way a weather model takes in a fresh reading. They are not exchanges. No abstract here describes a contract, a trader, or a payout.

The worked example with a stated lead time is the New York City study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. For the 2003 through 2008 seasons, researchers assimilated weekly web-based estimates of local infection rates and, after each update, produced a retrospective ensemble of forecasts rather than a single line. They report that skillful real-time predictions of peak timing could be made more than seven weeks before the actual peak. Confidence was not a separate slogan; it was the spread of the ensemble. A narrow spread meant the timing runs agreed. A wide spread meant they did not.

Melbourne shows the split between timing and size. Using Google Flu Trends for 2006 through 2014, a bootstrap particle filter and a mechanistic infection model called the epidemic peak accurately four to six weeks ahead. The magnitude of the peak and the overall burden were much harder to predict. The team chose among hundreds of observation models by treating forecasts like receiver operating characteristic curves. In Fuzhou, SARIMA, Prophet, Holt-Winters, and XGBoost were fit to cases from January 2013 through December 2021 and tested on 2022. XGBoost had the lowest mean squared error, root mean squared error, and mean absolute error on the training set and the test set. The case series was seasonal and cyclical, with an upward trend and large swings. Dante, described in Nature Communications, learns spatial, temporal, and surveillance structure instead of prescribing it, and it issues forecasts that stay coherent across state, regional, and national scales. It beat a Dynamic Bayesian Model for nearly all spatial units, flu seasons, geographic scales, and targets in a retrospective comparison, and it placed first in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's prospective 2018/19 FluSight challenge in the national competition, the regional competition, and the state competition.

Where the work is disputed is in what a good score is allowed to decide. The Melbourne authors say peak size and total burden do not come along for free when timing is skillful. CDC scientists, in a BMC Public Health paper on FluSight challenges hosted since the 2013-14 season, write that federal, state, and local officials need to understand how a forecast was made, how to interpret it, and how it performed in the past before it is built into a response. Heterogeneous surveillance, which Dante's authors flag, means a clean national curve can hide a thin state.

A listener reading a market on an outbreak can borrow that discipline without pretending these papers scored a venue. Name the target: peak week is not peak height, and peak height is not total burden. Name the lead time: more than seven weeks in the New York exercise is a different claim from four to six weeks in Melbourne. And do not treat one percentage as a measurement unless an ensemble, a past error, or both are visible. A contract price collapses a crowd into one number. These studies say that number is usable only for the question it actually answered, on the horizon where it actually had skill.

How It Works: Prediction Markets You Already Know: Futures Prices as Forecasts

A futures price is an agreement to exchange a commodity at a date that has not arrived. The research program treats that price as a forecast of the later spot: wait out the horizon, then ask whether today’s futures price was a systematically biased guess at the spot that printed. The object being tested is a later price, or the direction of that price, not a yes-or-no event that settles at a fixed payoff.

A 2009 neural-network study of crude oil walks through that test in days. The authors first built a benchmark from lagged, pre-processed spot prices and found a dynamic model of 13 lags the best short-term benchmark for direction. They then added pre-processed futures prices for one, two, three and four months to maturity, one maturity at a time and then all together. Directional accuracy was 78 percent one day ahead, 66 percent two days ahead and 53 percent three days ahead. In every experiment that included futures as an input, those prices held information on spot direction that the lagged spot alone did not.

An IMF working paper in 2011 scaled the same question to ten commodity futures and horizons out to two years. In-sample tests rejected efficient markets. Out of sample, the forecast from the futures market was hard to beat. Performance did not depend on the slope of the futures curve, against models that treat that slope as a signal, and it did not depend on whether prices were in an upswing or a downswing.

A 2013 paper in the Journal of Futures Markets split the record by group. Precious and base metals failed most tests of unbiasedness and were poor predictors of later price changes. Energy and agricultural futures fared much better. The authors found little evidence that liquidity explained the gap, and they documented a broad decline in the predictive content of commodity futures prices since the early 2000s.

A 2019 paper in Management Science separates fitting the futures curve from forecasting the spot. Pricing models can match the term structure of futures prices and its dynamics and still fail to generate accurate true distributions of spot prices. The authors conclude that reasonable expected spot curves need analysts’ forecasts, used alone or jointly with futures. Using both, rather than forecasts alone, does not change the short and medium expected spot curve by much, but long-term estimates differ significantly. Adding forecasts to futures, rather than using futures alone, likewise leaves the short and medium futures curve mostly intact and moves the long-term futures estimates.

Later work both extends the machinery and marks where an edge dies. A 2015 study explained the crude-oil futures curve with a dynamic Nelson-Siegel model and forecast it with a focused time-delay neural network on 24 years of prices, through recessions and crises. Forecasts one, three, six and twelve months ahead were significantly more accurate than the benchmark models, with the lowest errors across maturities. A 2024 paper trained a convolutional network on aerial images of winter hard red wheat and on cloud cover, as a proxy for yield, to forecast the wheat futures price 20 days ahead. It reports that positive alpha in a short window is possible when the algorithm and the data are distinctive, and that performance can deteriorate quickly once the imagery is easy to obtain or the strategy becomes crowded.

Read a futures price as a forecast of a later spot at a named horizon, and check whether that record has held out of sample. Do not read it as a probability on a contract that pays a fixed sum if a stated event occurs and nothing if it does not. Storage, interest and the shape of the curve can put the futures price far from today’s spot without making it a number between zero and one. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: The Marginal Trader: Why a Few People Can Set the Price

A prediction-market price is the terms of the next trade, not a head count of everyone who holds an opinion. The account willing to buy the next contract, or to sell it, is the marginal trader. Everyone else can watch, post, or sit on a position and leave the quote where it is. If that willing trader is careful and active, the quote can look like a forecast even when most accounts never update a belief. If that trader is one desk in a quiet book, the quote is that desk's number.

The Iowa Electronic Markets give a worked example of how thickness changes the number a reader should trust. On September 29, a thinly traded vote-share market forecast a 9-percentage-point Democratic popular-vote margin for the 2024 U.S. presidential election. A thickly traded winner-takes-all market, read the same day, priced an 85.7 percent chance that the Democrat would receive more votes than the Republican. A distribution drawn from both markets moved the margin forecast to 6 to 7 percentage points and the chance of a Democratic popular-vote win to 87.0 percent. Start with the thin book: 9 points. Bring in the thick book, where the win contract is the one with more trading behind it, and the margin shrinks by roughly 2 to 3 points while the win chance barely moves, from 85.7 percent to 87.0 percent. The paper, in PS: Political Science & Politics, still says significant uncertainty remains. Two contracts on related questions did not collapse into one number. The thin contract was the one that looked most decisive on the margin, and it was the one the authors flagged as thinly traded.

A second IEM result shows how large one active trader can be. A market-making program built for one set of IEM securities traded 34 percent of total market volume and recorded a Sharpe ratio of 9.9. In a small real-money market, one system was about a third of the tape. That is the marginal-trader idea in its stark form: the price is set by whoever keeps showing up with a bid and an offer, not by the median person who opened an account and left.

The same paper is also the dispute. It describes the program as profiting from the market's inefficiencies. Active, careful trading can pin a quote and still be evidence that the quote was off enough to harvest. Accuracy is not the same fact as dominance of volume. The 2024 IEM write-up does not treat the thick market as a cure for that. Combining the thin vote-share book with the thick winner-takes-all book changed the margin and left the authors saying uncertainty remained.

A listener who knows this reads a price as a trade, then asks who had to be willing for that trade to print. A thick winner-takes-all book and a thin vote-share book on the same night can disagree by points, as they did on September 29, 9 points against a blended 6 to 7. A book in which one program is 34 percent of volume can be liquid in the narrow sense that contracts change hands, and still be one trader's inventory. Play money and dollar markets are different stakes, but the mechanism is the same: the quote moves when someone hits it. Nothing in that mechanism says which side a listener should take.

How It Works: Streaks: The Gambler's Fallacy and the Hot Hand

A streak is a run of similar outcomes. Two opposite readings of that run have names. The gambler's fallacy says the run is due to reverse, as if independent events owed the other result. The hot hand says the run will continue, as if the person or the team had changed. If each draw really is independent, neither reading is information. If the draws are not independent — a pitcher, a lineup, a team on a road trip — the run might be a real change in the chance, a change only in what bettors believe, or both at once. A price that moves after a streak is not, by itself, proof of either story.

A 2014 paper in the International Journal of Financial Studies walked this through 34 National Basketball Association regular seasons. The authors estimated a seemingly unrelated regression on the full set of seasons and found that streaks significantly affected both the point spread and the difference in actual points. Read that in order. A team wins several in a row. The spread is the price bookmakers post for the margin. If the hot hand is only a story bettors tell, the spread can move while the actual margin does not. The pooled result said both moved: the price and the points. That is the paper's evidence that, across those 34 seasons taken together, a streak lined up with something in the game, not only with the betting line. Then the same paper estimated each season on its own. Differences showed up in particular for winning and losing streaks of six games or more. The pooled link did not hold in the same way every year once the run got that long. The listener's worked example is that six-game case: a price that jumps after six wins is consistent with the all-season finding that streaks moved spreads, and also consistent with the season-by-season finding that the relationship was unstable. The paper's own conclusion names both momentum effects and the gambler's fallacy. It does not hand the reader a rule for which one is operating in a single week.

A 2013 paper in the Journal of Gambling Business and Economics used six NBA seasons and split belief from profit. Wagering against the hot hand did not win more than efficiency implied. Ordinary least squares and two-stage least squares regressions still found that teams on streaks drew a significantly higher number of bets, so bettors behaved as if they believed in the hot hand. The closing point spread remained, in that study, an optimal and unbiased forecast of the outcome. Earlier work had assumed bookmakers ran a balanced book. This paper notes they may instead price as a forecast or shade a spread to exploit a known bias, which is why a closing line can look efficient even while the public piles onto a streak.

A 2026 paper in the International Journal of Sport Finance moved the same question to Major League Baseball starting pitchers and to two different prices. Pitchers were classed hot, cold, or neutral from context-adjusted recent performance. Short-term streaks predicted daily-fantasy outcomes. DraftKings salaries did not fully price that: hot pitchers were underpriced and cold pitchers were overpriced. Sportsbook lines, by contrast, tended to overreact, particularly for visiting hot starters and cold home starters. Simulated betting strategies built on those patterns did not yield statistically significant positive profits. The authors read that as sportsbooks adjusting prices to capitalize on user biases. The institutional point is the useful one: the same streak can be underweighted in a salary and overweighted in a betting line, and neither misweighting is the same thing as a strategy that pays.

A 2006 paper in Judgment and Decision Making supplies the clean independent case. Roulette spins in a casino are not a change in skill. Players still showed the hot hand and the gambler's fallacy, with significant differences from person to person. Across individuals, the two biases were positively and significantly correlated, which the authors treat as a sign of a common root. They speculate that root is locus of control. Where events are independent, both the expectation of reversal and the expectation of continuation are errors. Where events might not be independent, the sports papers disagree about how much of the streak survives into the result. The 2014 study finds streaks in the actual point difference as well as in the spread. The 2013 study finds the closing line unbiased and fading the streak unprofitable. The 2026 study finds mispricing in salaries and overreaction in lines, then finds no statistically significant profit from trading against either.

A listener who has those four results reads a post-streak price as a claim that still needs a reason. The reason might be that bettors are chasing a run — the 2013 result that streaking teams attract more bets, and the 2006 result that the same person can hold both sequence errors. The reason might be that something about the competitor changed — the 2014 result that actual margins moved with streaks in the pooled seasons, with the caveat that six-game streaks did not look the same every season. The 2026 result is the caution against treating either reading as a trade: a salary or a line can misweight a streak, and a simulated strategy that tries to exploit that misweighting still may not show a significant profit. None of this says which side of any live contract is the better buy. It says a run, by itself, is not the forecast. Source: doi.org

How It Works: Betting on Science: Replication Markets

A replication market is a contract on a narrow scientific event: will an independent team, running a published study again, get the same finding? Traders buy the claim if they think the result will hold and sell it if they think it will not. The price is the probability implied by those trades. The contract pays if the replication succeeds and pays nothing if it fails. Nobody in the market has to rerun the lab. They price the chance that someone else’s rerun will confirm the paper.

The worked example with figures in the record is a 2015 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Researchers set up prediction markets to estimate the reproducibility of 44 studies published in prominent psychology journals and then replicated in the Reproducibility Project: Psychology. Step one: the list was fixed at those 44 papers, so the questions did not drift as headlines changed. Step two: each study was a tradable claim about a real replication attempt, not a vote on whether the original authors still believed their result. Step three: the markets predicted the replication outcomes well. Step four: they outperformed a survey of individual forecasts. The same paper’s significance statement frames why a fast screen matters, using a cost estimate that is not itself a market result: the costs associated with irreproducible preclinical research alone have recently been estimated at 28 billion U.S. dollars a year in the United States. The markets are offered as a way to flag findings unlikely to replicate before that kind of money is spent again. The paper says there were no mechanisms in place to identify those findings quickly, and that prediction markets are well suited to bridge the gap.

A 2025 paper in the Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting starts from the design faults that sit under a result like that. Conventional replication markets, the authors write, suffer from thin liquidity, slow payouts, and limited expert engagement. A thin book means one arrival can shove the price. A slow payout means a trader who is right may wait until a replication finishes. Limited expert engagement means the people closest to the method may never show up. The proposed fix is a hybrid that blends three automated-market-maker models, reputation-based staking, and staged payouts. A university or a grant agency that sponsors a market can choose from three built-in pricing rules meant to keep prices meaningful even when only a few people are trading. Reputation-based staking lets standing in the field back a position, not only cash. Staged payouts pay along the way rather than only at the end. A play-money market prices a question by letting traders buy and sell a yes contract until the price settles. On Manifold, that stake is mana, not dollars. A contract marked at 25.1 percent, as on Manifold's question about whether another millennium prize problem will be solved in 2026, is a reputation price printed as an implied probability. No dollar changes hands when that number moves. The forecast, if it works as a forecast, works because traders care about being right in public.

How It Works: Following the Crowd: Herding and Information Cascades

An information cascade is a sequence in which a later decision-maker copies earlier actions and sets aside what they themselves know, because the earlier actions already look like evidence. In a market, that copycat logic can push a price away from the private information traders are sitting on. A flexible price is supposed to interrupt the cascade: once the price can move, the information in earlier trades is already in the number, so copying the last trade adds nothing new.

A 2005 internet experiment in the American Economic Review tested that idea, the theory of informational cascades in financial markets set out by Christopher Avery and Peter Zemsky in 1998. More than 6,400 subjects took part, including a subsample of 267 consultants from an international consulting firm. Walk the design the way the paper reports it. Subjects make decisions in a market whose price is free to move, rather than in a setting where only the previous person's action is visible. The flexible price prevented herding: people did not simply pile onto the last trade and ignore their own signal. Contrarian behavior still distorted prices. Even after 20 decisions, convergence to the fundamental value was rare. The consultants did not behave in a significantly different way from the other subjects. So a price that can move blocks one failure mode, blind copying, and still leaves another: trades against the crowd that pull the price off the value the signals would support, with convergence still uncommon after a long string of decisions.

A 2007 paper in the Journal of Finance put the same kind of question to people who do this for a living. Market professionals from the Chicago Board of Trade were observed in a controlled setting and compared with a student control group. Analysis of over 1,500 individual decisions found that the professionals were better able to discern the quality of public signals, and that their decisions were not affected by the domain of earnings. The student group did not show that same pattern. The authors treat the gap as a point about market efficiency: who is in the market changes how public signals get used.

A 2012 paper in Management Science looked for herding where the "price" is a funding tally, on Prosper.com, then the largest microloan market in the United States. Well-funded borrower listings tended to attract more funding after the researchers controlled for unobserved listing heterogeneity and payoff externalities. The lenders were not only mimicking. They inferred creditworthiness from peer lending decisions and used publicly observable borrower characteristics to moderate those inferences — active observational learning, which the paper calls rational herding, rather than passive copying, which it calls irrational herding. Obvious defects amplified the herd: poor credit grades increased a listing's herding momentum, because lenders inferred that the borrower must be better than the grade or the crowd would not have funded it. Favorable traits did the opposite. Friend endorsements weakened the herding effect, because lenders could attribute the crowd to a fact they could already see. Follow-up analysis found that rational herding beat irrational herding in predicting loan performance.

A 2018 paper in the Annals of Financial Economics tested herding in crypto-currency markets with the CSAD method of Chang et al. (2000). It used daily returns of six major crypto-currencies and the market index CCI30 from 07-08-2015 to 18-01-2018, and it checked up markets against down markets and high volatility against low volatility. Herding showed up in both up and down markets, which the paper reads as over-enthusiasm and over-reaction. Market volatility did not have a significant impact. Herding depended on market activity rather than on volatility.

A 2013 review in the Review of Behavioral Finance is the dispute on the shelf. More than two decades of work have produced real insight into investor herding, and the evidence is still inconclusive in places. The review says relatively little is known for markets other than equity markets, and that empirical methods still need to address current limitations. A crypto result, a loan-listing result and a trading-floor result do not automatically transfer to an event-contract book.

Read a fast price with that split in mind. A crowd all hitting the same side can be people ignoring what they know, or a price that has already absorbed what earlier traders knew, so the next copy is empty. It can also be contrarians shoving the number away from the signals, the failure the 2005 experiment still found after 20 decisions. And a visible fact can explain the herd without any hidden insight, the way friend endorsements did on Prosper: once the obvious merit is public, the pile-on says less. None of that says which side of any contract is cheap. It says a price is a record of decisions, and those decisions are not all information.

How It Works: Where the Money Sits: Collateral, Custody and Payout

On a collateralized prediction market, the dollar committed to a contract does not come back the moment the outcome is known. It sits until an oracle settles the claim and the winning position can be redeemed. A paper on settlement discounting puts the mechanism in one line: when collateral remains locked until oracle settlement, a near-certain dollar is a delayed dollar. The price therefore carries two things at once. One is a belief about the outcome. The other is a maturity-dependent discount for the wait between certainty and cash.

The authors recover that second piece from contracts that stay near certain, using the settlement times those contracts actually realized. They summarize the recovered curve as an annualized settlement wedge. The wedges they recover are positive, they change with maturity, and they change over time. That is the worked path through their own figures. Start with the raw prices of near-certain claims across horizons. The horizon gradient is how much those prices differ as the wait until settlement lengthens. Adjust the prices by the recovered wedge curves. The paper finds that this adjustment reduces the near-certainty horizon gradient by roughly 48 to 88 percent. Most of the raw pattern across maturities, on that measure, is a priced settlement friction, not forecast error alone. What remains after the adjustment is the part the wedge does not explain.

Two design findings sit next to that measurement. Neg-risk conversion compresses the discounts by recycling part of the position into synthetic collateral, so less capital has to sit idle for the full wait. Yield-bearing collateral flattens the term structure by cutting the opportunity cost of the lock-up: the dollar that cannot be withdrawn can still earn something while it waits. In both cases the price moves because the funding terms moved, not because the underlying event became more or less likely.

The paper does not claim the wedge removes the gradient entirely. A reduction of 48 to 88 percent leaves a residual. The result is also tied to a particular architecture: collateral locked until an oracle redeems the claim. A venue that pays from a different custody setup, or that lets capital turn over sooner, is outside what these curves measure. The limit is the one the measurement states.

A listener who knows this reads a price near certainty differently depending on when the dollar can actually leave. A contract that is already sure, but whose collateral cannot move until a later oracle date, is not the same object as a contract that settles at once. Part of the gap between those prices can be the wait, the productivity of the collateral, and whether the market recycles capital, rather than a different belief about the event. The paper's closing claim is that prediction-market prices aggregate information through a financial infrastructure whose funding conditions are measurable. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Why a Contract That Settles Next Year Trades Below Its Odds

A contract that pays if an event happens, and pays nothing if it does not, is a claim on cash that arrives only when the contract settles. If that date is a year away, the payout is a year away even for a trader who is already sure of the result. The money posted to hold the position sits as collateral until then. Cash that is locked cannot be spent, lent, or posted somewhere else in the meantime. Whatever that cash could have earned over the wait is a cost of holding the contract. A trader who has to borrow the collateral, or who simply has another use for it, will not hand over today the full amount they think the contract is likely to pay later. The price can sit below the probability that trader actually holds. The gap is a charge for time, not a quieter forecast.

Suppose a trader's own belief is that the event is more likely than not, and the contract cannot pay until next year. The most they will post today is that belief, reduced by what they give up while the collateral is stuck. The reduction tracks the return available on that cash over the life of the contract, plus any fee the venue takes for keeping the position open. A contract that settles next week has almost none of that wait. The same belief, on the same event, can therefore show a higher price on the near contract than on the far one. Reading the far price as if it were the near price treats two different claims as one forecast.

Venues that pay interest on collateral change the arithmetic. If the cash posted earns a return while it waits, the trader is no longer giving up the whole alternative yield. The time charge shrinks, and the price can sit closer to the probability the trader holds. A venue that pays nothing on margin leaves the full cost of the wait inside the price. Comparing a long-dated price on a venue that pays interest with one on a venue that does not, without knowing which is which, mixes a funding term into what looks like a probability.

The adjustment fails when capital is not what binds. A position limit, a fee that does not scale with time, or a resolution rule that can void or rewrite the contract can move the price for reasons that have nothing to do with the calendar. A thin market can print a number that no one was willing to defend with size. Play money, where the stake is reputation rather than cash that could have earned a return, does not carry the same time charge, so its long-dated price is not the same object as a cash price on a distant contract.

Once that is clear, a listener reads a far-dated price with the settlement date in view, and asks whether the collateral earns anything while it sits. A price below a stated belief is not, by itself, a claim that the event is less likely. It may be the cost of waiting to be paid.

How It Works: How Fast a Price Moves: Markets Against the Clock

A prediction-market price is a running yes-or-no answer, and the clock starts the moment public information changes the chance of that answer. A print that jumps in the first minute can look finished. It often is not. A paper posted to arXiv, “When Do Markets Fully Process Public Information? Evidence from Real-Time Prediction Markets,” separates a fast twitch from a full update. The setting combines binary payoffs, public signals the authors can time exactly, and high-frequency prices, so a change in the traded price can be set next to a change in a benchmark probability built only from information that is already public.

Prices in that setting are informative, and they become more accurate as resolution approaches. During the event, prices respond rapidly to public signals and move in the expected direction. Directional responsiveness is not the same as efficient updating. Relative to an out-of-sample benchmark probability model, a one-minute change in the benchmark probability is associated with only about a 0.64-for-one contemporaneous change in the market price. Walk that coefficient as the paper states it, without adding a second result. Take a one-minute move in the benchmark as one unit. About 0.64 of that unit shows up in the price in the same minute. The rest of that one-minute benchmark move is not in the price yet. The missing adjustment predicts future price drift over the following several minutes. That later drift is still there net of subsequent changes in the benchmark probability, so it is not only the market chasing a benchmark that kept moving after the first minute.

The authors then look at why the gap opens. Salient public signals are incorporated relatively quickly in liquid markets. The same signals generate substantially greater underreaction when liquidity is low. Underreaction gaps associated with salient states also predict stronger subsequent drift. The pattern they describe is gradual price discovery shaped by attention and trading frictions together. A market can see the public signal, move the right way, and still leave part of the benchmark’s update for the next several minutes. Liquidity changes how large that leftover is. Salience changes how hard the leftover pulls on later prices.

Nothing in the paper is a halt rule, a trading instruction, or a claim that one side of any live contract is the right side. The useful reading is narrower. A price that moves in the expected direction in the first minute is evidence that traders responded to public information. It is not evidence that the implied probability has absorbed the full benchmark update. In a liquid book, more of a salient signal lands quickly. In a book with low liquidity, the same kind of signal leaves a larger underreaction, and the paper finds that those gaps predict more drift afterward. Market-implied probabilities in this study are often directionally correct, yet the adjustment stays incomplete and depends, in a predictable way, on liquidity and salience. A listener who keeps that split in mind treats the first print as a start, then asks whether the book was liquid enough for that start to be close to the full public update. Source: arxiv.org

How It Works: Why the Prices in a Race Do Not Add Up to One

A multi-candidate race on a prediction venue is usually not one contract. It is a row of separate yes-or-no contracts, one name each. Each contract is written to pay a fixed unit if that name is the winner and to pay nothing if another name is. When the outcomes shut one another out, and when one of them has to happen, a complete set of fair yes prices should be worth that same unit. The numbers on the screen often add up to more than the unit, or to less. The gap is about how the contracts are quoted and cleared. It is not a second race hiding inside the first.

Each leg has a bid, the price at which a trader can sell, and an ask, the price at which a trader can buy. The figure a venue prints may be the last trade, the midpoint between bid and ask, or the price a buyer would actually have to pay. Adding the asks adds the expensive side of every book. Adding the bids adds the cheap side. Neither total is the cost of a sure payout, and neither has to equal the unit the contracts pay. A last-trade print on a quiet name can sit away from the live bid and ask, so a column of last prices is not a portfolio a trader could lift in one go.

Fees open the same gap from another direction. If the venue takes a cut from the premium, or from the payout, buying every yes is not a free conversion even when the printed prices add up to exactly the unit. The buyer pays the sum of the asks, plus any fee, and receives one unit only if the written rules really do pay exactly one winner. A total above the payout unit can be what is left after the spread and the venue's cut. A total below the unit can mean the books are stale, one outcome is missing from the menu, or sellers have not posted size on the names that look expensive.

A thin book does the rest of the work. On a contract with little size at the inside price, one small order moves that name and leaves the other names where they were. The sum jumps even though nothing about the race changed. Until someone else posts the other side, the total can sit away from the payout unit. A listener reads that sum next to the size on each leg. The sum alone is not a verdict on the field.

Some market designs try to pull the legs back together. A combined or basket order lets a trader buy yes on one name and no on the others in a single action, or sell a complete set, so the venue can match the package against one pool of collateral instead of five unrelated wagers. Negative risk, in that generic sense, is the idea that holding no on every candidate but one is the same economic position as yes on the remaining name, so the margin system should not charge as if those bets were strangers. When that netting works, a trader who sees yes prices adding past the payout can sell the expensive set, or buy the cheap set, and that trade is what drags the sum back toward the unit. When the venue does not net the positions, or when the book is too thin for the package to fill, the gap can sit there. None of that says which name is cheap. It says the column is not a pie chart.

Two ways the sum misleads should stay separate. The contracts may not cover every possible winner. And the resolution rules may pay more than one winner, or none, if the written criteria overlap or if the event is cancelled or voided. In that case a sum of one is the wrong benchmark. The rule comes before the arithmetic.

A listener who has this stops treating a total above or below a dollar as a forecast. The useful comparison is leg against leg, on the same side of the book, after asking whether the outcomes exclude one another, whether a fee sits between the printed price and the payout, and whether each contract has enough size that the printed price is a price someone can trade. The gap is a statement about market structure. It is not, by itself, a statement about who wins.

How It Works: One Question Inside Another: Correlated and Combinatorial Markets

A combinatorial prediction market prices not only standalone events but events defined as conditions and as Boolean combinations of a base set. The joint distribution is the hard part. Explicitly writing down every combination is infeasible once there are more than a few base events, and computing or updating prices in a logarithmic market scoring rule book is #P-hard. That is why these markets are difficult to run even when the questions look ordinary.

The scoring rule decides whether nested questions stay consistent. In the account in the Journal of Prediction Markets, scoring rules elicit probability estimates from individuals, while betting markets elicit consensus estimates from groups. A market scoring rule does both, and eliciting from a group costs no more than eliciting from an individual. For a bet on one event given another event, only the logarithmic versions preserve the probability of the given event. Logarithmic versions also preserve the conditional probabilities of other events, and so preserve conditional independence. Given logarithmic rules that already elicit relative probabilities of base event pairs, it costs no more to elicit estimates on all combinations of those base events.

That preservation is the difference between two linked questions and one joint book. A contract on a policy-rate cut and a separate contract on an inflation print can each trade briskly and still disagree about the conditional, because nothing in two standalone books forces the price of "cut, given a high print" to match the two marginal prices. A logarithmic market scoring rule is the version that, on a bet on one event given another, leaves the probability of the conditioned event unchanged. A reader who sees only the two standalone prices cannot check that conditional. The prices can all be locally active and still be jointly impossible.

Correlation adds an information problem on top of the arithmetic. A 2017 paper in the American Economic Review studies information spillovers when privately informed sellers hold assets with correlated values. A trade in one asset can provide information about the others. The information content of that trading behavior is endogenous: it is not a fixed leak, and it depends on the equilibrium being played. When assets are sufficiently correlated, that endogeneity leads to multiple equilibria. The equilibria are ranked by trade volume and by efficiency. Introducing post-trade transparency can increase or decrease both welfare and trading volume, depending on the asset correlation, the equilibrium being played, and the composition of market participants. Publishing the last trade is not automatically an improvement.

Two computational lines try to make the joint book runnable. A 2012 arXiv paper connects logarithmic market scoring rule markets to probabilistic belief aggregation and uses Bayesian networks to represent security prices. It gives a characterization of structure-preserving securities on decomposable networks, and it notes that earlier tournament results from Chen and coauthors are corollaries of that characterization. It also shows that for a very basic set of securities to be structure preserving, the Bayesian network graph must be decomposable. Securities that are not structure preserving are left to approximation. A second 2012 arXiv paper shows how to generalize the junction tree algorithm so the market can update the consensus distribution after a local probability edit, update each user's assets so prior trades can be reused without assets going negative, find the conditional state in which a user has minimum assets, and report expected assets given an edit. A 2014 proposal, Dynamic Asset Cluster, stores trades in compact asset blocks and builds a junction tree dynamically to compute minimum and expected assets, improving time and space over parallel junction trees for assets and probabilities.

The failure modes are the boundaries those papers already draw. With enough correlation there is more than one equilibrium, so a price is not a unique summary of what is known. Post-trade transparency can shrink volume and welfare instead of informing the next trade. If the securities people want are not structure preserving, the clean update does not exist and the venue is approximating. A listener who knows that reads two linked contracts differently: agreement is not guaranteed by the fact that both are listed, and a sharp move in one is not automatically news about the other until it is clear whether the books share a joint representation or are only two separate prices side by side.
