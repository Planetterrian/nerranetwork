EU summit to focus on China, Ukraine and bloc budget: DW

DW reports the deficit with China reached €360 billion ($400.4 billion) last year and is set to exceed that this year; the European Commission agreed with Beijing last week to curb hybrid-car imports, and trade chief Maroš Šefčovič's preliminary deal also covers access to rare earths. The EU accuses China of unfair state subsidies, industry groups say the import surge is closing factories, and Chancellor Friedrich Merz and President Emmanuel Macron want measures against market distortion, including a Franco-German "kill switch" that would cut China's market access if the deficit stayed unsustainable. Euronews reports oil remains above $100 a barrel despite a coordinated G7 release, and the International Energy Agency warned on Wednesday of renewed wholesale diesel price rises in Europe; Italian and Greek leaders want fiscal rules eased for temporary price measures, while more fiscally conservative states warn that would add to deficits and volatility. The seven-year budget needs unanimous approval; European Council President António Costa wants a deal by the end of the year so the law can be finished before January 2028, an Irish compromise has left both frugal governments and supporters of a larger budget unhappy, and a senior diplomat said leaders are not in a hurry and want a good deal rather than a fast one. DW says this summit is not expected to produce a breakthrough, a special summit is set for November, and pressure is rising ahead of elections next year in several member states; Euronews adds that Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's call for snap elections in Spain adds uncertainty. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas told Euronews the US-Russia diesel deal is a "gift to Putin," that "the majority of the financing of this war comes from oil," and that strikes on Russian refineries fit "defence of Ukraine" under international law. Zelenskyy told Piers Morgan he feels "betrayed" and that Ukraine is losing American support "not because of us"; Euronews, citing the Financial Times, reports US negotiators proposed that European countries unfreeze some immobilised Russian state assets into a vehicle controlled by Washington, a plan European delegates rejected in Miami last week, with a loan to Ukraine due to be raised in Brussels on Friday. The Closer Look

Poland looks to economic heights in a country transformed since Soviet days: The Guardian

The Guardian closes a series on Poland today with people in Warsaw describing wages that have risen along with the cost of living. Above the city, the Varso tower, finished four years ago and the tallest building in the European Union, carries a 310-metre spire over the Palace of Culture and Science, Joseph Stalin's gift. Cezary Błaszczyk, managing director of HB Reavis in Poland, which owns the tower, said "We look down on it," and that standing taller than the palace means the country got over communism and now has a strong economy. His colleague Natalia Kasperska said a slackline walker who crossed the 500-metre gap between the buildings a few months ago made "a symbolic walk from the past to the future."

The paper sets that view against four decades since Poland left Soviet-dominated rule. Excluding oil-exporting states and financial hubs, it says incomes have converged with western levels at the fastest rate in the world, from 40 percent of the EU average in the mid-1990s to 81 percent last year. National income passed $1 trillion, or £755 billion, last year, ranking Poland among the world's 20 largest economies. International Monetary Fund projections show income per person will soon overtake Japan and Spain. Deputy foreign minister Marcin Bosacki recalled queueing after school in the 1980s for up to five hours for butter and meat while the communist government imposed martial law. The Gdańsk shipworkers' Solidarność movement and Pope John Paul II's pilgrimage in 1979 preceded democracy in 1990, Nato membership in 1999 and EU accession in 2004.

The direction of migration has turned. For years Poles left to earn more abroad, and Polish plumbers became shorthand in Britain during the fights over free movement before the Brexit vote. In each year since 2021, twice as many Polish nationals left the United Kingdom as arrived. In 2024, Germany recorded more non-Germans moving to Poland than heading the other way for the first time on record. Architects Jedrzej Kolesinski and Krzysztof Gornicki, who moved to London in their twenties and led the Foster + Partners team behind the Varso, have come back and are designing Warsaw's new airport, which the paper calls the biggest greenfield infrastructure project in Europe. Gornicki said he can do global work from Warsaw and wanted to raise a family near his parents in a city safer, greener and more affordable than London. Electric trams, cranes and dockless e-scooters fill the streets below; orders at the oldest milk bar, Prasowy, now go through a touchscreen; Parade Square, once an asphalt expanse for Soviet tanks, has been planted as Warsaw bids for the 2040 summer Olympics. The Guardian also writes that incoming capital has driven up living costs and squeezed poorer Varsovians, that income inequality is among the highest in Europe, and that political divisions are widening ahead of a general election next year. Lead

Ukrainian soldier to go on trial over sabotage of Nord Stream gas pipelines: The Guardian

A Ukrainian special forces officer, Serhii Kuznetsov, went on trial in Hamburg on Wednesday, accused by German prosecutors of taking part in the blasts that damaged the Nord Stream gas pipelines on 26 September 2022. Prosecutors accuse him of being an accomplice to war crimes by attacking civilian infrastructure, causing an explosion, damaging property and disrupting public services. He denies any involvement, and Ukraine denies involvement. He will be identified in court as Serhii K under German privacy laws, though an extradition order used his full name. Prosecutors allege he prepared and carried out the detonation of at least four incendiary devices of up to 27 kilograms at a depth of about 80 metres under the Baltic Sea, near the Danish island of Bornholm, and that six accomplices — four deep-sea divers, an explosives expert and a ship’s captain — used a rented yacht and timer-controlled explosives. The blasts burst Nord Stream 1, Russia’s main gas supply route to Germany before Moscow cut supplies in August 2022, and damaged Nord Stream 2, which Germany had suspended before Russia’s full-scale invasion and which was never put into operation. In July, German state prosecutors accused Ukrainian state authorities of ordering the attack, arguing the aim was to halt gas supplies and stop Russia using gas revenue to finance the war; President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has denied his country was involved. Kuznetsov was arrested in August 2025 on a family holiday in Italy while travelling on a false passport, and extradited to Germany last November. A further suspect, Volodymyr Zhuravlyov, was arrested in northern Croatia in August, is fighting extradition after applying for asylum there, and denies any role. The German government has declined to comment. The Alternative für Deutschland has used the case to argue that German aid for Ukraine must cease and the pipeline be reinstated. A Russian request to participate as co-plaintiffs was rejected; Gazprom, which owns Nord Stream 2, had sought that role. Kuznetsov is expected to remain silent, and his lawyers are expected to offer affidavits from two military superiors that he is prohibited from revealing his actions as a soldier. How Europe’s most wanted cocaine trafficker turned Sierra Leone into the world’s first true narco state: The Guardian

The price of cocaine in Europe is the stake in a Guardian investigation published on Wednesday. Intelligence assessments cited by the paper credit a 35-year-old Dutch national, Jos Leijdekkers, with importing volumes large enough to crash the European market, and say that last year he halved the drug’s wholesale price. Those sources say he is also the son-in-law of Sierra Leone’s president, Julius Maada Bio, and that he ordered the abduction of a Freetown man, Alan Jalloh. A western official told the Guardian: “It is state capture by a criminal network on a scale never seen.”

The account of that capture, as the paper’s police and intelligence sources tell it, runs from a supermarket forecourt to a boarding at sea. Witnesses described a black Toyota Rav4 forcing through rush-hour traffic outside Goodies supermarket in Freetown. Men the witnesses called burly and white pulled on balaclavas, and dragged Jalloh, 40, and two friends into the vehicle. None of the three has been seen since. Two days later, on 25 October 2025, another three men disappeared. Within a week, nine people had been seized from the city’s streets, most in daylight. Police sources were told they were dismembered and that at least one was beheaded. No bodies have been found. An officer investigating Jalloh’s abduction said: “They were exterminated to hide the truth.” Intelligence sources told the paper that at least 14 people have been killed inside Sierra Leone on Leijdekkers’ orders, and that high-ranking police are believed to have been murdered on his say-so. Officers have fled the country, those sources said.

Five months after Jalloh disappeared, the cargo ship Arconian left Freetown. By May, Spanish civil guards boarded it about 100 miles off Morocco. A guard the Guardian names as Lucas Fernández described six gunmen in the hold and, nearby, 1,279 bales of cocaine weighing between 30 and 45 tonnes, which the paper calls the largest amount of the drug ever seized. Seventeen days later, Bio addressed Oxford University. The Guardian describes him as chair of Ecowas, a region it says is home to more than 400 million people, and as someone the EU has treated as a reliable ally, with aid to Sierra Leone stretching back more than 45 years. The paper says that continued even as intelligence detailed cocaine volumes bound for Europe, and that the UN overlooked its own evidence.

The same sources warn the network is expanding. A Dutch official said the entire west Africa region is at risk. A European police source said dozens of Leijdekkers’ gunmen descended on a Dutch village last month intent on retribution, and that cocaine disputes in Europe had entered a new era. The first recorded sighting of Leijdekkers in west Africa, the investigation says, was a six-vehicle convoy in Freetown in October 2022.
