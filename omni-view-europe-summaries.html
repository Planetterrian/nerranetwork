Both Sides: Genetically modified crop imports

The European Commission has approved or renewed authorizations for seven genetically modified crops, and the question is whether those import approvals should stand when governments in the member states would not vote them through.

The case for the decisions is the one the Commission acted on: favorable assessments by the European Food Safety Authority. UkrAgroConsult reports that the Commission approved or renewed seven events in June and September 2026 — four soybean events and three corn events — for food and animal feed. The expansion is particularly important, that report says, for the European feed sector, which relies heavily on imported soybeans and corn. The new and renewed approvals broaden the shipments that can enter the EU market for processing and use in animal feed. The list is soybean MON 94637, GMB151 crossed with DAS-44406-6, and MON 94313; corn DP202216 crossed with NK603 and DAS-40278-9; and renewals for corn NK603 crossed with T25, corn MON 87460, and soybean MON 87705. The Commission adopted the decisions after member states failed to reach a qualified majority either for or against.

The case against is the one member states put on the record in those votes: public opposition to genetically modified crops, the precautionary principle, scientific concerns, and political considerations. They did not gather a qualified majority to stop the authorizations. They also did not gather one to pass them.

What both the approvals and the objections leave in place is the scope of the decisions themselves: imports and processing only, with no authorization to grow the crops in the EU, a term of 10 years, and a requirement that products comply with EU labeling and traceability rules. Those authorizations now run for that 10-year term. Source: [ukragroconsult.com](https://ukragroconsult.com/en/news/european-commission-authorizes-imports-of-seven-new-gm-soybean-and-corn-events/)

Finland orders halt to work on Google data centre sites: Al Jazeera

Finland’s Supervisory Agency, the LVV, has ordered Tuike Finland, the company representing Google, to suspend by 23 October 2026 all preparatory work that would significantly alter the environment at planned data-centre sites in Muhos and Kajaani, until compulsory environmental impact assessments are completed. Google said in September it would invest 13 billion euros, about 14.6 billion dollars, in Finnish digital infrastructure over two years, and called the projects in Muhos, Vaala, Kajaani and Hamina its single biggest investment in Europe. Hanna Halmeenpaa, chair of the Finnish Association for Nature Conservation, told AFP that nature sites which should be preserved had been logged across a deforested area of more than 300 hectares. Tuike Finland must explain how it intends to proceed by 14 October, and the LVV said it could face enforcement proceedings if it does not comply. Google spokesman Sondre Ronander told AFP the company acknowledged shortcomings, would study the findings and follow the guidance, and said landscaping plans include planting trees across 130 hectares at Muhos; opposition parties last month called for a national permit system to prevent power shortages and soaring energy prices. Both Sides: Limits on new EU members

What is being decided is how the European Union would stay workable if Ukraine and other candidates join. The European Commission is set to unveil proposals on Tuesday, Kyiv Post reports, after months of delay while governments pushed different ideas.

Italian parliament backs Meloni’s plan to restart nuclear power: The Mighty 790 KFGO

Italy’s Senate gave final approval on Wednesday to legislation that lets the government restart nuclear power generation almost 40 years after a 1987 referendum closed the country’s reactors. The law requires the government to adopt implementing decrees within 12 months and creates an independent regulator while setting rules for decommissioning old facilities. Energy Minister Gilberto Pichetto Fratin said sustainable nuclear power together with renewables will make Italy a safe, independent and competitive country. The government estimates nuclear could reach at least 11 percent of the energy mix and save €17 billion in decarbonisation costs by 2050, with the national plan projecting a possible rise to 22 percent. Centre-left opposition parties opposed the measure and called for any new plants to be sited only on already developed or degraded land. A June poll by Demopolis found 51 percent support for restarting nuclear generation overall, though a majority opposed construction of a plant near their homes. The government argues that technological and safety advances have rendered the 1987 vote obsolete and that the move will shield the economy from energy-price surges linked to conflicts in the Middle East. Anti-nuclear campaigners maintain that safety concerns and lengthy construction times outweigh the benefits, and Italy currently relies heavily on energy imports including electricity from nuclear plants in neighbouring countries such as France. Five Star Movement senator Elena Sironi said any new plants should be located primarily in areas already developed, degraded or affected by decommissioned industrial activities without consuming additional land.
