Both Sides: Narrowing Metro Vancouver to water and sewage

Voters are being asked, in a Conservative platform promise, whether Metro Vancouver Regional District should be cut back to drinking water and sewage. Lorne Doerkson’s B.C. Conservatives say that if elected on Oct. 24 they would drop the regional role in housing, economic development, air quality and climate policy, and leave bulk water and sewage treatment for local governments. Daily Hive reports those two utilities already account for most of the regional operating and capital budgets.

Carson Binda, the Conservative candidate for Vancouver-Quilchena, called the present setup “not a utility” but “an unaccountable bureaucratic mess with a blank cheque,” and said Conservatives would “take the chequebook back, focus Metro on water and sewage, and make sure taxpayers get value for every dollar.” The party says consultant and contractor costs now exceed in-house salaries and benefits, cites growing debt and the operating budget, and argues the province and municipalities should take the other four services because that work duplicates what they already do.

Lynne Block, the candidate for North Vancouver-Seymour, said “a smaller, professional board will run this utility like the essential service it is.” The board itself, in July 2026, asked the province to allow a 25 percent cut in directors. Directors now come from 21 municipalities, Electoral Area A and the Tsawwassen First Nation.

On the North Shore Wastewater Treatment Plant, Trevor Withers, the candidate for North Vancouver-Lonsdale, said people “want clean water and working sewers” and not “a regional bureaucracy that can’t build a plant on budget and never answers for it.” Caroline Elliott, the candidate for West Vancouver-Capilano, said Conservatives would audit “what went so disastrously wrong under the NDP’s watch.” Construction began in 2017 at about $700 million, then aimed at 2020. The cost is now about $3.9 billion, with completion in 2030, and households face upward pressure on user fees. The district restarted an independent review this summer, with a final report expected in early 2027. A May 2025 Deloitte review, done with the province, has already led to stronger internal auditing and changes to board pay. In early September 2026 the district parted ways with chief administrative officer Jerry Dobrovolny, in that job since 2019. Civic election day is Oct. 17. Both Sides: A higher provincial tax on income over $190,405

What is on the table is a campaign promise, not a tax already in force. On Sunday in Comox, B.C. NDP Leader David Eby said that if the NDP is re-elected, British Columbians earning more than $190,405 a year would pay more provincial income tax, including a new bracket on income over $1 million. Canada’s National Observer reports the election is October 24, and that party documents date the changes to the 2027 tax year.

Eby’s case is that almost everyone would be untouched and the money would go to services. He said more than 96 per cent of residents who make $190,000 or less would see no tax increase, and that the highest earners would pay slightly more “to fund improvements in health care and to lower costs for British Columbians.” He also said it is right to ask people who earn a little more to pitch in when public services are needed. The party said B.C. would be the first large province with a bracket on individuals earning more than a million. The Vancouver Sun reports the proposal would lift the rate above $265,545 from 20.5 per cent to 22.5 per cent, the rate from $190,405 to $265,545 from 16.5 per cent to 18.5 per cent, and set 24.5 per cent on each dollar over $1 million. Eby said the taxes would raise an extra $1 billion a year. National Observer, citing party documents, projects $225 million in 2026/27, $925 million the next year, and $1 billion the year after, and puts the $190,405-to-$265,545 rate at 18.8 per cent.

The Conservative Party of B.C. answers that the plan would tax the doctors and specialists every community is fighting to recruit, while emergency rooms are still closing at night. Its statement said there is no problem the NDP do not think can be fixed by raising taxes, that the party has committed to no new taxes, and that a government led by Lorne Doerkson would make B.C. the place doctors choose to build a practice.

Both sides are responding to a proposal that, as announced, raises provincial tax only on income above $190,405 and only if the NDP is re-elected. Voters go to the polls October 24; NDP documents put any change in the 2027 tax year. Both Sides: AI data centres in Vancouver

Hundreds marched Saturday from Mount Pleasant to downtown against AI data centres, Yahoo News Canada reports. No AI Vancouver called it part of an international day of protest, invited municipal candidates, and said the question is now a campaign issue. The file is a rezoning application for a proposed Mount Pleasant AI data centre. In July, Vancouver city council sent that application back to staff for further public consultation, which will not begin until after the October 17 municipal election.

The case for a pause is put by Torin Larocque, founder of No AI Vancouver, and by four council candidates from COPE, the Vancouver Greens and Bright Future. Larocque says the July referral happened partly because hundreds had signed up to speak, and he wants voters to know which candidates are against AI. COPE's Leslie said the proposal sits in a system that prioritizes the ultra-wealthy over everyone else. COPE's Yi-Kieran said residents do not want the centres, that consultation has been almost absent, and that AI comes up at the door as often as housing and affordability. Both support a moratorium and say the city controls this zoning. Greens candidate Burns wants a moratorium until there is more regulation, but said she does not hear the concern often while door-knocking. Bright Future's Lin said the issue shows a broader need for change in local government. Their stated reasons centre on energy, water and consultation.

Burns's doorstep account and Yi-Kieran's are the two strongest reads in the coverage of how widely people raise it. Council's July vote did not impose a moratorium and did not approve the project. It ordered more consultation after October 17. A 2024 International Energy Agency report cited in the story said data centres, largely driven by AI, used roughly 1.5 per cent of global energy, with that demand projected to more than double by 2030, and with data-centre electricity use growing about four times faster than all other sectors. The fact both the pause advocates and that July order share is that the extra consultation has not started. It is not set to begin before the October 17 vote.
