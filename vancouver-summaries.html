The Closer Look

It can cost thousands to get rid of an old RV in B.C., so people are dumping them: Vancouver Sun

On October 31, a travel trailer appeared overnight in a cornfield off a quiet country road in Abbotsford. Neighbours later remarked that someone had taken care to back it neatly into the field before driving away. The Vancouver Sun reports that abandoned recreational vehicles are showing up in fields, forests, rest areas and parks because disposing of an old RV in British Columbia is difficult and costly.

Phil Pires, a junk-removal expert who owns Big Phil’s Rubbish Removal and specializes in RV recycling, says people think the units have scrap value, but they do not. The only metal is the frame, and the whole unit has to be dismantled to reach it: sawing through siding and layers of insulation, separating Fiberglas from wood and upholstery, and draining the grey and black water tanks. Landfills may take some pieces, while recyclers take others. Tanks that once held septic waste have to be handled carefully, as do old fridges and appliances that might contain ammonia. Pires says that work is expensive. Disposal of a small camper, including the kind that fits on the back of a truck, typically runs about one thousand dollars. Large trailers can cost several thousand dollars. If the RV is worth fixing and he can find a buyer, he charges for removal, accepts legal ownership and then sells it for refurbishing. In other cases, owners balk. Some pay a company about two hundred dollars to take a unit away. Pires says if that seems too good to be true, it usually is. He recalled a woman in a Maple Ridge community Facebook group offering several hundred dollars to have an old boat removed. He gave her a quote; she did not call back. Several days later, a neighbour saw the boat abandoned on the side of the highway in Deroche. Police investigated. Pires said he does not think she was charged.

Abbotsford police Sergeant Paul Walker says illegal dumping is not typically a police matter. Officers may get involved if the vehicle is stolen, connected to a crime or presents a public safety risk. RVs have a VIN, but tracing it is complicated because people sometimes do not update the registration when they sell. Walker says that in most cases these abandoned RVs and vehicles are uninsured and, quite often, have been off insurance for many years, and that proving who dumped one is challenging.

Abbotsford is home to a large number of RVs, old and new, and several large dealerships. Until recently there were three RV encampments in highway rest areas and truck parking lots. At one time close to eighty old vehicles were in the camps, some abandoned before being occupied by people looking for shelter. The province closed the camps as construction on Highway 1 progressed. In a statement, the City of Abbotsford said bylaw officers responded to four reports of abandoned vehicles in the last year, including two RVs, a trailer and a boat. When the provincial government closed the camps, the city responded to three calls related to occupied RVs on city land and worked to connect the occupants with support services. Abandoned RVs have also been an issue in the Chilliwack River Valley, where seven RVs were removed from the forest near the Chilliwack River in January during a cleanup of abandoned encampments. Patti MacAhonic, director of Electoral Area E, said crews seem to be getting the upper hand, although she has accepted the problem will never be solved. The best they can do, she said, is manage it. Source: vancouversun.com

Board CEO Rock Lefebvre said much of the pandemic-era price growth has been erased, which he called the best affordability in five years, while warning that higher borrowing costs complicate that opening. The Closer Look

The Aristocratic sign glows again in neon exhibit at the Post: Vancouver Sun

The giant neon sign for the Aristocratic restaurant is lit again at The Post, the office building in Vancouver’s former downtown post office. Last week the Museum of Vancouver lent it, with other pieces from its neon collection, to an installation called Lit! Neons of Vancouver. The signs are lined along a wall across from Deville Coffee at the 366 Dunsmuir Street entrance, facing the street. People can walk up to them while the building is open in the day, and they remain visible from the sidewalk at night.

The Aristocratic sign stood at Broadway and Granville from 1960 to 1997. It mixed red and blue neon, used four type styles, and carried a plastic panel of Risty, the restaurant’s mascot, a man in a top hat and monocle. The Vancouver Sun notes that in 1953 Neon Products counted 19,000 neon signs in the city, one for every 18 residents. The lot was later sold, the restaurant was demolished, and a Chapters bookstore was built there with a much smaller replica inside. The museum saved the original, three metres high, 3.3 metres long, and 0.3 metres deep, too big to fit through its doors. Aside from a brief spell hung outside the museum, it stayed in storage.

The restaurant was once part of a chain of 12 that started in 1928. The Broadway and Granville branch opened in 1938 and was the last one standing. A 1951 photograph shows a different Aristocratic sign at that corner; the sign now at The Post is the 1960 version. Beside it are a big “R” from the former Ridge Theatre at Arbutus Street and West 16th Avenue, a red-and-green Palm Ice Cream sign, the red neon of the American Hotel on Main Street, and a white neon sign for The Cookie Jar, a 1950s chain. The Ridge “R” went up in 1950 and came down when the theatre closed in 2013. It is lit by red neon and clear flashbulbs. The painted metal looks weathered after decades outside; the museum is keeping that patina. The other Ridge letters are still in Building 14, the warehouse where the museum keeps oversized objects, which is also where the Aristocratic sign had been stored. A 2001 replica of the Welding Shop and Engineering sign, which stood at 1600 Main Street from 1930 to 1974, is the only animated piece on display: a welder in a mask, a red torch, a green rod, and blue sparks that flash on and off.

Neon expert John Atkin told the Sun the signs sit at eye level, with overlapping colour the way they once worked on the street, and that the wall now has a purpose for people sitting with coffee. Karen Zajac, walking past, called the Aristocratic sign’s return fantastic and said one of her first dates was at the restaurant. The display has been in the works since 2019. Source: vancouversun.com

Top Stories

This $1.7M Vancouver home has an auction reserve of $72K. Yes, there’s a catch: Global BC

Global News reports that the City of Vancouver auctions 116 properties at city hall on Wednesday, starting at 10 a.m., in this year’s tax sale of homes whose owners have not paid taxes for three years. The reserve, or upset price, is the amount owed to the city. A Windsor Street house in East Vancouver, assessed by BC Assessment at more than $1.7 million, has a reserve of $72,051. Two side-by-side houses on Point Grey Road, valued around $5 million and $6 million, have upset prices of about $289,000 and $355,000. After auction day the current owners have a year to redeem by paying their taxes, which refunds the bidder plus interest. The city said there were only three instances in the last 10 years where a property changed hands from a tax sale, and that taxes on almost all properties have historically been paid within that year. The highest bidders must pay the upset price by noon Thursday. Retired notary Ron Usher said the law is structured to give the owner the maximum chance to sort the problem out, and that he suspects some sales are “whoops” rather than deliberate, citing inheritances. Realtor Sean Holden called bidding “an exercise in futility” and said he would not waste his time, citing how rarely sales are completed and the risk of not knowing a home’s condition. Source: globalnews.ca

Extortion-related violence peaked in January, when police recorded 44 extortion threats and 10 of those cases involved shootings. Since the beginning of the year, Surrey police have had 147 extortion threats reported, 21 of them involving shootings. In 2025, 49 of 133 extortion cases reported to police involved shootings. MacDonald said that as of Sept. 14 the Canada Border Services Agency had issued 188 removal orders and deported 111 individuals related to extortion cases. He said some victims are still targeted after suspects are arrested or removed, so investigations that look closed often are not. He also said the crime is not limited to Punjabi-Canadian business owners: employees, neighbours, suppliers and security guards are affected too. A $250,000 City of Surrey reward fund for information leading to arrests and conviction remains active. Source: vancouversun.com

Kirk LaPointe used his first speech as mayor of West Vancouver, after he and the new council were sworn in on the evening of Monday, November 2, to promise residents a greater voice in decisions, careful spending, and room for more people to build lives in the community. He defeated incumbent Mark Sager in last month’s civic election.

His career runs through newsrooms: managing editor of the Vancouver Sun, executive editor of the National Post, CBC ombudsman for English services, publisher and executive editor of Business in Vancouver, and an executive at Glacier Media, now Lodestar Media, until 2023. On the campaign he promised to bring the projected 2027 municipal tax increase down from nearly seven per cent to three per cent, and after 2027 to cap annual municipal spending growth at half the inflation rate. He promised to eliminate municipal pay parking, to revitalize the commercial villages of Ambleside, Dundarave, Horseshoe Bay and Caulfeild, and to seek provincial permission for a tax on vacant storefronts. His housing pitch was smaller-scale multi-unit housing for seniors, young families and first responders, while preserving neighbourhood character. An urgent and primary care centre was his first priority for a public-private partnership. He also proposed arts, skating and seniors’ facilities, restoring advisory committees, twice-monthly public question-and-answer sessions, and fewer closed-door council meetings. He told the inauguration that council cannot promise everyone the answer they want, but can promise respect, openness and an honest account of decisions. Both Sides: Pacific Link pipeline

Ottawa’s national-interest designation for the proposed Pacific Link oil pipeline is now the subject of eight separate Federal Court challenges from First Nations in B.C. The line, the first project designated under the Building Canada Act, would move an additional one million barrels a day from the Edmonton area to a new marine export terminal in Tsawwassen, largely along the Trans Mountain corridor. Global News puts the estimated cost between $35.2 billion and $42.7 billion. The final route still depends on design, review, consultation and approvals. Premier-designate Lorne Doerkson has pledged to work with Alberta and Ottawa to advance it, with major ongoing economic benefits for B.C. in return.

The federal case, as set out when Prime Minister Mark Carney and Alberta Premier Danielle Smith announced the designation last month, is that the line would reach more Asian customers, reduce dependence on the United States, and give Alberta, B.C. and Ottawa a long-term revenue source. Indigenous communities are to be offered at least a 10 percent ownership interest, backed by federal and Alberta loan guarantees. The Major Projects Office said it had consulted more than 130 Indigenous communities over three months; further consultation would set conditions, which Ottawa aims to finalize by September 2027 so construction can begin. National-interest status gives advance federal approval subject to those conditions, folding permits into one document.

The case against, from the nations filing, is that the designation was made without the consultation the law requires and without an impact assessment. St’át’imc Chiefs Council chair Justin Kane said the threat of a spill to Fraser River salmon, fishing rights and “our way of being” is heinous, and that the St’át’imc Nation as a whole was not consulted. The council, representing 11 communities, says the proposal means more than 500 river crossings, including two new Fraser crossings, and would increase tanker traffic in the Salish Sea. Tsartlip spokesman Adam Olsen called the process reckless and rushed, completed in less than three months for a project not expected to be finished until 2032, in fishing areas the nation is responsible for. Elder Art Adolph said governments and industry would push First Nations further into third-world conditions through harm to wild salmon. Grand Chief Stewart Phillip, president of the Union of B.C. Indian Chiefs, told corporations to beware when investing in B.C., because “we are not standing down.” Source: globalnews.ca Source: dailyhive.com Source: vancouversun.com

Feds list Port of Vancouver's $5.9 billion Roberts Bank Terminal 2 as project of national interest: Vancouver Sun

The federal government has named Roberts Bank Terminal 2, the planned container expansion at the Port of Vancouver in Delta, a project of national interest. Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon announced the listing Friday, October 30, at the Delta port, with Delta MP Jill McKnight, B.C. premier-designate Lorne Doerkson, Tsawwassen First Nation Chief Laura Cassidy, and Vancouver Fraser Port Authority president and CEO Peter Xotta beside him. The Surrey Now-Leader reports the step is meant to streamline approvals for the $5.9-billion project and to accelerate commercial operations by up to four years, to 2035 rather than 2039. Business in Vancouver reports the expansion is expected to add 2.4 million containers of capacity. The Vancouver Sun reports this is the second project fast-tracked by Prime Minister Mark Carney's major projects office.

MacKinnon called the Port of Vancouver Canada's largest by a long margin. He said it connects Canadian businesses to more than 170 markets and handles about one-third of Canada's trade outside the United States. He tied the expansion to a federal goal of doubling exports to non-U.S. markets by 2035, and said an investment in the port is an investment in farmers, manufacturers, producers, exporters, small businesses, and communities across the country. Carney said in a statement that the listing better connects the economy with the world and diversifies trade, and that Canada will not wait as the world competes for capital. The Surrey Now-Leader reports the designation lets the project bypass remaining federal permits and authorizations. The Major Projects Office will keep coordinating consultation with Indigenous communities, and a conditions document will be issued by the minister responsible under the act, currently Internal Trade Minister Dominic Leblanc. Senior government officials told a technical briefing that document can be ready within a couple of months. Environmental protections are among the conditions, including effects on southern resident killer whales, described as an endangered species, and on chinook salmon. The port authority has proposed avoidance, mitigation, and offsetting, including measures for underwater noise, and has committed to 102 hectares of fish-habitat offsetting.

Chief Laura Cassidy said Tsawwassen First Nation's support is not unconditional. She said the nation still has concerns about the long-term health of the Fraser River estuary and the Salish Sea, and that years of consultation produced accommodations for Tsawwassen harvesters, environmental measures, and commitments to ongoing oversight. She said the nation sought assurances the Building Canada Act would not be used to cut corners or weaken protections, and that Canada committed to upholding conditions the nation has long fought for and to managing impacts in the spirit of their treaty. Cassidy said a high priority is a breach in the Roberts Bank Causeway to improve habitat access for young salmon, and that major projects should aim for net environmental gain rather than only minimizing harm. City of Richmond moves to acquire Watermania aquatic centre through expropriation ahead of lease expiry: Daily Hive Vancouver

The City of Richmond is moving to take ownership of Watermania by expropriation as the long-term lease nears its expiry in March 2027. Daily Hive reports a formal expropriation notice dated July 3, 2026, was filed with the Land Title Office on July 6. It identifies 14300 Entertainment Blvd. Investments as the registered owner. Watermania sits at the Riverport Sports and Entertainment Complex in southeast Richmond, beside Silver City Riverport Cinemas, Splitsville Bowl Riverport and Richmond Ice Centre. The City operates the centre as a public recreation facility but does not own the building or the land; both have been leased from a private landowner since the centre was completed nearly 30 years ago. The notice says acquiring the property would serve the public interest by ensuring Watermania continues providing essential community services as a public indoor aquatic centre and fitness facility, and by supporting long-term financial and asset management through a fee-simple interest. Services could be provided by Richmond or by another public authority, person or organization acting on its behalf. The notice does not set out a purchase price or compensation offer. The private landowner has initiated a formal independent inquiry, scheduled to begin in late November 2026. Outgoing Mayor Malcom Brodie told Daily Hive that expropriation is used very infrequently and avoided whenever possible, that Watermania is an important recreation facility, and that the City’s goal is to secure its long-term future. He said that because the matter is before an independent inquiry, it would not be appropriate to comment further, and that the City respects the process and looks forward to a fair outcome.

The lease arrangement dates to the 1990s. Richmond voters approved borrowing about $20 million in a 1993 referendum to build a publicly owned aquatic centre on City-owned land. Council later cancelled that capital project because it wanted to avoid additional debt, and selected a proposal from the Riverport developer, which financed construction and leased the centre to the City. Watermania opened in 1997. BC Assessment’s latest roll values it at $49.4 million — $27.41 million for the land and $21.99 million for the building — as estimated market value as of July 1, 2025. In 2019 the City bought the adjacent Richmond Ice Centre, also privately developed and leased for public recreation, when that lease came up for renewal. The City said money previously spent on annual lease payments there would help fund renovations. Richmond Olympic Oval, by contrast, was built as a City-owned facility for the 2010 Winter Olympics. The City operates four public swimming facilities: year-round indoor swimming at Watermania and Minoru Centre for Active Living, and seasonal outdoor pools at South Arm and Steveston.

The people most directly affected are Richmond residents who use the indoor wave pool, two long waterslides and the 57-metre competition pool with a movable bulkhead, plus the fitness centre, diving boards and tower, whirlpools, steam room and sauna. Brodie’s stated case is that ownership secures that public use for the long term. The landowner’s response now on the record is the independent inquiry set for late November 2026. Daily Hive also notes a separate uncertainty over value: in August 2025, B.C. Supreme Court Justice Barbara Young granted the Cowichan Tribes Aboriginal title over about 740 acres of public and private land north and east of Riverport. Both Sides: Grocery prices

The B.C. NDP and the B.C. Greens are offering plans to lower grocery costs in the provincial campaign. The B.C. Conservatives say tax cuts, not shelf-price rules, are the way to help people pay for food. Voters finish casting ballots on Saturday.

NDP leader David Eby wants a cap on the mark-up on essentials such as milk, eggs, cheese, butter and poultry, applied to corporate retailers. He singled out Loblaws, Save-On-Foods, Walmart, Costco and Sobeys, which operates Safeway. The Greens propose government-owned and government-managed grocery stores, starting with 20 stores and a $100 million investment, sourcing local products and selling a basket of core goods 30 per cent below market prices. The Conservatives say lowering taxes puts more money in people's pockets for groceries.

Chris Bodnar, an assistant professor of agriculture at the University of the Fraser Valley, says milk is often a loss leader, with thin or negative margins, used to pull shoppers toward other goods. He says a cap gets attention but does not solve the problem, and that it misses climate change, conflicts that raise fertilizer and fuel costs, and the land and water B.C. needs to grow more food. The Vancouver Sun cites a Harvard Business School study that found small, temporary relief on capped items while major grocers raised prices on other products, and says the International Monetary Fund advises against broad grocery price controls because they send the wrong signals and can disrupt supply, preferring cash transfers. Hungary has capped staples since 2022 and, in 2025, capped retail profit at 10 per cent on more than 30 essential foods. France in 2023 negotiated a price freeze with major chains instead of legislating one.

Tammara Soma, an associate professor at Simon Fraser University, says public stores would give shoppers and farmers another option. She points to about 240 U.S. military commissaries, where shoppers save an average of 25 per cent, and to Mexico's subsidized stores selling staples below market prices. She says the five main B.C. grocers sometimes will not buy produce that looks slightly off, and a public outlet could take that food. The Sun notes that government-run stores carry an ongoing cost. Forty-nine percent of the vote made Melissa Granum Delta's new mayor Saturday night.

What You Need to Know: Melissa Granum is Delta's new mayor after taking 49 percent of the vote Saturday night, ahead of Dylan Kruger and George Harvie. Top Stories

Melissa Granum elected mayor of Delta: Vancouver Sun

Melissa Granum won the Delta mayoral race with 49 percent of the vote, 14,988 ballots, in results the Vancouver Sun reported at 10 p.m. Dylan Kruger was second with 28 percent, 8,567 votes, and George Harvie third with 23 percent, 6,985. The Sun said the early figures were advance and mail-in votes, and that Granum's lead held as more polls reported. Granum told the Sun there had been "a groundswell of support for hope for the future, and transparent governance and change," and that the campaign had been "about everyday residents working together for change." Source: vancouversun.com

Doug McCallum unseats Brenda Locke in Surrey: Vancouver Sun

Former mayor Doug McCallum unseated incumbent Brenda Locke, taking 31.9 percent of the vote to Locke's 23 percent with all 164 polls reporting, 42,741 votes to 30,886. Locke conceded just before 10 p.m., telling a solemn crowd, "Surrey voters have definitely spoken and this was not the result we counted on," then congratulated McCallum and, asked if she would run again, said, "Never say never." McCallum said, "We will address affordability, we will address your safety, and we will address your infrastructure," and said he planned to appoint Mandeep Nagra as deputy mayor. Mike Starchuk finished with 12.9 percent and Linda Annis with 12.39 percent. Safe Surrey won a council majority; the only councillor not on that slate is incumbent Harry Bains, from Locke's team, who received the most votes of any candidate. The rest of council is incumbents Doug Elford and Mandeep Nagra, former councillors Allison Patton and Laurie Guerra, and newcomers Meera Gill, Suhana Gill and Cheney Cloke. Elford called the result surprising and said residents could expect the slate to carry out its promises, including a four-year property-tax freeze, purchasing a police helicopter and seeking funding for another Surrey hospital, "as best as we can." A Leger poll for Postmedia News, conducted Sept. 23 to 30, had suggested Locke would receive a second term. Advance voting was 52,600 ballots, about 15 percent of registered voters, up 47 percent from 35,701 advance votes in 2022. McCallum previously served as mayor from 1996 to 2005 and from 2018 until his loss to Locke in 2022; Locke won that race by less than 1,000 votes. The Sun reported the two became rivals over policing, with McCallum wanting a municipal force and Locke wanting to keep the RCMP. Source: vancouversun.com

The Closer Look

Mike Hurley wins third term as Burnaby mayor: Freshet News

Mike Hurley has won a third term as mayor of Burnaby, and the Burnaby Citizens Association kept a majority on city council. Freshet News reported unofficial results from Saturday's vote. Hurley won a six-way mayoral race with 28,946 votes, 58.43 percent of 50,326 ballots cast. Burnaby First candidate Heymann Yip was second with 9,250 votes, 18.67 percent.

He thanked residents for electing him to another four years and said, "It's an amazing journey, an opportunity to get out there and help people, which is something that I live to do."

The council ballot had 42 candidates and several new parties. The BCA majority held. Three BCA incumbents did not run: Sav Dhaliwal, Pietro Calendino and Alison Gu. Three new BCA candidates were elected in their place: Cindy Lee, Ryan Erwin and Kiran Singh. New Burnaby Party incumbent Richard Lee remains the only non-BCA member of council. Freshet News said it was unable to reach Lee for comment on election night.

Joe Keithley, first elected with the Burnaby Green Party and switched to the BCA in December, led the council count with 18,073 votes, 5.51 percent. Cindy Lee was next with 17,429, 5.32 percent, then Ryan Erwin with 16,476, 5.03 percent. Lee said she had not expected that finish and credited support from her own community and from running with the BCA team. She said many residents had read the candidates' profiles and recognized her while she was door-knocking, and that "in Burnaby, our residents are really responsible. That's why we have a great city."

Incumbent BCA councillors Maita Santiago and Daniel Tetrault each kept a seat for a second term, with 16,292 votes, 4.97 percent, and 15,288, 4.66 percent. Richard Lee also won a second term, with 13,257 votes, 4.04 percent. Kiran Singh took the last council seat, finishing 1,180 votes ahead of independent Allen Chen: Singh had 13,209 votes, 4.03 percent, and Chen 12,029, 3.67 percent.

Voter turnout in Burnaby rose to 29.23 percent of eligible voters, from 19.7 percent in 2022. Source: freshetnews.ca
