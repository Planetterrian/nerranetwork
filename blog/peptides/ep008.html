Peptides Weekly Blog

Six unapproved peptides can be compounded in US pharmacies without an FDA safety finding.

By Patrick November 05, 2026 Ep 8 9 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents
The Week in Peptides Also Today Peptide Spotlight: Why Most Peptides Are Injected — and Oral Semaglutide Worth Knowing Evidence Ledger

What You Need to Know: Six unapproved peptides can be compounded in US pharmacies without an FDA safety finding. Health and Human Services has said it will not enforce against licensed compounders of that set, even though the FDA's drug center opposed the idea. A pharmacy company now says it will start dispensing them in November.

The Week in Peptides

HHS greenlights sale of six unapproved peptides despite FDA guidance: BioXconomy

In a late-October regulatory action, not a clinical trial, the US Department of Health and Human Services said it does not intend to take enforcement action against state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities, or licensed physicians who compound six peptide substances the Food and Drug Administration has not approved. The six are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500, the same set an FDA advisory committee recommended for compounding in July. The enforcement policy does not approve the drugs or find them safe or effective. It does ask for preliminary identity and quality standards, and officials describe the aim as moving patients away from research-use-only products sold online. Source: bioxconomy.com

HHS Allows Pharmacies to Dispense Six Unapproved Peptides: Demokratis

The policy, a regulatory notice rather than a safety trial, was announced on October 28, 2026, and signed by Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Peptides, in that notice, are short chains of amino acids that can be given by injection, and the FDA had restricted these substances in 2023. Lee Rosebush, chair of the American Academy of Peptide Medicine, called the shift a step away from gray and black markets and toward physician-supervised care. Fred Mills, chief policy officer for Americans for Safe and Effective Medicines, warned against treating consumers as test subjects. Source: demokratis.co.id

DataMEDS AI Secures Supply Agreement for Six Compounded Peptides Following FDA Advisory Committee Recommendations: Yahoo Finance

This is a company announcement, not a study in people. DataMEDS AI said its pharmacy subsidiary Corexa Health has an agreement to supply bulk drug substance and active ingredient for those six peptides, and that Corexa Pharmacy intends to begin dispensing compounded prescriptions in November 2026. The company said the peptides are not FDA-approved and have not been evaluated by the FDA for safety or efficacy. It said it plans to use a mobile app, Health Lives Here, to collect and report safety data under the Health and Human Services enforcement-discretion policy published in the Federal Register on October 30, 2026. Interim co-CEO Gerald Commissiong said the aim is a licensed-pharmacy option in place of unregulated research-use-only products sold online. Source: finance.yahoo.com

Oral semaglutide over 24 months in one Italian clinic: Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism

In a retrospective real-world study of 232 adults with type 2 diabetes who started oral semaglutide at an Italian diabetes clinic, not a randomized trial, HbA1c fell by 0.8 percentage points over 24 months, a reduction the authors called significant. The same observational follow-up reported sustained improvements in glycaemic control, body weight and lipid profile, with preserved renal function and high treatment persistence. The packet does not include the size of the weight change. Because people were not randomly assigned, the before-and-after changes cannot by themselves prove the tablet caused every shift. Source: europepmc.org

Oral semaglutide and cardiovascular events in insurance claims: Diabetes Therapy

In an observational claims study, not a randomized trial, new users of oral semaglutide who had type 2 diabetes and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease had a 17 percent lower risk of 3-point major adverse cardiovascular events and a 21 percent lower risk of a modified 3-point version than 28,639 new users of other noninsulin glucose-lowering therapies, among 10,878 oral-semaglutide users. Against 7,218 matched new users of DPP-4 inhibitors, 7,218 oral-semaglutide users had 22 percent and 24 percent lower risks of those same two outcomes. Against 21,572 SGLT2-inhibitor users, 7,491 oral-semaglutide users had 21 percent and 22 percent lower risks. The authors also reported significantly lower risks of broader cardiovascular composites, ischemic stroke and all-cause death versus the mixed comparator group. Association in billing records is not proof that the tablet caused the difference. Source: europepmc.org

Also Today

Shilpa Medicare invests in peptide capacity as GLP-1 demand keeps outrunning supply: BioProcess International

Shilpa Medicare is investing roughly fifty million dollars to add solid-phase peptide synthesis at its Raichur site in India, with commercial-scale output targeted for 2028, while the report says fill-finish is a tighter bottleneck than synthesis. Source: bioprocessintl.com

Six Peptides From Our January Guide Just Got a Federal Green Light. Licensed Fighters Still Cannot Touch Them: Boxing Insider

A sports outlet reports that licensed fighters still cannot use the six peptides that just received a US compounding green light. Source: boxinginsider.com

Peptide Spotlight: Why Most Peptides Are Injected — and Oral Semaglutide

A peptide drug is a short chain of amino acids, and the stomach and intestine are built to take chains like that apart. That is the practical reason most therapeutic peptides are injected: an injection skips digestion and puts the molecule into tissue or blood before enzymes can cut it up. Oral delivery has to solve two problems at once. The peptide has to survive the gut, and enough of it has to cross the gut wall to matter. A 2016 review of intestinal permeation enhancers assessed more than 250 enhancers tested in intestinal delivery models, mostly preclinical work, and stressed that enhancer and peptide have to arrive together at the small-intestinal lining in high enough concentration. Proprietary enhancer systems have reached clinical trials, but that review is not a trial of any one approved tablet. A companion 2016 review of lipid-based nanocarriers for oral peptides and proteins is likewise an overview of formulation design — particle size, surface charge, how much drug is loaded, how fast it is released — and how those choices change the trip through the gut and the resulting drug levels. It does not show that a lipid particle makes every peptide orally effective in people.

Oral semaglutide is the exception listeners actually meet in clinic. Semaglutide is a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, a peptide class that, in a 2019 review, is described as highly effective at lowering hemoglobin A1c and helping weight loss when given by injection. That same review says four injected agents in the class — albiglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide and semaglutide — also have cardioprotective effects, and that the need for shots is a major reason the class has been underused. The oral product pairs semaglutide with sodium N-[8-(2-hydroxybenzoyl) amino caprylate, known as SNAC. In that review's account of the formulation, SNAC limits destruction of semaglutide in the stomach and helps it cross the gastric membrane so some of the peptide reaches the bloodstream intact. The review calls this the first oral GLP-1 receptor agonist reviewed by the US Food and Drug Administration, and says the PIONEER program showed efficacy on HbA1c and weight similar to injected GLP-1 drugs already in use. Those are review-level statements. This packet does not include a bioavailability percentage, so a number for "how much gets in" is not established here.

The strongest human test in the packet is PIONEER 6, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled cardiovascular-safety trial in 3,183 people with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk. Median time in the trial was 15.9 months. Major adverse cardiovascular events — death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal heart attack, or nonfatal stroke — occurred in 61 of 1,591 people on oral semaglutide (3.8 percent) and 76 of 1,592 on placebo (4.8 percent), hazard ratio 0.79. The trial was built to rule out an 80 percent excess cardiovascular risk versus placebo. The published conclusion is that the cardiovascular risk profile was not inferior to placebo. The confidence interval ran from 0.57 to 1.11, so the result is also compatible with no difference. It is not proof that the tablet prevents heart attacks.

PIONEER 4, a randomized, double-blind, phase 3a trial, assigned 711 adults with type 2 diabetes at 100 sites in 12 countries to oral semaglutide, injected liraglutide, or placebo for 52 weeks, on a stable metformin background with or without an SGLT2 inhibitor. The primary question was change in HbA1c at week 26, with a plan to test superiority to placebo, non-inferiority to liraglutide within a 0.4 percentage-point margin, and then superiority to liraglutide, plus a confirmatory weight comparison. Dose levels used in that escalation are also left out of this briefing on purpose.

What is not established from these sources: that swallowing a peptide is generally equivalent to injecting it; that SNAC would rescue an unrelated peptide; a numeric bioavailability for oral semaglutide; the week-26 HbA1c and weight figures from PIONEER 4; and any claim that PIONEER 6 proved a cardiovascular benefit rather than non-inferiority. A 2019 review notes that suggested effects on cardiovascular mortality might not be fully clear until a later trial, SOUL, which is not reported in this packet.

Regulatory status, as these sources state it: the 2019 review describes oral semaglutide as the first oral GLP-1 receptor agonist reviewed by the FDA. Known risks that are actually in the packet are limited. Oral semaglutide is a specific formulated medicine. It is not a reason to treat unapproved compounded peptides, or research-use-only vials, as if the same stomach-absorption trick applied to them.

What it is: Oral semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist peptide paired with SNAC so some of it can cross the stomach wall instead of being injected.

Humans given it: Randomized trials assigned 3,183 people in PIONEER 6 and 711 in PIONEER 4; separate observational studies followed 232 clinic patients and compared thousands of new users in claims data.

Risks: PIONEER 6 found cardiovascular risk was not inferior to placebo, but the result still allowed no difference, and side-effect rates are not in these excerpts.

What it does not show: It does not show that peptides in general work as pills, that oral semaglutide beat placebo for heart events, or any dose to take.

Worth Knowing

  • A randomized heart-safety trial can clear a peptide tablet as not worse than placebo without showing that it prevents heart attacks.
  • Clinic follow-up and insurance-claims comparisons can track blood sugar and cardiovascular diagnoses in ordinary care, and still cannot prove the drug caused the change.
  • An enforcement pause on six unapproved peptides is not evidence that those peptides work, or that they are the same kind of product as an oral GLP-1 medicine reviewed by the FDA.

Evidence Ledger

  • More than 250 intestinal permeation enhancers have been tested in intestinal delivery models, mostly with a preclinical emphasis, and co-presentation of enhancer and peptide at the gut lining matters. Review. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27320643
  • Lipid nanocarriers for oral peptides have been reviewed for how composition and particle properties relate to gut interaction and drug levels, not as a human efficacy trial. Review. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27080735
  • SNAC is described as protecting semaglutide in the stomach and aiding gastric absorption, and oral semaglutide is described as the first oral GLP-1 receptor agonist reviewed by the FDA. Review, 2019. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31436480
  • In a randomized trial of 3,183 people, oral semaglutide was non-inferior to placebo for major cardiovascular events, with event rates of 3.8 percent and 4.8 percent. Randomized human trial. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31185157
  • PIONEER 4 randomly assigned 711 people to oral semaglutide, injected liraglutide, or placebo, with HbA1c at week 26 as the primary endpoint; outcome figures are not in the excerpt. Randomized phase 3a trial. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31186120

Peptides are a chemical class, not a quality stamp — the evidence rung is what tells them apart.
