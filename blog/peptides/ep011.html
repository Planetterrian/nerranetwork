Sixty-two percent of women on GLP-1s moved up a breast-density category, driving extra mammogram callbacks.

By Patrick November 26, 2026 Ep 11 8 min read Listen to podcast
Follow Peptides Weekly Apple Podcasts RSS
Listen to this episode

The Week in Peptides Also Today Peptide Spotlight: GHK-Cu: The Copper Peptide in Skincare Worth Knowing Evidence Ledger

What You Need to Know: In an observational comparison of paired screening mammograms, not a randomized trial, about sixty-two percent of women who had used GLP-1 drugs for weight loss moved up at least one breast-density category. The same unpublished meeting report found a callback rate of 22.4 percent, against an average U.S. recall rate of about 10 percent, and it does not establish a change in long-term breast cancer risk.

The Week in Peptides

Study finds increased breast density and callbacks among women taking GLP-1 drugs: Mezha.net

Researchers compared screening mammograms from before and after at least a year of GLP-1 receptor agonist use for weight loss in 107 women ages 34 to 79, an observational before-and-after review rather than a randomized trial, and the work is scheduled for the Radiological Society of North America meeting in Chicago from November 29 to December 3, 2026, and has not yet been peer-reviewed for a journal. The drugs named include Ozempic and Wegovy. Participants lost between six and 107 pounds, with an average loss of 40 pounds, and the reviewers looked at 214 mammograms. Specialized software showed breast density up by at least one BI-RADS category in 62.5 percent of participants, eight women moved up two categories, and no decreases were observed; Everyday Health, quoting lead author Dr. Eva C. Gombos of Brigham and Women’s Hospital, reported that the share classified as dense nearly tripled, from about 15 percent to about 42 percent. A Reuters account of the same meeting report said density increased in every case and that greater weight loss tracked with a greater density increase. Depending on the reviewer, radiologists saw smaller breasts in roughly 58 to 79 percent of patients and increased skin thickening in about 9 to 18 percent. Nearly 22.4 percent were called back for additional imaging, compared with an average U.S. recall rate of about 10 percent; two women had technical recalls for positioning or imaging issues, four were referred for biopsies, and all four results were benign. Gombos said the pattern may reflect disproportionate loss of breast fat, so mammographic density rises even if absolute fibroglandular volume decreases, and both she and Cleveland Clinic breast radiologist Melanie Chellman, who was not involved, said it is not known whether this kind of density rise changes long-term breast cancer risk. Chellman said radiologists have long seen smaller, less fatty, denser breasts after weight loss with or without a GLP-1, and that what is new is how common substantial weight loss has become. Gombos said the findings do not suggest that patients should be concerned about taking GLP-1 medications. Source: mezha.net · wtvbam.com · everydayhealth.com

STEP Young trial shows semaglutide benefits in children: Nature Medicine

A Nature Medicine news report on a phase 3 study says children with obesity lost significantly more weight with semaglutide than with placebo, and that more than one third reached a body mass index below the threshold for obesity. Source: nature.com

Health Canada seizes peptides from Toronto shop, warns of health risks of unregulated market: CBC

After earlier regulator warnings about unregulated peptide products, CBC reports that Health Canada has seized peptides from a Toronto shop and warned about health risks of the unregulated market. That is an enforcement action, not a clinical study of any named peptide’s effect. Source: cbc.ca

Duke Signs On to LSALT Peptide Heart Surgery Kidney Trial; Genentech Study It Hosted Ended Early: Tech Times

Tech Times reports that Duke has signed on to an LSALT peptide trial in heart-surgery-related kidney injury, and that a Genentech study the center hosted ended early. The item is a site-and-trial announcement, not a published patient outcome for LSALT peptide. Source: techtimes.com

Pharmacies Inundated by Peptide Requests: Drug Topics

Drug Topics reported on November 19, 2026, that patients and prescribers are flooding pharmacies with peptide questions, and that an expert framed the pharmacist’s role as turning that confusion into counseling. This is a professional-practice report, not a trial result. Source: drugtopics.com

Also Today

Observational Study Links GLP-1 Therapy to Reduced Pain and Circumference in Lipedema: Bioengineer.org

Source: bioengineer.org

Not So Fast: Kentucky Holds The Line On Peptide Compounding: JD Supra

JD Supra reports that Kentucky is holding the line on peptide compounding, a legal and regulatory development rather than a clinical finding. Source: jdsupra.com

newsGP - National peptides inquiry to probe vertically integrated telehealth: Royal Australian College of General Practitioners

The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners reports a national peptides inquiry that will probe vertically integrated telehealth. Source: www1.racgp.org.au

Peptide Spotlight: GHK-Cu: The Copper Peptide in Skincare

GHK is a human tripeptide: glycine, histidine, and lysine, written in the chemistry as glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine. A 2018 review in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences, carried on Europe PMC under the title “Regenerative and Protective Actions of the GHK-Cu Peptide in the Light of the New Gene Data,” is the main source here. The title names GHK-Cu, the copper-bound form that shows up in skincare claims. The abstract’s statements are written for GHK, the peptide itself. These two papers do not describe a cosmetic authorization, a drug approval, or an investigational trial phase for GHK-Cu.

In plain terms, the review says GHK stimulates outgrowth of blood vessels and nerves, increases synthesis of collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycans, and supports dermal fibroblasts. Collagen and elastin are structural proteins in connective tissue. Glycosaminoglycans are sugar chains that hold water in that matrix. Fibroblasts are the cells that make much of it. On that account, GHK is being described as a signal that pushes repair machinery already present in tissue, not as a filler that sits in the skin and props it up. The abstract says an ability to improve tissue repair has been demonstrated for skin, lung connective tissue, bony tissue, liver, and stomach lining. It does not, in the text provided, say which of those demonstrations were in cells, which in animals, and which in people. That missing split matters. A review can gather real experiments and still leave a listener unable to tell the rung.

The same abstract lists cell-protective actions the authors, “according to our current knowledge,” describe as health-positive: multiple anti-cancer activities, anti-inflammatory actions, lung protection, restoration of fibroblasts from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, suppression of NFκB (which they call a molecule thought to accelerate diseases of aging), activities they label anti-anxiety, anti-pain, and anti-aggression, DNA repair, and activation of cell cleansing through the proteasome. The proteasome is the cell’s protein-disposal system. The authors then point to genetic data as a possible explanation for why one short peptide would touch so many processes: multiple biochemical pathways regulated by GHK. That is a review-level interpretation of gene data, not a new human experiment, and it is not a measurement of wrinkles, wounds, or cancer outcomes in a counted group of people.

The second paper is older, and it is not a GHK study. In 1983, investigators reported that L-ascorbic acid, combined with copper(II) ion or with a copper(II) tripeptide, extensively cleaved several viral DNAs and proteins under in vitro conditions. The tripeptide was diglycyl-L-histidine, a copper-binding three-amino-acid peptide related in kind to GHK, not identified in the paper as GHK. Neither ascorbate alone nor the copper tripeptide alone caused apparent changes in those molecules. Among transition metals tested with ascorbate, copper and iron were the two most effective. Catalase, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide, partially inhibited DNA scission; superoxide dismutase did not. The authors take that pattern to suggest that hydrogen peroxide is involved, and that one product is probably the hydroxyl radical generated from that peroxide. Among reducing agents they tested, ascorbate was the most effective at causing DNA scission and protein cleavage. Evidence level: cell-free chemistry on viral DNA and proteins, not a human safety trial, and not a test of a cosmetic GHK-Cu cream.

What these sources do not establish is as important as what they list. They do not report how many people were given GHK or GHK-Cu. They do not report a randomized human comparison of a copper-peptide cream against a plain cream for any skin outcome. They do not show that anti-cancer activity in the review’s summary becomes fewer cancers in patients. They do not show that the 1983 cleavage reaction happens in living skin, or that it does not. A laboratory list of repair pathways is not a drug claim, and a product label that borrows that list has not, in these papers, been shown to match a measured human result.

The chemical risk that is actually on the page is specific. A related copper tripeptide, inert by itself in that assay, cut viral DNA and protein once ascorbate was added, through a peroxide-linked route the authors tie to the hydroxyl radical. That is a reason not to treat “copper peptide” as automatically gentle, and it is not a recipe, a dose, or a reason to start or stop any product. Purity and contamination of unregulated products are a separate problem these two papers do not document.

What it is: GHK is a human three-amino-acid peptide, glycine-histidine-lysine, and the review title refers to its copper-bound form, GHK-Cu.

Humans given it: These abstracts do not report how many people, if any, were given GHK or GHK-Cu.

Risks: In a 1983 test-tube study, a related copper tripeptide plus ascorbate cleaved viral DNA and protein, with hydrogen peroxide and a likely hydroxyl radical implicated.

What it does not show: Neither paper shows a counted human skin, wound, or cancer outcome, or that cosmetic use matches the repair list in the review.

Worth Knowing

  • An observational mammogram comparison, not a cancer-outcome trial, means a density-category rise after GLP-1 weight loss can change what a screening image looks like without answering whether cancer risk moved.
  • A phase 3 report in children with obesity means semaglutide separated from placebo on weight in that trial, and more than one third crossed below the obesity threshold on body mass index, which is a trial result rather than a reason to start or stop treatment.

Evidence Ledger

  • A 2018 review describes GHK as stimulating vessel and nerve outgrowth, increasing collagen, elastin, and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, supporting fibroblasts, and touching repair and cell-protection pathways, without stating human headcounts; review-level evidence. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29986520
  • A 1983 in vitro study found that ascorbate plus copper ion or a copper-diglycyl-histidine complex cleaved viral DNA and proteins, while either partner alone did not, with catalase partially blocking DNA scission; cell-free chemistry. https://europepmc.org/article/MED/6654857

Education is the point of the week: a density shift on a mammogram, a phase 3 weight result in children, and a copper-peptide review that never counts the people.
