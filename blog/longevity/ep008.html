An observational study of 120,000 older adults links bone drugs to 24 percent lower dementia risk.

By Patrick November 11, 2026 Ep 8 11 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents
The Week in Longevity Also Today Mechanism of the Week: Caloric Restriction and Fasting Worth Knowing Evidence Ledger Trial Tracker

What You Need to Know: An observational study of more than 120,000 older adults links nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, drugs used for bone loss, to a dementia risk as much as 24 percent lower. That finding is an association among people already taking the drugs, not a trial that assigned them to protect the brain, and the report does not show any regulator has approved them to prevent dementia. Elsewhere this week, an eye-risk warning, a chemotherapy aging model, and a two-year calorie trial all turn on the same distinction: a marker or an association is not a longer healthy life.

The Week in Longevity

Common osteoporosis drugs linked to 24% lower dementia risk in major study: ScienceDaily

In a study of more than 120,000 older adults, not a randomized trial, researchers at the University of Hong Kong found that patients taking nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates were significantly less likely to develop Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, by as much as 24 percent, and published the work in Alzheimer's & Dementia. Those drugs are widely prescribed to prevent bone loss and fractures; the paper does not report regulatory approval for preventing dementia. The same team had already reported that osteoporosis and fractures are independent risk factors for dementia, and that people living with dementia are more likely to fall and break bones. More than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia, a figure the authors say is projected to reach 139 million by 2050, which is why they frame existing drugs as a prevention question rather than a proven brain treatment. Shared risk factors in the report include older age, being female, and low physical activity. The authors call a second use for these bone drugs a possibility, not a demonstrated cause. Source: sciencedaily.com

Stronger flu shot linked to nearly 55% lower Alzheimer's risk: ScienceDaily

In an analysis of nearly 200,000 adults ages 65 and older, not described as a randomized trial, researchers at UTHealth Houston found that people who received a high-dose influenza vaccine had a nearly 55 percent lower risk of developing Alzheimer's, compared with a 40 percent reduction previously associated with the standard-dose vaccine. The report does not say regulators have approved influenza vaccines to prevent Alzheimer's. The comparison is between people who received one shot or the other, so it cannot by itself separate the vaccine from whatever else differed in those groups. Source: sciencedaily.com

GLP-1s are given an FDA warning for rare eye condition risk: STAT News

After this show's November 4 look at GLP-1 evidence beyond weight loss, the Food and Drug Administration warned that GLP-1 drugs carry a potential risk of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, a rare condition sometimes called an eye stroke, and required companies to inform patients and doctors. The agency said it began looking after a 2024 observational study linked Ozempic and Wegovy to the condition. The condition is caused by insufficient blood flow to the optic nerve, typically causes sudden vision loss, and in severe cases can lead to blindness; the report says there are currently no proven treatments. These sources do not report approval of GLP-1 drugs as a treatment for aging. Source: statnews.com Source: statnews.com

How Breast Cancer Chemotherapy May Impact Biological Aging and Loss of Healthy Life Years: Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology News

In a population-based simulation, not a trial that assigned an anti-aging treatment, researchers at Georgetown University's Lombardi Comprehensive Cancer Center estimated the lifetime balance of curing breast cancer against possible acceleration of biological aging, and reported the work in JAMA Network Open under the title "Chemotherapy-Induced Biological Age Acceleration and Lifetime Outcomes in Breast Cancer Survivors." Senior author Jeanne Mandelblatt said female breast cancer survivors are the largest segment of the 18.6 million cancer survivors in the United States, and that some chemotherapy that extends survival also causes treatment-induced molecular damage that accelerates biological age. She said that acceleration can persist for years, may not be visible for many years, and can mean earlier frailty, cognitive problems, and other aging diseases. Lead author Swarnavo Sarkar said the model accounts for chemotherapy improving breast-cancer survival while also damaging normal tissues, including by increasing senescent cells that have stopped dividing, resist cell death, and secrete molecules that add tissue damage and inflammation. Source: genengnews.com Source: medicalxpress.com

Anti-Fibrotic Drug Rentosertib Reduces Proteomic Clock Age in Humans: Fight Aging!

In human data from a phase 2 trial of the TNIK inhibitor rentosertib for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, researchers report that multiple proteomic aging clocks moved younger, which Fight Aging! presents as evidence the drug touches mechanisms of aging rather than fibrosis alone. Rentosertib was developed by In Silico Medicine and is described as anti-fibrotic. A proteomic clock reading is a biomarker change, not a measured extension of life, and these sources do not report approval of rentosertib for aging. The same commentary notes that rapamycin and related molecules already have comparatively robust data for affecting mechanisms of aging to some degree, which is a contrast in evidence, not a result from this trial. Source: fightaging.org

Mitochondria-derived extracellular vesicles mediate Alzheimer's disease pathology through transmitting microglial Ggt1: Nature Aging

In patient brain samples and mouse models, Li, Xia, Sun, Jia and colleagues report a pathological role for mitovesicles in Alzheimer's disease, and identify Ggt1 as an enriched cargo implicated in driving that pathology, in a paper published online in Nature Aging on November 10, 2026. This is human tissue plus animal models, not a treatment trial. Source: nature.com

Inflammaging and Poor Sleep Contribute to One Another: Lifespan.io

In a review of substantial literature, not a new trial, scientists publishing in Aging Cell concluded that poor sleep quality and inflammaging worsen each other. The claim is a synthesis of existing studies, not a measured effect in a newly enrolled group of people. Source: lifespan.io

Also Today

Evidence for Cerebrospinal Fluid Flow Through the Brain to Stagnate in Alzheimer's Disease: Fight Aging!

Fight Aging! describes cerebrospinal fluid drainage, including the glymphatic system, as declining with age and especially in Alzheimer's, with a role in clearing amyloid-β, α-synuclein, tau, and TDP-43, which is a mechanism account rather than a new human trial. Source: fightaging.org

NAD blood levels don't fall with age, study finds, challenging longevity claims: Nutraceutical Business Review

A study reported by Nutraceutical Business Review found that NAD levels in blood do not fall with age, a result the outlet says challenges longevity claims built on that decline. Source: nutraceuticalbusinessreview.com

Cepharanthine Identified as a Novel Senolytic: Fight Aging!

Fight Aging! reports cepharanthine, a plant extract, as a newly identified senolytic candidate, and notes that low-cost candidates of this kind have not tended to generate compelling human data. Source: fightaging.org

Mechanism of the Week: Caloric Restriction and Fasting

Caloric restriction, in the papers gathered here, means eating less energy than one would eat freely, without falling into malnutrition. A 2012 review in the American Journal of Human Biology calls it the most potent environmental intervention known to delay the onset of aging and extend life in diverse experimental organisms. That is an animal and laboratory claim, not a human lifespan result. The same review treats aging as the largest risk factor for many cancers, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes, and argues that knowing how restriction delays aging could eventually support treatments. It does not itself test one.

A 2013 review in the Journal of Cardiopulmonary Rehabilitation and Prevention sets a working definition: energy intake well below ad libitum, at least 10 percent in humans and at least 20 percent in animals. Looking across animal studies and a limited number of human trials, those authors say restriction has the potential to delay cardiac aging and to help prevent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease through effects on blood pressure, lipids, inflammatory processes, and possibly other mechanisms. Those are risk-factor and intermediate findings. The review says the impact of caloric restriction on human health is not fully understood, and that modest restriction inside a broader lifestyle program is likely to improve heart health in overweight and obese people on the basis of known cardiometabolic effects. It also says more study is needed for older adults and for people with serious illness such as heart failure. None of that is a measured human lifespan.

The strongest human experiment in this set is the two-year randomized trial reported in 2015 in The Journals of Gerontology, Series A, the study tied to the Comprehensive Assessment of the Long-term Effect of Reducing Intake of Energy, or CALERIE. Investigators randomized 218 nonobese adults aged 21 to 51 years, average body mass index 25.1, either to an intervention designed to reach 25 percent restriction or to an ad libitum diet. Eighty-two percent of the restriction group and 95 percent of the comparison group finished. The restriction group achieved 11.7 percent restriction, not the 25 percent design target, and maintained 10.4 percent weight loss. Weight change in the ad libitum group was negligible. Resting metabolic rate adjusted for weight change fell more with restriction at 12 months, but not at 24 months. Core temperature barely differed between groups. Plasma triiodothyronine fell more in the restriction group at both 12 and 24 months. The authors call sustained restriction feasible in nonobese humans and say the pattern on correlates of survival and disease risk suggests potential benefits for aging-related outcomes that further human studies would have to show. They did not measure how long anyone lived.

Closer to humans than a mouse, and still not a person, is a 2026 cross-sectional study of rhesus monkeys in the journal Skeletal Muscle. Eighteen animals on 30 percent restriction were compared with 18 controls. High-depth RNA sequencing of skeletal muscle showed overexpression of genes involved in energy metabolism, mitochondrial function, signaling, and oxidative stress response, and underexpression of genes connected to immune response, extracellular matrix organization, apoptosis, and ribosomal RNA processing. Alternative splicing was a major route by which restriction changed genes involved in muscle function, metabolism, and aging. The authors conclude that restriction preserves skeletal muscle by enhancing metabolism and limiting degeneration and inflammation, through mechanisms conserved across species. That is an animal transcriptomic study. The abstract does not report a lifespan difference.

A 2025 Scientific Reports experiment asked a related question in human astrocytes in a dish, using the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxyglucose as a caloric-restriction mimic before stimulation with interleukin-1β. In that cell model, 2-deoxyglucose reduced oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification in the presence of the inflammatory signal, and lowered expression of the pro-inflammatory genes TNF, IL-6, and C3, alongside changes in chromatin accessibility. Glucose deprivation and the ketone β-hydroxybutyrate were also associated with lower cytokine expression, with additive effects when combined with 2-deoxyglucose. The authors suggest that targeting glycolysis might eventually inform therapies for neurological disease by changing astrocyte-driven inflammation. This is a cell study of a laboratory inhibitor, not evidence that a person should take 2-deoxyglucose, and these sources do not describe it as an approved aging treatment.

What the animal record cannot answer is set out in a 2007 review in The Journal of Nutrition. In rodents, lifespan extension scaled with how severe the restriction was, and the benefit shrank when treatment started later in life. A model in that paper suggested that a 48-year-old man who held 30 percent restriction until an expected death at 78 might gain 2.8 years of life expectancy. That number is a projection from animal patterns, not a result measured in men. The same review says animals remain hungry after long restriction, that neuroendocrine profiles fit ongoing hunger, and that it is questionable whether people could restrict for decades without long-term support. It also leaves open whether drugs that suppress appetite would reproduce the effect, or whether the biology of hunger itself is part of the mechanism. Exercise and cold can create energy deficits too, the authors note, but animals answer those deficits differently, with only a minor impact on lifespan.

No paper in this set shows that caloric restriction extends human life. The strongest finding is the randomized trial in 218 nonobese adults: about 12 percent restriction for two years was feasible and shifted some correlates of survival, including a thyroid hormone, while the adjusted metabolic-rate difference at one year was gone at two. These papers do not report that any regulator has approved caloric restriction as a medical treatment for aging.

Worth Knowing

  • An association in older adults already taking bone drugs is not evidence those drugs prevent dementia.
  • An analysis of older adults who received a flu shot is not a trial showing that vaccination prevents Alzheimer's.
  • A population simulation of breast-cancer survivors separates longer survival from later loss of healthy years driven by treatment damage, including senescent cells.
  • A two-year randomized trial of eating less in nonobese adults changed some survival-linked markers and did not measure lifespan.

Trial Tracker

  • CALERIE (Comprehensive Assessment of the Long-term Effect of Reducing Intake of Energy): caloric restriction versus ad libitum diet; two-year randomized trial in 218 nonobese adults, results reported in 2015; no next dated milestone is named in these sources.

Associations and clock shifts are clues about aging, not proof that a drug, a shot, or a smaller plate has added years.
