The Week in Longevity Mechanism of the Week: GLP-1 Drugs Beyond Weight Loss Worth Knowing Evidence Ledger Trial Tracker

What You Need to Know: Every extra hour of daytime napping tracked with a 13 percent higher death risk in 1,338 older adults, an observational link rather than proof that naps cause death. The same week, a small survey tied steadier daily schedules to less pain and depression without showing that routine is the cause, and major trials of semaglutide did not slow Alzheimer's disease. Pooled randomized trials still show fewer heart, kidney, and death events with GLP-1 receptor agonists in type 2 diabetes; that record is not a test of aging itself.

The Week in Longevity

Daytime Napping and Longevity: Study of 1,338 Older Adults Reveals Concerning Findings: Medical Dialogues

Scientists from Mass General Brigham and Rush University Medical Center followed 1,338 older adults in the Rush Memory and Aging Project, an observational study published in JAMA Network Open, not a trial that assigned naps. Participants wore wrist activity monitors for 10 days; health was then tracked for up to 19 years. Every additional hour of daytime napping was linked to a 13 percent higher mortality risk, and one extra nap a day to a 7 percent higher risk. People who napped in the morning had a 30 percent higher mortality risk than those who napped in the afternoon. Irregular sleeping schedules were not associated with higher risk. Lead author Chenlu Gao said excessive daytime sleeping could indicate illness, chronic conditions, sleep disturbance, or a disrupted internal clock. The researchers stressed a link, not a cause, and said further studies are needed before wearable nap patterns have established clinical value. Source: medicaldialogues.in Source: thenews.com.pk

Regular Daily Routines Linked to Less Pain, Depression in Older Adults: JournalArta

University of Missouri researcher Eunjin Tracy surveyed 67 older adults, an observational study that does not show routine causes less pain or depression. Thirty-seven reported insomnia, and those with insomnia reported more pain and more depression than the others. Participants with more consistent times for waking, eating, socializing, and going to bed tended to report less physical pain and fewer depressive symptoms, whatever their sleep quality. Tracy said that regardless of sleep quality, people who reported a regular daily routine had lower levels of physical pain and less depression than those without one. The National Council on Aging says roughly half of adults age 60 and older report insomnia symptoms. The write-up points to the circadian rhythm, including social jet lag between workdays and days off, as one possible explanation, not as a proven mechanism. Source: journalarta.com Source: aroundprague.cz

Ozempic and Mounjaro may have a surprising effect on your brain: ScienceDaily Fitness

What is new against the November 11 discussion of these drugs is a ScienceDaily report that major trials found semaglutide did not slow Alzheimer's disease, even as other studies have linked GLP-1 medications to a lower risk of dementia. Those are different rungs: a trial result that did not show slowing of Alzheimer's, and a reported association with dementia risk that is not that trial result. Scientists suspect the drugs may do more to preserve memory and thinking in people with obesity or diabetes, especially before cognitive problems appear; that suspicion is not established as a trial finding in this report. Ozempic and Mounjaro are named as weight-loss drugs in the report. Source: sciencedaily.com

Partial Reprogramming's Outcome Depends on Cell Type: Lifespan.io

A preprint study of partial reprogramming in three human cell types found that younger epigenetic ages and better function do not always go together. That is a cell study, not a human trial, and it is not a result a listener can buy or take from this report. Source: lifespan.io

Researchers at Mount Sinai Hospital in New York City are launching a trial to study a three-part approach to longevity: exercise, a daily supplement, and an anti-fungal drug: LinkedIn

Mount Sinai Hospital researchers in New York City are launching a trial, not reporting a completed human result, of exercise together with rapamycin, described in the post as an anti-fungal drug, and spermidine, described as a supplement. Whether any regulator has approved rapamycin or spermidine for an aging-related use is not established in this report. Source: linkedin.com

Mechanism of the Week: GLP-1 Drugs Beyond Weight Loss

Glucagon-like peptide-1 is a hormone the gut releases when a person eats. A 2017 review in Nature Reviews Nephrology describes it as an incretin that helps control glucose by acting on pancreatic islet cells, food intake, and the movement of the gut. The same review says those insulin-releasing actions are reduced in type 2 diabetes, which is why GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors were developed to treat high blood sugar in that disease. It also describes a proposed gut-kidney axis, a fast feed-forward loop that may regulate fluid and electrolytes after a meal, as a reason researchers have asked whether the hormone acts on the kidney directly.

The strongest evidence is pooled randomized, placebo-controlled trials, not a single new experiment dated this week.

A 2024 meta-analysis in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology combined 11 trials and 85,373 participants, 29,386 women and 55,987 men. Among 67,769 participants with type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced a composite kidney outcome by 18 percent versus placebo, hazard ratio 0.82. That composite was kidney failure, meaning kidney replacement therapy or a persistently very low filtration rate, a sustained drop in estimated glomerular filtration rate of at least 50 percent, or death from kidney failure. Kidney failure alone was reduced by 16 percent, hazard ratio 0.84. Major adverse cardiovascular events, meaning cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, or non-fatal stroke, fell by 13 percent, hazard ratio 0.87. Death from any cause fell by 12 percent, hazard ratio 0.88. After the main search, the authors added the SELECT trial, registered as NCT03574597, which enrolled people with cardiovascular disease and a body mass index of 27 or higher who did not have diabetes. The reductions just cited are those reported for the type 2 diabetes trials. The review is registered as PROSPERO CRD42024528864. This is randomized-trial evidence in diabetes, and for SELECT in cardiovascular disease with elevated body mass index, not a trial whose question was aging.

A 2021 meta-analysis in the same journal, including AMPLITUDE-O, covered eight trials and 60,080 patients with type 2 diabetes. GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 14 percent, hazard ratio 0.86. That review set out to estimate a kidney composite of new macroalbuminuria, doubling of serum creatinine or at least a 40 percent decline in filtration rate, kidney replacement, or death due to kidney disease. It is registered as PROSPERO CRD42021259711.

A 2019 meta-analysis in the same journal pooled seven trials and 56,004 participants: ELIXA of lixisenatide, LEADER of liraglutide, SUSTAIN-6 of semaglutide, EXSCEL of exenatide, Harmony Outcomes of albiglutide, REWIND of dulaglutide, and PIONEER 6 of oral semaglutide. Overall, the drugs reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 12 percent, hazard ratio 0.88. The abstract states that treatment has beneficial effects on cardiovascular, mortality, and kidney outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes. These three papers are meta-analyses of randomized placebo-controlled outcome trials.

Weaker evidence comes from observational comparisons, not randomization. A retrospective cohort in an Eastern China healthcare database, 2018 to 2024, included 105,391 patients starting a GLP-1 receptor agonist or one of seven other glucose-lowering drugs. After propensity-score matching weights, and in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol Cox models, several other drugs were associated with higher event rates than GLP-1 receptor agonists. For major adverse cardiovascular events the hazard ratios were 1.19 for SGLT-2 inhibitors, 1.42 for insulins, 1.30 for sulfonylureas, and 1.45 for alpha-glucosidase inhibitors. For major adverse kidney events they were 3.09 for DPP-4 inhibitors, 6.11 for insulins, and 4.56 for meglitinides. For major adverse liver outcomes they were 1.48 for DPP-4 inhibitors, 2.06 for insulins, 1.55 for alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and 1.43 for meglitinides. That is an adjusted association in Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes, not proof that the GLP-1 drug caused the difference.

A target-trial emulation using U.S. MarketScan claims looked at adults with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and type 2 diabetes. A GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with an SGLT-2 inhibitor was compared with either drug alone, in on-treatment Cox models. Against GLP-1 receptor agonist alone, 4,606 combination patients were matched to 18,424; over a median 6.3 months the combination was associated with a 39 percent lower risk of major adverse liver outcomes, hazard ratio 0.61, and a 35 percent lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, hazard ratio 0.65. Against SGLT-2 inhibitor alone, 5,368 combination patients were matched to 21,472; over a median 6.0 months the figures were a 43 percent lower liver risk, hazard ratio 0.57, and a 20 percent lower cardiovascular risk, hazard ratio 0.80. This is an emulation from insurance data, not a randomized trial, and the follow-up is a few months.

These abstracts do not show that heart or kidney differences are independent of weight loss. They do not enroll people to test frailty, biological age, or lifespan. Drug-versus-drug hazard ratios can still reflect why a clinician chose one medicine, even after statistical balancing. A claims emulation with follow-up measured in months is not the same evidence as a multi-year placebo-controlled outcome trial.

These papers describe GLP-1 receptor agonists as treatments for high blood sugar in type 2 diabetes, and popular reporting also calls some of them weight-loss drugs. Approval to slow aging is not established in these sources.

SELECT, NCT03574597, is a registered human outcome trial that the 2024 meta-analysis added after its main search. The clearest limit on the strongest finding is this: the 18 percent kidney and 13 percent cardiovascular reductions versus placebo are from randomized trials in type 2 diabetes, not from a trial of aging, and they do not separate any effect from weight change.

Worth Knowing

  • An observational link between longer or more frequent daytime naps and higher mortality means nap patterns can mark declining health; it does not mean the naps themselves were shown to cause death.
  • A small survey linking steadier daily schedules to less pain and fewer depressive symptoms, including among people who slept badly, is an association, not evidence that changing a schedule treats either problem.
  • Major trials that did not show semaglutide slowing Alzheimer's sit on a different rung from studies that have only linked GLP-1 drugs to a lower dementia risk.
  • Randomized trials in type 2 diabetes have tied GLP-1 receptor agonists to fewer cardiovascular, kidney, and death events than placebo, which is a disease-outcome record, not a demonstrated treatment for aging.

Trial Tracker

  • SELECT (NCT03574597): semaglutide in people with cardiovascular disease and body mass index of 27 or higher without diabetes; included after the main search in the 2024 meta-analysis of GLP-1 receptor agonist outcome trials. No next milestone is named in these sources.
  • AMPLITUDE-O: included in the 2021 meta-analysis of GLP-1 receptor agonist cardiovascular outcome trials in type 2 diabetes. No next milestone is named.
  • ELIXA, LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, EXSCEL, Harmony Outcomes, REWIND, and PIONEER 6: placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcome trials of lixisenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide, exenatide, albiglutide, dulaglutide, and oral semaglutide, pooled in the 2019 meta-analysis. No next milestone is named.

Nap patterns and disease-outcome drug trials both describe risk; neither one, in this week's sources, is a demonstrated way to slow aging.
