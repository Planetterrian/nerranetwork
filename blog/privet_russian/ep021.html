  • Russian (Cyrillic): хлеб

Transliteration: khleb

English: bread

Example sentence: Я ем хлеб.

Example translation: I eat bread.

Memory hook: "Khleb" sounds like "club" — imagine a bread club sandwich!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): молоко

Transliteration: moloko

English: milk

Example sentence: Кот пьёт молоко.

Example translation: The cat drinks milk.

Memory hook: "Molo" like "mellow" — think of mellow, creamy milk.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): сыр

Transliteration: syr

English: cheese

Example sentence: Сыр вкусный.

Example translation: The cheese is delicious.

Memory hook: "Syr" rhymes with "stir" — stir the cheesy sauce!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): яблоко

Transliteration: yabloko

English: apple

Example sentence: Я люблю яблоко.

Example translation: I love an apple.

Memory hook: "Yabloko" starts like "yummy" — a yummy apple snack.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): мясо

Transliteration: myaso

English: meat

Example sentence: Мясо на тарелке.

Example translation: Meat on the plate.

Memory hook: "Myaso" sounds like "my so" — "my so favorite meat!"

  • Russian (Cyrillic): овощи

Transliteration: ovoshchi

English: vegetables

Example sentence: Овощи полезные.

Example translation: Vegetables are healthy.

Memory hook: "Ovo" like "oval" — oval-shaped veggies on your plate.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): чай

Transliteration: chay

English: tea

Example sentence: Пей чай.

Example translation: Drink tea.

Memory hook: "Chay" rhymes with "day" — tea time every day!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): вкусный

Transliteration: vkusnyy

English: delicious

Example sentence: Это вкусный хлеб.

Example translation: This is delicious bread.

Memory hook: "Vkus" sounds like "boost" — a taste that boosts your smile!

Grammar Spotlight:

Russian adjectives like "вкусный" (delicious) change their ending to match the noun they describe. For masculine nouns (like хлеб), it ends in -ый. For neuter nouns (like яблоко), it becomes "вкусное". This is similar to English where we just say "delicious bread" or "delicious apple" without changes, but Russian adds the matching ending so the words "agree" — like best friends dressing alike!

Word Origins — Deep Dive: чай

Did you know that the Russian word чай and the English word tea are actually the same word? They both come from the ancient Chinese word for tea, "chá." This word started in China thousands of years ago, then travelled along the Silk Road: one path went north through Central Asia into Russian as "чай" around the 1600s, while another path went west by sea through Portuguese and Dutch traders into English as "tea." But be careful — the Russian word "магазин" sounds like the English "magazine" but actually means "shop," a fun false friend that can surprise you in a grocery store! Next time you see the English word tea, remember it's cousins with the Russian чай — they both come from the same Chinese root. That connection is your secret shortcut to remembering it forever.

Cultural Corner:

In Russia, tea is more than a drink — it's a warm way to welcome guests and share stories. Families often gather around a samovar (a special tea urn) for hours, sipping чай with jam or cookies, showing how hospitality and conversation are at the heart of Russian daily life.

Practice Challenge:

For kids: Draw your favorite food from today's list (like хлеб or яблоко) and say its Russian name out loud three times while showing it to a grown-up. For adults: Next time you make a snack or order at a café, practice saying "Это вкусный" (This is delicious) about what you're eating or drinking!
