Top Story

Datasection Loses an 80 Billion Yen AI Order from Tencent Amid U.S. Export Controls: KuCoin

HuoXing Finance reports that Japan-listed Datasection had leased about 15,000 Blackwell GPUs primarily to Tencent, and that a ¥80 billion AI order was canceled after U.S. export-control rules issued November 16. The company cut full-year revenue guidance from ¥162 billion to ¥17 billion, a decline of about 89 percent. The new rules require an export license for any data center globally that provides substantial computing power to Chinese headquarters enterprises using controlled advanced chips, and they add Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil to enhanced licensing. Asian operators are struggling to secure bridge financing and are hunting new customers; loan-to-value ratios offered by lenders have fallen from 70–80 percent in the summer to 50–60 percent. Compute redirected from the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia is hitting the market at discounted prices and shorter terms, with supply pressure expected to intensify over the next 6 to 12 months, while buyers favor U.S.-based, immediately deliverable, short-term, low-down-payment capacity. The B300 rental price index has softened but has not collapsed, and global demand is still described as strong. Source: kucoin.com

Silicon

Tesla's 2027 3nm wafers go to Google and Microsoft first: Electrek

Electrek, citing Commercial Times, reports that Tesla design partner Global Unichip is giving its limited 2027 TSMC 3-nanometer allocation to Google's Axion 2 and Axion 3 server processors and to Microsoft's in-house chips ahead of Tesla's AI5. Wccftech, citing China Times, names Microsoft's Maia among those cloud parts and says profit and order visibility are why cloud chips take the line. The reported 2027 Tesla quota cannot support annual shipments for a fleet of millions of cars; Tesla delivered roughly 1.6 million vehicles last year. Limited AI5 volume is expected to go first to Optimus, which ships in small numbers, with large-scale use in cars only after N3 capacity expands further in 2028. Elon Musk has said cars will remain on AI4 and that AI5 is going to Optimus. Electrek also reports AI5 memory was cut from 144GB to 72GB and then set at 96GB, AI4.5 RAM was cut from 32GB to 24GB because RAM is expensive, and AI4 itself now costs twice as much on memory and storage price hikes. Source: electrek.co Source: wccftech.com

Penguin revives retired DDR4 for AI servers: EE Times

EE Times reports that hyperscalers are already reclaiming older DRAM, while enterprises have been slower to reuse DDR4 to meet memory-expansion demand in AI servers. Source: eetimes.com

Intel names Raptor Lake-S NEXT in a driver package: TechPowerUp

TechPowerUp reports that version 2618 of Intel's Display Virtualization Drivers for Bartlett Lake-S, released November 26, lists "Raptor Lake-S NEXT PV" beside Bartlett Lake-S Hybrid MR4 and 12P MR2. Version 2400 in May covered only Bartlett Lake-S, an LGA1700 family for edge and embedded systems. Prior leaks cited a 16-core (8P+8E) SKU and a 20-core (8P+12E) SKU on the original Raptor Lake architecture; Intel has not officially announced the lineup. Gigabyte has hinted at it in BIOS updates for B760 and H610 boards, and the report positions it as a cheaper DDR4/DDR5 hybrid alternative under Core Ultra 400 "Nova Lake-S." Source: techpowerup.com

Data Centres & Power

SpaceX installs Megapacks at the Starbase data-center site: Data Center Dynamics

SpaceX's data-center plan at Starbase, Texas, is announced: Data Center Dynamics reports the company has installed $250 million worth of Tesla Megapack batteries at the site while seeking to develop a data center and natural-gas power plant of up to $24 billion. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Vodafone to take Safaricom's footprint to 35MW: Data Center Dynamics

Safaricom's data-center expansion is announced. Data Center Dynamics reports Vodafone plans to triple the footprint to 35 megawatts from 9.1 megawatts across six facilities. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

FCC approves a million-satellite space data-center filing: Data Center Dynamics

Data Center Dynamics reports the FCC has approved a SpaceX application to deploy one million satellites for data centers in space. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Supply Chain & Policy

Restricted Blackwell racks turn up at a Shanghai project: Engadget

Engadget, citing Bloomberg's review of Chinese regulatory filings, reports Shanghai Shuoyao listed a GB300 NVL72 rack at the Yangtze River Delta Project as collateral for a loan of about $14 million (96 million yuan) from Shanghai Innovation Bank. One GB300 NVL72 holds 72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs and 36 Grace processors and can weigh about 3,300 pounds; a public warranty serial traces that rack to Gigabyte, which declined to name the buyer and said transactions follow applicable law. KuCoin adds that Shanghai Kezhan reported a GB200 server at the same site against a 90 million yuan loan late in 2025 from a leasing firm tied to a Shanghai government agency, and that Caixin describes Kezhan's majority owner as a Tokyo firm said to be controlled by Alice Huang. Kang Wei is listed as president of the facility; Alice Huang took a controlling stake in Megaspeed in 2023 and left in August 2025, while Shuoyao calls the relationship ordinary commercial cooperation. Bloomberg says it found no proof Megaspeed diverted the hardware, the company denies wrongdoing, the Justice Department is investigating export-rule compliance, and Commerce blacklisted Megaspeed this week; Hopper-generation chips were also found at the site, and the facility may eventually house as many as 800,000 servers. How the racks arrived is unclear. The New York Post reports Commerce opened the Megaspeed inquiry in 2024, sought to interview Alice Huang in June 2025 — the month Nvidia put the firm on a no-sell list — and that BIS cited a significant risk of diversion of advanced computing commodities. Source: engadget.com Source: kucoin.com Source: nypost.com

Beijing pushes into the boards under AI servers: Tom's Hardware

Tom's Hardware reports China's AI-hardware push is moving past GPUs to the printed-circuit boards underneath them, with Beijing racing to corner copper foil and micro-drill bits. AI servers such as Rubin Ultra are pushing board stacks toward 78 layers on denser laminates, drill bits are wearing out 10 times faster than normal, and high-speed signaling is stretching demand for smooth copper foil. Source: tomshardware.com

Texas Comptroller forwards TSMC's Brazos application: NeoTeo

The Texas Comptroller has recommended TSMC's Jobs, Energy, Technology and Innovation application for consideration by the governor and College Station ISD, while the company's investment decision on the proposed Brazos County logic fab remains pending. The application, from TSMC Arizona Corporation as JETI filing J0055, models a $22,619,235,774 investment on about 3,232 acres for 2-nanometer or more advanced logic, with 2,099 employees at full operation in 2032, and names Arizona as the alternative. KBTX reported the land was under contract rather than purchased. Any Brazos County incentive would be a separate Commissioners Court decision, not part of the state review. Source: neoteo.com Source: neoteo.com

Also Today

Netherlands plans G20 talks on the U.S. MATCH Act: Traders Union

Traders Union reports the Netherlands plans to raise U.S. MATCH Act export controls in talks at the G20. Source: tradersunion.com

Huawei's AI memory design versus Samsung HBM4: TechRepublic

TechRepublic reports Huawei's AI memory design has twice the interface width of Samsung's HBM4. Source: techrepublic.com

SpaceX cuts Blackwell rental rates: Benzinga

Benzinga reports SpaceX has cut Nvidia Blackwell GPU rental rates by up to 35 percent, and that Anthropic may not renew an H200 deal. Source: benzinga.com

TSMC ties multi-year AI-chip demand to inference: NeoTeo

NeoTeo reports that at TSMC's November 24 Europe OIP workshop in Munich, executives expected AI-chip demand to stay strong for years as inference grows, with technology senior director Jonathan Chang citing industrial automation, autonomous vehicles, humanoid robots, and drug and chemical discovery, and the company describing workloads moving from hyperscale data centers to the edge. Source: neoteo.com

The Teardown: The Wafer Bid Away From the Console

A memory wafer is started for one product. Capacity and investment that move to HBM and server DRAM, where AI customers pay more and sign long-term agreements, are starts that never become the conventional DRAM a console maker was counting on to get cheaper. The products differ. The queue does not.

EE Times lays out the price path that follows that queue, using TrendForce's revised contract-price forecast. Conventional DRAM contract prices were revised up to an increase of 90 to 95 percent quarter over quarter in the first quarter, then a further 58 to 63 percent in the second quarter. The fourth-quarter increase has moderated to 10 to 15 percent. That last figure is still an increase, and it sits on a record base, so a buyer who waited for the curve to roll over is paying the compounded level, not last year's contract. Meaningful ramp-ups of new capacity are not expected until the second half of 2027, and that capacity is expected to contribute meaningfully only in 2028. Until those wafers exit the line, the shortage is a scheduling fact, not a headline.

Walk a console bill of materials through those quarters. For decades the machine launched near cost and the price fell as components cheapened. Memory was the part makers could count on for that decline. Sony told investors that rising memory prices increase the PlayStation 5's bill of materials. The disc edition launched at $499. After a second increase in less than a year, effective April 2026, that disc edition costs $649.99. A 90 to 95 percent contract jump in one quarter, followed by 58 to 63 percent in the next, does not have to be the entire retail gap to explain the direction: the component that used to fund a price cut is now a reason for a price rise, on top of advanced wafers that no longer make a same-complexity die meaningfully cheaper. The fourth quarter's 10 to 15 percent does not unwind the first two jumps. It adds to them.

The same bid shows up in who captures the silicon that gaming once paid to develop. In Nvidia's fiscal first quarter of 2023, gaming and data center were nearly equal, at 43.68 percent and 45.25 percent of revenue. By fiscal 2026 the entire graphics business, gaming included, was 11.45 percent. The second use is now large enough to outbid the first for memory from those three suppliers.

That allocation stops being the right start for the memory maker when new capacity contributes in volume in 2028 and the shortage that lets an AI buyer outbid a console contract no longer clears the line. Until those wafers contribute, a long-term HBM agreement still takes the start, and conventional DRAM remains the residual even after a near-doubling of its own contract price. Source: eetimes.com
