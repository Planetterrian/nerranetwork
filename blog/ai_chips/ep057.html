Twelve million TPP now needs a US licence if a data centre serves China.

By Patrick November 17, 2026 Ep 57 10 min read Listen to podcast Watch on YouTube
Listen to this episode
Contents
Top Story Silicon Data Centres & Power Supply Chain & Policy Also Today The Teardown: A Million Dollars a Megawatt in the Tank On the Horizon

What You Need to Know: Twelve million TPP now needs a US licence if a data centre serves China. The Bureau of Industry and Security interim final rule sets that cap at roughly 750 H100s or about 330 B200s, and it also licences advanced-chip shipments into Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Centres already serving the covered customers have 90 days to get a licence.

Top Story

The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security released an interim final rule requiring a licence to ship advanced accelerators to any data centre worldwide that provides more than 12 million aggregate TPP to companies headquartered in Macau or Country Group D:5, which includes China, TechPowerUp reported. TPP is a chip’s peak dense throughput multiplied by the bit length of the operation; an H100 rates about 15,800 TPP, so the cap is roughly 750 H100s or around 330 B200s, and it also covers linked data centres and indirect access such as a cloud reseller. Fortune and Silicon UK report that a licence is now required to export, re-export or transfer advanced semiconductors to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, which the rule treats as a higher-than-normal diversion risk. Data centres already serving the covered customers have 90 days to obtain a licence. Only commercial recommender systems are exempt; anything that generates text, images, video or code does not qualify. BIS said the rule was written by and at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council. Source: techpowerup.com

Silicon

Nvidia Rubin HPC GPU Unveiled at SC26 With 50,000+ CUDA Cores: Tech Times

Nvidia announced Rubin HPC on Monday at the SC26 supercomputing conference in Chicago: a Vera Rubin-family GPU with more than 50,000 CUDA cores, hardware for sparse-data acceleration and seventh-generation NVLink, due in 2028. AI Weekly reports that the current Rubin tops out at 28,672 CUDA cores, and that NVLink 7 keeps the 3.6 terabytes per second of per-GPU bandwidth already shipping on NVLink 6. Nvidia named four patterns the sparse hardware is meant to speed up: sparse matrix operations, stencil computations, sorting and reductions. The same conference material describes a Vera CPU rack of 192 CPUs, about 300 terabytes of memory and 1 petabyte of storage, arriving in early 2027. Tech Times calculates that on the June 2026 TOP500 list El Capitan posted 1.809 exaflops on LINPACK and 17.41 petaflops on HPCG, about 1 percent of LINPACK speed, the memory-bound gap this part is aimed at. Source: techtimes.com · aiweekly.co

MLPerf Training v6.1 Adds Post-Training Test: Tech Times

MLCommons on Monday published MLPerf Training v6.1, adding the first post-training benchmark and the first results from Nvidia’s Rubin-generation GPUs and Google’s Ironwood TPU. Twenty-four organizations submitted 153 systems that produced 250 results; 57 of those systems, 37 percent, ran in the cloud, up from 29 percent in v6.0 six months ago, twelve used 1,000 or more accelerators, and the largest used 10,240. On the new Qwen3.5-397B reinforcement-learning test, Nvidia reports 10.44 minutes on Vera Rubin NVL72 at 1,024 GPUs, Google Cloud reports 18.02 minutes on 640 Ironwood TPUs that reached 0.765 accuracy against a 0.69 target, and CoreWeave reports 22.78 minutes on 256 GB200 GPUs across four racks, with 128 GPUs on training and 128 on generation. Those runs differ in chip generation, chip count and how the work was split, so Tech Times says they show what each system did rather than which chip is faster. On DeepSeek-V3 at 1,024 GPUs, Nvidia reports 4.14 minutes for Rubin and 8.03 minutes for GB300; a 10,240-GPU GB300 NVL72 cluster across 160 racks set the round’s fastest absolute time at 1.38 minutes. Nvidia claims up to 2x per-GPU versus an optimized GB300 NVL72 baseline, and CoreWeave reports 17.65 minutes for DeepSeek-V3 on 256 Rubin GPUs against 48.40 minutes on its own GB200 at the same count, a comparison it says MLCommons has not verified. Source: techtimes.com

Samsung is developing custom HBM4E memory for Meta's AI chips on a 2nm base die: MLQ.ai

MLQ.ai reports that a Korean outlet says Samsung is developing custom HBM4E for Meta’s AI chips on a 2nm base die. The report describes development, not a shipped part. Source: mlq.ai

AMD Says MI455X Delivers 30% Higher Token Throughput Than Nvidia: THE ELEC

THE ELEC reports that AMD says the MI455X delivers 30 percent higher token throughput than Nvidia. iTWire frames the same claim as a 30 percent inference lead over Nvidia’s Vera Rubin, and says the MI450X widens the Instinct MI400 series. Source: thelec.net · itwire.com

Data Centres & Power

HPE-Built Lux AI Cluster Debuts at No. 14 on the TOP500: HPCwire

At SC26, HPE announced that Lux, an AI cluster it developed with AMD for Oak Ridge National Laboratory, debuted at number 14 on the TOP500. HPE says Lux is the first AI system to support the US Department of Energy’s Genesis Mission. Source: hpcwire.com

Vertiv Opens ATLAS Lab to Test AI Power and Cooling Infrastructure: HPCwire

Vertiv opened the ATLAS lab in Columbus, Ohio, on November 17 to prove power, thermal-management and controls infrastructure for AI factories before next-generation compute reaches the market. The lab is built for system-level testing of density, speed, scale, complexity and dynamic load profiles. Source: hpcwire.com

UC Berkeley and Berkeley Lab Join Schmidt Science Computing Network: HPCwire

The University of California, Berkeley and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory will join the Schmidt Science Computing Network, a distributed network meant to expand advanced computing available for science, HPCwire reported. The move was announced November 16. Source: hpcwire.com

Supply Chain & Policy

Singapore's Megaspeed under criminal investigation over Nvidia chip exports, US confirms: TRT World

The US Departments of Commerce and Justice confirmed on Monday that they are conducting a criminal investigation into Singapore-based cloud company Megaspeed over suspected semiconductor export-control violations. Commerce launched the inquiry in 2024 after detecting suspicious chip-diversion activity, requested Nvidia’s sales records in May 2025, and Nvidia placed Megaspeed on an internal no-sell list the following month. Bureau of Industry and Security agents then inspected Megaspeed sites in Singapore with local law enforcement and interviewed then-executive Alice Huang; the Justice Department subsequently took the lead. The agencies said authorities under the Trump administration have charged 17 defendants in six cases involving billions of dollars worth of semiconductors, and that monetary penalties for export-control violations had increased 18-fold compared with the previous administration. Fortune, citing a November 16 Straits Times report, says Indonesian officials who inspected PT Megaspeed International Indonesia found unused server racks with missing GPUs. Source: trtworld.com · techpowerup.com · fortune.com

Singapore defends status as a trusted business hub after the U.S. rolls out new AI chip controls: Fortune

A Singapore government spokesperson said on November 16 that Singapore does not condone companies using their association with Singapore to circumvent other countries’ laws, and that the government is engaging US authorities and affected companies on the new measure. Thailand’s vice minister for commerce, Kirida Bhaopichitr, told CNBC the country is prepared, does not want to be a transshipment hub from the US to China, and will scrutinize data centres more closely. Indonesia said it is reviewing the rule and seeking industry input on data-centre operations and investment. TRT World reports that Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called the move economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction, and said Beijing would take necessary measures. Malaysia’s Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry said on November 13 it is checking with customs whether chips brought into Malaysia were re-exported in accordance with the law. Digital Realty’s Serene Nah said the measure is still an interim rule and licence details are not out yet; the company operates three facilities in Singapore and, in June, opened a Cyberjaya campus designed for approximately 32 megawatts. Source: fortune.com · trtworld.com

NVIDIA Board of Directors Establishes National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee: NVIDIA Newsroom

Nvidia’s board has established a standing National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee to oversee national-security policy, export-control compliance and global trade risk. Director Suzanne Nora Johnson will chair it, with directors Stephen C. Neal and John O. Dabiri as members; it will meet at least quarterly and retain independent outside experts. The company’s National Security Team and Global Trade Compliance Team will report to the committee and to interim CEO Colette Kress on day-to-day operations. Source: nvidianews.nvidia.com

TSMC puts construction safety ahead of capacity milestones in its 2026 supplier awards: digitimes

At its 2026 Supply Chain Management Forum in Hsinchu on November 17, TSMC said this year’s supplier awards gave more weight to construction safety, sustainability, global support and localization. Digitimes reports that shift as a change in how the company judges suppliers, ahead of capacity milestones. Source: digitimes.com

Also Today

Intel Says D270 GPU Delivers Up to 5.9x Nvidia's Throughput: THE ELEC

THE ELEC reports that Intel says its D270 GPU delivers up to 5.9 times Nvidia’s throughput. Source: thelec.net

A supercomputing lab on any desk: Nvidia unveils DGX Spark HPC Dev Kit with 512 GB of memory: Notebookcheck

Nvidia has unveiled a DGX Spark HPC Dev Kit with 512 gigabytes of memory, a step up from the 64-gigabyte and 128-gigabyte Spark configurations already on the market. Source: notebookcheck.net

Chip Industry Technical Paper Roundup: Nov. 17: Semiconductor Engineering

Semiconductor Engineering’s November 17 paper roundup includes hardware-based location checks for AI-chip smuggling, HBM4 controller partitioning, and embedded ECC for AI weight protection. Source: semiengineering.com

The Teardown: A Million Dollars a Megawatt in the Tank

Immersion cooling removes heat by putting the server in a dielectric liquid instead of blowing air across a heatsink. The fluid touches the board, so the thermal path is component to liquid to a heat exchanger, not component to air to a coil. In single-phase immersion the liquid stays liquid; pumps move it, and a heat exchanger dumps the heat elsewhere. Because the bath does the cooling, the hall no longer has to be designed around airflow, and the method uses no water for evaporative cooling. Data Center Knowledge notes that this also cuts some operational risk by removing fans and airflow dependencies, and that the technique has existed for decades. As of 2026 it is still not how major operators cool production halls. Headlines have been pilots, demonstrations and proofs of concept, including Microsoft’s experiment earlier this decade. Direct-to-chip liquid, which runs coolant in pipes beside the processor and leaves the server in a conventional rack, is the liquid method projected to grow.

The money is why the tank loses the bid. Data Center Knowledge cites a study putting immersion at about $1 million per megawatt of IT load, against $650,000 per megawatt for direct-to-chip. That is $350,000 more capital for every megawatt before a watt of IT is turned on. Operators trying to energize capacity quickly feel that gap on day one. The operating side looks better, and the article gives a worked cluster rather than a slogan. Adam Silva Consulting estimates that cooling a 64-rack AI cluster over 10 years costs $42 million with advanced air, $31 million with direct-to-chip liquid, and $28 million with single-phase immersion. Air to immersion is a $14 million difference across that decade. Direct-to-chip to immersion is a $3 million difference. The tank wins the 10-year cooling bill, and it wins by less against the liquid method operators can adopt without throwing out the rack. A $350,000-per-megawatt premium has to be earned back from that $3 million gap, plus whatever footprint and energy the bath saves. The article does not convert the 64 racks into megawatts, so the payback cannot be stated as a number of years; the comparison that is in the source is already enough to see the problem. Three million dollars of operating advantage against a capital premium measured per megawatt is a hard sell when the alternative keeps the cabinet, the lift, and the technician’s existing procedure.

The rest of the hesitation is how the building is run. Airflow stops being the constraint that sets row pitch and white-space layout, so the architecture can change, and the article argues it should. Servers come out of a tank, not a cabinet. Monitoring, service workflows and technician training all move with that. Direct-to-chip also changes design, but it preserves more of the server-and-rack habit, which is why incremental adoption is easier. The point at which the $1 million-per-megawatt tank stops losing to the $650,000 direct-to-chip install is the point at which heat density and water limits make a $3 million, 10-year edge on a 64-rack cluster, and a $14 million edge against advanced air, worth redrawing the hall and retraining the floor. Data Center Knowledge’s condition is concrete: if power-hungry training and inference keep expanding, and if pressure to cut water and energy rises with them, the bath gets more attractive. If that boom flattens before operators make the capital and the cultural commit, immersion stays where it is in 2026, a pilot beside a production hall still built for racks. Source: datacenterknowledge.com

The tank stops losing to the six-hundred-fifty-thousand-dollar install when heat density and water limits make a three million dollar, ten-year edge on that sixty-four-rack cluster, and a fourteen million dollar edge against advanced air, worth redrawing the room and retraining the floor. Data Center Knowledge’s test is plain. If training and inference keep getting hotter, and if the pressure to cut water and energy rises with them, the bath gets harder to refuse. If that build-out flattens before the capital and the crew change, immersion remains what it is in twenty twenty-six: a pilot next to a production hall still built for racks. On the horizon, centres already serving the covered customers have ninety days from the interim final rule to get a licence.
