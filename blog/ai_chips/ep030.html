What You Need to Know: Four workers are dead after a steel-structure collapse at TSMC's Fab 22 construction site in Kaohsiung, and the company has stopped similar steel work at its construction sites across Taiwan. Synaptics has announced an edge microcontroller family with on-package DRAM and up to 400 GOPS of AI acceleration. Dell's chief executive says the memory shortage gets worse in 2027, with meaningful new fab supply not expected until 2029 or 2030.

Top Story

Four workers have died after a steel structure collapsed during a hoisting and installation operation at Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co's Fab 22 site in Nanzih District, Kaohsiung, part of the Southern Taiwan Science Park. The Kaohsiung Fire Department received a report at 4:53pm yesterday that four people had been injured; it dispatched 10 vehicles and 22 personnel. Preliminary investigations by the Southern Taiwan Science Park Bureau suggest bolts on a connecting plate of an outer steel structure on the third floor suddenly snapped, and the workers and heavy steel components plunged to the ground. Three workers were pronounced dead last night; the fourth, surnamed Huang, died in intensive care this morning. The four men worked for a domestic contractor. TSMC said it and its construction contractor immediately suspended related construction work at the site, and suspended similar steel structure work at all of its construction sites across Taiwan pending investigation. The bureau has sent labor inspection personnel to determine cause and liability. Fab 22 is scheduled to produce chips on 2-nanometer and more advanced process technologies, with construction of its Phase 4 fab set to be completed next year. Source: taipeitimes.com Source: independent.co.uk

Silicon

Synaptics Announces Astra SR200 Multimodal Edge AI Processors with Google Coral NPU: TechPowerUp

Synaptics has introduced the Astra SR200 Series, which it calls an xMCU: a microcontroller class meant to run multimodal edge AI that conventional MCUs cannot hold in on-chip memory. The announced parts pair heterogeneous, AI-native compute with integrated high-bandwidth custom DRAM scalable to hundreds of megabytes, so larger models and multimodal workloads can stay on the device. The series uses the Synaptics Torq AI engine and the Coral NPU from Google Research, and Synaptics says it is the first pairing of an Arm Cortex-M85 with a Cortex-M52. EE Times, reporting from Synaptics Tech Day in San Jose on October 20, gives the family up to 400 GOPS of AI acceleration and 256 MB of custom DRAM, with vision, audio, and hardware-rooted security. Three pin-compatible tiers are specified: SR210 at 200 GOPS for always-on voice and sensing, SR230 at 300 GOPS adding vision, display, and optional audio, and SR250 at 400 GOPS with dual vision and display paths. Target devices are AI wearables, battery-powered sensors, connected cameras, streamers, and industrial vision and voice systems; John Weil, vice president and general manager for IoT and edge AI processors, said the strongest traction so far is body-worn electronics. The parts are announced, not described as shipped. Source: techpowerup.com Source: eetimes.com

Socionext Expands Agentic AI to Advanced, Large-Scale SoC Physical Design: Semiconductor Digest

Socionext says it is extending agentic AI from front-end design into physical design of large-scale system-on-chips on advanced process technologies, in collaboration with Cadence. The company is applying that flow to the physical design of a large-scale 3-nanometer SoC, using the Cadence InnoStack AI Super Agent, and plans to expand its use across SoC projects. Retrieval-augmented generation, Model Context Protocol support, customized AI skills, and AI agents are how Socionext says it is putting internal design knowledge into floorplanning, custom timing-analysis checks, and planned checklist automation. President and chief operating officer Hisato Yoshida said the company expects up to 5X overall productivity gains through Cadence's agentic AI workflow. Cadence senior vice president Chin-Chi Teng described the InnoStack agent as open enough for a customer to load its own methodologies. Source: semiconductor-digest.com

NTT Consortium Developing Mass-Production Tech for Optical Chiplets: Semiconductor Digest

A consortium advised by NTT is jointly developing mass-production technologies for optical chiplets. The participating companies are the developers; NTT's stated role is advisor. The item is a development program, not a production start. Source: semiconductor-digest.com

Data Centres & Power

Amazon pledges €16bn investment in Germany in 2027: Data Center Dynamics

Amazon has pledged a €16 billion investment in Germany in 2027. Data Center Dynamics reports that the pledge includes expanding data centers and AI infrastructure, high-speed satellite internet, and logistics. This is a pledged investment year, not an energized site, and the report does not assign the euros to a named campus. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Georgia electric cooperative plans 50-MW home battery deployment: Utility Dive

Central Georgia Electric Membership Corporation plans a 50-megawatt home-battery deployment. Utility Dive reports the batteries are intended to reduce system peaks and energy costs as hyperscale data centers seek to connect to its grid. The 50 megawatts are batteries on the cooperative's system, not a data-center IT load. Source: utilitydive.com

OVHcloud has 110MW of data center capacity up for grabs: Data Center Dynamics

OVHcloud has 110 megawatts of data-center capacity available to grow its customer base, the company said on its annual earnings call, Data Center Dynamics reports. That figure is capacity the operator says it can sell, not a new site breaking ground. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Supply Chain & Policy

Dell CEO Says the Memory Shortage Will Get Worse in 2027: TechPowerUp

Michael Dell, Dell's chairman and chief executive, told The Wall Street Journal that the memory shortage will get worse next year as AI data-center buildout keeps absorbing DRAM and high-bandwidth memory. TechPowerUp reports Dell is sitting on roughly $95 billion of AI server orders it has not delivered, which Dell called the largest backlog in its history, and that memory—not demand—is what is holding shipments. Accelerators in those servers need HBM; the servers around them still need conventional DRAM. Dell does not expect meaningful new supply until 2029 or 2030, because new memory fabs take around four years to build. That lines up with comments TechPowerUp attributes to Micron's chief executive, that supply will be much tighter in 2027 and 2028 than this year, and to SK hynix's chief executive, who expects the shortage to last through 2030. Dell is coping by buying DRAM only when final assembly is close and by locking long-term supply agreements. Source: techpowerup.com

Micron Invests in New York Critical Minerals Processing With Indium Corporation: Semiconductor Digest

Micron has announced strategic funding so Indium Corporation can expand refining and high-purity materials manufacturing in Rome, New York, restarting domestic processing of critical minerals used in advanced semiconductor manufacturing. The deal sits inside a plan Micron announced in July to invest up to $3 billion to strengthen the U.S. semiconductor supply chain. The companies will enter a long-term supply agreement giving Micron access to those high-purity minerals, which Micron's chief procurement officer, Ben Tessone, tied to the company's technology roadmap and to reducing reliance on concentrated global refining. Micron also restated plans to increase expected U.S. fab and technology spend to more than $250 billion through 2035. New York Governor Kathy Hochul said the Clay fab investment is months ahead of schedule and called it the largest private investment in New York State history. Source: semiconductor-digest.com

TSMC Plans 3nm Chip Production At Second Japan Factory: Electronics For You BUSINESS

TSMC plans to manufacture 3-nanometer chips at the second factory of Japan Advanced Semiconductor Manufacturing, its Kumamoto joint venture—a plan, not a line in production. Total equipment investment in the venture is expected to exceed $23 billion. TSMC's Japan account said production and investment would be implemented in phases based on market demand and would proceed on the premise of Japanese government support. In August, TSMC and Sony Semiconductor Solutions agreed to a separate Kumamoto joint venture, Advanced Vision Semiconductor Manufacturing Corp., for next-generation image sensors, with commercial production scheduled to begin in 2029, according to Focus Taiwan as cited in the report. Source: electronicsforyou.biz Source: timesofindia.indiatimes.com

ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent place over $35 billion worth of Nvidia chip orders: Reuters

Reuters reports, as an exclusive, that ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent have placed more than $35 billion worth of Nvidia chip orders. The figure is orders placed, not chips described as delivered. Source: reuters.com

BigGo Finance, citing The Elec, reports Elon Musk approached Intel, TSMC, and Micron to buy existing fabs, was turned down, and shifted Terafab to partnerships, with Intel contributing its 14A process while Musk has limited TSMC to a subcontractor role at most. South Korea's Ruling Party Lawmaker Slams AI Data Center Grid Code Plan: BigGo Finance

A ruling-party lawmaker in South Korea has criticized a planned AI data-center grid code and urged an exemption for projects already underway. Source: finance.biggo.com

Tens of thousands of Nvidia GPUs sit idle in Meta's Prometheus supercluster: The Register

The Register reports that tens of thousands of Nvidia GPUs in Meta's Prometheus supercluster sit idle, held ready for a failure. Source: theregister.com

Shadeform launches Flex VMs: Data Center Dynamics

Data Center Dynamics reports Shadeform's Flex VMs turn bare-metal GPU clusters into single-tenant virtual machines on its marketplace, and that an approved node can be enrolled in about 30 minutes. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Contrel, FSE combine laser and plasma processes for advanced packaging: digitimes

Digitimes reports Contrel and FSE are combining laser and plasma processes for advanced packaging. Source: digitimes.com

Apple Expands $600 Billion U.S. Manufacturing Plan: Pulse 2.0

The Teardown: Why the Fiber Array Cannot Touch the Die

Co-packaged optics does not become a volume product when the photonic die lights up on a bench. It becomes a volume product when a factory can attach light and electricity at the same time, on every insertion, without destroying the only connector the module will ever trust. Semiconductor Engineering's panel on scaling co-packaged-optics test past a single die puts the constraint in the handling, not in the laser. Fiber alignment is the bottleneck on the optical engine. The fiber array unit is supposed to couple light without ever contacting the device. Crash it during bring-up and the array is damaged; leave contamination on it and the measurement is junk, so cleaning is part of the test cell, not a side chore.

Dave Armstrong of ISE Labs, an ASE Group subsidiary, draws the power line that keeps many insertions at one die. If the main ASIC is an 8-kilowatt GPU, with multiple connectors around multiple sides, tester resources have to deliver that power and pull the heat back out. That combination is hard to extend past one die. Matt Griffin of Teradyne agrees that most production solutions today are single-site, and that multi-site probe cards would relieve the fiber-alignment bottleneck if they work. Ira Leventhal of Advantest treats the connector as the part that will not scale on optimism. A ball-grid array already teaches the same lesson: socket the device too many times and the balls are at risk. The device-side optical connector is designed to be attached once and, the hope is, never disconnected. A test-side connector that survives 20 to 50 insertions is not a production tool. It needs to last for tens of thousands of insertions, or 100,000. The fix the panel describes is a hardened metal on the tester side. That hardened metal then mates to a receptacle on the device that is not hardened. Careless mating can wear out the device connector before the spec lifetime, which means the test method can scrap the part it was built to qualify.

Kazuo Yamaguchi of Keysight separates the easy simultaneous contact from the one that actually characterizes the engine. DC pads plus an optical probe are a mechanical problem. Modulator and photodetector performance is validated through S-parameters, so optical contact and high-frequency radio-frequency contact have to land together. Probe cards fit only a limited number of RF lines, and their bandwidth is limited, while today's devices are characterized beyond 200 gigahertz. At wafer and die insertions, simultaneous connection is an RF probing problem sitting on top of fiber alignment. Leventhal adds warpage: alignment has to hold in multiple axes, either from the top or from the side, while a dense ball-grid array still has to make reliable electrical contact. Griffin's map of the package makes the geometry worse at module level. Every insertion has an optical interface and an electrical interface. Those interfaces can be on the top, the bottom, or both. At module level they can sit on two to four sides of the package, across some number of optical engines. Optical alignment by itself, he says, is not the large challenge. Doing it on every face, at the same time as power and RF, is.

A worked pass through Armstrong's split shows where single-site is still the rational cell and where it stops paying. Take the 8-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides. Tester power delivery and heat extraction already strain a one-die insertion, so adding a second site does not buy throughput if the tester cannot feed both parts. Photonic-integrated-circuit wafer test, in his experience, is single-site today, with some hope of moving up if probe-card makers—he names Technoprobe—can put more optical sites on one card. Near-packaged optics, by contrast, is typically a single-connector device. High-volume output of that class forces multi-site, because one connector and one alignment per tester hour will not clear the line. He calls insertions two and three the fertile ground: they are the longer, more complex insertions, and they are where multi-site has room to remove time. Griffin's hope is the same from the optical side. Multi-site fiber alignment is what lets the industry ramp faster. It is not here as a production default.

Single-site remains the right choice while three conditions hold at once: the device under test is an 8-kilowatt-class ASIC whose connector count and power exceed what the tester can deliver to a second die; the optical probe must never touch the die, so each site still needs its own clean fiber array; and the measurement that matters is an electro-optic S-parameter past 200 gigahertz, which a probe card cannot fan out across many RF lines. That choice stops being the right one when the product is a single-connector near-packaged engine headed for high volume, insertions two and three dominate the test time, and a hardened test connector can survive on the order of 100,000 matings without chewing through the softer receptacle on the device. Until that connector and a multi-site fiber card both exist, adding sites multiplies crashes and contamination faster than it multiplies good parts. Source: semiengineering.com

Modulator and photodetector proof is S-parameters, so light and high-frequency radio-frequency probes have to land together. Probe cards carry only so many radio-frequency lines, and their bandwidth is limited, while these devices are characterized past two hundred gigahertz. Leventhal adds warpage. Alignment has to hold on several axes, from the top or the side, while a dense ball-grid array still has to connect. Griffin's package map puts an optical face and an electrical face on every insertion, sometimes top, sometimes bottom, sometimes both, and at module level on two to four sides across several optical engines. Optical alignment alone, he says, is not the hard part. Doing it on every face at once with power and radio frequency is. Walk Armstrong's split. The eight-kilowatt GPU with connectors on several sides already strains one tester insertion, so a second site buys nothing if the tester cannot feed it. Photonic-integrated-circuit wafer test, in his experience, is single-site now, with a chance to rise if probe-card makers, and he names Technoprobe, can put more optical sites on one card. Near-packaged optics is usually one connector. High volume of that class forces multi-site, because one alignment per tester hour will not clear the line. He calls insertions two and three the fertile ground, the long insertions where multi-site can take time out. Single-site stays rational while that eight-kilowatt ASIC still exceeds what the tester can give a second die, while each site still needs its own clean fiber array that must not touch the die, and while the measurement that matters is an electro-optic S-parameter past two hundred gigahertz that a probe card cannot fan out. It stops being the right cell when the product is a single-connector near-packaged engine in high volume, insertions two and three own the test time, and a hardened test connector can survive on the order of a hundred thousand matings without destroying the softer receptacle on the part. Commercial production at the TSMC and Sony image-sensor venture in Kumamoto is scheduled for 2029. Amazon's Germany pledge, including data-center expansion, is dated to 2027, and Dell does not expect meaningful new memory-fab supply until 2029 or 2030.
