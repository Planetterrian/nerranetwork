Nvidia ships CUDA 13.5, taking Vera Rubin from early access into production deployments.

By Patrick November 05, 2026 Ep 45 10 min read Listen to podcast Watch on YouTube
Listen to this episode

What You Need to Know: Nvidia has shipped CUDA 13.5, moving Vera Rubin into production on a platform it has described as up to 144 GPUs per rack. Samsung’s second Taylor, Texas fab is reported fully booked, mostly by AMD, for 2 nm HBM base dies. SEMI counted 3,756 million square inches of silicon wafers shipped in the third quarter.

Top Story

Nvidia on Wednesday shipped CUDA Toolkit 13.5, moving the Vera Rubin platform from early developer access to general availability for enterprise production, and shipped it with Linux driver 620.70.06. The platform pairs Rubin GPUs with Vera CPUs over NVLink-C2C. Nvidia said in June 2026 that Vera Rubin systems would pack up to 144 GPUs per rack and deliver more than 7 exaflops of AI-for-science throughput and 5 petaflops of native FP64. CUDA 13.5 adds tuned matrix-multiplication heuristics and kernel adjustments for the platform’s NVFP4 and MXFP8 operations, which Nvidia says lift throughput without per-shape autotuning. Rubin and later GPUs support the Windows Display Driver Model and Microsoft Compute Driver Model but no longer support Tesla Compute Cluster mode, so applications and deployment scripts built around TCC need reworking. The release also extends Windows on Arm support to DGX Station for Windows, and it adds Multi-Process Service v3 weighted timeslicing plus locality-domain controls that let an application choose uniform streaming-multiprocessor partitions or partitions that draw on every available SM. Source: finance.biggo.com · developer.nvidia.com

Silicon

Samsung's 2 nm Taylor Fab 2 Reportedly Fully Booked, Mostly by AMD: TechPowerUp

Korea’s Maeil Business Newspaper, citing industry sources, reports that Samsung has booked the entire planned output of Taylor Fab 2 in Texas, with most of it going to AMD. The fab will make HBM base dies for AMD and NVIDIA on a 2 nm process, and AMD’s volume alone is described as enough to stretch the fab’s capacity. Samsung declined to comment, saying it cannot confirm customer-related details. The base die is the logic chip at the bottom of an HBM stack; Samsung plans to pair the Texas-made dies with DRAM produced in South Korea and ship finished HBM. Current HBM4 and sampled HBM4E use 4 nm base dies, so 2 nm or smaller is aimed at later HBM generations. The report follows Lisa Su’s October visit to Samsung, when Samsung reportedly wanted foundry orders from AMD in return for HBM AMD needs, and a March memorandum that made Samsung the primary HBM4 supplier for the Instinct MI455X. Source: techpowerup.com

Arm Delivers a Record Second Quarter, Reaching $1.44B in Total Revenue: TechPowerUp

Arm, in a shareholder letter published November 4, 2026, reported second-quarter fiscal 2027 results for the quarter ended September 30, 2026. Total revenue rose 27 percent year over year to $1.44 billion, which Arm called its highest-ever second quarter. Licensing and other revenue was $773 million, up 50 percent year over year, and royalty revenue was $670 million, with data-center royalties doubling year over year. Partners reported more than 10 billion Arm-based chips shipped in the quarter, an increase of 1.5 billion chips year over year, on a platform Arm says is already used by more than 30 million developers. Arm’s own note on the quarter said both its IP business and its new silicon business are showing rapid growth on the same compute platform. Source: techpowerup.com

AMD Guidance Points to Its First Quarter Ever With More Revenue Than Intel: TechPowerUp

AMD guided fourth-quarter revenue to approximately $17 billion, plus or minus 3 percent, or roughly $16.5 billion to $17.5 billion, while Intel expects $15 billion to $16 billion. The bottom of AMD’s range sits above the top of Intel’s, so the crossover holds unless either company misses guidance badly. AMD’s Data Center segment hit a record $8.5 billion in the third quarter, up 97 percent year over year, on EPYC demand and the launch of Instinct MI450 Series GPUs inside Helios racks. AMD expects that segment to grow about 50 percent sequentially in the fourth quarter, while Client and Gaming is expected to drop by more than 20 percent. Intel’s fourth-quarter outlook includes a two-week shutdown at Fab 28 in Israel that Intel says will cost about $300 million in fourth-quarter revenue. Source: techpowerup.com

Universal Quantum Launches iQPU Integrated Trapped-Ion Processor: HPCwire

Universal Quantum on November 4, 2026 launched the iQPU, an integrated quantum processing unit meant to be manufactured, scaled, and deployed as a unit rather than a loose assembly of ion traps and control electronics. It combines a trapped-ion compute surface with built-in ultra-low-noise control and modular chip-to-chip interconnects the company calls UQConnect. The launch was announced from Haywards Heath, England, and Hamburg, Germany. Source: hpcwire.com

Data Centres & Power

Modular AI Data Centers Gain Ground as Crusoe Debuts Unit: Data Center Knowledge

Crusoe has debuted the Spark Liquid, a factory-built modular data-center unit rated at 3.5 megawatts, as a faster path to AI capacity than a conventional hall. Source: datacenterknowledge.com

APS expects sales to double by 2041, plans extending coal use to 2038: Utility Dive

Arizona Public Service, in an integrated resource plan filed Friday with regulators, expects sales to double by 2041 and peak demand to grow about 3.4 percent a year over the next 15 years, driven by industry and data centers. The plan proposes extending coal use to 2038. Source: utilitydive.com

atNorth signs deal with Calentix for heat reuse at SWE04 data center in Sollefteå, Sweden: Data Center Dynamics

atNorth has signed a deal with Calentix to reuse heat from the SWE04 data center in Sollefteå, Sweden, with the first delivery of heat expected in 2029. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Local data center moratoria, limits, and restrictions voted in across the US: Data Center Dynamics

Local data-center moratoria, limits, and restrictions were voted in across the United States, and most of the anti-data-center measures passed, with Ohio leading the charge. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

Supply Chain & Policy

Nvidia tells data center partners to cut China exposure, limits RTX chip sales to mainland: Data Center Dynamics

Nvidia has told data-center partners to cut China exposure and is limiting RTX chip sales to mainland China, as the United States and China both move to decouple. Source: datacenterdynamics.com

TSMC stock in focus as Tesla reopens Texas Terafab talks: ad-hoc-news.de

A November 2 report by El Fondo, citing supply-chain sources and a DigiTimes account, said Elon Musk had softened his earlier position and that talks could reopen toward TSMC taking primary responsibility for building and operating Tesla’s planned Terafab in Texas. Tesla and SpaceX had previously been described as planning to build and run the plant themselves, with TSMC potentially limited to subleasing part of the site. Equity ownership and investment arrangements are still under negotiation, and Tesla, SpaceX, and TSMC had not immediately commented. Intel was previously linked after Musk said Tesla planned to use Intel’s next-generation 14A process; the latest report did not clarify whether that role would change. On SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call, Musk said he aimed to have a test chip operating around May or June 2027 at an Austin research fab, and described volume production at Terafab in the third or fourth quarter of 2028 as an aspiration. Source: ad-hoc-news.de

Nintendo ROM chip supplier Macronix quits to focus on chips for AI data centers, reports indicate: Tom's Hardware

Macronix, one of Nintendo’s ROM chip manufacturers, is shutting down that business to reallocate manufacturing to NOR and NAND flash chips that the AI industry demands, according to reports. Source: tomshardware.com

Worldwide Silicon Wafer Shipments Increase 13.4% Year-on-Year in Q3 2026, SEMI Reports: Semiconductor Digest

SEMI’s Silicon Manufacturers Group reported worldwide silicon wafer shipments of 3,756 million square inches in the third quarter of 2026, up 13.4 percent from 3,313 million square inches in the same quarter of 2025 and up 5.1 percent from 3,573 million square inches in the second quarter of 2026. Masatsugu Miura, chairman of SEMI SMG and a SUMCO sales and marketing general manager, said the increase was supported by AI-related demand that has broadened beyond advanced logic and memory to power devices and photonics, plus stronger industrial demand from robotics, AI server power supplies, and general-purpose industrial semiconductors. Bonding and advanced packaging also continued to support demand for 300 mm polished wafers. Source: semiconductor-digest.com

Semiconductor Market Continues to Accelerate in Q3 2026: Semiconductor Digest

The European Semiconductor Industry Association said global semiconductor sales reached $517.7 billion in the third quarter of 2026, up 146.8 percent from the third quarter of 2025. Source: semiconductor-digest.com

Intel Says EMIB Wafer Utilization Tops 90% as AI Chips Drive 70-Bridge Era: BigGo Finance

Intel says EMIB wafer utilization tops 90 percent as AI chips drive what it calls a 70-bridge era. Source: finance.biggo.com

GlobalFoundries Data Center Sales Up 81% Ahead of TSMC Deal: FourWeekMBA

GlobalFoundries’ data-center sales are up 81 percent ahead of a TSMC deal. Source: fourweekmba.com

Silicon Photonics Hits the Big Time: EE Times

EE Times reports a land grab in silicon photonics aimed at the power wall of copper interconnects, including a $125 million funding round for Salience Labs’ optical circuit switching. Source: eetimes.com

DIGITIMES reports that a TSMC and ASML photomask target for 2031 puts High NA EUV suppliers on the clock. Source: digitimes.com

The Teardown: When the Unterminated Link Runs Out of Millimetres

A die-to-die wire inside an advanced package is not a shorter version of a board trace. Semiconductor Engineering, in a system-level signal-integrity piece by Muhammad Hassan, Mahmoud Mahany, and Mohamed Saleh, treats the channel as a stack of discontinuities: microbumps, through-silicon vias, redistribution layers, a silicon interposer or a silicon bridge, then the package transition into the next die. Each of those steps changes impedance and disturbs the return path. At multi-gigahertz rates the loss on those microscopic wires is dominated by skin effect, so insertion loss falls steeply and almost linearly with frequency. The same channel also changes character. At low frequency it behaves like a resistive-capacitive lump. At high frequency it behaves like a transmission line. That shift disperses the edge, spreads symbols into their neighbors, and shows up at the receiver pad as a shorter eye and more jitter.

Crosstalk, not the dielectric, is the louder noise source. Routing gaps of 0.5 micrometres to 2 micrometres let capacitive and inductive coupling inject more noise than the physical dielectric attenuates. The copper itself is on that same scale: traces of 0.5 to 2 micrometres, embedded in deposited silicon dioxide or a low-k film that the foundry has planarized flat, because any waviness misaligns the mask and snaps the wire. A conventional board, by contrast, tolerates thickness variation and uses coarse geometries above 50 micrometres. Checking the die, the interposer, and the package as three separate models misses the coupling that only exists when they are stacked.

Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express is the spec that turns that physics into two different electrical contracts. Semiconductor Engineering walks the split. Advanced packaging, the 2.5D interposer or bridge tier, is specified for an ultra-short reach of 2 millimetres or less, at up to 64 gigatransfers per second per pin under UCIe 3.0, with 64 single-ended mainband data lanes per module. Those signals are left unterminated at the receiver, and the energy target is 0.25 picojoules per bit. Standard packaging, the 2D laminate tier, stretches the channel to 25 millimetres, at 16 to 32 gigatransfers per second per pin, across 16 single-ended mainband lanes per module. The receiver uses 50-ohm parallel ground terminations to kill reflections off the longer trace, and the energy cost is 0.5 picojoules per bit. Physical-layer latency on the open spec is described as up to sub-2 nanoseconds. Compliance is not a lab courtesy: the extracted insertion loss, S21, has to stay strictly above a mandatory voltage-transfer-function mask.

Take the energy numbers as a worked budget, using only those published targets. One bit on the advanced-package path is budgeted at 0.25 picojoules. The same bit on the laminate path is budgeted at 0.5 picojoules, twice the energy, because the termination has to absorb the reflection the short unterminated link never builds up. A module on the advanced path also carries 64 mainband lanes against 16 on the standard path, four times the lane count, at a peak rate of 64 gigatransfers per second against a standard-package band of 16 to 32. The bargain is the reach. Two millimetres is the advanced-package ceiling in that description; 25 millimetres is what the terminated laminate is allowed to span. Stretch the unterminated, 0.25-picojoule link toward that longer run and the reflection the termination was invented to kill comes back, the eye that already lost height to the linear roll-off closes further, and the S21 curve fails the mask even if the arithmetic of the link still “works” on a short channel.

The unterminated advanced-package choice stops being the right electrical design once the physical span leaves that 2-millimetre reach, or once a bump map cannot keep aggressors inside the 0.5-to-2-micrometre gap regime without the coupling exceeding dielectric loss. At that point the designer is on the standard-package contract: fewer lanes, a lower transfer rate, a 50-ohm termination, and 0.5 picojoules per bit. Source: semiengineering.com

Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express writes that physics as two contracts. Advanced packaging, the interposer or bridge, is for a reach of two millimetres or less, up to sixty-four gigatransfers a second per pin under UCIe three point zero, with sixty-four single-ended mainband lanes in a module. Those lines are unterminated at the receiver, budgeted at a quarter of a picojoule per bit. Standard packaging, the laminate, may run out to twenty-five millimetres, at sixteen to thirty-two gigatransfers a second, on sixteen mainband lanes, with fifty-ohm terminations to ground so the reflection dies, at half a picojoule per bit. Physical-layer latency on the open spec is described as under two nanoseconds. The extracted insertion loss has to stay above a mandatory voltage-transfer mask. One bit on the short path is budgeted at a quarter of a picojoule. The same bit on the laminate path is half a picojoule, twice the energy, because the termination has to swallow a reflection the short link never builds. The advanced module also carries four times the lanes, sixty-four against sixteen, at a peak of sixty-four gigatransfers a second against a standard band of sixteen to thirty-two. The price of that bargain is the span: two millimetres versus twenty-five. Push the unterminated quarter-picojoule link toward the longer run and the reflection returns, the eye that already shrank under the linear roll-off closes further, and the insertion-loss curve fails the mask even if the link math still looks fine on a short channel. That unterminated choice stops being the right electrical design once the span leaves two millimetres, or once the bump map cannot hold aggressors inside those half-to-two-micrometre gaps without coupling beating the dielectric. Then the contract is the laminate one: fewer lanes, a lower rate, a fifty-ohm termination, and half a picojoule a bit.
