Kenya's court stopped a 50-bed US Ebola site until the deal is disclosed.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Kenya's High Court halts work at a US-backed Ebola quarantine site

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reports that Kenya's High Court ruled on Thursday that the government breached the constitution by allowing a United States-backed Ebola quarantine site at a military airbase without disclosing the deal or seeking public comment. Judge Patricia Nyaundi ordered any further work stopped until those conditions are met. The 50-bed site sits inside Laikipia airbase near Nanyuki, in central Kenya, and was meant to quarantine people exposed to Ebola, including US citizens. Seven American aid workers who had been in the Democratic Republic of the Congo quarantined there in July. In June, Kenyan police opened fire on protesters against the site and at least one person was killed; the week before Thursday's ruling, hundreds marched in Nanyuki and at least two people were killed and one wounded. The same court had suspended construction in late May. It found that Health Minister Aden Duale had not obeyed that order; he later told the court he had ordered an immediate halt. Kenya's first imported case of the Bundibugyo Ebola strain — a form of the virus with no approved vaccine or treatment — was recorded in early October in a traveller from Congo via Uganda. As of mid-November the country had two deaths and seven other cases, all of whom recovered, and no new cases since October 19. The World Health Organization, the UN health agency, declared the outbreak an international public health emergency on May 17. The government has not commented on Thursday's ruling.

Context & perspectives: The dispute turns on whether a foreign health site on a military base can be built before the public sees the agreement. Residents of Nanyuki and Laikipia, in protests and in the case, say the United States is shifting Ebola risk onto Kenyans; their strongest reason is proximity — the beds are inside a Kenyan airbase, and people living beside it were not shown the deal. Kenyan and US authorities, before this ruling, publicly restated their commitment to the plan even after the May suspension; their reason is the outbreak itself — a quarantine for exposed people, including Americans who had worked in Congo, while Bundibugyo still has no approved vaccine. Nyaundi's ruling does not decide that medical question. It says residents were left "standing outside the sealed wooden window," able to tell that decisions were being made and denied a chance to know or take part. The court's own record also shows the May stop was not followed until Duale later said he had ordered work to cease.

Major world stories (3)

1) Two men arrested in Germany over an alleged synagogue plot

What happened (neutral): Deutsche Welle reports that Germany's federal prosecutor on Thursday ordered the arrest of two Iraqi citizens, identified as Mostafa Al-R. and Ameer J., in Heidelberg and Cologne. Prosecutors say they are strongly suspected of membership in a domestic terrorist organization. More than 250 officers took part, and searches at 17 sites in three states were still going on. Prosecutors say Mostafa Al-R. was an associate of Mohammad Al-Saadi in the United States, accused of setting up a group called HAYI — Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiyya — which they say was responsible for attacks on US, Israeli, and Jewish institutions in Europe from March 2026, after the outbreak of Donald Trump's war with Iran. They say the two men founded a separate organization at the end of 2025 to prepare further attacks in Germany and Europe, gathered funds, and recruited people for explosive attacks. On two nights in late March 2026, prosecutors wrote, unknown assailants went — presumably with explosives — to synagogues in Cologne and in one other European city; the plan was not carried out, for reasons not yet known. The lead, prosecutors said, came from the BfV, Germany's domestic intelligence agency. The two were expected before judges later on Thursday for a decision on investigative detention. One other person is still being sought.

Context & perspectives: The prosecutor's account is the only detailed public case so far: a plot prepared, then not carried out, with the March approach to two synagogues as the clearest alleged act. The men have not had a quoted reply in this report. Deutsche Welle notes the story is still developing.

Questions to consider: Did judges later on Thursday order the two men held in investigative detention?

2) Strikes in Gaza kill an education official and seven other people

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reports that Israeli attacks killed at least eight Palestinians across Gaza on Thursday, including Khaled Abu Nada, undersecretary of Gaza's Ministry of Education, and his wife, in an air strike in western Deir el-Balah. Medics at Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital said six other people were wounded, several critically. Al Jazeera's Ashraf Abu Amra, reporting from Deir el-Balah, said Israeli Apache helicopters fired two missiles at a residential apartment during dawn prayers, in an area crowded with tents. In the north, a drone strike on a group in Gaza City's Shati refugee camp killed three and wounded four, al-Shifa Hospital said; a strike on Tuffah killed three; a drone attack on Nassr wounded several people. The deaths come during a ceasefire that took effect last October.

Context & perspectives: Everything turns on who Abu Nada was, and what killing him during a ceasefire is for. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defence Minister Israel Katz said the strike killed a Hamas figure responsible for the group's economy and education portfolios — their reason for the strike is that those portfolios are Hamas administration, not a civilian school office. Gaza's Ministry of Interior accuses Israel of trying to spread chaos by killing security and police commanders, after Mohammed Abu al-Ouf, internal security chief for northern Gaza, was killed with his wife and two daughters early Wednesday, and Naim Abu Nahl, Gaza's internal security director, was killed the day before. Health Ministry spokesperson Zaher al-Wahidi told Al Jazeera that more than 1,600 Palestinians have been killed, including 130 this month, and more than 6,000 wounded, in Israeli attacks since the ceasefire, and called it systematic targeting of "all the pillars of life." The ministry's overall toll since October 2023 is 74,746 killed. Neither statement concedes the other's description of Abu Nada.

3) Explosion and fire shut a Bangkok oil refinery

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reports that an explosion and fire at Bangchak Corporation's Phra Khanong oil facility in eastern Bangkok injured at least seven people on Thursday, four of them seriously, according to the city's Erawan Emergency Medical Centre. The company said it shut all production units, contained the incident, and brought the situation under control. The Bangna District Office told people nearby to leave at once and said the cause was under investigation. A city alert said the fire was under control but told people to avoid the area. The site is described by the company as a refinery with a maximum capacity of 120,000 barrels a day. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul said he was monitoring the situation, that it appeared under control, and that the fire did not seem to be in the main production area, without further detail. Al Jazeera's Tony Cheng reported the blast was heard at about 3 p.m. local time, that the site is near Bangkok's main port and supplies much of central Thailand's oil imports, and that the highway and most major routes there were still open.

Context & perspectives: An explosion at the same refinery in July 2012, then owned by Bangchak Petroleum, caused a large fire and no reported injuries. At the time, people asked why a plant of that size stood in what is now a residential part of the city. Cheng said industrial accidents have occurred in the area in the past couple of years. The extent of Thursday's damage is not yet known.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) India and the United States reset the tariff argument

What happened (neutral): The Hindu reports that a new proposal from the USTR — the Office of the United States Trade Representative, which negotiates US trade deals — puts the tariff framework on India back to what it was when trade talks began. The same report says experts argue the tariffs are not a substantial threat and that India should not rush into a deal. Deutsche Welle reports that India has denied US claims that New Delhi's policies promote excess manufacturing capacity, and that India has told the United States to resolve tariff concerns in the talks. Reuters reports that President Donald Trump's new tariffs are aimed at prising global trade from China's grip.

Context & perspectives: New Delhi's case, as the government states it and as The Hindu's account of experts adds, is that this paper is a return to the opening bid, not a blow that forces a signature, and that the excess-capacity charge is wrong. Washington's case, as Reuters describes the tariff aim, is larger than the India text: use tariffs to pull trade away from China, so India's terms sit inside that contest rather than beside it. The articles do not record either government giving ground.

2) Seabirds carry sensors toward fishing boats that switch tracking off

What happened (neutral): The New York Times reports that researchers are fitting seabirds with GPS — satellite location trackers — and audio gear to find fishing vessels that operate with no tracking signals, and to judge the environmental hazards those boats pose. A boat that turns its signal off is hard to see from satellites that read those signals; a bird that follows the boat can record where it went and what it sounded like.

Context & perspectives: The report is a methods story, not a count of boats found. It does not name a fleet, a fine, or a government response.

Progress watch (1)

1) War and drought squeeze Sudan's farms and water

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reports that attacks on electricity infrastructure and drought are threatening Sudan's harvests and leaving millions without water. The people most exposed are farmers whose livelihoods depend on that power and on rain that has not come.

Context & perspectives: The article presents both blows together: grid attacks from the war, and drought, hitting the same season. It does not name a relief shipment, a repair crew, or a ceasefire step for the power lines.

Understanding the Issue: Why a Russian ship hit far from Ukraine may already be stuck

A strike on a Russian ship in the Baltic or the Mediterranean is easy to read as the land war spilling, at random, into other seas. Deutsche Welle's account of those operations points somewhere more specific. Russia has only a narrow strip of Baltic coast, between Finland and Estonia, and uses that water to supply Kaliningrad — a Russian region separated from the rest of the country — and to export oil on what is called a shadow fleet. Ukraine had already struck oil terminals in that same arc, at Ust-Luga and Primorsk, near St. Petersburg. Farther south, a corvette of Russia's Black Sea Fleet could not sail home: Turkey has closed the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, the straits that join the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, so the ship was escorting cargo ships in the Mediterranean instead. Elisabeth Braw of the Atlantic Council told Deutsche Welle this is not a hunt for any tanker selling oil above a price cap; the cargo ship Sparta II, hit off Finland, was suspected of carrying military equipment, which Moscow denies, and the point Kyiv is making is that it can find such ships in whatever water they are in. Officially the goal is to break logistics; military experts and politicians also read the strikes as a signal of strength toward Western allies before any peace talks. Ukraine has hit far from the front before, including an oil refinery in Omsk, in Siberia, and Russian military facilities in Syria before the Assad government fell in December 2024. When the next report names a ship far from Ukraine, check which of three labels it actually uses — warship, alleged arms cargo, or shadow-fleet tanker — and whether Moscow denies the cargo. Source: dw.com

Media-literacy note

A court order, a prosecutor's charge, and a minister's claim are three different kinds of statement. The first is a ruling you can read; the second is an allegation still to be tested; the third is an official saying what an investigation found, which is not the same as the file itself. When two governments describe the same dead official in opposite terms, the useful next step is the document each one is willing to publish, not a third summary that blends them. Pick one story from today and open the outlet you did not start with.
