Russia holds a fifth of Ukraine, and Washington floated trading Donbas for NATO.

By Patrick November 14, 2026 Ep 236 16 min read Listen to podcast

Today's lead story (1)

1) Washington floats a Donbas withdrawal in exchange for NATO

What happened (neutral): Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said on Saturday that American negotiators asked, for the first time, whether Ukraine would pull its remaining forces out of the eastern Donbas if it could join the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, or NATO, the BBC reports. He said he would send a package to a referendum only if it included security guarantees, no limits on Ukraine's army, and money to rebuild. The BBC reports that envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff met him in Dublin on Thursday after talks with Russian President Vladimir Putin in Moscow the day before, and that Russia occupies roughly a fifth of Ukraine, including most of Donetsk and Luhansk. US officials had previously said NATO membership was off the table; Zelensky said the question made him think "Nato is back on the agenda," while calling talks with Putin "an extremely difficult conversation."

Context & perspectives: Kyiv has long wanted alliance membership as the deterrent inside any deal, and Zelensky has refused to give up sovereign land even as Moscow demands the Donbas. Moscow's stated terms have included limits on the size of Ukraine's forces, territorial concessions, and guaranteed neutrality; Ukraine has rejected those as leaving it unable to stop another invasion. Kremlin comments, the BBC reports, suggest that position has not moved. France 24, citing remarks released by Zelensky's office, places the same American question after the latest round of talks in Abu Dhabi rather than after the Dublin meeting, and quotes him warning that the Kremlin's bid to rewrite Europe's security setup "lead[s] to wars or escalations" and that "Real security in Europe means the EU and NATO." The BBC adds that ceding the sliver Ukraine still holds in the Donbas would be small on the map and large as a symbol, because it would accept the Donbas as Russian, and that First Lady Melania Trump last month helped broker a temporary ceasefire in occupied Oleshky for evacuations and aid.

Contested: Zelensky's case is that a deal without guarantees, an unconstrained army, and rebuild funds is not a peace he can even ask voters to weigh, because membership is what would deter the next attack, and he is not willing to retreat from territory on a question alone. The Kremlin's case, as Putin and Russian officials have stated it, is that another NATO state on Russia's border is unacceptable, and that neutrality plus a smaller Ukrainian force is the condition that removes the threat Moscow says it is fighting. Zelensky himself flags the limit on his reading: Putin's signals make the conversation extremely hard. The articles do not record Moscow conceding any of its terms.

Questions to consider: If a written package with guarantees, no army cap, and rebuild funds appears, does Zelensky put it to a referendum?

Major world stories (3)

1) US airspace halted after aging communications overloaded

What happened (neutral): The Federal Aviation Administration, the US agency that runs civil airspace, issued a rare nationwide ground stop on Friday after aging radar and telecom links controllers use between facilities began to falter at 3:36 p.m., The New York Times reports. France 24 reports that more than 8,200 flights, over 31 percent of those scheduled in the United States, were canceled, that the stop lasted nearly five hours, and that it was the longest in the country since September 11, 2001. Officials blamed outdated equipment and overloaded routers. The stop has been lifted and flights have resumed, though Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said recovery would take time and that he expected schedules back to normal by the Thanksgiving travel rush.

Context & perspectives: The Times reports this was only the third nationwide ground stop the agency has issued this century, and the second-longest after September 11, 2001; a shorter one followed a pilot-alert outage on January 11, 2023. More than 1,000 aircraft were airborne and bound for US airports during the stop. On a typical day more than 2 million people pass airport security; on Thursday the Transportation Security Administration screened nearly 2.5 million. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was never an indication of a cyberattack and that lives were never in danger, while adding that investigators were still looking and would not rule an attack out. France 24 reports officials said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. Duffy said last year Congress approved 12.5 billion dollars to upgrade facilities and technology, which he has called a down payment, and on Friday night he asked for another 50 billion dollars in emergency funding before the end of the year, up from a previous 30 billion dollar request, after which he said control of Congress and the budget switches from Republicans to Democrats.

Contested: Duffy's argument is that the same antiquated links will fail again unless Congress treats modernization as an emergency and delivers the larger sum while Republicans still hold the budget. Some Democratic lawmakers, the Times reports, have already asked whether the agency has spent the money it has been given efficiently and effectively — a check on writing another emergency cheque before that record is clear. The articles do not quote those lawmakers conceding that the current kit is adequate.

Questions to consider: Does Congress approve emergency air-traffic funding before control of the budget switches at the end of the year?

2) Sudan's aid plan is less than half funded

What happened (neutral): The International Organization for Migration and the UN refugee agency say funding gaps have run down emergency shelter and basic household goods for Sudan, Deutsche Welle reports. The United Nations plan needs 2.9 billion dollars to reach 20.4 million people; 47 percent has been raised. A total of 33.7 million people in the country depend on humanitarian aid. Some 15.4 million need emergency shelter and household items, and lack of money has let agencies assist only 10 percent of them.

Context & perspectives: More than three and a half years after fighting began between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, about 8.6 million people have fled inside the country, more than 4.6 million have left for neighbouring states, and another 5.2 million have gone back to ruined towns anyway. Death estimates run from 40,000 to 250,000; updated counts are unavailable while fighting, curbed satellite internet, and the exit of many observers continue. Marina Peter, chair of the Sudan and South Sudan Forum in Germany, told DW that some people have survived on roots, that food rations in refugee camps have been cut, and that child mortality is above average while the warring parties still block access. Ivo Körner of Caritas International told DW that the United States, Germany, and the European Union are giving far less than four or five years ago. Caritas still supports refugees and host communities with cash, drinking water, basic medical care, and seeds, but its 2026 Sudan projects have been financed only by donations. On October 23 the United Kingdom and 31 other partners, including Germany, said 19.5 million people — about 41 percent of the population — face acute food insecurity. Körner said he hopes for German Foreign Office money and expects opportunities to be tighter than in earlier years.

3) France's Saturday marches turn on pensions and policing

What happened (neutral): Campaign groups, students, and Yellow Vest protesters are due to march together in Paris this Saturday — a fifth straight Saturday — joining demands on wages, education, and civil liberties, France 24 reports. The Hindu reports that thousands of arrests, police beatings, and limits on marches have fed an anti-government swell that shows no sign of easing before next year's presidential election. The New York Times reports that the same weekend's demonstrations, and recent youth-driven protests in Belgium, Germany, Greece, Italy, and Portugal, sit on a budget fight: as people live longer, pensions are routinely the largest items in Europe's national budgets.

Context & perspectives: Union leaders, France 24 reports, warn that protest fatigue is setting in, while police officers are seeking better pay for the risk of policing the marches. France 24 also reports that many teachers back the students who have blockaded high schools for nearly two months, and that headteachers gathered on Friday saying threats and violence have become unbearable and asking the government to protect them.

Contested: Sébastien Ducros, a 36-year-old data analyst who supports the student protests, told the Times that dilapidated schools lose out because "a huge portion of the money is going toward retirees," and that people like his parents, about to retire, could give some pension back. Laurent Grinwald, 72, a retired postal worker, told the Times he is not eager to have his pension cut for other services after decades of work, especially with rents and the cost of living up. Jorge Galindo, director of the Spanish think tank EsadeEcPol, called it "a generational clash" and noted that Spain has some of Europe's most generous pensions. Neither Ducros nor Grinwald is quoted conceding the other's budget math.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) The US loses its measles-elimination status

What happened (neutral): The Pan American Health Organization, the regional health body for the Americas, has revoked the United States' measles-elimination status after 26 years, Deutsche Welle reports, citing a Wall Street Journal account of people familiar with the decision. An independent commission of experts reporting to the organization's director concluded the country no longer qualified, a source close to the process told CNN. The test was whether one strain had spread without a break for 12 months or longer. A source familiar with the findings told Reuters the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found 12 months of continuous transmission. The agency has recorded 5,182 confirmed cases as of Thursday, across 45 states, New York City, and Washington, D.C. — more than double 2025's total of 2,289. About 7,500 people have been infected and 13 have died since 2025.

Context & perspectives: Nearly all of this year's cases are in people who were unvaccinated or whose status is unknown, and roughly one in nine patients were hospitalized, CNN reported, citing the CDC. Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina professor who chairs an independent measles panel and who presented the CDC report to the regional body on Tuesday, told Reuters: "It is a fully preventable disease. It is unconscionable that we have this amount of measles." Johns Hopkins epidemiologist William Moss told Reuters the loss hurts the country's reputation as a public-health leader and means large outbreaks will continue. The period has coincided with greater vaccine hesitancy under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who for years has shown skepticism about measles-vaccine safety without scientific proof. CDC Director Erica Schwartz, before the decision, said the agency would keep working with states to encourage vaccination. To regain the status, a country must stop transmission and stay outbreak-free for at least 12 months.

2) Starship reaches orbit; the booster does not come home

What happened (neutral): SpaceX launched Starship, which it calls the world's largest spaceship, on a 15th test flight meant to put the craft in orbit and release 15 satellites for its Starlink network, France 24 reports. Deutsche Welle reports the company had planned, for the first time, to catch and later reuse both stages. The spacecraft continued into orbit. The booster, which was supposed to return to the pad, did not.

Context & perspectives: France 24 reports the booster splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico minutes after liftoff. Deutsche Welle reports the booster exploded over the Gulf of Mexico. Neither account publishes wreckage imagery or a company statement that would show which description the hardware supports; both agree the upper stage flew on and the booster was not recovered.

Progress watch (1)

1) Dakar closes the first Olympic event hosted in Africa

What happened (neutral): The Summer Youth Olympics in Dakar ended Friday after two weeks, the first time an African country has hosted an Olympic event, Deutsche Welle reports. Nearly 2,700 young athletes competed in 153 medal events across 25 sports, African nations took home a record medal haul, and Senegal won an Olympic gold for the first time, with 136 Senegalese athletes in the games. China led the gold table with 18, Japan took 11, Russia eight, Egypt six, and Ethiopia and Senegal five each.

Context & perspectives: International Olympic Committee President Kirsty Coventry said expectations had been exceeded and that the games showed what can happen "when an entire country comes together for its young people." The complication is political: it was the first Olympic event since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 in which Russian athletes competed under their own flag. Chile, Paraguay, and Thailand have confirmed bids for the next Summer Youth Olympics, in 2030.

Understanding the Issue: How the worker-to-retiree ratio locks a national budget

A common picture of Europe's pension fights is that they are only a quarrel about whether older people have been too comfortable. The mechanism is a headcount. Germany's public pension insurance had 3.73 contributors for every retiree in 1957; by 2025 that ratio was 2.1, and by 2045 it is projected to fall to 1.73, which in headcount is 35.35 million contributors financing 16.82 million retirees, Deutsche Welle reports in a data check on the generations. To keep contribution rates from jumping with that shift, the pension system already receives about 118 billion euros a year in government subsidies, roughly a quarter of the federal budget, so a rising share of everyone else's taxes is already spoken for before schools, housing support, or training are debated. The same check finds the squeeze is not abstract: retirees on federal basic income support rose from 257,000 in 2003 to 738,000 in 2024, and low-income households' rent share rose from about 24 percent of income in 1990 to nearly 36 percent in 2021. In 2025, 11 percent of the population spent more than 40 percent of disposable income on housing, and among people at risk of poverty that figure was 35 percent. Public education spending on people under 30 has doubled since 2011, from about 4,000 euros to about 8,000 euros a person each year, yet in 2022 it was still 4.6 percent of gross domestic product against an OECD average of 4.7 percent — more money, a middle-of-the-pack share. Youth researcher Simon Schnetzer told DW the fair historical question is whether earlier generations had it hard too, and that young people are answering a different one: whether they have been given conditions to live, work, and keep the system funded. Read a street report against that ratio and that budget share, and the argument stops being "old versus young" and becomes "what is already committed before this year's argument starts." Next time a pension protest is covered, check whether the piece states the contributor-to-retiree ratio and the subsidy share, or only the crowd. Source: dw.com

Media-literacy note

When two outlets describe the same remark, write down the place each one attaches to it before you decide the diplomacy has moved. A status revoked by a regional health body is a decision; the case count underneath it is a separate document, and both deserve a look. Funding percentages and medal tables are checkable; a mood about a generation is not, until someone shows the ratio.

Go deeper: The BBC places the Donbas question after Kushner and Witkoff met Zelensky in Dublin, following Moscow, and notes Washington had taken NATO membership off the table; France 24 places that question after talks in Abu Dhabi and adds his line that only the EU and NATO are real European security.

The test was whether one strain had circulated without a break for twelve months or more. Johns Hopkins epidemiologist William Moss told Reuters the loss damages America's reputation as a public-health leader and means large outbreaks will continue. The stretch has come with more vaccine hesitancy under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who for years has doubted measles-vaccine safety without scientific proof. CDC Director Erica Schwartz, before the decision, said the agency would keep working with states to encourage vaccination. A country gets the status back only after transmission ends and it stays outbreak-free for at least twelve months. SpaceX sent Starship up on a fifteenth test flight, France 24 reports, aiming to reach orbit and release fifteen satellites for its Starlink network. To really understand the pension anger behind those European marches, look at the headcount, not the stereotype that the fight is only about whether older people have been too comfortable. Germany's public pension insurance had three point seven three contributors for every retiree in nineteen fifty-seven. By twenty twenty-five the ratio was two point one. By twenty forty-five it is projected at one point seven three — thirty-five point three five million contributors financing sixteen point eight two million retirees, Deutsche Welle reports in a data check on the generations. Holding contribution rates steady already costs about one hundred eighteen billion euros a year in government subsidies, roughly a quarter of the federal budget, so a rising share of everyone else's taxes is committed before schools or training are argued. Retirees on federal basic income support rose from two hundred fifty-seven thousand in two thousand three to seven hundred thirty-eight thousand in twenty twenty-four. Low-income households spent about twenty-four percent of income on rent in nineteen ninety and nearly thirty-six percent in twenty twenty-one. In twenty twenty-five, eleven percent of the population spent more than forty percent of disposable income on housing, and among people at risk of poverty that share was thirty-five percent. Public education spending on people under thirty has doubled since twenty eleven, from about four thousand euros to about eight thousand euros a person each year, and in twenty twenty-two it was still four point six percent of gross domestic product against an OECD average of four point seven percent.
