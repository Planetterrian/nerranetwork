One million orbital computers won U.S. approval, moving heavy data work off Earth.

Today's lead story (1)

1) U.S. clears a million orbital computers

What happened (neutral): The U.S. Federal Communications Commission, the agency that licenses radio uses including satellites, has authorised SpaceX to deploy one million orbital computing modules under a plan named Starmind, Al Jazeera reports. Chairman Brendan Carr said the approval lets SpaceX “deploy 1,000,000 orbital compute modules to process massive amounts of data to be used back here on Earth,” and called it a first in the country’s history. He said that under President Donald Trump the United States was moving so this technology “creates opportunity, drives investment, and delivers benefits for the American people.”

Context & perspectives: SpaceX says the satellites would run artificial intelligence, software that finds patterns in huge data sets, in a low-latency sun-synchronous orbit and beam the results back, using the sun for power. The company calls that the fastest way to scale because electrical power on Earth is getting harder to find, and describes the plan as low-cost and high performance. Under the licence, deployment must begin no later than November 2033; 100,000 modules, 10 percent of the maximum, no later than November 2035; half by November 2038; and the full constellation by 2040. SatFleet Live, which tracks satellites in real time, counts 16,928 active satellites now, of which 11,190, or 66 percent, are SpaceX Starlink internet satellites. Data centres on the ground need land, water and power and throw off heat. An article the Environmental and Energy Study Institute, a U.S. nonprofit, published in 2025 said large data centres can use up to 5 million gallons of water a day, about the use of a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people. Those pressures have brought protests in the United States, Kenya and India. In July, opponents of rapid data-centre growth held 142 protests across 42 U.S. states, and Al Jazeera says polling data showed opposition was a key issue for many voters in this month’s U.S. midterm elections.

Major world stories (3)

2) New Zealand’s final count leaves a new centrist party with the deciding seats

What happened (neutral): Final results of New Zealand’s November 7 election, released Friday, leave Prime Minister Christopher Luxon’s centre-right coalition one seat short of a majority, Al Jazeera reports. Luxon’s National Party and its partners, populist New Zealand First and libertarian ACT, won 65 seats: National 40, ACT 13, New Zealand First 12. Parliament has 130 members, so 66 are needed. Labour, the largest opposition party, won 36. Qiulae Wong’s new centrist Opportunity Party won 7 seats.

Context & perspectives: Al Jazeera ties the shortfall to lost support in a stagnant economy. Luxon has been prime minister since 2023. The Associated Press likewise reports a deadlocked final result with the centrist newcomer as the decider. If no agreement is reached, voters could return to the polls next year. Luke Oldfield, a political scientist at Victoria University of Wellington, told Reuters a deal was likely and that a right-hand bloc was highly likely, not a second election.

Contested: The count both camps have to live with is 65 seats against a 66-seat majority, with Wong’s 7 the only ready bridge. Luxon told a news conference that New Zealanders need a government that is stable, works together and will last the full term — the case for adding one partner rather than sending the country back to the polls. Wong has promised a universal basic income of up to 370 New Zealand dollars, about 208 U.S. dollars, a week, more action on climate change, a ban on bottom-trawling fishing, healthcare reforms, and a kinder style of politics, and she has pledged to do everything she can to ensure a stable government. That package is the price of her seats. It collides with partners Luxon already has. Winston Peters of New Zealand First promises tighter immigration and withdrawal from the Paris Climate Accord, the global climate treaty; he has called opponents “morons” and told a Chinese-born lawmaker to “go back to your country.” Deputy Prime Minister David Seymour of ACT campaigned against a greater governance role for the Indigenous Maori minority and promised large cuts to state spending. Wong’s own pledge of stability does not erase that clash, and Luxon’s case for a lasting government still depends on a partner those two may not accept. Oldfield’s reading is that a right-hand deal is still the likely end.

Questions to consider: Will Luxon name a governing partner, or will the alternative of a fresh election next year become the path?

3) Cologne court orders the archdiocese to pay 550,000 euros

What happened (neutral): On Thursday the Higher Regional Court of Cologne ruled that the Archdiocese of Cologne must pay 550,000 euros, about 603,341 dollars, to a woman now 60, Deutsche Welle reports. Melanie F. was subjected to serious sexual abuse by a priest in the late 1970s and early 1980s. She had already received 360,000 euros, so the court added 190,000 euros. Before this, the highest court award in a similar case was 300,000 euros, granted by the Regional Court of Cologne in June 2023.

Context & perspectives: The Seventh Civil Senate pointed to an abortion performed without the knowledge or consent of the plaintiff, then 15, as a reason for a substantial increase. She reportedly became pregnant twice during the abuse. Born in 1966, she lived in a Catholic orphanage in Bonn. Bernd U., then a theology student and volunteer, later an ordained priest, took her and a boy two years older to live with him. From 1979 he abused her in his residence at the parish and threatened to send her back to the orphanage. He became legal guardian of both children. Cardinal Joseph Höffner, then Archbishop of Cologne, approved that arrangement. The higher court held the archdiocese liable under official liability and overruled the 2023 regional ruling, which had said the abuse was not sufficiently linked to his role as a priest and had happened in his capacity as a caregiver. The higher court said he gained her trust in a pastoral role the judges called a “cover,” that he would likely not have become foster father so easily without the cover of a celibate priest, and that most of the abuse took place in the rectory. Deutsche Welle reports that the 2023 ruling met disbelief inside an archdiocese already in turmoil over how Cardinal Rainer Maria Woelki handled abuse cases, after decades of church teaching that a priest is a priest in all he does.

4) Costa Rica bars a U.N. rapporteur at the airport

What happened (neutral): Costa Rica barred Francesca Albanese, the United Nations special rapporteur on the Occupied Palestinian Territories, an independent expert named by the U.N. Human Rights Council who does not speak for the U.N. itself, Al Jazeera reports. She was halted and held on Thursday at Juan Santamaría International Airport. Omer Badilla, director-general of the immigration authority, said she was refused entry because she is the subject of an international alert over links to terrorist acts, and that police enforce the law when national security is at risk. He did not say which country issued the alert.

Context & perspectives: She was appointed in 2022 and was travelling to receive an honorary doctorate from the University of Costa Rica. She has faced pushback from Israel and the United States over her advocacy for Palestinians and her accusations that Israel has committed genocide and other war crimes. The United States imposed sanctions on her after she criticised Washington’s policy on Gaza. President Laura Fernandez, in office since May, is a close ally of President Trump and has pursued closer trade ties with Israel. Israel declared Albanese persona non grata in 2024.

Contested: Badilla’s account is that an international alert tying her to terrorist acts made entry a national-security refusal, and that Costa Rican police had to apply that rule. The strongest case for going further, as Israel’s ambassador to the U.N., Danny Danon, put it in praise of Costa Rica, is that someone who uses a U.N. mandate to spread propaganda, legitimise terrorist organisations, desecrate the memory of the six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust, and vilify the United States and Israel should face consequences. Albanese called the detention a disgrace and appalling, wrote that “you see the face of the empire the day you touch a nerve,” and told Semanario Universidad that being on the U.S. sanctions list did not explain a stop in Costa Rica: “We are not in the United States. We are in Costa Rica,” and “Is Costa Rica a sovereign country?” She said the only country she knew she could not enter was Israel. Badilla never named the alert’s author, and Albanese did not dispute that she knew she was on the U.S. list.

Economy, science & technology (2)

5) Eurozone inflation is back at a three-year high

What happened (neutral): Inflation in the eurozone, the group of countries that use the euro, has doubled from 2 percent in December 2025 to 4 percent in October 2026, its highest level in three years, as conflict in the Middle East disrupts oil and gas, Al Jazeera reports. Energy inflation rose to 21 percent in October, the highest since December 2022, when it was 25.5 percent in the first winter after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, according to Eurostat, the European Union’s statistics office.

Context & perspectives: The European Central Bank, which sets interest rates for the eurozone, lists Lithuania at 6.1 percent, Bulgaria at 5.9 percent and Greece at 5.7 percent as the highest rates. Cyprus went from 0.1 percent at the end of last year to 5.4 percent, Italy from 1.2 to 5.6, Belgium from 2.2 to 5.2, and France from 0.7 to 3.7. Inflation eased in Slovakia, from 4.1 percent to 2.9 percent, and Al Jazeera also lists easier readings in Estonia (3.6 percent), Latvia (3.1), Austria (3.5) and Malta (2.3). Eurostat says a typical basket of food, housing, energy and transport now costs 69 percent more than in 2000, with about two-fifths of that increase in the past four years. In 2022 prices jumped by more than 8 percent in a single year. Prices in Hungary have more than tripled since 2000, so something that cost 100 euros then costs about 335 euros now, after a 17 percent surge in 2023 alone. In Athens, Giorgos, a 40-year-old journalist, said his pay rose from 800 euros to 1,100 euros over two years and he is still borrowing to get by. In London, Emma, 33, called nearly 10 pounds for a return Tube ride ridiculous. The Wall Street Journal adds a newer national reading: inflation in France and Spain fell more than expected in November as fuel prices declined, but remained too high for the European Central Bank, which is expected to raise its key rate for a third time since the war between the United States and Iran began.

6) A Gaza survey finds more than one in eight young children stunted

What happened (neutral): A nutritional survey by UNICEF, the United Nations Children’s Fund, and its partners in accessible areas of the Gaza Strip between May 31 and June 15, 2026, found that 12.2 percent of children under five were stunted, Al Jazeera reports. Only 22.4 percent of children aged six to 23 months received a minimum acceptable diet. Seven-year-old Nabigha al-Zaqzouq is 99 centimetres tall and weighs 10 kilograms. Five-year-old Tala Zidan is 94 centimetres, about 15 to 16 centimetres shorter than the average for her age, and her father says her height has barely changed in two years.

Context & perspectives: Nutritionist Nada Abu al-Nada says average food availability was about 2,580 calories per person per day before the war and estimated average consumption has fallen to about 1,000. At Nasser Hospital, endocrinologist Hani Sayed Zuhair says growth-problem cases in a single clinic session have risen from about 15 to 20 before the war to between 30 and 40 now. Families describe bread and canned food, and meals from charitable community kitchens. Nabigha’s mother, Alia, said that after the ceasefire they are living under a suffocating blockade and asked that medicines and treatments be allowed in. Tahrir Zidan said he has not been able to buy even one kilogram of meat, a chicken, or a kilogram of fruit.

Progress watch (1)

7) A Shandong city turns retired buses into cafes and keeps transit jobs

What happened (neutral): Zaozhuang, in eastern China’s Shandong province, has turned retired buses into mobile cafes and in doing so helped save the jobs of more than 200 transit workers who were facing layoffs, the South China Morning Post reports. In 2023 Liang Yu left her job as a station attendant with the city’s public transit group to become a barista, according to the Southern Metropolis Daily. A decommissioned bus, refitted as a mobile coffee shop, was the start of that work.

Context & perspectives: The complication is the reason the jobs were at risk: ride-hailing apps and electric bikes have been pulling riders off the old bus network, so the cafe conversion is a rescue inside a shrinking service, not a return of the old ridership.

Understanding the Issue: Why a change of party rarely rewrites Iran policy

A common misreading is that a new majority in Washington resets policy toward Iran the way a new board resets a company. The record Al Jazeera sets out is a sanctions machine built across parties. After the 1979 Islamic revolution, Democratic President Jimmy Carter began imposing sanctions once the U.S. embassy in Tehran was seized, eight months after the U.S.-backed monarch Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi was overthrown. Democratic President Bill Clinton later widened that isolation with “dual containment” of Tehran and Saddam Hussein’s Iraq, and in 1996 signed sanctions on foreign companies investing in Iran’s energy sector; the bill passed the House of Representatives 415 to 0. After the September 11, 2001 attacks, Iran cooperated with the United States against the Taliban, and months later Republican President George W. Bush still placed Iran in an “axis of evil” with Iraq and North Korea. Historian Golnar Nikpour told Al Jazeera that proclamation shocked both reformists in Tehran and U.S. military leaders who had been working with Iran. Law professor Maryam Jamshidi told Al Jazeera that, until Trump, the heaviest rounds of Iran sanctions came under Democratic presidents Clinton and Obama, and that both parties have treated post-revolutionary Iran as an enemy to constrain. Reading an energy-price story beside that record means a leader’s call to end a war is not, by itself, a forecast that Iranian oil and gas return to market. The concrete check is whether a new Congress records a vote that keeps, tightens, or actually lifts secondary sanctions of the 1996 kind, rather than a sentence about ending the fighting. Source: aljazeera.com

Media-literacy note

When two outlets describe the same event, put their documents side by side and mark which sentence is a measurement, which is a named person’s claim, and which is the writer’s conclusion. A court judgment, a licence, and an election return are checkable in a way a headline adjective is not. If a figure has no comparison — a previous year, a legal threshold, a headcount — treat it as incomplete until you find one.
