Seven naval drones sank Russia's Merkury in Libya, extending Ukraine's war far past the Black Sea.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Ukraine says drones sank a Russian warship in a Libyan port

What happened (neutral): President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukrainian forces used seven domestically made naval drones to sink the Russian corvette Merkury — a small warship — at Benghazi port in Libya in the early hours of Sunday, CNBC reported. He called the Merkury one of Russia's newest ships, commissioned in 2023, during the full-scale war, and posted video of an explosion. CNBC said the strike also damaged the Russian dry cargo ship MV Angara, which Zelenskyy said Moscow used to move weapons, and destroyed a Ka-27 military helicopter on the corvette. Satellite imagery taken Sunday, CNBC reported, showed the Merkury mostly underwater and capsized. The Security Service of Ukraine, the SBU, said the attacks showed that Russia's military fleet and logistics have no safe routes even far beyond the Black Sea. The same CNBC account placed the strike beside a suspected Ukrainian drone hit on a Russian cargo ship in the Baltic Sea on Saturday.

Context & perspectives: The United Nations Support Mission in Libya, UNSMIL, said it was seriously concerned, warned that Libya cannot afford to become a battleground for foreign conflicts, and called for an immediate, independent, and transparent investigation. The Kremlin did not immediately answer CNBC; the Guardian's Europe live blog reported that, asked about the reports, the Kremlin said information would come from Russia's Defence Ministry. On the cargo ship, the outlets do not agree. CNBC reported that Zelenskyy said the MV Angara was damaged, and the New York Times reported that he said drones sank the corvette and hit the dry cargo vessel. The Guardian's live blog reported that Zelenskyy said the Angara was also sunk. The satellite finding CNBC cites is about the Merkury, not the Angara.

This is a contested claim about what the strike was for, and about what should follow.

Zelenskyy called it an exemplary Ukrainian operation in response to Russia's war, and said Ukrainian forces would continue operations in designated waters. The SBU said such operations destroy logistical capacity and force Russia to pay for aggression even at a strategic distance from Ukraine.

UNSMIL's case is about Libya, not about who is winning the sea war: a foreign strike in a Libyan port pulls a country that is not a party into someone else's conflict, so the facts should be established independently and those responsible held to account. Neither Kyiv nor UNSMIL, in these reports, conceded the other's point. Moscow has not yet given its own account.

Major world stories (3)

1) UN climate summit ends with no agreed cover text

What happened (neutral): The UN climate summit known as COP31 — the Conference of the Parties, the annual meeting where governments try to agree a shared climate text — ended in Antalya, Turkey, without a consensus cover decision, CNBC reported. The meeting had opened on November 9. After talks ran almost two days past schedule, the United Kingdom, the European Union, and others rejected a draft that did not mention fossil fuels. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was deeply disappointed that no consensus was reached. France24 reported that the final agreement was rejected on Sunday after record overtime. More than 90 countries did back a separate pledge to raise electricity's share of global final energy use from about 23 percent to about 35 percent by 2035, CNBC reported.

Context & perspectives: Talks had also covered funding for developing nations and how to respond to scientific warnings, including that the 1.5 degrees Celsius temperature target is likely to be overshot. Ireland's climate minister Darragh O'Brien, speaking for the EU, told Reuters the conference was hijacked by some oil states when asked about Saudi Arabia's role; CNBC said it had contacted Saudi Arabia for comment. The EU said the text does not respond to the science and that it will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels. The UK government said the presidencies' text does not meet the moment and moves backward on the science. Philip Davis, prime minister of the Bahamas, said that three years ago in Dubai nations agreed to transition away from fossil fuels, that small island states produce less than 1 percent of global emissions, and that Antalya exhausted their patience. Mohamed Adow, director of Power Shift Africa, called it the worst COP ever and said rich countries blocked a just transition mechanism. Albert Salamanca of the Stockholm Environment Institute Asia told CNBC the lack of consensus was tantamount to a death sentence for vulnerable communities still unable to respond in time. The World Wildlife Fund said the lack of progress on finance for developing nations has real-world consequences for the most vulnerable. Nonsikelelo Nkomo-Mabobe of Solidaridad welcomed food-security language in the proposed Antalya Pact but said a finance gap remains and that funding must be easier for frontline communities to access and traceable once disbursed.

The draft's failure is contested, and the named split is over what the failure was.

The shared ground is narrow and factual: there is no adopted cover decision, and the rejected draft did not mention fossil fuels. From there the accounts diverge.

The EU's reason for voting the text down is that a cover decision which never names fossil fuels would launder the status quo and ignore the science. Davis ties that to a promise already made in Dubai, and to islands that emit very little and live with the damage.

Adow's reason is different. He argues the decisive blockage was not only missing fuel language but rich countries stopping a just transition mechanism, which he describes as abandonment of developing nations. Nkomo-Mabobe, who works on fair supply chains, did not treat the whole text as empty: she said food-security wording was a promising sign, and still said the money is neither easy to reach nor easy to trace.

2) Oxfam says contamination covers nearly all of Gaza

What happened (neutral): A report by the charity Oxfam and the Conflict and Environment Observatory, CEOBS, says Israel's use of explosives and incendiary weapons has caused environmental contamination across more than 99 percent of Gaza, Al Jazeera reported. The enclave has about 2.3 million people. Oxfam international executive director Amitabh Behar said military assaults on the environment are environmental war crimes that threaten life now and for generations, and that Palestinians are exposed through air, food, water, and contact with contaminated soil and debris. Al Jazeera reported that the assault has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians, and that more than 1,500 Palestinians have been killed since a ceasefire was signed in October 2025.

Context & perspectives: The report uses satellite imagery, remote sensing, scientific analysis, and other open-source data. It points to the highest concentrations in Gaza City, Jabalia, and the North Gaza governorate. A study cited in the report estimated direct conflict emissions at more than 1.3 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by January 2025. By October 11, 2025, Oxfam estimates, Israeli ground operations had directly affected about 283 square kilometres of Gaza's 365 square kilometres, or 78 percent. The UN Environment Programme estimates 61 million tonnes of debris since October 7, 2023, about 29 tonnes of rubble per person living in Gaza now. More than 90 percent of Palestinians have been forcibly displaced, most of them more than once. Oxfam estimates the rubble would take 2.1 million dump-truck loads to clear, which it equates to 736 trips around the planet. In September 2025 a UN international commission of inquiry said Israel had committed genocide in Gaza. The International Court of Justice is hearing a genocide case; in January it said it was plausible that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza. Behar said the destruction would breach Israel's legal obligations if it has degraded the conditions needed for human safety. Oxfam said a full independent investigation is needed to identify and reduce the risks. The articles provided here do not include an Israeli government response to this report.

3) NATO meets after Russian missiles fell in Poland

What happened (neutral): German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said on Monday, as the 72nd NATO Parliamentary Assembly continued in Berlin, that the alliance will defend every centimetre of NATO territory, Deutsche Welle reported. NATO is the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the defence alliance of 32 countries. Addressing Russian President Vladimir Putin, Wadephul said the alliance is defensive but prepared to defend its countries. Polish state prosecutors said the three objects that crashed in southeastern Poland on Friday were Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk called the latest violation an intentional provocation. It came during a wide-ranging Russian attack on Ukraine and at the same time as European military drills in Poland.

Context & perspectives: Representatives of NATO members were also due to meet in Brussels on Monday to discuss the Polish airspace violations. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte was expected to address the Berlin assembly around midday local time, then attend a reception with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy was due to speak to the assembly by video link. Wadephul said he was in close contact with Polish Foreign Minister Radoslaw Sikorski and assured Warsaw of full solidarity. The Guardian's Europe live blog reported that Poland is expected to ask allies for further military support, and that the UN Security Council will meet to discuss Russian attacks on civilian targets, including the consulates of Austria and the Netherlands in Kyiv. Deutsche Welle reported that Russian drones and missiles have repeatedly entered Polish airspace during strikes on Ukraine, and that violations have also been reported in Romania, Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia. On Saturday, Finland reported a suspected violation by a manned Russian military helicopter near Loviisa. Moldova, which is in neither the EU nor NATO, also reported a violation this weekend.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) Ghana's employed population has more than doubled since 1992

What happened (neutral): The number of employed people in Ghana has more than doubled over the past 35 years, from six million in 1992 to 13.3 million in 2025, AllAfrica reported, citing the Ghanaian Times on a Ghana Statistical Service report. The GSS is the national statistics office. Most of those workers remain in the informal sector, the part of the economy where jobs are typically outside formal contracts and protections.

Context & perspectives: The report, as described, raises concerns about job security, earnings, and working conditions even as the headcount of employed people has grown. The headline on the Ghanaian Times account says productivity has risen while workers' earnings have lagged. The summary provided here does not give a separate productivity percentage or a wage figure.

2) A lab test finds rule-breaking spreads more easily than good examples

What happened (neutral): In an online experiment led by Lucas Molleman, an assistant professor of developmental psychology at the University of Amsterdam, people who saw someone from their own political camp break a rule outright were nearly twice as likely to do the same, Deutsche Welle reported. Researchers recruited 750 adults in the United States, roughly half Democrats and half Republicans, a week before the 2024 presidential election. Participants watched an animated shooting star cross 100 squares and were told to click where it shone brightest, always near the middle. Squares farther right paid more, so cheating paid. Choosing alone, they picked the farthest-right square — the most blatant violation — about 23 percent of the time. After seeing someone from their own party make that choice, they did so almost 40 percent of the time. Small violations had only a small effect. Just over 4 percent went further than the person they had watched. Seeing someone stick to the rule rarely changed what others did.

Context & perspectives: The task had no real-world stakes, and nobody was punished. Each person chose first, then saw what a previous participant had done and which party that person supported, and could change their answer. The task was repeated six times. Some people followed the rule every time, and others broke it every time, whatever they saw. In computer simulations that placed digital versions of the participants in groups of 100, the handful prone to escalating sometimes pushed the whole group toward heavy rule-breaking. Molleman said a few bad actors like these can take off the guardrails of the group.

The dispute is about what kind of spread this is.

Molleman's reading follows the mechanism in the numbers: a blatant break by someone on your own side moved the extreme cheat from about 23 percent to almost 40 percent, while a good example barely moved anyone, and a small share then went further than the person they copied. On that account, guardrails fail from the bad example, not from the absence of a good one.

Stephen Reicher, a social psychologist at the University of St Andrews who was not involved, told the Guardian that if influence depends on who the other person is, it does not spread the way a disease does. He noted that people who see a riot often turn up afterward to help clean up.

Eugen Dimant, a behavioral economist at the University of Pennsylvania who was not involved, told Science News that the lack of punishment matters, because punishment and social pressure also shape whether rule-breaking spreads, and that is not how the world works. The study itself already shows a limit on simple copying: some participants never moved, no matter what they saw.

Progress watch (1)

1) Liberia concludes a power-and-road deal with a gold miner

What happened (neutral): Liberia's government has concluded a 5.7 billion dollar infrastructure deal with Bea Mountain Mining Corporation, AllAfrica reported, citing the Liberian Investigator. The terms would add 270 megawatts of electricity through four new power plants — more than three times the capacity of the Mount Coffee hydropower plant — and build a 146-kilometre highway.

Context & perspectives: The deal would be paid for largely with extra revenue the gold miner owes as gold prices rise. That is the main complication: the new plants and the road are still to be built, and the money described here depends on gold prices staying high enough to produce that extra revenue.

Understanding the Issue: Why a climate text can die without a vote

People often picture a climate summit as a legislature: a text is drafted, countries vote, and the majority wins. That is the wrong picture of the main political statement.

What follows is this host's own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding copied from one of today's articles.

The overarching statement at these summits is adopted by consensus. In practice, that means the text goes through only if no participating government blocks it. There is no simple show of hands that lets a large majority overrule a holdout.

Because the rule is consensus, the argument is about the words that every government will put its name to, not about which side has more flags in the room. A paragraph one bloc will not accept can sink the whole cover text even when many other governments wanted that paragraph in.

Narrower promises work differently. A group of countries can announce a pledge among themselves without every government signing the summit's single cover text. A news report can therefore be true that "a deal failed" and also true that "dozens of countries backed a separate target." Those are not the same document.

That split is the useful test. When you next read that a climate meeting succeeded or collapsed, check which paper is being described: the consensus cover text, which needs near-universal acceptance, or a coalition pledge, which does not.

Media-literacy note

When two reputable outlets describe the same speech, compare the verb. "Damaged," "hit," and "sunk" are not interchangeable, and only one of them may match what was shown. On a summit, separate the document that needed everyone's consent from the pledge a smaller group announced on its own. Primary statements — a president's post, a UN mission's note, a court order — are worth more than a headline's summary of them. If a report names no response from the government it accuses, say that the response is absent, and do not invent one.
