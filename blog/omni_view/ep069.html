New US tariffs on 60 economies over forced-labor practices could raise prices for British shoppers and slow already-fragile global growth.

Top stories (5)

1) US proposes fresh tariffs on 60 economies over forced labor

What happened (neutral): The US Trade Representative has outlined new duties targeting dozens of countries, including the UK, with proposed rates of 10% or 12.5% depending on each economy’s forced-labor rules. The move follows a Supreme Court decision that struck down many earlier tariffs. Officials say the duties aim to curb trade linked to forced labor.

Perspectives: The strongest case for the tariffs rests on the principle that trade policy should penalize supply chains that rely on forced labor, protecting both workers abroad and domestic industries that already comply with higher standards. Supporters note that the proposal distinguishes between economies with partial or full prohibitions, creating a sliding scale rather than a blanket penalty. Critics argue the duties risk raising costs for consumers and disrupting supply chains at a time when global growth is already under pressure, and they point out that the legal basis has already faced court challenges. Both sides agree the tariffs would affect a wide range of imported goods; they differ on whether the labor-rights goal justifies the economic friction.

Questions to consider:

  • How might these duties affect everyday prices in the UK and elsewhere?
  • What verification methods will determine which countries face the higher rate?
  • How have similar past tariffs altered trade patterns?

2) Ukrainian drones strike St. Petersburg oil terminal

What happened (neutral): Ukrainian drones hit an oil terminal in St. Petersburg, setting part of the facility ablaze hours before an economic forum hosted by President Putin. Ukrainian President Zelenskyy confirmed the strike, which targeted infrastructure in Putin’s birthplace. Russian authorities have not yet issued a detailed public response.

Perspectives: The strongest case for the operation rests on Ukraine’s need to demonstrate that it can reach deep into Russian territory and disrupt energy exports that fund the war effort. Advocates note that striking an oil port ahead of a high-profile forum carries symbolic weight and may complicate Russia’s ability to project economic stability. Opponents highlight the risk of escalation and potential civilian or economic fallout beyond the immediate military target, arguing that such strikes could harden Russian public resolve rather than weaken it. Both sides agree the attack occurred near a major Russian city hosting an international event; they differ on whether the military benefit outweighs the danger of broader retaliation.

Questions to consider:

  • What does the choice of target reveal about Ukraine’s current strategy?
  • How might Russia respond in the coming days?
  • How are energy markets reacting to the strike?

3) OECD warns of global slowdown tied to US-Iran conflict

What happened (neutral): The OECD lowered its global growth forecast, citing damage from the ongoing US-Iran conflict as a key factor. The organization said the fighting is already producing lasting effects on trade, energy prices, and investor confidence. Economists expect the drag to continue into the second half of the year.

Perspectives: The strongest case for viewing the conflict as the dominant drag rests on its direct impact on energy routes and investor sentiment across multiple regions simultaneously. The OECD’s revision reflects measurable declines in projected output rather than speculation. Others emphasize that domestic policy choices in major economies, including tariff plans and fiscal measures, are compounding the external shock and may prove more decisive over time. Both sides agree the conflict has introduced new uncertainty; they differ on the relative weight of geopolitical versus policy-driven factors.

The US responded with strikes on an Iranian military site on Qeshm Island. Negotiations between Washington and Tehran appear to have stalled following the exchange.

Perspectives: The strongest case for treating the airport strike as a major escalation rests on the involvement of a third-country civilian facility and the direct risk to international travel and commerce. Kuwaiti officials have stressed that interceptions occurred but damage was still sustained. Others note that both sides have framed their actions as defensive, with the US citing protection of its forces and interests. Both sides agree the ceasefire is under renewed strain; they differ on which party bears primary responsibility for the latest round.

5) UK workless households reach highest share in two years

What happened (neutral): Office for National Statistics data show 14.4% of UK households had no one in employment in the first quarter of the year. The figure is the highest since 2017 and marks a two-year peak. The rise coincides with broader economic pressures including tax changes and inflation.

Perspectives: The strongest case linking the rise to recent policy rests on the timing of tax increases and their reported effects on hiring and hours worked, particularly in retail and hospitality. The ONS numbers provide a clear household-level measure rather than an individual unemployment rate. Others point to longer-term structural shifts, such as an aging population and changing participation patterns, that predate the latest fiscal measures. Both sides agree the household employment rate has deteriorated; they differ on how much of the change is cyclical versus policy-driven.

Questions to consider:

Understanding the Issue: How the US proposes and adjusts tariffs

Most coverage of new tariffs treats them as sudden presidential decisions, yet the process involves a structured review by the Office of the US Trade Representative under statutes such as Section 301. The USTR first identifies practices it deems unfair, invites public comment, holds hearings, and then recommends duty rates that can later be modified or challenged in court. This administrative path explains why the current proposal distinguishes between economies with full versus partial forced-labor bans and why earlier tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court. Knowing the sequence shows that proposed rates are not final policy but starting points subject to negotiation, litigation, and congressional oversight. Next time you see a headline announcing “new tariffs,” ask: which statute authorizes them, what comment period has occurred, and which countries have already signaled they will challenge the duties at the WTO or in US courts.
