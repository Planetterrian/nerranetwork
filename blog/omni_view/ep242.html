Up to half a percent of GDP is what Britain's Treasury ties to today's EU reset.

By Patrick November 20, 2026 Ep 242 14 min read Listen to podcast Watch on YouTube

Today's lead story (1)

1) Britain and the EU tie a post-Brexit reset to a later fight over tuition fees

What happened (neutral): British Prime Minister Andy Burnham is in Brussels to announce a deal resetting Britain's relationship with the European Union, the bloc it left a decade ago, The New York Times reports. He is meeting European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa. France24 reports the two sides are hoping to strike several deals meant to open closer cooperation. The Guardian reports that European Union sources say today's reset will not take effect without a further agreement on tuition fees for European Union citizens, a decision senior diplomats had already put off for six months. During a meeting with businesses, Burnham said the sides were "pretty much there, and it's a really good deal," and that Treasury analysis out today puts the gain at up to 0.5 percent of British gross domestic product, which he called the single biggest growth intervention in this Parliament.

Context & perspectives: The tuition-fee condition is the lever. European Union sources told The Guardian that deferring the fee level was meant to give London leverage in parallel talks on "Made in Europe" legislation, rules designed to protect European supply chains against China. Making the food-and-drink deal contingent on fees is described by those sources as a way to neutralise that leverage. France, The Guardian reports, insisted last night that Britain could not have voting rights on food-and-drink legislation. Al Jazeera reports London is signalling a willingness to align more closely with the European Union on various policies.

The dispute turns on whether a reset announced today is a finished bargain or a bargain the European Union can still reopen. Burnham's case is that the package is essentially done and is the largest growth step this Parliament, on the Treasury's own figure. The European Union sources' case is that warmth toward a former member is compatible with holding the food-and-drink terms until fees are settled, so London cannot spend the next six months trading access for supply-chain concessions. France's limit on voting rights is the part of that case already stated in public: closer rules, without a seat on the legislation.

Questions to consider: Will the text published from Brussels state that the food-and-drink terms wait on a tuition-fee agreement?

Major world stories (3)

1) Russian strikes hit Kyiv's bridges; Poland says a missile struck its southeast

What happened (neutral): Russia hit three bridges over the Dnipro River in Kyiv and killed eight people in one of the largest air attacks of the war, the BBC reports, days after Ukraine damaged the Crimean Bridge. Ballistic missiles struck the Podilsko-Voskresensky, Darnytskyi and Southern bridges, which were closed to traffic. Ukraine's air force told the BBC that Russia launched almost 100 missiles and about 400 drones. Prime Minister Serhiy Koretsky said 28 people were injured. Al Jazeera, citing Prime Minister Sergii Koretskyi, reported the same toll: eight killed, including six in Kyiv, and 28 wounded. The BBC reported two of the dead at the Sknyliv railway station in Lviv. Deutsche Welle reported an earlier city count from Mayor Vitali Klitschko of at least three killed and 13 injured in the capital. France24 reports Poland said no one was hurt after a Russian missile hit southeast Poland during the attack on Ukraine. The BBC reported a projectile landed about 45 kilometres, or 28 miles, from the border. Deutsche Welle reported Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski saying investigators were at three sites — Rudnik nad Sanem, Molodycz and Nowy Lipowiec — and Prime Minister Donald Tusk saying there was no danger from the air.

Context & perspectives: President Vladimir Putin had said retaliation for the Crimean Bridge strike would be "far more painful." Russia's Defence Ministry told Al Jazeera the Kyiv road and rail bridges were targeted because they carry weapons and military equipment, and that energy and transport sites were also hit. First Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov said nuclear plants cut operations, causing outages in Kyiv and 10 regions. Klitschko asked people not to drive into the city unless necessary and asked employers to allow remote work where they can. The BBC said the barrage began hours after European leaders, including Burnham, President Emmanuel Macron and Chancellor Friedrich Merz, said they were ready for negotiations with Moscow. Al Jazeera reported Deputy Foreign Minister Sergey Ryabkov later saying a visit by three European Union representatives to Russia is "under consideration," while any Ukrainian membership of NATO remains unacceptable to Moscow.

2) Baghdad strikes a deal with Kataib Hezbollah over an alleged oil-smuggling ring

What happened (neutral): Iraq has reached a compromise with the Iran-backed militia Kataib Hezbollah after Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi readied security forces to move against the group, France24 reports, citing three senior security officials. Officials had accused the group of running a smuggling ring that siphoned off hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of state-owned oil. Under a deal brokered by senior Shi'ite political leaders to avert a confrontation, government forces were expected to move into the group's stronghold of Jurf al-Sakhar on Friday.

Context & perspectives: The sequence in the France24 account is a prepared security move, then a political bargain to stop that move becoming a clash, then an expected entry into the stronghold the next day. The article does not quote Kataib Hezbollah's response.

3) Bukele registers for another term after El Salvador drops presidential term limits

What happened (neutral): President Nayib Bukele registered his candidacy on Thursday at the Supreme Electoral Tribunal, Al Jazeera reports, after his party Nuevas Ideas used its supermajority in Congress to remove presidential term limits and extend terms from five years to six. Last year the same majority abolished second-round votes and brought national elections forward by two years, to February 2027. The 45-year-old is widely expected to win on the political support from his crackdown on violent cartels. Last month he sent about 2,500 soldiers to Haiti after four Salvadoran members of a United Nations-backed force went missing; Al Jazeera says Port-au-Prince welcomed that deployment.

Context & perspectives: What the constitution now allows, and what that does to competition, is the split. Bukele told the crowd, "In El Salvador there is total, complete open democracy," and "We're sending a message to the world that El Salvador will never return to the past." His stated reason is the break with the cartel violence of that past, and the article says the crackdown is why he is widely expected to win. Juan Pappier of Human Rights Watch, quoted by The Associated Press in Al Jazeera's account, said Bukele is seeking a third consecutive term "after packing the court, rewriting the constitution to his liking and jailing or forcing into exile those who dare criticise him," and that "the absolute concentration of power in his hands has been the defining feature of his administration." The Bertelsmann Transformation Index is cited as accusing him of a de facto one-party state. Al Jazeera also reports that many analysts question the Haiti deployment, saying he is using that crisis to enhance his reputation — a limit attached to the export of his security model, not a concession from Bukele himself.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) COP31 draft drops fossil fuels, with a Friday deadline still open

What happened (neutral): Australia, co-host of the COP31 United Nations climate talks in Turkey, unveiled a draft agreement on Thursday that drops any mention of fossil fuels, France24 reports. Nearly 200 countries have until Friday to strike a final text, though these talks often run long. Deutsche Welle reports the meetings have been in Antalya since November 9, that an earlier draft this week included a fossil-fuel phaseout, and that Thursday night's text omits gas, oil and coal. The summit is taking place during a record-breaking El Niño, climate-linked disasters from Europe to Nepal, and an energy shock from the Iran war, France24 reports.

Context & perspectives: Three years ago at COP28 in Dubai, nations pledged to move away from fossil fuels with no target or timeline, Deutsche Welle reports. Last year's COP30 in Brazil ended with no mention of fossil fuels. Oil- and gas-producing countries have strongly resisted putting that issue back on the table. France's ecological transition minister, Monique Barbut, called the omission a "black mark." Harjeet Singh, convenor of the Fill the Fund campaign, said, "You cannot solve a fossil fuel crisis by pretending we don't need to phase out fossil fuels." Mohamed Adow, director of Power Shift Africa, said the slimmed text had "gone backwards." Australia's climate minister, Chris Bowen, who is leading the negotiations, said, "We will be consulting and negotiating until it is very clear to us that we have a consensus."

Campaigners and Barbut treat naming coal, oil and gas as the only honest response to a crisis those fuels drive. The producing countries' recorded position is resistance to reopening a phaseout that Dubai left without a date. Bowen's stated rule is that the text moves only when the nearly 200 parties can live with it — which is how a line that was in an earlier draft can leave the later one.

Questions to consider: Does Friday's text, or an overtime text, put fossil fuels back in?

2) Lufthansa tells staff it will cut 1,000 jobs at Frankfurt and Munich

What happened (neutral): Lufthansa plans to cut 1,000 jobs at its Frankfurt and Munich hubs, Deutsche Welle reports, citing a company letter seen by the German news agency DPA and other outlets. The letter was reportedly signed by chief executive Jens Ritter and four other board members. In the first nine months of the year the airline recorded an operating loss of 180 million euros, about 197 million dollars. The board said the loss averages 20 million euros a month and that this is the third year in a row of not being financially successful. The letter said Lufthansa "is currently not competitive in terms of its cost structure, efficiency and productivity." At the end of 2025 the group employed about 67,100 people, up 1 percent from the year before.

Context & perspectives: The same letter sets other cuts: employee travel benefits, fewer crew overnight stays abroad, and less short- and medium-haul flying by replacing some A320 aircraft with A220 jets. It also plans to fit ten aircraft a month with Starlink satellite internet and to charge for onboard access. Deutsche Welle reports the timing of the job cuts is unclear, and that it is unknown whether they are part of a September 2025 plan to slash 4,000 administrative posts through artificial intelligence and digitalisation. Rising fuel costs from the US-Israeli war with Iran are cited as a further strain. The article does not name a union response.

Progress watch (1)

1) Hong Kong's public-housing wait falls to a low not seen in more than a decade

What happened (neutral): Hong Kong's waiting time for standard and light public housing fell to 4.4 years in the 12 months to the end of September, from 4.8 years at the end of the previous quarter, the South China Morning Post reports — the shortest composite wait in more than 10 years. The Housing Bureau said on Friday that the composite wait, covering both types of public housing, declined across urban areas, extended urban areas and the New Territories. The wait is still measured in years, not months.

Understanding the Issue: Why a growing clean-power share can still leave fossil fuels in charge of energy

People often hear "renewables are booming" and picture coal, oil and gas already shoved to the edge of the energy system. Deutsche Welle's look at whether the Paris temperature limit can still be met shows a split between two different measurements. In 2015, governments agreed in Paris to do what they could to stop the world overheating. Since then, coal, oil and gas have fallen to a record-low share of global primary energy — all the energy supplied, not just what comes out of a socket — and they still account for around 86 percent of that supply. Mark Maslin, professor of Earth System Science at University College London, told Deutsche Welle, "We're still burning stuff like [it's] the 19th century." Electricity is the faster-moving slice: wind, solar and hydropower accounted for around 34 percent of global electricity production in 2025, and an Ember analysis found that record solar growth last year meant clean energy met all new global electricity demand, so new fossil generation was held back. Maslin said about 2 trillion dollars is going into new renewable projects, twice the investment in fossil fuels. The United Nations now says overshooting the Paris temperature threshold is inevitable, and that the time spent above it should be kept as short as possible, with heating brought back down by the end of the century, because a longer overshoot raises the risk of self-reinforcing changes such as large-scale forest die-back and melting permafrost. A summit sentence about "phasing out" or not naming fuels at all is a political line; the checkable companion is whether a cited figure is primary energy, still mostly fossil, or electricity, where the clean share is larger and rising faster. Source: dw.com

Media-literacy note

When two official counts of the same night differ, write down who gave which number before treating either as the full toll. A draft text and a signed deal are not the same object: look for what was removed between drafts, and for what a government says must still be agreed before today's announcement takes effect. Primary documents — a court registration, a housing-bureau table, a company letter quoted in full — beat a headline that collapses a condition into a finished result.

Go deeper: The New York Times reports Burnham is in Brussels to announce a reset deal; The Guardian adds that European Union sources say today's deals will not take effect without a later tuition-fee agreement, and it is the Guardian that carries the Treasury figure of up to 0.5 percent of GDP.
