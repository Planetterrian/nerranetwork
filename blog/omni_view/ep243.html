Denmark gives Russia's defence attaché seven days to leave after an alleged kill plot.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Denmark expels a Russian defence official over an alleged murder plot

What happened (neutral): Denmark’s security and intelligence service, PET, says Russian intelligence planned and started an attempt to kill a Ukrainian-American near Copenhagen, the BBC reports. Danish media name him as the rapper Kyivstoner; his real name is Albert Vasiliev. PET says it stopped people who meant to kill him at a September concert. Three people were stopped in a car near the venue on September 25, and firearms and knives were in the vehicle. Two people have been charged with attempted murder and with assisting a foreign intelligence service; both pleaded not guilty. PET says the people were tied to Loyal to Familia, a banned Danish gang it describes as known for serious violent crime. Foreign minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen said Russia’s defence attaché — the officer who represents a military at an embassy — must leave within seven days, and he called the alleged attempt intimidation meant to make Denmark waver in its support for Ukraine. He also wants the European Union, the bloc of European states, to limit Russian travel: a Schengen visa, the short-stay visa that usually lets a visitor move across much of Europe, should count only in the country that issued it. Russia’s embassy called the allegations unsubstantiated and said Copenhagen gave no evidence of Moscow’s involvement. Russian state media, citing the foreign ministry, say Moscow will answer the expulsion. On Saturday, European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen — the head of the European Union’s executive — said she condemned an assassination attempt against a US-Ukrainian citizen in Denmark, orchestrated by Russian intelligence, and called it an attack on European Union soil.

Context & perspectives: In July, Russia’s FSB security service accused Vasiliev and the Ukrainian authorities of organizing a terrorist attack on military facilities and personnel in Russia; he denied that on social media. France 24 reports that Danish authorities have also announced tighter restrictions on visas for Russian tourists, beside the expulsion.

Contested: Rasmussen’s case is that the plot was pressure on a government backing Ukraine, not a private quarrel: armed men were stopped near a concert, linked by PET to a banned gang, after Russia’s own security service had already accused the performer. Von der Leyen treats that as an attack on European Union soil and says Russia’s hybrid threats — mixes of covert and open pressure — are becoming less hybrid and more real. The embassy’s case is that Denmark has not shown Moscow’s hand. It says a “Danish model” has appeared, in which a “Russian trace” is found and used for a propaganda spectacle, to frighten Danes and justify a long confrontation. The two charged people have pleaded not guilty; the embassy does not accept the intelligence claim, and Copenhagen has not, in the embassy’s telling, produced the evidence it asked for.

Questions to consider: Will European Union governments take up Denmark’s request that a Russian short-stay visa work only in the country that issued it?

Major world stories (3)

1) Iran says it shot down a US drone as Hormuz talks stay stuck

What happened (neutral): Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, a branch of its armed forces, said on Saturday that a new air-defence system shot down a US MQ-9 drone — an uncrewed US military aircraft — over the Strait of Hormuz, Al Jazeera reports, citing Iranian state broadcaster IRIB. The Guard said it was the 58th US MQ-9 brought down by Iranian forces. The strait is the narrow sea lane through which, in peacetime, one-fifth of the world’s oil and gas passed. The war, launched by the United States and Israel on February 28, has killed thousands, disrupted shipping, and pushed energy prices higher. A June memorandum of understanding was never fully carried out because the two sides disagreed on freedom and security of navigation; attacks have resumed from time to time since July. In August, The Washington Post reported that the United States had lost 45 MQ-9 drones, about 25 percent of that fleet, at a cost of more than $1.3 billion, during combat operations in Iran. On Friday the US Treasury said it had sanctioned four Iranians it called “oil trustees,” people it says collected money from illicit oil sales to evade sanctions and a naval blockade. Among them are Mohammad Javad Bavand, a former Guard intelligence officer, and Majid Taj, brother of Iranian Football Federation president Mehdi Taj.

Context & perspectives: Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi said on Friday that seven conditions Iran sent the United States in September “must be implemented for the Strait of Hormuz to reopen,” including an end to the war and the lifting of sanctions and the naval blockade on Iran’s southern ports. He said Iran would negotiate how to carry those conditions out, that the timetable was “flexible,” and that talks depended on American confidence-building steps. He ruled out sending Iran’s stockpile of 60 percent enriched uranium abroad, but said a “fair solution” could be reached and that some mediator proposals were “worth examining.” He called the current “no war, no peace” state undesirable, and said Iran could not accept a deal that would “hand victory to the other side.” At the White House the same day, President Donald Trump said Tehran wants a deal, but not one acceptable to him: “We want to make a real deal, not a fake deal that they’ve been doing for 51 years.” He had said last week that a deal to end the war was unnecessary. Vice President JD Vance, who had blamed the war for Republicans losing both chambers of Congress this month, declined to say whether he would have authorized the February strikes to stop Iran developing a nuclear weapon. He said that decision was Trump’s, and that his job was to support the president.

Contested: The split is what must happen before ships move freely again. Araghchi’s case is that reopening Hormuz is the last step, not the first: end the war, lift sanctions and the blockade, then talk about the timetable, with room for mediators on the uranium stockpile but no transfer of that 60 percent material abroad, and no text that declares the other side the winner. Trump’s case is that Iran’s offer is the old pattern under a new name — a deal he will not sign — and that Washington does not need an agreement to end the war. Vance did not claim the February strikes as his own call.

2) Paris court jails three officers for the 2020 beating of Michel Zecler

What happened (neutral): A Paris criminal court has convicted three police officers of intentional, racist violence in the 2020 assault of Michel Zecler, a Black music producer, and of falsifying a police report, Al Jazeera reports. Aurélien Lapersonne, 29, Philippe Tondeux, 50, and Pierre Plantier, 37, were each sentenced to five years in prison, part of it suspended, and barred from the police. Zecler, 47, co-founded the independent rap label Black Gold Corp. Officers stopped him outside his Paris studio on November 21, 2020, accusing him of not wearing a face mask required under COVID-19 rules, then pushed him inside. Over about five minutes they punched, kicked, and used batons for some 30 blows. He suffered head trauma and a complete rupture of a biceps tendon. The presiding judge said the officers had been dishonest in their report to shield themselves from discipline and the law, with no regard for the criminal consequences Zecler faced. Lapersonne, who filed the report, had claimed Zecler struck out with his arms and legs; asked if he had made that up, he told the court, “Absolutely.” He said he left the officers’ blows out of the report out of fear. All three admitted excessive force and apologized. They denied racism. The judges said Zecler’s account — that he heard the slur “sale negre” — had been consistent, and that the false report damaged the officers’ credibility. The New York Times reports that security cameras filmed the beating and that it set off a national backlash.

Context & perspectives: Music journalist Olivier Cachin told the French paper Sud Ouest that Zecler works in the background and is not a “look-at-me” type. Tondeux said he regretted “the image I have given of the police.”

3) Three Salvadoran soldiers die in Haiti as a prison deal is announced

What happened (neutral): Three Salvadoran soldiers were killed in Haiti while handling explosives, El Salvador’s military said on Friday, Deutsche Welle reports. It is the first officially reported deaths since a 2,000-member Salvadoran contingent was sent last month to the gang-hit Caribbean country. One soldier died while being treated in the neighboring Dominican Republic, where he had been airlifted. Another was wounded and is out of danger. The military did not say where the blast happened or what explosives the soldiers were handling. President Nayib Bukele sent the troops to rescue four Salvadoran airmen — members of the United Nations-backed Gang Suppression Force — who went missing after a gang ambush on October 18 in Port-au-Prince. Those extra troops are not part of that force, and the job has widened toward Haitian gangs. On Friday, Haiti announced an agreement with El Salvador to build three new prisons, after security and justice minister Patrick Pelissier visited El Salvador. A Salvadoran company will build them with a Haitian firm, he said. Haitian prisons are notoriously overcrowded; in 2024, gang raids on two of the biggest freed thousands of inmates.

Context & perspectives: Pelissier visited El Salvador’s maximum-security Terrorism Confinement Center, known as CECOT, and praised it: “Once you enter, there is no hope of ever seeing the outside world again.” El Salvador has imprisoned more than 90,000 people, with no family visits, since a state of exception began in 2022. The military said it extended condolences to the three families.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) Geneva agrees to “elaborate” killer-robot rules by 2028, not to negotiate a ban

What happened (neutral): The 128 countries at a United Nations conference in Geneva have agreed to “elaborate” a possible accord on lethal autonomous weapons — machines that can kill without a human making the final decision to fire — over the next two years, Al Jazeera reports. The Saturday decision came under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, a treaty on weapons considered especially harmful. Nearly 100 nations had pushed for swift, legally binding rules. The United States, Russia, and Israel opposed that push. The convention decides only if every member agrees. Diplomats said Russia’s objections on procedure led to some formal sessions being called off, and talks on the final wording ran through the night. Swiss chair Julien Thoeni said some countries rejected the word “negotiate,” so the text says “elaboration” instead. Reuters headlines the same decision as talks aimed at regulating these weapons by 2028.

Context & perspectives: Thoeni said more than ten years of talks had produced a significant step for humanitarian disarmament, and that the process should decide which weapons international humanitarian law — the rules for protecting civilians in war — can ban, and which it can permit but regulate. Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of 300 organizations, called the mandate “watered down” and said it offered no clear route to binding rules. Executive director Nicole van Rooijen told Agence France-Presse that autonomous weapons are already in combat, including systems that use artificial intelligence to choose targets, and that delaying a binding instrument puts civilians at risk. Verity Coyle, deputy director for arms at Human Rights Watch, told the agency the outcome was disappointing and did not provide the urgent action needed, “but the technology is racing ahead.”

Contested: Everyone in the room is arguing about weapons that can pick a target and fire without a person making that last call, under a treaty that cannot move unless every member says yes. Van Rooijen’s case for a binding ban now is that the machines are already being used, so a slower paper exercise leaves civilians exposed while software improves. Coyle’s addition is urgency: a mandate that does not force new rules loses the race to the technology. Thoeni’s case for the wording that passed is that “elaborate” was the verb objectors would sign, after “negotiate” was rejected, and that a unanimous step after a decade still lets states sort bans from regulated permissions. The United States, Russia, and Israel opposed the faster, binding route; Russia’s procedural objections were enough to cancel some formal sessions because one member can stop the convention.

Questions to consider: When the elaboration is written, does it name any weapon that must be banned, or only a process for talking further before 2028?

2) An Anthropic mathematician says Claude helped prove a 167-year-old prime-number claim

What happened (neutral): Levent Alpoge, a mathematician on the technical staff of Anthropic, an artificial-intelligence company, says he used the company’s Claude tool to produce a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, Deutsche Welle reports. He posted the claim on his website on Friday: “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.” The hypothesis, stated by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, is about how prime numbers — numbers divisible only by themselves and 1 — are spaced as they get larger. It is one of seven Millennium Prize Problems chosen by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers $1 million for a solution to each. Alpoge said the work with the system generated more than 250,000 pages, and that he had not yet mastered all the mathematics involved. He wrote that Claude “far outpaced me near the finish line.” The proof still has to be checked by other mathematicians. Martin Bridson, president of the Clay institute, called the announcement one of historic significance in a statement quoted by the Associated Press, and said it holds the promise of a dramatic change in what people think is possible in mathematics. He added: “But the journey from today’s announcement to a place of real understanding will be long and difficult.”

Context & perspectives: In September, OpenAI said its technology had helped with a problem on the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe how fluids such as water and air move — another of the seven prize problems. That claim also still needs independent checks. Confirming Riemann’s pattern would matter for a wide range of further mathematical work, Deutsche Welle reports; until other mathematicians sign off, it remains a claim, not a settled theorem.

Progress watch (1)

1) A US judge approves Bayer’s Roundup payouts, up to $7.25 billion

What happened (neutral): A judge in Missouri has approved a settlement under which Germany’s Bayer will pay up to $7.25 billion, about €6.6 billion, over the next two decades to resolve US lawsuits from people who say they should have been warned that the weedkiller Roundup could cause non-Hodgkin lymphoma, Deutsche Welle reports. The deal applies nationwide. People already ill, and people who develop the cancer later, could receive an average of between $10,000 and $165,000, depending on how they used the product, their age at diagnosis, and how severe the illness is. Deutsche Welle reports that some lawyers say the payments are too low. The decision could be appealed.

Context & perspectives: The approval is a completed court result, not a promise of a future fund: money is now allocated against thousands of claims, with the appeal risk still open and with critics of the sums already on the record.

Understanding the Issue: Why one country’s visa is not a private key to the whole continent

A common picture of European travel is that a short-stay visa is a national courtesy, so the country that stamps it can later shrink it to its own borders by itself. What follows is this host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from today’s articles. The Schengen system is a shared external door: most European Union countries, plus a few neighbors, dropped routine checks on the borders between them and kept checks on the outside edge. A short-stay visa is usually issued by one state but written for the whole area, because the point of abolishing internal controls was that a visitor cleared once should not be stopped at every next frontier. The issuing country does the paperwork and is supposed to be the main destination, yet the document is not that country’s private pass. Any state on the external border can still refuse entry if the traveler fails the common conditions — purpose, funds, return, security flags — even with a visa in the passport. That is a refusal at the door, not a rewrite of the visa’s map. Changing the map, so that a visa issued in one capital works only there, is a change to a shared instrument. It needs the states that issue the common visa to agree, because a Dane who cannot issue a Europe-wide visa also cannot, alone, un-issue the Europe-wide visas other capitals have already printed. Reading a demand to “limit Russian travel” against that mechanism means asking which lever is actually being pulled: tighter national practice at one window, refusals at the border, or a rule change that rewrites where the visa is valid. The check, next time a government says it will confine a Schengen visa to one country, is whether the text is a national instruction to its own consulates or a proposal the other issuing states still have to adopt.

Media-literacy note

When two governments contradict each other on a plot, write down the charge, the denial, and the piece of evidence each one actually offers — a seized weapon, a named agency, a missing document — before you adopt either story. A court verdict and a diplomatic accusation are not the same kind of fact: one has a judgment you can read, the other is a claim until charges are tested. For a scientific announcement, the useful next document is the proof other specialists can check, not the press line that a famous problem has fallen. If you read only one outlet on a contested expulsion or a stalled negotiation, you are reading one capital’s file.

Go deeper: The BBC reports the firearms and knives in the car, the banned-gang link, and Rasmussen’s request that Russian short-stay visas count only in the issuing country; France 24 adds von der Leyen’s line that this was an attack on European Union soil, and reports that tighter limits on visas for Russian tourists have been announced, which is a step beyond a request still sitting with the bloc.

To really understand a demand that one capital confine a visitor’s papers to its own borders, you have to see that a European short-stay visa is a shared door, not a private key. What follows is this host’s own explanation of that mechanism, not a finding from today’s articles. Most European Union countries, plus a few neighbors, dropped routine checks on the borders between them and kept checks on the outside edge. A short-stay visa is usually issued by one state but written for the whole area, because the point of abolishing internal controls was that a visitor cleared once should not be stopped at every next frontier. The issuing country does the paperwork and is supposed to be the main destination, yet the document is not that country’s private pass. Any state on the external border can still refuse entry if the traveler fails the common conditions, purpose, funds, a return ticket, security flags, even with a visa in the passport. That is a refusal at the door, not a rewrite of the visa’s map. Changing the map, so that a visa issued in one capital works only there, is a change to a shared instrument. It needs the states that issue the common visa to agree, because a government that cannot print a Europe-wide visa by itself also cannot, alone, un-print the Europe-wide visas other capitals have already issued. Next time a government says it will confine that visa to one country, check whether the text is an instruction to its own consulates or a proposal the other issuing states still have to adopt.
