Two-hour daily power cuts now reach ninety-two million people across Iran.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Iran rations power as winter gas runs short

What happened (neutral): Iran has begun rolling electricity cuts of about two hours a day, Al Jazeera reports, across a grid that supplies 92 million people. Mohammad Allahdad, head of the national electricity company Tavanir, told state television that distribution limits would start Sunday because of the fuel supply. President Masoud Pezeshkian has asked people to use less power; in a televised interview with state broadcaster IRIB, the Islamic Republic of Iran Broadcasting, the office lights were off and daylight was let in instead. The country holds part of the world’s largest natural gas field and the world’s third-largest proven oil reserves, yet a United States naval blockade of Iranian ports, sanctions, and strikes on energy sites have left the system short. Government spokeswoman Fatemeh Mohajerani warned that the gas gap could reach 430 million cubic metres a day this winter, against about 300 million in earlier years. Saeed Tavakoli, managing director of the National Iranian Gas Company, said four processing plants damaged early in the fighting had cut capacity by 230 million cubic metres a day, of which about 100 million had been restored. The company warned that 11 of Iran’s 31 provinces could struggle, especially Mazandaran, Golestan, and North Khorasan. Pezeshkian told the ISNA news agency that attacks on gas infrastructure had made a hard winter more likely, and told investors on November 21 that Iran faced a “silent economic war” tougher than a war with missiles, the state agency IRNA reported. An Al Jazeera reading of the grid puts about 86 percent of electricity on natural gas, about 7 percent on oil-fired plants, about 5 percent on hydropower, and about 2 percent on nuclear power, mainly the Bushehr reactor. Energy Minister Abbas Aliabadi said in August that war damage had taken 4,200 megawatts offline and that repairs were under way. Shortages and cuts were already happening before the United States and Israel attacked Iran on February 28.

Context & perspectives: Tehran has refused Washington’s terms for ending the war, which Al Jazeera describes as stuck. Some gas plants can switch to diesel or fuel oil when gas is tight, which is why a gas shortfall shows up as timed power cuts rather than only as cold homes.

Major world stories (3)

2) Israeli rivals split on a new strike before a government exists

What happened (neutral): Interim Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot are still short of a governing majority, Al Jazeera reports, with a first deadline of December 6 to form a government. Eisenkot’s Yashar bloc has 48 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, Israel’s parliament, 13 short of a majority. Arab parties hold 19 seats and could back him from outside the cabinet. The ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism alliance, often called UTJ, has not endorsed either man and could join either side. Likud, Netanyahu’s party, has 46 recommendations from lawmakers after that refusal. Eisenkot has ruled out serving under Netanyahu and wants a unity cabinet in which leadership is shared. On Tuesday, Defence Minister Israel Katz called for preemptive strikes before Iran could finish a chemical-weapons programme he says is already under way. In mid-November, U.S. media reported that Netanyahu sent Mossad chief Roman Gofman to Washington to urge joint strikes, citing intelligence that Tehran was working on chemical weapons. Shin Bet head David Zini, National Security Council chair Shmuel Ben Ezra, and ambassador to the United States Yechiel Leiter are also reported to back new strikes. On November 19, President Donald Trump told Fox News there was “indication that Iran is looking at gases, looking at bio,” and did not commit to military action before the end of the year.

Context & perspectives: Netanyahu says Israel needs a government free of what he calls the “taint” of Arab-party participation so it can answer an imminent Iranian threat. Eisenkot has told his party he has seen no imminent threat that requires action before a new government is sworn in. Avigdor Liberman, newly seated on the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Defence Committee and therefore able to see classified intelligence, said he had seen no justification for new Israeli strikes. Military chief Lieutenant General Eyal Zamir and researchers inside Mossad, the foreign intelligence service, also reportedly see no case for an immediate attack, while defence officials have warned Netanyahu that new strikes could widen into a regional war. Gofman has reportedly argued for renewed action.

This disagreement turns on whether the intelligence shows a threat that cannot wait for a cabinet. Katz’s case is that a chemical-weapons programme is already moving, so waiting is the risk, and that a government tied to Arab parties would be too constrained to act. Eisenkot and Liberman’s case is that people with access to the same secret picture do not see that urgency; Liberman said that if a strike came now, “we’ll know that it’s completely motivated by politics and not security concerns.” Their limit is numerical as well as evidentiary: Eisenkot is still 13 seats short, and UTJ can still tip the math toward Likud. Katz’s limit is that the military chief and Mossad researchers are reported not to share his timetable.

Questions to consider: Will a government be in place by December 6, or will a strike be ordered before that date?

3) Solomon Islands and Australia sign a security treaty that bars foreign bases

What happened (neutral): The Solomon Islands has signed a security treaty with Australia that checks China’s influence in the South Pacific, the Associated Press reports. The New York Times adds that a 2022 Solomon Islands agreement with China had raised concern it could let Beijing place a military presence on the islands, and that the new accord precludes such bases.

Context & perspectives: The new text is aimed at one specific fear from that 2022 pact: a Chinese base. The articles do not quote a Chinese response, and they do not say the 2022 agreement itself has been torn up.

4) Houthis claim hits on a Saudi refinery; Riyadh reports drones down and 11 hurt

What happened (neutral): Iran-backed Houthi forces in Yemen said on Wednesday they had again struck Saudi Arabia, Deutsche Welle reports. Military spokesman Yahya Saree said “accurate and direct” attacks on Ras Tanura — the kingdom’s largest oil refinery and a major crude export terminal — had set fires there, and that an attack on Riyadh’s financial district was “carried out in three phases, with strikes hitting their targets accurately.” Saudi defence officials said air defences shot down four Houthi drones at Ras Tanura, and that 11 people were injured when a tower in the financial district was hit. The same building was struck on November 19, with no injuries then.

Context & perspectives: The two accounts do not describe the same result. Saree says the refinery attacks caused fires and that the Riyadh strikes landed as planned, framing them as retaliation in a civil war that has run for more than a decade and has widened with the Iran fighting and tension in the Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf’s oil chokepoint. Saudi defence officials say four drones never reached Ras Tanura, and they are the source for the injury count at the tower. Deutsche Welle does not cite an independent check that fires broke out at the refinery. Saree’s strongest reason for treating the operation as a success is his claim of accurate, phased hits on a terminal that matters to oil exports. The Saudi officials’ strongest reason for treating it as a failed raid plus a costly hit is the interception count, plus the fact that the same tower was attacked six days earlier without injuries. Each version leaves a gap the other side would point to: a claimed fire without an outside confirmation, and an interception claim that still sits beside 11 wounded people.

Economy, science & technology (2)

5) A climate disaster fund has far more requests than cash

What happened (neutral): The Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage, built to help countries recover from climate disasters and slow changes such as rising seas, is already short, Deutsche Welle reports. A first call drew 2.8 billion dollars in urgent requests, with 342 million dollars available. By this summer only about half of the money pledged had arrived. The European Commission signed a 25 million euro contribution agreement on the sidelines of COP31, the latest U.N. climate conference, writing down a pledge first made at COP28. Spain pledged a further 15 million euros. Other loss-and-damage gifts were scarce. The board has provisionally set aside 21.6 million dollars for Nepal after an August glacier collapse that killed more than 1,500 people, with 5,600 more missing. Nepal must still file a request saying how the money will be spent, and an earlier request after Kathmandu floods is already waiting. Pakistan, which pushed the fund onto the agenda after 2022 floods that affected 33 million people and killed more than 1,730, has two projects in and has been told the cap is 200 million dollars. Minister of State for Climate Change Shezra Mansab Ali Khan Kharal told Deutsche Welle Pakistan had still not seen anything on the ground.

Context & perspectives: Nepali climate-finance expert Bibek Raj Kandel said asking a country in an emergency to write a proposal adds bureaucracy that does not match the urgency. Kharal said Pakistan does not want a climate disaster to become more debt, and wants grants rather than loans. Abid Qaiyum Suleri, executive director of the Sustainable Development Policy Institute in Islamabad, called the promises hollow and said the form of the money matters as much as the sum for a heavily indebted country.

6) Japan’s price gauge hits its fastest pace since 2023

What happened (neutral): A Bank of Japan measure that strips out fresh food and special factors rose 3.5 percent in October from a year earlier, the fastest pace since September 2023, the Japan Times reports. The Bank of Japan is the country’s central bank. The paper says the reading backs the case for another interest-rate increase.

Context & perspectives: The figure is a policy input, not itself a decision to raise rates. No date for a next meeting appears in the report.

Progress watch (1)

7) Bangladesh raises the garment minimum wage by 36 percent

What happened (neutral): What’s actually being done is a pay decision, not another round of talks. Bangladesh has raised the minimum wage for garment workers after weeks of protests, the Associated Press reports. Al Jazeera puts the rise at 36 percent. The protests have cost the country hundreds of millions, and workers have not yet accepted the offer.

Context & perspectives: The raise is the government’s answer to the walkouts. The open complication is that the people it is meant to pay more have not agreed to it.

Understanding the Issue: How a winter gas shortage becomes a timed power cut

A common misreading is that a country with huge oil and gas reserves goes dark only if the fuel itself has vanished, or that a short daily cut means the whole grid has collapsed. The host’s own explanation of the mechanism is this. In a gas-heavy system, the same fuel often heats homes and turns the turbines that make electricity. In winter, heating demand rises just as the network is least able to spare gas for power stations. Operators then have two scarce things to protect: pressure in the gas pipes, and the steady frequency of the electric grid. If they let every customer draw at once, either the gas pressure falls until more plants trip offline, or the grid frequency slips and larger areas go dark without warning. A rolling cut is a queue. Neighbourhoods, or whole districts, lose power for a set stretch so that hospitals, water plants, and some industry can stay on, and so that the same homes are not dark for the entire day. That is a ration, chosen in advance, not the same event as a storm tearing down lines. It also explains why fixing a damaged turbine does not, by itself, end the cuts: if the missing piece is fuel to burn, a repaired plant still sits idle. When you next read a story like this, separate three lines that are often folded into one: how much generating equipment is broken, how much fuel is actually reaching the plants, and whether the darkness is on a published schedule or arriving without warning.

Media-literacy note

When two official voices describe one explosion, write down who claims a fire and who claims an interception before you decide which picture you believe. A wage figure, a treaty clause, and a death count are all checkable against the office that issued them, not against the loudest headline. If a story matters to you, open the second link and see which sentence that outlet actually adds.

To really understand a timed blackout, separate a fuel shortage from a collapsed grid. People often hear darkness in an oil-rich country and assume the fuel is gone, or that a short daily cut means the system has failed outright. This next part is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from one of today’s articles. In a gas-heavy system the same fuel often heats houses and spins the turbines. Winter is when heating demand climbs and spare gas for power stations shrinks. Operators are then guarding two things at once: pressure in the gas pipes, and the steady frequency of the electric grid. If everyone draws at the same moment, pressure can fall until more plants trip off, or frequency can slip and a much wider area goes dark with no schedule. A rolling cut is a queue. Districts lose power for a set stretch so hospitals, water plants, and some factories can stay on, and so the same homes are not dark all day. That is a ration decided in advance. It is not the same thing as a storm pulling lines down. Repairing a turbine does not end the cuts if the missing piece is fuel to burn. A mended plant can still sit idle. Next time you meet a story like this, split three facts that headlines often merge: how much equipment is broken, how much fuel is reaching the plants, and whether the darkness arrives on a published schedule or without warning.
