About 1.8 million people face emergency hunger in Yemen as strikes widen.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Yemen strikes widen as hunger hits emergency levels

What happened (neutral): Yemen’s internationally recognised government has launched air strikes across Houthi-held territory, and Colonel Majed al-Nuzaili, a military spokesman, said government forces carried out 726 attacks in the 24 hours before Friday and claimed 121 fighters and commanders were killed or wounded, Al Jazeera reported. Al Jazeera’s Yousef Mawry, reporting from Sanaa, said the Saudi-led coalition that backs the government hit more than six governorates overnight, including a gas station in Sanaa’s Tahrir district that injured 13 people, three of them critically, and a later strike that the Houthis said killed a man and his wife in their home. He also described raids on a fuel station in Bani Matar, on Taiz, and five raids in Mocha that targeted a power station. Houthi-affiliated media reported that a Saudi air strike killed 11 civilians, most of them children, in al-Turbah in Taiz; Al Jazeera could not confirm that toll, and coalition forces did not comment. The government-run Saba news agency reported that Houthi attacks on government locations in Taiz on Saturday killed three civilians, including a 10-year-old boy, and wounded three others, including an infant. Coalition officials said a Houthi missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted. The claims and casualties could not be independently verified. The World Food Programme and the Food and Agriculture Organization — the United Nations agencies for food aid and farming — warned on Friday that hunger in Yemen is likely to worsen over the next six months. About 1.8 million people faced emergency levels of hunger in government-held areas. More than 300,000 people have been newly displaced since the escalation began, mostly in Taiz, Lahij and Aden. The agencies said the Houthis encircled Taiz in early October, that Houthi-held areas were becoming harder to reach, and that the group was holding 73 United Nations staff. Aid groups say donor cuts, including by the United States and other Western countries, have deepened the crisis, with food and fuel prices soaring.

Context & perspectives: The Iran-backed Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014, and Saudi Arabia led a coalition from March 2015 in support of the recognised government. Fighting resumed in July after a 2022 ceasefire collapsed, amid tensions around the United States-Israel war on Iran. Al-Nuzaili said fighting was ongoing in Ma’rib, al-Jawf, Taiz, Lahij, Shabwah, Midi and al-Dhale.

This is CONTESTED. The shared ground is that strikes and ground fighting have resumed and that the two United Nations food agencies, not either army, produced the hunger and displacement figures. Colonel al-Nuzaili’s case is that 726 attacks in a day are a military campaign against Houthi fighters and commanders, with 121 of them killed or wounded, and that Houthi fire — including a missile toward Riyadh and attacks that Saba said killed civilians in Taiz — is what government forces are answering. The Houthi account, carried by Houthi-affiliated media, is that a Saudi strike in al-Turbah killed 11 civilians, most of them children, so the offensive is landing on families, not only on fighters. Al Jazeera could not confirm that civilian toll, the coalition did not comment, and neither side’s casualty claims were independently verified — a limit each account has to live with.

Major world stories (3)

1) Spain votes Sunday on Sanchez’s record

What happened (neutral): Voters in Spain head to a snap election on Sunday that could bring the far-right Vox party into national government for the first time in five decades, half a century after Francisco Franco’s dictatorship ended, Al Jazeera reported. The campaign, which closed on Friday, turned on housing and immigration. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez, 54, of the Socialist Workers’ Party, has been in power since 2018. Polls suggest the conservative People’s Party will win the most seats but will need Vox, projected third, to reach the 176 seats required for a majority in the 350-seat Congress of Deputies. Most surveys put the two together at that number, which would give People’s Party leader Alberto Nunez Feijoo a coalition or a minority government backed by Vox. Sanchez called the vote in October after parliament rejected two measures meant to protect renters, following protests over housing costs and evictions. The European Commission forecasts growth of 2.6 percent for Spain this year, against 1.2 percent for the eurozone. France 24 reported that Vox built its campaign on immigration after tens of thousands of people crossed into Ceuta, a Spanish city in North Africa, from Morocco in July. The South China Morning Post noted that Europe is opening an electoral cycle over the next 12 months that could shift the balance inside the European Union.

Context & perspectives: Alvaro Sanchez-Garcia, a political scientist at Spain’s Centre for Political and Constitutional Studies, told Al Jazeera the vote has been framed as a yes or no on Sanchez, and that switchers mostly stay inside the same left or right bloc. In 2023 Sanchez’s party finished second and he stayed in office with support from parties to his left and from Catalan and Basque groupings. Sanchez has clashed with United States President Donald Trump, refused a request from other NATO members — the North Atlantic Treaty Organization — to lift defence spending to 5 percent, and called Israel’s war in Gaza a genocide. On Friday he told a radio interviewer, “I can’t conceive of us losing,” and told a Madrid rally, “This election about one thing only: Whether Spain moves forward or goes backwards.”

This is CONTESTED. Sanchez’s case, in his own framing, is that Spain has never been in better shape: faster growth than a stagnant eurozone, and a progressive record that has made him a reference point for the European left. The limit his own allies cannot wish away is the October defeat of the renter measures that forced this vote. Feijoo’s case, put to Sanchez in a televised debate last week, is that national income is not a meal — “You can’t eat GDP” — and that alleged corruption and opportunism require a change of government. Isabel Labayen, 64, a longtime People’s Party voter, asked how Spain can be praised abroad when people cannot afford a house or food, or see a doctor in days rather than months. Vox’s case, as France 24 described the campaign, is that the July arrivals in Ceuta made immigration the issue that should decide the government. Polls are not ballots, and Feijoo still needs Vox for 176 seats.

Questions to consider: Do the People’s Party and Vox together reach 176 seats when Sunday’s count is finished?

2) Trump backs Baghdad’s push on oil routes and borders

What happened (neutral): United States President Donald Trump on Friday endorsed Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi’s campaign against oil smuggling, Al Jazeera reported. In a Truth Social post he wrote that Washington supports efforts “to assert state authority throughout Iraq, stop the smuggling of Iraqi oil, end corruption, and bring all weapons under the exclusive control of the state,” and to build an Iraq that “makes its own political decisions.” It was Washington’s first comment on the tension between Baghdad and Erbil, the seat of the semi-autonomous Kurdish region. On Tuesday al-Zaidi moved to put the region’s border crossings and several oilfields under federal control. On Wednesday Iraq’s top court ruled that Baghdad may deploy federal forces along all international borders, including the Kurdish region’s. On Thursday Iraq closed two crossings there, one with Syria and one with Iran. Talks between Baghdad and Erbil are due on December 1, sponsored by the head of Iraq’s Supreme Judicial Council.

Context & perspectives: The crackdown started in Jurf al-Sakhar, also called Jurf al-Nasr, in Babil province south of Baghdad. Kataib Hezbollah, an Iran-backed armed group the United States designates a terrorist organisation, has held the town since 2014, when it and allied groups took it from ISIL, the Islamic State, and most Sunni residents were displaced. Senior Iraqi security and oil officials told Reuters that the group has run an oil-smuggling network there, siphoning state crude worth hundreds of millions of dollars since 2021. Nine senior security and oil officials have been dismissed and referred to the courts. Kataib Hezbollah denies the allegations. Al-Zaidi gave the group until November 20 to leave. A day before the deadline, after reported intervention by Esmail Qaani, head of Iran’s Quds Force, government forces entered and set up a joint operations centre; Kataib Hezbollah units are due to withdraw gradually as army, federal police, energy police and Popular Mobilisation Forces units deploy in phases. Harakat al-Nujaba and Kataib Sayyid al-Shuhada have also refused to disarm. After armed groups missed a September 30 deadline to hand over weapons, the government pushed that deadline to June 2027. Government spokesperson Haider al-Aboudi told the Iraqi News Agency that Iraq lost an estimated $100 billion between 2008 and 2025 from oil smuggled through the Kurdish region. Salim Zakhour, a constitutional-law researcher, told Al Jazeera the American post tells Kurdish leaders that Baghdad’s measures have strong American backing.

3) El Nino floods hit Piura and add to Ecuador’s toll

What happened (neutral): Cities in Peru and Ecuador are flooding after downpours linked to an exceptionally strong El Nino — a warming of the sea surface in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific that shifts wind and rain worldwide — Al Jazeera reported. In Piura, northern Peru, waist-high water filled streets after 12 hours of heavy rain that began on Thursday. Luis Vasquez, chief of the National Institute of Civil Defence, told RPP radio that preliminary reports put the number of affected families in Piura at about 2,000. Floodwater forced the Jose Cayetano Heredia Hospital to move patients from its respiratory-infection ward to the auditorium, and more serious patients to intensive care. The Plaza de Armas and many businesses and public buildings were also hit. Peru’s armed forces have sent personnel and pumps; some residents said machines stopped for lack of fuel. The national weather service warned that moderate to heavy rain could continue in parts of the region for four days. President Keiko Fujimori postponed a trip to Colombia and said she plans to travel to the affected areas. The navy blamed El Nino for ocean swells that damaged Pacific-coast infrastructure and temporarily closed dozens of ports. In Ecuador, the National Secretariat for Risk Management said on Friday that 34 people had died and 47,000 had been affected. In Manta, two rivers broke their banks; resident Fernanda Navarrete said she had been “soaking in mud for four days” and had lost furniture, clothes and appliances. Peru and Ecuador are among several Latin American countries that have declared national emergencies. This year’s El Nino is projected to be the strongest on record.

Context & perspectives: Resident Pierre Garavito, whose refrigerator was floating in his Santa Margarita home, told reporters this was only the first heavy rain of the episode and that he had lost everything. The South China Morning Post carried the same Piura toll from Vasquez and the same picture of waist-high water after Thursday’s rain.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) Uganda opens Africa’s first trial of an mRNA Ebola shot for children

What happened (neutral): Africa’s first clinical trial of an mRNA vaccine against the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola has begun in Uganda, aimed at the strain behind the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Al Jazeera reported. The state-run Uganda Virus Research Institute said on Friday that the trial is with Moderna, the United States company that makes the vaccine. It is the second trial of the shot. Moderna’s first human trial began in Canada in August and enrolled adults aged 18 to 65. The Ugandan trial is the first to test safety and immune response in children aged 1 and above, and aims to enrol 224 volunteers. Richard Hatchett, chief executive of the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations, which is helping fund the vaccine, said children are often hit hardest when Ebola strikes and are too often last to get new vaccines. A second candidate, the ChAdOx1 vaccine from India’s Serum Institute and the University of Oxford, is due to begin testing in the Democratic Republic of the Congo on Monday. Gavi, the alliance that helps lower-income countries buy vaccines, signed a purchase commitment last week with the Serum Institute for 1.5 million doses and plans to have hundreds of thousands ready if trials succeed and the World Health Organization and the Congolese government approve them. Emanuele Capobianco, Gavi’s director of global health security, said he hoped those trials would finish in early 2027 and that the deal could bring deployment forward by months. On Thursday, Congolese government data showed at least 12,021 confirmed Ebola infections and 5,904 deaths since the outbreak began in mid-May. Uganda declared its own outbreak over on July 28, after 20 infections and two deaths, the Ministry of Health said.

Context & perspectives: The Ugandan trial does not replace the Congolese outbreak response. It asks whether this shot is safe and whether it prompts an immune response, including in children, before any wider use.

2) Indian workers in Germany rise sevenfold, and many jobs stay empty

What happened (neutral): The number of Indian workers paying into Germany’s social-security system has grown sevenfold in a decade, to more than 140,000, Deutsche Welle reported, many of them in information technology and health care. Pratyay Saikia, 25, from Haryana, answered a 2022 advertisement from Magic Billion, a recruiter placing Indian workers in checked jobs abroad. He arrived in Baden-Württemberg with 180 other Indians for apprenticeships, finished a three-year apprenticeship at Metzgerei Lederer, a butcher’s shop, and now works there full time. A 2024 Bertelsmann Foundation study concluded Germany needs 288,000 foreign skilled workers a year until 2040 to replace retirees. FMC Group, a consultancy, noted that more than 369,000 skilled jobs went unfilled in 2025. Germany is also seeking electricians, mechatronics technicians, solar-panel installers and truck drivers, not only nurses and software specialists.

Context & perspectives: Daniel Raja, managing director of NM NextMittelstand, a Düsseldorf recruiter linking German firms with Indian workers, said hiring an electrician from India is still new for many Mittelstand firms — the small and medium-sized companies — so they hesitate, and that workers’ councils sometimes resist because they believe “cheap Indian labor” will take jobs or cut wages. David Kipp, of the German Institute for International and Security Affairs, said he is sceptical of lowering language requirements, because people with little German have less contact with German institutions and are easier to exploit. Saikia had reached B1, an intermediate level, and still found the theory classes hard until co-workers helped and his German improved.

Progress watch (1)

1) Mining explosives kill students beside a Kunar school

What happened (neutral): An explosion on Saturday near a middle school in Asadabad, in Afghanistan’s eastern Kunar province, killed students and injured scores more. Deutsche Welle, citing the Interior Ministry, reported at least seven students killed and nearly 100 injured. The ministry’s spokesperson said early reports point to negligent storage of explosives at a mining-company depot in the Andir Sir area, beside the school. Provincial police spokesman Farid Dehqan said the blast was around 10 a.m. Samiulhaq Haqbayan, the provincial information director, told the German press agency DPA that the explosives were for mining and for clearing hazardous rocks along the Kunar-Nuristan highway. No one has said what ignited them. The ministry said an investigation is ongoing. Al Jazeera quoted Mawla Dad, head of Kunar Provincial Hospital, who put the toll at at least six students killed and 97 injured, and said those figures were preliminary and could change. Mawlawi Sami-ul-Haq, Kunar’s director of information and culture, told Al Jazeera the blast was in a house beside a middle school in Andar Ser, used by people in mining who had stored dynamite there. Interior Ministry spokesman Mufti Abdul Mateen Qani said initial investigations point to negligence. Investigative teams were at the scene. It was the third explosion near a school or religious school in a week. Two blasts near religious schools in Kapisa and Farah killed five students in total; local officials blamed unexploded ordnance from earlier wars.

Context & perspectives: The two outlets differ on the first count: the Interior Ministry figure carried by Deutsche Welle is seven dead and nearly 100 injured; the hospital chief’s figure carried by Al Jazeera is six dead and 97 injured, and he said it could change. Both trace the blast to mining explosives stored beside the school, not to an attack. Mining is a major industry in a country rich in iron ore, copper, lead, zinc, gold, coal and gemstones, and safety standards at mining sites are often poor. Gemstone mining is growing in Kunar.

Understanding the Issue: Why the party in first place can still miss the government

A common misreading of a European election night is that the party with the most seats has won the government. That is not how this kind of parliament works. The following is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from a separate study. A prime minister who loses a vote he or she treated as essential can dissolve the chamber and pick an early date, which is what a snap election is: the calendar moves, the rules for forming a government do not. In a multiparty chamber, governing requires a majority of seats, or enough partners to supply one, not a plurality. A first-place party that is short of that line must bargain with a rival whose voters it spent the campaign opposing, or stay in opposition while a second-place leader assembles a wider set of partners. Regional and nationalist parties can matter as much as the national runner-up, because their seats count the same on the night a government is proposed. Campaigns that sort voters into two blocs make that arithmetic starker: few people cross the aisle, so the result is less a swing of individuals than a test of which bloc can hold its own voters and then find allies. Abroad, a leader can be admired for foreign-policy stands that never appear on the domestic ballot, where rent, food and a doctor’s appointment decide the vote. When you next read a “winner” headline from a parliament like Spain’s, check whether any combination of named parties can actually seat a government — not only who finished first.

Media-literacy note

Casualty claims in a war, and the first hours after a blast, often arrive from one side’s spokesman or one hospital, and they move. When two outlets give two tolls, keep both and note who counted. A poll is a survey of voters before they vote, not the seat total. For the stories that will still matter next week, open a second outlet from another country and look for the document underneath: a court ruling, a United Nations food analysis, a company or ministry statement.

To really understand a result like Spain’s, you have to separate the party that finishes first from the party that gets to govern. A common misreading of a European election night is that the largest pile of seats is the government. It is not. What follows is my own explanation of the mechanism, not a finding from a separate study. A prime minister who loses a vote treated as essential can dissolve the chamber and pick an early date. That is a snap election. The calendar moves. The rule for forming a government does not. In a multiparty chamber, governing takes a majority of seats, or enough partners to supply one, not merely first place. A leader who is short of that line must bargain with a rival the campaign just opposed, or stay out while someone else gathers a wider set of partners. Regional and nationalist parties can matter as much as the national runner-up, because each of their seats counts the same when a government is proposed. Campaigns that sort voters into two blocs make the arithmetic starker. Few people cross, so the night tests which bloc held its own voters and can then find allies. A leader can be admired abroad for stands that never appear on the domestic ballot, where rent, food and a doctor’s appointment decide the vote.
