Six drones targeted a US base in Bahrain, ending two months of calm.

Today's lead story (1)

1) Strikes return over Bahrain after a two-month lull

What happened (neutral): Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — a branch of Iran’s armed forces — said it launched six drones at a United States air-defence radar at the Al-Riffa helicopter base in Bahrain, France 24 reported, in the second claimed strike on the Gulf kingdom in three days. Bahrain’s military said its air defences intercepted several Iranian aerial attacks before they reached their targets, CNBC reported, and the foreign ministry said the systems shot down Iranian drones. There was no immediate confirmation from US or Bahraini authorities that the radar was hit. Sirens sounded, the first warnings since September, and blasts were reported around 4 a.m. local time and again around 6:30 a.m. Bahrain, home to the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet — the American naval command for the Gulf — told residents to stay calm and go to the nearest safe place. The interior ministry posted that guidance after two threat warnings.

Context & perspectives: The Guardian, in a live report, quoted Hayat Ibrahim, 65, telling Agence France-Presse that the blasts were what she had hoped would not return after the quiet. Two days earlier the Guards said they had hit a US early-warning radar in Bahrain; US Central Command, which runs American military operations in the region, said it had shot down two Iranian drones with no casualties or damage. CNBC reported that President Donald Trump has said in recent days he might hold off further attacks on Iran if there are no new provocations from Tehran or the Iran-backed Houthis in Yemen, while keeping a US naval blockade. The same report said the Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis intercepted two Houthi ballistic missiles launched toward Riyadh and three drones in Saudi Arabia’s Eastern Province. France 24 carried the Guards’ further line that the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow oil route at the mouth of the Gulf — “is closed,” and that any American aggression would meet a harsher response. Bahrain’s foreign ministry said the kingdom rejects having its territory used as an arena for settling scores and holds Iran fully responsible.

The attacks are contested in what they achieved and what they justify. The Guards’ stated case is retaliation for what they call hostile US actions, with a warning that further American force will be answered more heavily, and with the Hormuz claim attached to that same statement. Bahrain’s stated case is that the weapons were stopped short of their targets, that the pattern is a systematic hostile approach, and that Iran, not Bahrain, owns the consequences of firing at a kingdom that hosts a US fleet.

Questions to consider:

  • Do US or Bahraini authorities later confirm any damage at Al-Riffa, or only interceptions?

Major world stories (3)

1) Cape Verde votes, with a woman on the presidential ballot for the first time

What happened (neutral): Voters began casting ballots in Cape Verde, where President Jose Maria Neves faces five opponents, including Joana Rosa, the first woman to run for president since independence in 1975, Al Jazeera reported. More than 300,000 people are eligible, from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. local time, in a country often cited for stability in a volatile region. A winner needs more than 50 percent; otherwise the top two meet in a runoff on November 29. Results are expected within two to three days.

Context & perspectives: Neves, of the African Party for the Independence of Cape Verde, the country’s oldest party, was prime minister for 15 years and became president in 2021. His party won 37 of 72 parliamentary seats in May, and he appointed Francisco Carvalho, then mayor of the capital, Praia, as prime minister. Rosa, of the Movement for Democracy and a former justice minister, comes from a party that governed for 10 years before that May vote. The presidency in this hybrid system has veto power and a mediating role, not day-to-day control of government. The islands have about 500,000 residents and more citizens abroad than at home, mostly in Portugal and the United States; nearly 77,000 diaspora voters are registered at embassies. The vote turns on emigration and scarce jobs. Roselma Evora, a professor of political science and gender studies at the University of Cabo Verde, told the Associated Press that many voters feel distant from a political class they think does not understand daily difficulties.

Rosa’s case is that government should let young people build a future on the islands and stop the exodus. Neves’s case is a second term built around a youth summit aimed at the same emigration problem. Each treats leaving as the crisis; they differ on whether the answer is a summit under the incumbent or a change of who holds the presidency.

Questions to consider:

  • Does anyone clear 50 percent in the count expected within two to three days, or is the November 29 runoff on?

2) ASEAN opens in Manila with energy shortages on the table

What happened (neutral): Leaders of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations — an 11-country bloc that wants a single market but cannot impose decisions on members — opened their summit in Manila, running November 15 to 17, Al Jazeera reported. The bloc counts nearly 694 million people, about 8.4 percent of the world. Protesters met arriving leaders over the cost of basic goods and over Pax Silica, a US-led artificial-intelligence supply-chain initiative, Al Jazeera’s Barnaby Lo reported.

Context & perspectives: Oil and gas shortages tied to the US-Israel war on Iran are the energy issue import-dependent members brought into the room. The Philippines, Vietnam, and Malaysia are described as among the most exposed; Manila declared a national energy emergency in March. Millions of the bloc’s citizens live and work in the Middle East. Governments also worry the conflict could reach the Strait of Malacca, the narrow sea lane between Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. US tariff increases of 10 to 12.5 percent since President Donald Trump’s second term have pushed the bloc to trade more with others. Trump is expected at a special meeting with members on November 17, then at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation gathering in China on November 18–19. Other guests named by Al Jazeera include India’s Narendra Modi, Canada’s Mark Carney, Brazil’s Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva — the first sitting Brazilian president to visit the Philippines — and Russia’s foreign minister Sergey Lavrov. On the South China Sea, China and four members — Brunei, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Vietnam — plus Taiwan have long disputed a waterway believed to hold undersea oil and gas. A “code of conduct” negotiated since 2005 was meant, under a 2023 pledge, to reach a finished draft this year; at least 10 rounds were held this year, the last in October, led by Malaysia.

3) Portuguese cities march over rents

What happened (neutral): People marched in cities across Portugal against surging home prices, demanding government action on housing, Al Jazeera reported, while the Associated Press described the crowds as tens of thousands. Al Jazeera’s count was thousands. Authorities have begun changes aimed at lowering prices. Protesters told Al Jazeera that those steps are not enough.

Context & perspectives: The two counts are the outlets’ own: the Associated Press headline says tens of thousands; Al Jazeera says thousands. Neither report in the material names a new law, a rent figure, or a date for the next government step.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) Commerce secretary presses for looser rules on chips used outside China

What happened (neutral): Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has pushed a less strict version of controls on sales of advanced artificial-intelligence chips to data centers outside China that still serve Chinese firms, The New York Times reported. In southern Malaysia, a center known as A201 is set to open next year with hundreds of millions of dollars in Nvidia’s most advanced servers, and plans to supply that computing to a Chinese tech giant. The Times said the setup is legal and sidesteps a US ban on selling the most advanced chips directly to Chinese firms. The operator is a client of Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm Lutnick formerly led and then handed to his sons. He is now writing rules that would substantially affect that business.

Context & perspectives: The Trump administration bans cutting-edge chip sales to China and has debated for much of the past two years how far to limit sales to Southeast Asian data centers. Both the ban on direct sales and the legality of a third-country center, as the Times describes them, sit under the argument. China hawks in that debate treat centers like A201 as a way for Beijing to get around the ban. Lutnick’s push is against limits that would cut Nvidia’s Asian sales by giving Washington more oversight of chips going to international data centers. The Times said that softer line has drawn criticism from lawmakers and others in the administration. The article does not name a date for a final rule.

2) South Korea’s top tax-exam scorer admits using AI

What happened (neutral): The top scorer on South Korea’s national exam for aspiring tax accountants confessed to using generative artificial intelligence to cheat, The Guardian reported. He had seemed too strong to be real: one wrong answer out of 160 questions across two exams, in April and July. The admission landed on Sunday. Organisers of the national college entrance exam this Thursday now face higher stakes, the same report said.

Context & perspectives: The Guardian said the scandal followed months of speculation about those near-perfect marks. It did not describe a penalty already imposed.

Progress watch (1)

1) Starship is caught by the launch tower

What happened (neutral): SpaceX recaptured its Starship rocket after a return from orbit, a first, the Associated Press reported, with the vehicle caught by arms on the launch pad. France 24 said the catch came after the booster was not caught the day before. Smaller earlier models have landed again, France 24 noted, but they need heavy legs and are not fully reusable.

Context & perspectives: The new fact is the catch itself, not another plan to try. The articles do not give a landing-site name, a crew, or a next flight date.

Understanding the Issue: How a chip ban can miss the customer who rents the computer

A common mix-up is to treat a chip ban as a ban on the work the chip can do. This explanation is the host’s own, not a finding from one of today’s articles. Export rules of this kind usually attach to a sale: who may buy the physical processor, and which destination that box may be shipped to. A data center in a country that is allowed to buy the chips can still rent the computing — the hours of calculation — to a customer in a country that is not allowed to buy the chips. The processor stays where it was legally sold. The data and the answers move. People who want a tight ban argue that the customer of the computing is the real end user, so oversight has to follow the service contract, not only the shipping label. People who want a narrower rule argue that once a sale is legal in the country where the machines sit, policing every later user of the service reaches past the chip and into ordinary cloud business. Reading coverage of any new control, the useful check is whether the draft limits the buyer of the hardware or also the customer who pays for the computing time.

Media-literacy note

When two governments describe the same night, put each claim next to what was independently confirmed — interceptions, damage, or neither. A live quote from a resident is evidence of what that person heard, not of what a radar did. For a rule fight, read who would gain or lose money if the looser text wins, then read the security argument on its own terms. Primary statements — a military release, a court order, an election commission count — beat a headline that has already chosen a verb.

Go deeper: France 24 carries the Guards’ claim that the Strait of Hormuz is closed; CNBC reports Trump may hold off further strikes if Tehran and the Houthis offer no new provocations, while keeping a naval blockade.
