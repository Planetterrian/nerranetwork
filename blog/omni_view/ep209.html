Understanding the Issue: How an upper house can constrain a government even after lower-house victories

Most coverage treats a parliamentary election win as full control, yet many systems give the upper house independent power over constitutional matters. In the Czech Republic the Senate must approve amendments and judicial appointments even when the lower house is held by the governing coalition. This structure traces to post-1989 constitutional design that deliberately split legislative authority to prevent rapid majoritarian change. The same pattern appears in other bicameral European systems where upper chambers serve as slower-moving checks. The Czech Senate, for example, retains a decisive role in confirming Constitutional Court judges and passing constitutional amendments despite the lower house’s broader legislative authority. Knowing this, when a prime minister promises sweeping reforms after a lower-house victory, readers can check whether the upper house composition allows those reforms to pass. The next time a government claims it now has a free hand, verify whether constitutional rules require approval from a separately elected chamber. Historical examples from the last three decades show that upper-house majorities have repeatedly delayed or altered government priorities in several European states. This separation of powers encourages negotiation between chambers rather than unilateral action by the party that wins the more visible lower-house contest. The result means pro-Western opposition parties control the chamber, the upper house of parliament. The election on Saturday was the first electoral test for Babis after he won parliamentary elections in October 2025 that returned him to power after a break of four years since his first term as premier. Final results from the two-day ballot showed that candidates directly linked to the opposition parties won 22 of the 27 seats up for grabs in the 81-member Senate, retaining a previous opposition majority.
