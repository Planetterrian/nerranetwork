Nearly two hundred nations weigh a draft that admits one point five degrees will be passed.

Today's lead story (1)

1) A new COP31 draft still omits fossil fuels as overtime runs on

What happened (neutral): Diplomats from nearly 200 countries are still in Antalya, Turkey, on a second day of overtime at COP31, the United Nations climate summit, after a Friday close slipped, Deutsche Welle reports. The latest draft still does not mention fossil fuels, the main driver of human-caused warming. Al Jazeera reports that the new draft would, for the first time, acknowledge that global warming is on course to pass the 1.5C threshold. German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider called the draft “not acceptable” and “a step backward from previous decisions on climate protection.” Former Greenpeace chief Jennifer Morgan said it would be better to end with no deal than with a text that “sets us back,” and that “Saudi Arabia’s fingerprints are all over” the draft. She also said this year’s Turkish presidency was “not well prepared” and had left four days of shuttle diplomacy with no text.

Context & perspectives: CONTESTED. The dispute turns on whether a thin text is better than none. The Turkish COP presidency has suggested it is better to accept the current draft than to leave with no resolution at all — a finished line, even a weak one, keeps nearly 200 governments inside one decision rather than scattering them. Schneider’s case is that a conference whose only job is climate protection cannot adopt a text that moves backward from earlier decisions; Germany, he said, cannot support that. Morgan’s case, aligned with the German minister, is that a decision which vulnerable islands and people reject would set the process back, and that oil-producing states shaped the omission. She praised Germany for refusing the text and contrasted this presidency with last year’s Brazilian one, which she said had spent the year pulling countries together. Deutsche Welle also notes fossil fuels are the flashpoint while energy prices are high and the US-Israeli war with Iran continues. The summit stays open until there is consensus, or until governments choose to leave without one.

Questions to consider: Will Antalya close with an adopted text or with no decision at all?

Major world stories (3)

1) Cost-of-living marches continue across Europe, with two very different French counts

What happened (neutral): Sunday is a third straight day of protests from Portugal to France over the cost of living and affordable housing, France24 reports, after large demonstrations on Friday and smaller ones on Saturday; in France the marches have now run for six weekends. Al Jazeera reports that organisers counted 150,000 people in France on Saturday, down from 200,000 the week before and from 800,000 on October 17, the first of the weekly demonstrations. France’s Ministry of the Interior said turnout had “significantly declined,” putting Saturday at 82,500, compared with 106,000 a week earlier. The two counts are not reconciled in either report: organisers and the ministry are measuring the same Saturday and publishing different totals.

Context & perspectives: Al Jazeera reports the movement began in mid-September with high school students calling for better learning conditions, then widened into a national campaign over prices, public services, and police brutality. Marches have been largely peaceful, with sporadic violence at the edges and repeated accusations of police brutality. Last month, the same weekly rallies produced the worst rioting in years, with hundreds of shops looted and buildings attacked and set on fire. Unions and activists have called for more protests in the coming weeks. No government reply to those demands is quoted in today’s reports.

2) Somalia expels the UAE ambassador after years of security cooperation

What happened (neutral): On November 19, Somalia ordered the United Arab Emirates’ ambassador to leave and recalled its own envoy from Abu Dhabi, Al Jazeera reports — the sharpest break yet with a partner President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud once called “one of the greatest pillars” of the war against al-Shabab, the al-Qaeda-linked armed group. The UAE said it had already withdrawn its diplomatic staff from Mogadishu in January. The expulsion came 10 months after Somalia annulled commercial, defence, and security agreements, accusing Abu Dhabi of undermining its sovereignty.

Context & perspectives: CONTESTED. Mohamud’s own framing, two days before the order, was familial and absolute: “A brother does not burn your house. A brother does not buy your property without your knowledge. A brother does not divide your family.” National security adviser Awes Hagi Yusuf Ahmed wrote that, after years of engagement, the federal government judges UAE policy a threat to national security — financing rival regional administrations and armed actors outside federal control. The strongest case for the break is that a state cannot fight al-Shabab, or hold a federation together, if a wealthy partner arms and funds the regions that resist the centre. Anwar Gargash, a senior Emirati official, answered that ties “remain deeper and stronger” despite the criticism, and that Mogadishu is letting its internal political disputes strain the relationship — a case that the president’s fight with semi-autonomous regions, several of them close to Abu Dhabi, is being exported into foreign policy. Abu Dhabi’s foothold grew in Mohamud’s first term, 2012 to 2017: the embassy reopened in 2014, and he inaugurated a UAE-funded military training centre the next year, while the UAE also built commercial and security ties with Puntland, Jubbaland, and Somaliland, which declared independence in 1991. The UAE remains a major trading partner and a hub for Somali businesses. Neither side, in this account, spells out which annulled agreements still bind anyone.

3) Indonesia searches for about 50 people after a Java factory fire

What happened (neutral): Indonesian police recovered seven body bags on Sunday while rescuers searched for about 50 workers still missing after a fire tore through a factory in western Java, the South China Morning Post reports. Firefighters were still hitting hotspots more than a day after the blaze. Police said one body bag might hold body parts from several people. The heat left the recovered dead unidentifiable by sight, so authorities are collecting DNA from relatives.

Context & perspectives: The search and the DNA collection are the response named so far. The reports do not give the factory’s name, the cause of the fire, or a full death toll.

Economy, science & technology (2)

1) South Korea’s education minister resigns after exam-day phone alerts

What happened (neutral): President Lee Jae Myung has accepted the resignation of Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin, the BBC reports, after last-minute steps to stop cheating tools threw Thursday’s Suneung — the annual college entrance exam, taken by about 500,000 students — into confusion. A week earlier, Han Won-seok, top scorer on the national tax accountancy exam, admitted using a microcamera, a phone, and an AI tool; he got one of 160 questions wrong. Choi told Yonhap he could not escape criticism that the ministry “failed to prepare in advance.” The ministry said 111 candidates were found with unauthorised smart devices. For the first time, the national disaster-alert system was used to catch cheaters: three alerts between 08:00 and 15:00 local time made phones sound even on silent, including tourists on data roaming, unless the phone was off or in airplane mode. Candidates had to cover shirt and jacket buttons and submit eyewear to inspection.

Context & perspectives: France24 also reports the Sunday resignation, centred on that tax-exam admission. Lee has been calling for an overhaul of the exam system. Students told local media they feared ordinary glasses would be treated as smart glasses; one wore contact lenses to avoid suspicion. The Suneung can last up to nine hours and shapes careers for millions of South Koreans.

2) Kenya weighs mandatory internet meters

What happened (neutral): A bill before Kenya’s National Assembly would make consumption-based internet billing mandatory, Al Jazeera reports. The Kenya Information and Communications (Amendment) Bill, 2025, sponsored by Aldai MP Marianne Jebet Kitany, would require each customer to have a unique meter number, with providers monitoring use, turning it into readable detail, and billing by consumption. Providers would also send billing-system information, including meter numbers, to the Communications Authority of Kenya at least once a financial year. The stated aim is to stop unfair billing so people pay for the data they use. The bill does not expressly require recording message content or websites visited.

Context & perspectives: The Communications Authority has urged lawmakers not to make metered billing compulsory. Telkom Kenya has warned of consequences for privacy, affordability, and investment. Demas Kiprono of the International Commission of Jurists Kenya argues the regulator already can handle billing complaints, and questions whether identifiable usage records are necessary and proportionate. Critics want the text to say who may see the records, how long they are kept, and when they may be shared. Kenya’s Data Protection Act, 2019, already governs personal information; opponents want Parliament to say how it would apply here. The assembly’s Departmental Committee on Communication, Information and Innovation is considering the bill.

Progress watch (1)

1) Rhine cargo capacity returns as gauges climb out of the lows

What happened (neutral): Germany’s Federal Institute for Hydrology expects water in large western rivers, including the Rhine, to return to average or above in the next few days after sustained rain, Deutsche Welle reports. At Kaub, near Koblenz — a bottleneck on the Middle Rhine — the gauge could reach some 3.3 metres, more than 3 metres above the last low-water readings. The Weser and Danube are also expected to rise, with smaller increases on the Elbe and Oder. Fabian Spiess, deputy manager of the inland navigation association BDB, said each ship again has much more cargo capacity. The complication he names is that mid-year load cuts caused losses that will not be made up soon; in 2018, a similar low-water year, better months at the end did not repair the damage. A conservation NGO said the rain is temporary relief and does not undo harm to amphibians and water insects in small, moist habitats.

Understanding the Issue: How sending an envoy home differs from tearing up a treaty

People often hear “the ambassador was expelled” and picture a treaty torn in half. That picture mixes three different acts. What follows is the host’s own explanation of the mechanism, not a claim drawn from today’s articles. Under the Vienna rules that most capitals still use, a receiving state may declare a diplomat persona non grata — no longer welcome — and the sending state is expected to withdraw that person; the embassy as an institution can remain, sometimes with a lower-ranking chargé d’affaires. Recalling your own ambassador is the mirror move: you pull your representative without necessarily closing the building or ending trade. A third act, annulling a named defence or commercial agreement, is a legal step of its own and has to say which contract dies. Staff can also be gone for months before an expulsion order is issued, which means the public rupture and the practical absence of diplomats need not fall on the same day. Reading today’s coverage, the useful split is between a political signal — who is allowed to stay in the capital — and a legal inventory of which contracts still bind. Next time an expulsion leads the news, look for a list of cancelled agreements separate from the sentence that sends the envoy home; if that list is missing, the legal relationship has not been described yet.

Media-literacy note

When two institutions count the same crowd, write down both numbers and who produced each before you pick one. A draft climate text is a primary document: the presence or absence of a single phrase is the fact, and a minister’s rejection is a separate fact. Diplomatic quotes travel well as slogans; the checkable piece is which agreements were annulled, and on what date.

Go deeper: Deutsche Welle reports that the Antalya draft still does not mention fossil fuels and that Germany’s environment minister calls it unacceptable; Al Jazeera reports that the new draft would, for the first time, say warming is on course to pass 1.5C.
