Three million in Kyiv could lose heat, water and sanitation under Russia's winter strikes.

By Patrick November 16, 2026 Ep 238 13 min read Listen to podcast Watch on YouTube

Today's lead story (1)

1) Winter strikes hit the systems that keep Ukrainian cities running

What happened (neutral): The New York Times reported that Russia has opened an intense bombing campaign, weeks earlier than expected, against the infrastructure that keeps people alive through winter, and that daily strikes on the energy system have raised fears Kyiv, a city of three million, could become all but uninhabitable in the coming months. American-led talks resumed last week in Abu Dhabi after a nine-month lull, and in those talks Russia set out hard-line demands that stood little chance of acceptance by Ukraine and its European allies.

Context & perspectives: President Volodymyr Zelensky said after a recent attack, "They want to come to the table holding our energy system hostage." The Times reported that this unbending line indicated Moscow had no real interest in discussing peace now, because it believes the winter campaign will strengthen its negotiating position entering the spring. Knocking out power each winter has become Moscow's route to aims it has not achieved in nearly five years of war; cities are in their most precarious position yet because the grid is more fragile after years of strikes and air defenses are dwindling. A meeting on Thursday among President Trump, President Vladimir Putin and China's Xi Jinping in Shenzhen left it unclear whether the three would discuss the war.

Major world stories (3)

2) Ahmad Tibi elected a deputy speaker of Israel's Knesset

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reported that Ahmad Tibi, chairman of the Joint List, an alliance of parties representing Palestinian citizens of Israel, was elected on Monday by fellow members as a deputy speaker of the Knesset, Israel's parliament. Tibi, leader of the Ta'al party inside that alliance, told Al Jazeera the post was "a rightful achievement for the Joint List, which secured 11 seats" in the October 27 elections. Parties representing Palestinian citizens — the Joint List and the United Arab List, known as Ra'am — together hold 19 seats in the 120-member house, and the bloc endorsed Gadi Eisenkot after President Isaac Herzog tasked him this month with forming the next government.

Context & perspectives: Tibi said Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud party and National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir "attempted to prevent my election, but they failed despite their reckless incitement," and that the Joint List will be represented in all committees. On Sunday he called for a criminal investigation into Netanyahu and his aides. Israel's Channel 12 reported that Netanyahu's chief of staff, Ido Norden, contacted the police commissioner's office before the elections, alleging that three Ra'am activists in southern Israel carried large sums of cash and vouchers meant to sway voters; police relied on a former employee of the prime minister's office and arrested the activists after raids without valid search or arrest warrants. Tibi wrote on X, "No one is above the law, not even Netanyahu." He first entered the Knesset in 1999 and was a deputy speaker from 2006 to 2022.

3) Ecuador's president says US troops are not a base

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reported that President Daniel Noboa denied on Sunday that the United States has a military base near the port city of Guayaquil, after voters rejected a referendum that would have allowed foreign military sites. The AFP news agency last month documented an unusual deployment of US aircraft at Taura Air Base, dozens of tents and infrastructure work, identified about 20 US military flights there from June to mid-October, and obtained documents referring to US soldiers.

Context & perspectives: The then-air force commander, General Mauricio Salazar, asked the armed forces in a September letter to clarify whether the deployment was legal, AFP said; he was later removed and reassigned to less senior duties. Quito and Washington have been deepening cooperation against drug trafficking. The Pentagon, its Southern Command and Ecuador's air force did not respond to requests for information when AFP published in October. Human Rights Watch has called the partnership "perilous," arguing that both governments have committed serious human rights violations fueled by framing confrontations with organized crime as a kind of war. Noboa had campaigned to reverse the constitutional ban on foreign bases; Ecuadorans voted against that change in a referendum in November last year.

CONTESTED: The dispute turns on what the tents, flights and documents at an Ecuadoran airfield amount to after voters kept the ban.

Noboa told the outlet Altavoz that US personnel are there only for "temporary activities," sometimes a specific mission or operation: "We work together. It's not an American base. It's an Ecuadoran base." His reason is operational partnership against regional drug trafficking, not a permanent foreign site. He did not address the aircraft AFP described, and he sidestepped a request to visit Taura.

Human Rights Watch's reason for calling the same partnership perilous is the war framing itself: equipment, training, intelligence and direct military support, paired with that framing, have gone along with serious human rights violations by both governments. The watchdog's limit is that it is judging the partnership, not publishing its own count of who is on the base. Senior Ecuadoran officers had already asked for a legal clarification, and the commander who asked was reassigned — a fact that sits beside Noboa's temporary-mission account without settling it.

Questions to consider:

  • Will Quito or Washington publish the orders covering the US flights AFP counted at Taura from June to mid-October?

4) Nigeria's president approves 28,000 more soldiers and 50,000 police

What happened (neutral): AllAfrica, citing Daily Trust, reported that the campaign council of the All Progressives Congress, President Bola Tinubu's party, said he has approved recruitment of 28,000 additional soldiers and 50,000 police officers to tackle insecurity across Nigeria.

Context & perspectives: The announcement is the council's account of a presidential approval, not a published timetable for when those recruits would be trained or deployed.

Economy, science & technology (2)

5) WHO warns drug resistance could kill 39 million in 25 years

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reported that the World Health Organization, the United Nations health agency, warned on Monday that growing antimicrobial resistance — germs surviving the medicines meant to kill them — could cause 39 million deaths over the next 25 years and make routine surgery and childbirth life-threatening. In 2021 it was directly responsible for more than one million deaths and linked to about 4.7 million others. In 2023, one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections reported to the agency was resistant to the antibiotics used to treat it.

Context & perspectives: Jean-Pierre Nyemazi, acting director of the agency's antimicrobial-resistance department, said resistance is "threatening the future of modern medicine," and that unlike COVID-19 these infections "have been taking people's lives for years without us noticing." A review of 183 countries found nearly nine in 10 now have a national action plan, up from about four in 10 in 2017, but Pravasha Prakash, a technical officer, said only 42 countries — less than one in five — had a plan that was financed, implemented and monitored. Sylvia Bertagnolo, head of surveillance, said that without testing, clinicians must treat without knowing the cause or which antibiotic will work. The agency released two tools: one listing tests that should be available from primary care to referral hospitals, and one compiling 73 laboratory procedures from sample collection to identifying the germ and testing which drugs it resists. It named infection prevention, vaccination, water and sanitation, and careful use of these medicines as the main levers, and said progress is still too slow for 2030 targets.

6) Lebanon sets December 14 for commercial flights at a second airport

What happened (neutral): Al Jazeera reported that Lebanon's second airport, Rene Mouawad in the northern town of Qlayaat, is scheduled to begin test flights on Independence Day, November 22, with commercial flights now expected on December 14, when many Lebanese living abroad come home for the winter holiday. Prime Minister Nawaf Salam and other officials took a test flight from Beirut in June. The airport had been aimed at mid-2026; Israel's war delayed it, and construction delays pushed commercial service from November 22 to mid-December.

Context & perspectives: Rafik Hariri International, just south of Beirut, is the country's only functioning airport and has been a political chokepoint: in February 2025, supporters of Hezbollah, the Iranian-backed Shia armed group, blocked the road to the capital when two Iranian planes were barred from landing. Inflation is expected to hit 17.5 percent this year, the World Bank predicts the economy will contract by 6.4 percent in 2026, and previous estimates put damage since 2023 at more than $14 billion. Recovery from the 2019 crash has been held back by Israel's assault and the US-Israel war on Iran.

CONTESTED: Everything turns on whether a small northern runway is an economic project or a political alternative to Beirut's single airport.

Lebanese officials say the reason is economic, not political: growth in one of the poorest, most underserved regions. That case rests on the north's lack of air links and on the holiday traffic the December 14 date is meant to catch.

Bassel Doueik, a political analyst, argues Hezbollah's ability to block the Beirut airport road is a strategic advantage whenever tensions with the government rise, and that Qlayaat decreases that advantage only somewhat because it is too small to replace Hariri. Marcel Baloukji, a political and security analyst, argues the state gains room to maneuver and a fallback if Beirut's airport is shut in a future war, especially for foreign nationals and diplomats, while conceding it does not change much for Hezbollah's arms or internal political calculations. Both analysts accept the airport cannot substitute for Hariri; they differ from the officials on whether that limited backup is still a political fact.

7) A rural Malawi hospital has electricity after a 48-hour promise

What happened (neutral): AllAfrica, citing Nyasa Times, reported that Local Government Minister Ben Phiri said Makanjira Rural Hospital now has electricity, as he had promised within 48 hours, and that he thanked the people who restored the power.

Context & perspectives: He blamed a commissioning mix-up for the outage that prompted the deadline. The report does not say how stable the restored supply is.

Understanding the Issue: How a promise to one ally can empty a promise to all

People often hear a nonaggression pact as extra safety for the country that signs it. Al Jazeera's account of leaked demands, obtained by The New York Times, The Economist and several Ukrainian outlets, shows a different mechanism: Russia wants the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, the alliance that treats an armed attack on one member as an attack on all 32, to pull troops out of Central and Eastern Europe and to stop expanding. Negotiator Alexey Drobinin said agreements could be reached with individual member states, which would then receive additional security guarantees from Moscow. Former Russian diplomat Boris Bondarev, who quit the foreign ministry over the 2022 invasion, told Al Jazeera that a string of such deals would leave each European country face to face with Russia, and that the pacts would most likely bar a signatory from joining a later conflict between Russia and another European nation. That bar is the hinge: collective defense works only if allies may still fight when one of them is attacked. The demands were put to US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, not to European leaders. Maria Kucherenko of Kyiv's Strategic Intelligence Institute told Al Jazeera that Moscow prefers to talk to Washington, keeps demands maximalist, and puts politics ahead of security. Reading a communiqué, check whether any "individual guarantee" still lets that country fight if a neighbor in the same alliance is attacked. Source: aljazeera.com

Media-literacy note

When two outlets describe the same talks, read the demand each one actually quotes before you accept a summary of "hard line" or "breakthrough." A rights group, a campaign council and a ministry are not the same kind of source: the first investigates, the second advocates, the third governs. Primary documents — a court charge sheet, a referendum text, a surveillance table — settle more arguments than a second headline on the same event.
