Three judges split as the Fourth Circuit lets Maryland enforce gambling laws against Kalshi.

What You Need to Know: Three judges split as the Fourth Circuit lets Maryland enforce gambling laws against Kalshi's sports event contracts. Kalshi dropped its own Louisiana case and moved to join the federal government's suit against the state, while Polymarket US moved to list oil and gas up/down contracts. New York's mayor called these markets troubling, and Polymarket's founder called accounts of Donald Trump Jr.'s role overstated.

Top Story

The Fourth Circuit on Tuesday affirmed a Maryland district court's refusal to preliminarily block state officials from enforcing Maryland gambling laws against Kalshi's sports event contracts. The published decision is per curiam and split: Judges Roger Gregory and Stephanie Thacker affirmed, Judge DeAndrea Benjamin dissented, and the only line they share is that the district court's judgment is affirmed. Gregory assumed the contracts were swaps and still found no sign Congress meant to strip Maryland's authority, writing that nothing in the Commodity Exchange Act or the CFTC's regulations "converts agency inaction into a determination carrying sweeping preemptive consequences"; Thacker concluded the contracts are not swaps and wrote, "What is happening here is sports betting." Benjamin's dissent treats them as swaps whose payments depend on an event with economic consequences, so the Act's exclusive-jurisdiction provision should stop Maryland from regulating them. Maryland Attorney General Anthony Brown said Wednesday that the ruling means Kalshi must meet the same consumer-protection standards as every other sportsbook serving Maryland residents. Better Markets legal director Dominick Freda, whose group filed an amicus brief for Maryland and who pointed to similar results in the Sixth and Ninth Circuits, said the judges in the majority "just couldn't agree on which of Kalshi's arguments to avoid state gambling laws was worse," because a bet on who wins Sunday's game is a sports bet. Source: legalsportsreport.com

Polymarket US lists WTI crude and natural gas up/down markets: RG.org

QCX LLC, doing business as Polymarket US, self-certified a WTI crude oil futures-price contract, product code CPWTIF, in a CFTC filing dated November 17 and said it intends to list the product no earlier than November 19, 2026; a November 18 changelog announced WTI and natural gas up/down markets. Windows follow CME Globex hours, from 6:15 p.m. ET Sunday to 5 p.m. ET Friday, and each window settles on the mid price Chainlink's futures data stream publishes at the start and end, from bid and ask quotes on the front-month contract — the changelog says that figure is not a traded price or an exchange settlement price, and Chainlink decides which contract month the stream follows and when it rolls. The filing's example is a one-dollar contract on whether the WTI futures mid will be above $100 at 2:30 p.m. ET on December 8, with a position-accountability level of $25,000; Polymarket US submitted it as a single event contract, said it intends to rely on that filing for a later certification of a class of swaps, and asked the CFTC to keep the product explanation confidential. The same account says Polymarket US recently filed fee tiers with the CFTC that differed from its public fee page, and that it barred Louisiana users from opening new positions this month after a state judge's temporary restraining order. How It Works: Overconfidence: Forecasts That Are Too Sure

Overprecision is an excessive confidence that one knows the truth, and a 2013 paper in Psychological Science measured it without taking a spoken confidence interval at face value. The authors inferred how narrow a person's uncertainty was from how little that person moved a point estimate when the cost of guessing too high differed from the cost of guessing too low.

That cost gap is the worked example, and the paper supplies the direction rather than a numeric table. If overshooting the true value is the more expensive mistake, someone who is genuinely unsure should slide the guess toward the cheaper error. A larger slide means a wider uncertainty. The precision implied by the slide can then be set against how much the person actually knew. People adjusted less than that knowledge justified. Subjective confidence drove the shortfall: they acted as if the right answer sat in a tighter band than their record supported.

A second kind of overconfidence appears when the forecast is a probability piled onto models. The 2020 paper "When are Bayesian model probabilities overconfident?" finds that a posterior over models often concentrates on a single model even when other measures of fit, or of forecasting ability, show no strong preference. A moderate change in the sample can shift that concentration onto another model. The authors derive the sampling variance of the Bayes factor in univariate and multivariate linear regression.

Pooling several forecasts does not, by itself, erase the first problem. A 2012 paper in the Journal of Modelling in Management tested Cooke's classical model, equal-weight linear pooling, and the best-expert approach on a large database of real-world expert judgments, using leave-one-out cross-validation. Both linear-pooling methods significantly outperformed the best expert, so more than one voice helped. Linear pooling still did not effectively counteract expert overconfidence. Dependence cut the other way by method: Cooke's classical model was more sensitive to expert dependence than equal weights, and high dependence generally produced much poorer aggregated results, while equal weights were more robust across dependence levels. The paper also says that broadening subjective intervals or distributions may occasionally mitigate overconfidence, and that the number of experts and the number of seed questions also affect calibration of the aggregated distribution.

Whether "overconfidence" is one trait is disputed in this literature. A 2011 paper in the Journal of Behavioral Decision Making found that miscalibration on an interval-production task correlated across two sets of general-knowledge questions of varying difficulty, given at different times, which is the pattern of a trait. The hard-easy effect split by measure: the more difficult the task, the greater the overprecision, and also the greater the underplacement of one's performance relative to others. Dispositional optimism and self-efficacy showed no relation to that interval miscalibration. Measures of a disposition to positive illusions correlated with one another, did not correlate with the interval-production task, and correlated negatively with optimism about societal risks. In a final study, that same disposition did predict miscalibration when the same questions were scored by a probability-evaluation technique. The authors' conclusion is that overconfidence is not a unitary construct but a series of overlapping ones.

A calibration chart is the picture of the gap those papers are measuring. Stated probability is on one axis; the share of those statements that later came true is on the other. If contracts priced near the top of the scale resolved yes about as often as that price implied, the points would lie near the diagonal. Overprecision is the bend away from that line: high stated chances come true less often than the price suggests, and low stated chances come true more often. None of these papers tests a named prediction-market venue, and none of them identifies a contract as too high or too low. What changes for a listener is how to read a price that has rushed toward certainty. That price is a claim about frequency. It still has to be checked against how often similarly sure prices, on that kind of question, were right — and against whether the people behind the price were independent, or were all updating on the same story.
