Seventeen no-action letters end, putting event-contract venues under one reporting rule.

What You Need to Know: Seventeen staff no-action letters are out, and fully collateralized event contracts now sit under one federal reporting rule. The same Monday brought a Utah trading halt at Kalshi and a new surveillance claim about Nvidia-linked wallets on Polymarket. None of this is a view on which contract is cheap.

Top Story

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission on Monday approved a final rule that puts certain fully collateralized event contracts on the reporting regime in Parts 15 through 18, outside the swap-reporting rules in Parts 43 and 45, replacing staff no-action letters that have exempted such venues from swap reporting since 2017. The rule's preamble says U.S. exchanges listed about five event contracts a year from 2006 through 2020 and certified roughly 1,600 in 2025, while FM Intelligence calculations put combined Kalshi and Polymarket volume at $172.4 billion in the third quarter, up from $98.5 billion in the second. Chairman Michael S. Selig said, "The CFTC will no longer regulate market participants through a patchwork of no-action letters, which serve as band-aids for unworkable regulations," and called the rule "an important step in future-proofing the regulatory framework for event contracts." A position is reportable at 125,000 contracts of one dollar, or $125,000 in notional value, against a default of 25 contracts for other commodities, and exchanges must keep each trader's name, address, email, phone number, occupation and employer, plus the names of anyone holding 10 percent or more of the account, for five years after the contract ends. The definition stays limited to fully collateralized contracts — Kalshi, which in March set out plans to offer margin trading through affiliate Kinetic Markets, argued in its comment letter that margined event contracts share the same features, and the agency declined to widen the definition — with some duties starting 60 days after Federal Register publication, large-trader provisions on the later of that date or July 26, 2027, and trader-identification data after 12 months. The same day, staff in the Division of Market Oversight and the Division of Clearing and Risk said bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins may count toward the 12-month operating-cost reserve, at a minimum haircut of 20 percent on bitcoin and ether and 2 percent on payment stablecoins, but not toward the separate liquidity requirement, because those assets are not cash or highly liquid securities under U.S. accounting rules; the advisory is a staff view, not a rule. The Ecosystem

Kalshi halts prediction market trading in Utah after federal court loss: Crypto News

Kalshi has suspended operations in Utah, so more than 177,000 users can no longer open new positions, after a federal court allowed the state to enforce its gambling laws against the platform. Spokesperson Jacki McGavick told The Salt Lake Tribune on November 23 that the company would keep challenging Utah and intended to return once its legal position was vindicated; existing customers can close positions and withdraw funds, and Kalshi plans to enforce the block with GPS after first using registration data. Utah Attorney General Derek Brown welcomed the decision on Monday, saying gambling is prohibited under the state constitution, and said his office would consult prosecutors on next steps. Kalshi and co-founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara face 14 criminal charges — 12 felony counts related to online gambling promotion, one felony count alleging a pattern of unlawful activity, and one misdemeanor — which remain allegations, not convictions. A New Mexico court has separately restricted Kalshi sports contracts across four tribal territories. At a November 20 hearing, U.S. District Judge Robert J. Shelby said the CFTC, which is seeking to stop Utah from enforcing state gambling restrictions against federally regulated prediction markets, faced a steep hill, and the court had not ruled on that emergency request when Kalshi announced the suspension. 16 Polymarket wallets turn $20,000 into $350,000 before Jensen Huang's shock NVIDIA return: CryptoSlate

Bubblemaps said Monday that 16 Nvidia-only Polymarket accounts wagered about $20,000 and earned roughly $350,000, a return near 1,750 percent, on markets tied to Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia. Eleven accounts were funded on November 23 between 03:49 and 10:44 UTC, after President Donald Trump publicly urged Nvidia to rehire Huang at 02:54 UTC, and each placed a first bet within about six minutes of funding; the last funding cleared about three hours before Nvidia announced the reinstatement at 13:55 UTC, with some purchases as cheap as 1.5 cents a share. Five accounts funded on November 16, between 15:39 and 21:12 UTC, do not fit that public timeline: the first deposit arrived before Trump's support was reported at 16:52 UTC. Bubblemaps reported the operators as unidentified and the on-chain links as unestablished, and Nvidia's SEC filing dates the reinstatement to November 22 while omitting the hour. Yahoo Finance, citing Bubblemaps, said a trader under the name 82727skjwojq took more than $101,000 in profit and held no other active positions when the piece was written. The CFTC's February 25 advisory defines the prohibited conduct as misappropriating material nonpublic information in breach of a duty of trust and confidence; Polymarket says it prohibits insider trading and uses machine-learning systems, blockchain analysis and trade surveillance, and Senator Elizabeth Warren has called for an investigation into such activity on prediction markets. How It Works: Combining Forecasts: Why an Average Often Wins

A combined forecast is a new probability built from several old ones. The usual construction is a weighted average: each forecaster, poll or model gets a weight, and the weights are chosen so that past errors shrink. The long record in this literature is that the optimized mix often loses to the plain one. An equal-weight average of the candidate forecasts is repeatedly more robust than methods that try to beat it. Researchers call that the forecast combination puzzle.

A 2015 paper on arXiv sets out why the puzzle appears. The explanations it examines are estimation error, weighting formulas that do not fit the data, and screening that drops models before the combination is formed. It adds a split the earlier account left out. Combining for adaptation means you do not know which single forecast will win, so you want a mix that stays close to whichever one does. Combining for improvement means you believe a blend can beat every candidate, including the best. Using an improvement method on an adaptation problem, or the reverse, can itself create the puzzle. The paper's proposal is a multi-level AFTER strategy, a sequential weighting scheme that treats the simple average as one more candidate. The procedure can fall back on equal weights when estimated weights are too noisy to help, and it can still up-weight a strong forecast when the data support that.

A 2023 paper, also on arXiv, argues that the puzzle is partly a testing problem, not only a forecasting problem. Combinations are usually built in a way that leaves tests of predictive accuracy with low power and without proper size control. Under the null of no inferior accuracy, the test statistic does not follow the standard normal distribution that researchers typically assume. Its asymptotic distribution is non-standard. A test that is too ready to accept the simple average will keep crowning it. The same paper says that low power can be avoided, when it is feasible, by estimating the combinations more efficiently. It shows the point both for a functional of interest and for predictive densities, and it uses a short example on daily financial returns to show how a researcher can sidestep the puzzle in practice.

Probability forecasts fail in a further way, set out in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society in 2010. Linear pooling, a weighted average of probabilities, is the popular method. Take two or more distinct probability forecasts that are each calibrated, so that events occur at the rate the forecast assigns. Any non-trivial weighted average of those forecasts is necessarily uncalibrated, and it lacks sharpness. The average is pulled inward relative to the sharper input. Recalibration is required even in that ideal case. The authors' tool is a beta-transformed linear opinion pool: a beta curve, fit together with the weights, that can push the pooled probability outward or pull it inward after the linear pool is formed. They illustrate it in a simulation and in a case study of statistical forecasts and National Weather Service probability-of-precipitation forecasts.

A 2021 paper on arXiv takes the practical objection. Dynamic combinations, which change weights as a series moves, can forecast well and still never be used, because they cost too much to run and because a stack of models is hard to explain. Model compression trains one model to imitate the ensemble. In the experiments, the compressed model had comparable predictive performance and a drastic cut in computational cost. The compressed individual model with the best average rank was a rule-based regression model, so the step also made the forecast easier to read. The paper notes that time series make compression harder than the neural-network cases usually studied, because the data keep evolving.

Read a market price as one already-combined forecast, not as a raw observation. Line that price up with a poll, a model and another venue, and an equal-weight average is the combination this research treats as the robust default when the job is adaptation and the sample for estimating weights is thin. Estimated weights earn a place when the job is improvement and the weights can be estimated without spending the gain on estimation error. A straight average of calibrated probabilities is not itself a calibrated probability. It needs a recalibration step, which is what the beta transform is for, before the blend is treated as sharp. That is a way to see what a published probability is — a pool, with a pooling rule, and the rule changes the number — not a method for choosing a contract.
