Two-year GPU rental contracts now let compute buyers hedge on Polymarket.

What You Need to Know: Polymarket has opened cash-settled contracts on Nvidia GPU rental rates running out to December 2028, a public curve for a cost labs, clouds and lenders have been locking in for years. Tickmill is hiring a head of prediction-market product in Europe while ESMA and the UK regulator still treat many event contracts as banned binary options. The CFTC’s final reporting rule now has a staggered compliance clock, the earliest piece of it 60 days after Federal Register publication.

Top Story

Shayne Coplan, Polymarket’s founder and CEO, said this morning the platform has launched an AI compute marketplace that prices on-demand rental rates for Nvidia’s H100, B200 and B300, with terms out to December 2028. Traders buy and sell cash-settled binaries on whether a monthly average rental rate clears a threshold — the write-up’s example is an H100 average above $3 per GPU-hour in December 2026 — and the venue implies a forward curve from those prices, settled to the OCPI index of actual on-demand rentals rather than cloud list prices, available on the Bloomberg Terminal, with no delivery of GPUs. Coplan likened that curve to a yield curve for compute costs: “The entire AI economy is built on these prices, yet until now, there has been no public market to reveal their future trajectory.” Kalshi has listed GPU rental contracts since March 2026, covering the H100, H200, B200, A100 and RTX 5090 against the Ornn index, and in July published market-implied forward curves from those contracts. The Ornn index showed about $3.21 per GPU-hour for the H100, $6.48 for the H200, $9.74 for the B200 and $10.88 for the B300, while Polymarket’s early implied curve put the H100 near $2.14 by the end of 2028, a decline of more than 30 percent, on volume still in the tens of thousands of dollars per threshold and only hundreds of dollars on H200 contracts — a thin market. Source: kucoin.com

The Ecosystem

Tickmill posts a prediction-markets product role: Cryptonews.net

Tickmill has advertised a Head of Product, Prediction Markets, under its European operations, a step toward a product very few retail brokers have launched. The posting does not say whether the rails will be built in-house or plugged into a third-party venue such as Kalshi, but the role is written to work with internal engineering and with external exchanges, market makers, liquidity providers and technology vendors. It also leaves open whether the offer would be aimed at clients in the EU, the UK or other jurisdictions, and Tickmill did not respond to requests for comment. In July, ESMA reminded firms that products marketed as event contracts may fall under the long-standing retail ban on binary options, and may also count as bets under national gambling law or fall under MiCA if offered in tokenized form and not as financial instruments. The UK Financial Conduct Authority’s March 2026 perimeter report said the financial prediction-market products it had seen were binary options and remain under its permanent retail ban, with further work on access possible and no formal rule change or timetable announced. In the United States the CFTC’s position is that event contracts on registered designated contract markets sit in federal derivatives law, while several state regulators and prosecutors have challenged that, especially on sports; Predicted put sports and multi-leg parlay contracts at 83 percent of US notional volume in the third quarter of 2026, with parlays alone at 44 percent. Source: cryptonews.net

CFTC event-contract reporting rule gets a compliance calendar: The National Law Review

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s final rule on data reporting for certain fully collateralized event contracts, Release No. 9330-26, was published November 23, 2026, and the compliance dates are staggered. Sections 16.03(a), (b)(2), (b)(3) and (f) take effect 60 days after publication in the Federal Register; sections 16.03(b)(1), (c), (d) and (e) take effect on the later of 60 days after publication or July 26, 2027; section 16.03(g) takes effect 12 months after publication. The Federal Register date is what starts that clock, and the National Law Review says firms should compare current reporting with the new framework now because the earliest date arrives quickly. Source: natlawreview.com

How It Works: Why Would Anyone Trade? The No-Trade Theorem

A no-trade theorem is a result about information, not about courage. If two people start from the same beliefs, and each knows the other is rational, and each knows that the other knows that, a private signal is a weak reason to bet. The offer itself is news. Once you fold the other side’s willingness to take the opposite side back into your estimate, the gap that made the trade look attractive shrinks. Keep passing that message — I still want to trade, knowing that you still want to trade — and the disagreement that was purely about information can disappear. What is left is not a price. It is a refusal.

A security pays according to some state of nature. Both traders share a prior. One of them sees something private. If rationality is common knowledge, the first trader’s bid tells the second something about that private signal, and the second trader’s answer tells the first something back. Each round is a message, not just a negotiation over cents. The classic claim, as the computational paper restates it, is that trade gets attributed to heterogeneous beliefs: people bet because they do not start from the same place. Under a shared starting point and common knowledge of rationality, information alone is not supposed to produce a mutually acceptable bet.

“Will AI Trade? A Computational Inversion of the No-Trade Theorem” keeps the common beliefs and asks whether limited computation can restart the betting. Agents sit in an unfolding game. Computational power sets how complex a strategy an agent can run. The central finding inverts the textbook picture: a stable no-trade outcome, a Nash equilibrium, is reached only when “almost rational” agents have slightly different computational power. When their power is identical, they may fail to converge. The adjustments they keep making are, the authors argue, a form of trade. If those agents can strategically under-use their compute, the instability gets worse. In Matching Pennies, that option eliminates any chance of equilibrium. On this account a busy quote screen is not proof that someone is simply wrong. It can be proof that two equally powerful reasoners cannot finish the regress of “I know that you know.”

“No Trade Under Verifiable Information” pulls the other lever. It asks what happens when private information does not merely hint at a security’s value but reveals, or verifies, the true payoff. Disagreement then collapses for a plainer reason: one side is no longer holding a clue. That side is holding the answer. The authors say the condition speaks to three settings: insider trading, the link between no-trade results and markets that look illiquid, and trading that uses public blockchains and oracles. An oracle that publishes a checkable outcome is, in this language, a machine for ending a disagreement. Before it speaks, traders can still differ. After it speaks, the theorem’s conditions are closer to being met, and a fresh bet on the same fact has less to stand on.

The two papers dispute the classic result in opposite directions, and neither supplies a count of trades, a dollar volume, or a test on a named prediction market. The computational paper says the no-trade equilibrium is fragile once rationality is bounded, and fragile again if agents may choose to think less. Identical power, not different beliefs, is what keeps the adjustments going. The verifiable-information paper says the classic silence comes back when private information is strong enough to pin down the payoff — an argument that some quiet markets are quiet because someone already knows, not because nobody cares. A large earlier literature, which that paper points to without naming a single decisive rebuttal, has already tried other exits: drop the common prior, drop common knowledge, allow bounded rationality, allow ambiguity aversion. These two abstracts add computation and verification to that list. They do not retire it.

A listener who has the map reads a print as a broken condition, not as a verdict. A trade means something failed the no-trade setup: beliefs that were never shared, a hedge that is not a bet on the fact, unequal computing power, a refusal to use the compute one has, or information that does not yet verify. A silent contract can be the theorem working, especially once an oracle or a resolution source has made the payoff checkable to both sides. None of that says which side of a live contract is the better one. It says the first question sits with the person on the other side of the print: what, other than a shared reading of the same public facts, brought them here? Source: arxiv.org Source: arxiv.org
