A Louisiana judge barred event contracts for ten days after the attorney general sued.

What You Need to Know: Judge Jeffrey Johnson barred named prediction-market operators from offering event contracts to Louisiana residents for ten days after Attorney General Liz Murrill sued. The order is not a final ruling on whether those contracts are illegal gambling. A hearing on a preliminary injunction is set for November 30.

Top Story

Attorney General Liz Murrill sued Kalshi, Polymarket and a wide group of other operators on November 12 in the 21st Judicial District Court for Tangipahoa Parish, and Judge Jeffrey Johnson signed an ex parte temporary restraining order that afternoon at 3:48 p.m. The order is not a judgment that anyone broke state law. It restrains the named defendants from offering, listing, clearing or otherwise facilitating event contracts — including contracts tied to sports, elections and awards — for anyone in Louisiana without a state gaming license, requires geofencing, and is set to expire after ten days unless extended, with a preliminary-injunction hearing at 9 a.m. on November 30. The petition names 31 corporate entities, among them Kalshi and affiliates, QCX LLC doing business as Polymarket US, Robinhood, ForecastEx, Crypto.com-related entities, Coinbase, Gemini, Novig, Underdog, IG Group and ProphetX. Kalshi had sued Louisiana officials in federal court on October 23, QCX sued on October 27, and Kalshi sought its own restraining order in that federal case on November 12; a federal judge had not ruled on that request as of Thursday evening. RG.org, citing The Advocate, reported that as of 7 p.m. Thursday, Kalshi users in Louisiana could still buy event contracts. Kalshi head of litigation Jovy Dedaj wrote on X: "The operation of federally-regulated exchanges is the exclusive province of the federal government. Full stop." Source: cryptotimes.io

Novig places third on a 30-day volume tally: Fortune

Fortune, citing the PM Pulse newsletter and research firm Aldrin, reported that Novig handled $1.8 billion in trading volume on U.S.-regulated exchanges over the past 30 days, behind Kalshi at about $33.3 billion and Polymarket U.S. at $6.3 billion, about $300 million ahead of Crypto.com, with Novig's figure up 80 percent from the prior 30 days. How It Works: Base Rates: Where a Good Forecast Starts

A base rate is the frequency of an outcome in a class of similar past cases, counted before anyone argues about the case now on the table. Using it means starting with that frequency and then adjusting for what is specific. Skipping it — treating the story in front of you as if the class had no history — is base rate neglect.

An AAPG Bulletin paper walks through the miss with a set of real wells. Lundin Petroleum drilled 25 exploration wells on the Norwegian Continental Shelf from 2011 through 2015. The relevant base rates were the historical exploration success rate in that area and the sizes of fields already found there. Against those frequencies, the paper says Lundin explorers consistently and significantly underestimated geological probability of success and overestimated success-case volumes. The mechanism is sequential. First, write down how often similar wells hit and how large similar finds were. Second, write the prospect-specific geology beside that, not instead of it. Third, notice that a portfolio of case-only estimates will not match the historical distribution even if each write-up sounds careful. The author argues explorers ignore those frequencies because the frequencies do not feel like they are about this prospect, and that the miss shrinks when the historical rates are integrated into the prospect evaluation rather than left in a separate file.

A 2024 paper on robust forecast aggregation, posted on arXiv, asks what happens when the people you are combining are themselves neglecting the base rate. Two experts each see a private signal and each state a probability for a binary world state. A parameter lambda, from zero to one, measures how much base-rate information they fold in: zero is complete ignorance of it, one is perfect Bayesian updating. The score is worst-case regret against an omniscient benchmark, the standard taken from Arieli, Babichenko and Smorodinsky in 2018. The result is a V-shape. Regret does not fall in a straight line as experts become better Bayesians. An intermediate lambda, short of one, can produce lower worst-case regret than perfect posteriors. The reason is about the aggregator, not about the world. The aggregator does not know the experts' information structure. Extreme, fully updated probabilities can be harder to combine safely across the worst case the aggregator has to fear. The authors also propose an aggregator meant to keep regret low when lambda itself is unknown, and they say they ran an empirical test of the neglect model. The abstract does not report that test's measurements, so the finding to keep is the V-shape, not a claimed improvement on live markets.

A Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience study separates who resists the miss. On the lawyer-engineer problem, the Cognitive Reflection Test predicted performance on conflict items, where the base rate and the stereotype point opposite ways. Short-term memory predicted the nonconflict items. In the scanner, conflict items brought greater activation in the anterior cingulate cortex even when the person still answered with the stereotype. Higher reflection scores went with greater posterior cingulate activation on those conflict items, a region the authors tie to shifting attention between outside cues and an internal check. That is one classic task, not a trading log.

Reference class forecasting is the project-management version of the same start. A Project Management Journal paper compared it with forecasts built by tracing the future path of the specific project, including methods based on Monte Carlo simulation and earned value management. On real project data, forecasts taken from the actual outcomes of similar past projects performed best for both cost and time.

The dispute is inside that literature, not a claim that base rates never help. The AAPG paper treats neglect as a correctable error. The arXiv result says that, for an aggregator who does not know how the experts are informed, a perfect Bayesian posterior is not automatically the input that minimizes worst-case regret. A listener reading a market price can hold both. The price is a case-level number from whoever is trading. The base rate is the hit rate of the class that case belongs to. If those two are far apart, the useful question is which class the contract actually resolves into, and whether the traders started from that class or from the story. Neither comparison is a reason to take a side. Source: arxiv.org

On the lawyer-engineer problem, the Cognitive Reflection Test tracked performance when the base rate and the stereotype pointed opposite ways. Short-term memory tracked the items where they did not conflict. In the scanner, conflict items lit the anterior cingulate more, even when the person still picked the stereotype. Higher reflection scores lined up with more posterior cingulate activity on those items, which the authors link to shifting attention from the outside cue to an internal check. That is one lab task, not a book of trades. Reference class forecasting is the same start in project work. A Project Management Journal paper set forecasts drawn from how similar projects actually finished against forecasts that tried to trace the path of the project in hand, including Monte Carlo simulation and earned value management. On real project data, the similar-projects method won for both cost and time. The argument inside this stack is not that base rates are optional. The wells paper treats neglect as a fixable mistake. The aggregation paper says a perfect Bayesian number is not automatically the safest input when the combiner is blind to how the experts know what they know. A price on a contract is one case-level number from the people trading it. The base rate is the hit rate of the class that contract belongs to. If those two sit far apart, the useful question is which class the resolution rules actually put the contract in, and whether the traders started there or started from the story. That comparison is a reading habit. It is not a side to take. Before we go, Kalshi's request for a federal restraining order in the Louisiana fight was still without a ruling as of Thursday evening.
