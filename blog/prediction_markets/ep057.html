Ninety million dollars a month in speech contracts is now frozen for Kalshi traders.

By Patrick November 18, 2026 Ep 57 7 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode

What You Need to Know: A monthly run of about ninety million dollars in Kalshi contracts on what President Trump says is paused while the CFTC tests whether the exchange's self-certification was false. Kalshi has until December 11 to answer. Separately, the agency published the agenda for its December 15 roundtable on round-the-clock derivatives trading.

Top Story

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has stayed certification of several Kalshi "mention" contracts and asked the exchange to pause trading in unresolved Trump-related markets, under an order dated November 13 that opens proceedings on whether Kalshi's self-certification was false.

The contracts, self-certified in February 2025 as the TRUMPSAY series, cover words the president might say in November, countries and companies he might mention, and nicknames he might use; the order states, "Given the available supporting evidence, including that Kalshi has not attempted to establish that the Contracts are non-manipulable with respect to the Staff Advisory's guidance, the Commission presumptively believes the certification is false."

Kalshi confirmed Tuesday that the pause began at the end of last week, settled contracts remain settled, the nicknames market still appears on the site, and a separate contract on words Vice President JD Vance might use at Wednesday's New York Times DealBook Summit was still open Tuesday morning.

Kalshi has until December 11 to show the certification is not false, after which the CFTC can lift the stay, allow amended terms, or continue the proceedings; mention contracts had been generating $90 million a month, Finance Magnates reported, citing Reuters.

In July the agency fined former White House teleprompter operator Gabriel Perez $172,539.02 for trading on advance access to speeches, and Briefs Finance reports a three-year trading ban and a penalty above $172,000; Kalshi later took down sports mention markets at the regulator's request and has reported more than 50 law-enforcement referrals in the second and third quarters.

Spokesperson Brooke Nethercott said the stay is because the contracts "may be susceptible to manipulation," consistent with a staff advisory from two months ago, while Kalshi — described by Briefs Finance as the only U.S.-regulated exchange offering mention contracts — has argued that "The words of political leaders like Presidents and Fed chairs cause billions of dollars of movement" in other markets, and spokesperson Dani Lever said the company is complying; a separate CFTC probe into how Kalshi communicated about new products remains open, a spokesperson told CNBC. Source: financemagnates.com Source: briefs.co

The Ecosystem

CFTC releases the December 15 agenda on 24/7 trading: CFTC

The date was already public; what the Commodity Futures Trading Commission added on November 17 is the agenda for a December 15 roundtable, 1 to 4 p.m. Eastern, at its Washington headquarters, on whether derivatives markets can trade 24/7. Attendees are set to discuss overnight and weekend trading, tokenization, margin, infrastructure, staffing needs, and operational readiness. Chairman Michael S. Selig said he wants to hear how 24/7 trading would affect business models so the Commission can tailor its regime, and the meeting is open to the public and will be livestreamed on CFTC.gov. How It Works: Ask the Experts or Ask the Market? The Delphi Method

A Delphi forecast is a managed conversation with a panel, not a price that updates whenever someone trades. Experts are asked, usually for a medium- or long-range estimate, to judge how some phenomenon will evolve. Their answers are subjective. A facilitator turns those answers into something closer to a shared number by aggregating them — descriptive statistics are the example given in the forecasting-software literature — and sends that aggregate back. The experts see the group result, revise, and the next round starts. Rounds continue until the estimates converge on stable points. The point of the loop, as a 2014 account in International Letters of Social and Humanistic Sciences puts it, is to shrink disagreement without directly steering the panel: expert feedback drives the study to a conclusion.

The RAND Corporation developed the method at Santa Monica, California, for long-range forecasting in management science. A later variation, described in Advances in Fuzzy Systems in 2021, keeps that same back-and-forth with the experts and changes only the estimation step, replacing ordinary summaries with triangular fuzzy numbers. The paper illustrates the variation with a numerical example; the account available here does not reprint that example's inputs, so the mechanism is the useful part: communication stays Delphi, the math that folds the answers together does not.

A tourism study published in Sustainability shows what a finished Delphi looks like when the subject is a direction, not a tradable event. Researchers collected judgments from experts in tourism and hospitality about Thailand. The stabilized view was that Thailand could keep strengths that matter to visitors across income groups, and could keep the cultural distinctiveness the authors call "Thai-ness," in a region where tourism development is growing more important. The same panel pointed at repairs: better training programs for people in tourism organizations, a less strict immigration policy, adoption of new information technology, and tourism information that is more current. There is no price path in that result. There is a consensus a ministry or a business can read as a planning brief.

Two further designs show how the panel can be mixed with a machine without becoming a market. A 2020 paper in Information notes that experts often already have time-series software and often do not use it. The authors' fix is a "virtual expert": add, into the same aggregate the humans see, a forecast from the Prophet library, a Facebook tool usable in Python or R. The stated gain is time and cost — fewer hours from data analysts — not a claim that the software outranks the room. A 2013 paper in the Journal of Business Economics and Management goes further into finance. It pairs Evolino recurrent neural networks, fed orthogonal inputs, with a Delphi expert evaluation, and uses that combination in a portfolio decision. A statistical study in the paper is described as demonstrating reliability and describing accuracy, and a trading simulation is used to show what the model can do. Those pages do not hand this show a scoreboard against a prediction market, so no such score is stated here.

That is the practical split. Delphi pays in reputation inside a closed round: you revise because you saw the group, and the facilitator decides when the number is stable enough to publish. A market pays, when it pays in money, for being on the right side of a defined contract, and the number moves whenever a trade prints. Manifold's own description of its mana markets is the near case: prices there are forecasts from traders with reputation at stake, not dollars. An organization that needs a long-range qualitative brief — which strengths to protect, which practices to fix — is doing what the Thailand tourism panel was built to do, and a Delphi loop fits that job because the output is a revised expert consensus rather than a contract that must resolve. An organization that needs a number someone can trade against a dated, checkable event is doing a different job. Neither design, on the evidence in these papers, replaces the other. A listener who has watched a Delphi round should read a market price as a live bid, not as a panel that has been asked twice and then closed.
