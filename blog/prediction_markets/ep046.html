ESPN's new memo bars talent from trading nonpublic sports news, including on Polymarket.

What You Need to Know: ESPN's new memo bars talent from trading nonpublic sports news, including on Polymarket. Front Office Sports reviewed the guidance, circulated last week, and an ESPN spokesperson declined to comment. The language updates 2023 betting rules and does not identify any employee violation.

Top Story

Front Office Sports reported that ESPN's talent department circulated a memo last week, several days before the midterms, and that FOS reviewed it; an ESPN spokesperson declined to comment. The memo says on-air talent may not bet on sports news where their jobs give them nonpublic information, including through prediction markets, and it names Polymarket while not naming Kalshi. Examples in the memo are awards votes, player personnel decisions, and sports media deals. Front Office Sports described the language as an update to guidance distributed in 2023 ahead of ESPN Bet, a product Awful Announcing reports no longer exists; that earlier policy barred using, disclosing, or providing access to nonpublic job information for betting-related purposes, including influencing someone else to bet or passing information to a sportsbook operator. Talent also may not bet on games they are assigned to cover, and insider reporters are barred from wagering on the sports they cover. The report identifies no particular employee violation or disciplinary action, and naming Polymarket marks an example in the memo, not an exemption for other platforms. Source: frontofficesports.com Source: awfulannouncing.com Source: rg.org

How It Works: Why Traders Hold Losing Positions Too Long

The disposition effect is a split in how a position gets closed: winners are sold early, and losers are held. On an event contract, that split is a trader cashing a contract that has moved in their favor while leaving a contract that has moved against them untouched. The quoted price is the price of contracts people are willing to trade. A loser nobody will sell does not revise that quote.

A second frame sits on top of the single position. A Journal of Finance paper finds that the disposition effect for a stock weakens when the portfolio is at a gain, and is large when the portfolio is at a loss. The authors call that pattern the portfolio-driven disposition effect. They find it in four independent settings: U.S. archival data, Chinese archival data, a U.S. experiment, and a Chinese experiment. It survives controls for extreme returns, portfolio rebalancing, tax considerations, and investor heterogeneity. The evidence they offer is that investors keep one mental frame for the stock and another for the portfolio, and that the two frames together generate the pattern.

When the portfolio is at a gain, the tendency to sell the winner and hold the loser weakens. When the portfolio is at a loss, that tendency is large. The contract most in need of a sale, if new evidence has turned against it, is the loser. That is the contract the pattern holds onto, and the hold is largest when the whole book is down. The winner is the contract the pattern releases, and releasing a winner does less to pull the quote toward bad news the holder has not traded.

A Frontiers in Psychology experiment with 193 professional traders complicates the idea that the pattern is always a mistake. The design was within-subject: the same traders decided on mean-reverting securities and on securities that do not mean-revert. Before any intervention, their disposition-effect trades pointed in the direction that aligned with profit maximization. On a mean-reverting security, selling into strength and holding a loser can be the rational trade, because the price is expected to come back. A simple informational intervention that made the disposition effect salient changed the level of the effect on both kinds of security. That intervention improved trader returns when the decisions were on non-mean-reverting securities. It had a negative impact on returns when traders used it for mean-reverting securities. A nudge can be large, and it helps only when the security behaves the way the nudge assumes.

On Xueqiu, a Chinese social-investment platform, a separate paper matches post data to actual trading data. Social-media information significantly reduces the disposition effect, and the reduction runs through negative information. Shown negative information, individual investors adjust positions in a more rational way, in that paper's account. Investment experience, users followed, region, and gender all change how well the information works. That study is not about event contracts. It is about whether the information in front of a holder can loosen the hold.

Set the three findings next to a price someone is reading as a forecast. The Journal of Finance result says a losing book makes the hold on losers larger, so the contracts that would register bad news are the ones least likely to trade. The Frontiers result says that hold is not automatically an error: if the question mean-reverts, keeping the loser can be the profit-maximizing trade, and a warning that pushes a sale can cut returns. The Xueqiu result says negative information is the channel that gets holders to adjust, so a price can sit still until the bad news is in front of the people holding the losing side, not merely until the news exists. A listener who knows that is reading the trades that cleared, minus the losing positions people would not close. On a question that comes back, stillness can be sense. On a question that does not, stillness can be the missing sell. Source: doi.org Source: doi.org Source: arxiv.org
