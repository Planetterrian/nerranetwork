Ohio gives licensees until October 30 to stop serving prediction markets or risk their licenses.

By Patrick October 22, 2026 Ep 30 7 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents

What You Need to Know: Ohio’s casino regulator has told licensed operators and suppliers to stop supporting prediction-market platforms that offer sports event contracts, and to confirm they have stopped by October 30, or face license consequences. The warning follows a passed cease-and-desist deadline for ten platforms and a new federal lawsuit from Polymarket US. Utah, separately, has sued Kalshi and market maker Susquehanna in state court.

Top Story

The Ohio Casino Control Commission, in an October 20 letter, told licensed operators, suppliers, and applicants that providing services to companies offering sports event contracts in Ohio without a state sports gaming license raises suitability concerns and can lead to denial, nonrenewal, suspension, or revocation. Services named in the warning include market making, liquidity provision, integrity monitoring, data security, and other technology support, and the commission ordered licensees to stop that support and confirm compliance by October 30. Interim executive director Andromeda Morrison told the commission the notice follows August 2025 guidance and a Sixth Circuit ruling that let Ohio enforce its gaming laws against Kalshi; cease-and-desist letters went on October 2 to Underdog, Gemini Titan, Coinbase, Prophet X, Novig, Robinhood, Polymarket, Plus500, Moomoo, and Webull, with an October 16 stop date that has passed. Polymarket US then sued Ohio’s attorney general and Casino Control Commission officials in federal court in the Southern District of Ohio, seeking preliminary and permanent injunctions, arguing the Commodity Exchange Act gives the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction and that Ohio’s in-state licensing rules violate the Commerce Clause. The complaint says Ohio has made unlicensed sports gaming a felony and that a license, if available, would limit operations to people physically in the state; it says, “Ohio is attempting to relitigate a debate Congress settled over 50 years ago.” Governor Mike DeWine has told Attorney General Andy Wilson to begin lawsuits against the ten operators who have not complied, telling the Statehouse News Bureau, “The law is very clear. The court decisions have been very clear,” and that the steps “are not self-executing.” Morrison said the commission will vigorously defend Ohio’s sports gaming laws. Source: gamblinginsider.com Source: cdcgaming.com Source: coinmarketcap.com

The Ecosystem

Utah sues Kalshi and Susquehanna over the state gambling ban: Gizmodo

Utah has sued Kalshi in state court over the state’s gambling ban and, according to Gizmodo, wants millions from the firm it calls a “back-alley gambling den” for allegedly running an illegal gambling business. The Sports Geek reports Susquehanna is a defendant in that state-court case. On Yahoo Finance, Scott Melker said this is the first time a prediction-market market maker has also been sued, and that Susquehanna firms were served for making markets in the products and accused of facilitating illegal gambling. Source: gizmodo.com Source: thesportsgeek.com Source: finance.yahoo.com

FanDuel defends VIP videos and discloses a prediction-market trial: Gambling Insider

In an October 20 letter to Senator Richard Blumenthal and Representatives Valerie Foushee and Paul Tonko, FanDuel executive Cory Fox said about thirty Cameo videos featuring athletes or entertainers went to VIP customers over two years, and denied the videos were meant to induce continued gambling. Fox said the VIP program aims “to provide enhanced service and encourage customers who choose to wager to use FanDuel rather than another platform,” and the company did not turn over individual wagering histories, citing customer privacy. Separately, FanDuel said FanDuel Predicts began a ninety-day VIP trial on September 15 for fewer than sixty customers, with enhanced service and benefits, no personalized celebrity videos, and no plans to add them. Source: gamblinginsider.com

CFTC issues rules on the swap definition: JD Supra

JD Supra reports that the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has issued an interim final rule and a proposed rule on the scope of the swap definition, the federal category event-contract venues use when they argue their contracts are swaps. Source: jdsupra.com

How It Works: What Volume Does and Does Not Tell You

A price is a number. Volume is the trail of how that number got there, and the trail is not the same thing as the information behind it. A paper posted on arXiv treats a prediction market as a Bayesian inverse problem: the unknown is a yes-or-no outcome, and the data are a history of market-implied probabilities plus the volumes traded along the way. The observation model sits in log-odds space. Conditional on volume, each price increment is drawn from a latent mixture of trader types. Informed flow, uninformed flow, heavy-tailed microstructure noise, and adversarial or manipulative flow can all produce an increment. The only observables the model requires are price and volume. Volume, in that setup, is a conditioner, not a proof. It changes which mixture you think generated the move. It does not, by itself, say the move was informed.

Start with a prior on the outcome. Observe a path: a sequence of implied probabilities and the volume attached to each step. Convert the probability path into log-odds so increments are additive. At each step, condition on that step’s volume and ask which type-mixture is likeliest to have produced an increment of that size. Update the posterior on the outcome only to the extent the outcome-conditional laws of those increments are separable. The paper’s identifiability criterion is Kullback–Leibler separation between those laws. If the law of increments when the event happens is hard to tell apart from the law when it does not, the history is not informative, however long the path and however large the volume. The paper also states posterior concentration and finite-sample error bounds under regularity assumptions, and it defines stability as how much posterior odds move when the observed price–volume path is perturbed. Realized information gain is the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the posterior from the prior; expected information gain is mutual information between the outcome and the history. Synthetic experiments in the paper are reported as validating the concentration rates and the identifiability thresholds. They are not a field test of any live venue.

A second result, from a Mathematical Finance paper on dynamic trading volume, says why a large volume number can be a property of the market’s plumbing rather than of anyone’s forecast. In a market with finite depth and constant investment opportunities, a large investor with a long horizon and constant relative risk aversion trades a safe asset and a risky asset. Trading volume in that model approximately follows a Gaussian, mean-reverting diffusion, and it increases with depth, with volatility, and with risk aversion. Finite depth also rules out leverage and short sales, because those positions may not stay solvent even if the investor can trade continuously. Read that against the inverse-problem paper and the split is clear. One paper says volume scales with how deep the book is, how much the price jumps around, and how risk-averse the large trader is. The other says that once volume is observed, you still have to separate informed types from uninformed types, noise, and adversarial flow, and that separation can fail.

The failure mode the arXiv paper names is type-composition confounding, or an outcome–nuisance symmetry: the same price–volume path can be produced by different mixes of trader types, so the inverse problem is ill-posed. In that regime, adding more of the same history does not tighten the posterior in a way you can trust. A listener who knows this reads a busy market and a quiet one differently, and does not treat the busy one as automatically better. A large volume says many contracts changed hands, or, in the finite-depth model, that depth, volatility, or risk aversion were high enough to generate a lot of trading. It does not say the traders were informed, that the types were identifiable, or that a small perturbation of the path would have left the implied probability where it is. A thin history can be uninformative because there is little to condition on. A thick history can be uninformative because the increment laws do not separate. The useful question is whether the path distinguishes the outcome, not whether the volume figure is large.
