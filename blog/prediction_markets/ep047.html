Four more firms agreed to halt Ohio sports contracts, cutting where those products can be offered.

What You Need to Know: Four more prediction-market firms have agreed to halt sports contracts in Ohio, narrowing where those products can be offered. Gambling Insider names Robinhood and Coinbase among them. Separately, a trade report puts Polymarket at nearly 90 percent of Canadian prediction-market demand, and another report says markets are boasting 98 percent midterm accuracy while experts advise caution.

Top Story

Gambling Insider reported on November 8, 2026, that Robinhood and Coinbase are among four more prediction-market firms agreeing to halt sports contracts in Ohio. The step, as the outlet describes it, is an agreement by the firms. It is not a court judgment, and it is not a new statute. The products being stopped are sports contracts from those firms, so people in Ohio lose one more route to that contract type from this group. The word “more” in the headline matters: the outlet frames these companies as further firms taking the halt, which means the Ohio sports-contract stop is already wider than Robinhood and Coinbase alone. For anyone trying to tell a filing from a ruling, this item stays on the agreement side of that line. The jurisdiction is Ohio. The date on the report is November 8, 2026. Source: gamblinginsider.com

How It Works: Whose Belief Is the Price? The Debate Over What a Price Averages

A binary event contract pays a fixed sum if a stated event happens and pays nothing if it does not. Scale that promise to one dollar and the trading price is a number between zero and one. The argument over what that number averages is an argument about who is allowed to pull it, and with how much force. It is not an argument about which side of any live contract is cheap.

Picture a room of traders, each holding a private chance. Anyone who thinks the event is more likely than the price wants to buy. Anyone who thinks it is less likely wants to sell. Each trade nudges the price toward the side that just acted. Trading can stop when the people still in the market no longer find it worth moving. Under a strict set of assumptions — traders care only about expected payout, they can buy or sell as much as they like, and everyone with a view can reach the book — that stopping point is a weighted average of the beliefs in the room. The weights are not equal votes. A trader with more money behind a view pulls harder. In that narrow case, a contract at 60 cents is not a poll of heads. It is a money-weighted balance. Reading it as a 60 percent chance is a reading of that balance, not of a show of hands.

The critique is that those assumptions do the work, and they often fail. A risk-averse trader will not stake enough to drag the price all the way to their belief, because a wrong bet hurts more than the expected gain feels worth. The price can then sit away from the average belief even when everyone can see the gap. A budget limit does the same job: a trader who is out of money stops pulling, whatever they think. Access does it too. A book closed to people in a given state or country averages only the beliefs of people allowed to trade. Hedging adds another pull. Someone who loses money if the event happens may buy the contract as insurance, not because they think the event is likely. That purchase lifts the price without lifting their stated chance.

Replies to that critique do not deny the gap. They ask when it is large. One line of argument says that if many small traders can take the other side of a hedger, insurance demand gets absorbed and the price moves back toward people trading the event itself. Another says the gap shrinks when the contract is a tiny slice of each trader’s wealth, because risk aversion bites less on a small stake, and widens when the stake is large relative to what a trader can lose. Neither reply restores a simple average of opinions. Both treat the distance between price and average belief as a fact about constraints — risk, budget, access, and why people are in the market.

Manifold, read at 2026-11-08 11:09 UTC, had a market asking whether, in early 2028, an AI will be able to generate a full high-quality movie from a prompt. Yes was at a 44.9 percent implied probability. The market had 4,759 traders and 8,949 mana of 24-hour volume. It opened on 2023-02-20. Manifold’s own description says it uses play money, mana, not dollars, and that its prices are forecasts from traders with nothing but reputation at stake. Walk that through the averaging question. There is no dollar budget forcing a trader to size a view. The pull comes from a willingness to spend mana and to be seen as wrong. A trader who thinks the chance is far from 44.9 percent can move the print only to the extent the play-money book lets them. The 44.9 percent figure is where that book cleared at the read time. It is not a count of how many of the 4,759 traders would raise a hand for yes, and it is not a dollar-weighted average of beliefs, because no dollars are in the stake.

Set a second Manifold reading beside it, from the same timestamp. A market asking whether, if Trump wins, the United States enters a recession before 2027 as measured by the Sahm rule showed yes at a 6 percent implied probability. It had 223 traders and 7,000 mana of 24-hour volume, and it opened on 2024-03-11. Same venue, same play-money unit, very different participation. The movie question has drawn thousands of traders over years. The recession question has drawn a few hundred. Nothing in the listing calls either book thin, so this show will not attach that label. What the two readings do show is that “the price” on one venue is not one kind of average. A contract with 4,759 traders and a contract with 223 traders are both mana-weighted clearings, and the smaller crowd is a smaller set of beliefs being averaged, even before anyone asks whether those beliefs are careful.

A listener who knows this reads a contract differently. The price is the clearing number: what buyers paid and sellers accepted, on that venue, at that time, in that venue’s unit. On a dollar book it is evidence about the traders who could and did show up, weighted by what they were willing to risk in money. On Manifold it is evidence about traders risking mana and reputation, which the venue itself says is the stake. It is not, by itself, the average opinion of a public, a newsroom, or everyone with a view. A book closed by geography averages a smaller room. A book where only reputation is at stake is a different object from a book where a wrong trade costs dollars. None of that says which side of any contract is cheap. It says what the number is allowed to mean, and it says the meaning changes with the stake.
