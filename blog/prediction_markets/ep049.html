Political markets hit $396 million last week, with Polymarket still taking most of it.

By Patrick November 10, 2026 Ep 49 10 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode

What You Need to Know: Political markets hit $396 million last week, with Polymarket still taking most of it. That lead is a category fact, not a sports fact: Kalshi and Polymarket US both still run mostly on sports. Monday also brought new federal complaints in Missouri and Arkansas, and a still-thin public file on the CFTC's sports-contract rule.

Top Story

Cryptopolitan reported that political prediction markets reached $396 million in the first week of November, up 34.2 percent from the week before, with Polymarket still accounting for most of that volume and still ahead of Kalshi in politics and elections. Polymarket's own volumes expanded 39.4 percent week on week, and political prediction volume has almost tripled since early October, which the report attributes mostly to U.S. midterm trading. On Kalshi, political predictions were about 1.5 percent of volume while sports were 85.2 percent; on Polymarket US, sports were 95.4 percent, while elections betting was about 12 percent of Polymarket volume and its third-largest category. In politics, Polymarket has run ahead of Kalshi by 300 to 900 percent depending on the category. Polymarket open interest reached $762.65 million in early November, and total value locked briefly broke above $400 million at the end of October on DeFi Llama data the piece cites, with DEX volumes about 30 percent higher in recent weeks than in the slower summer months. The same report says a front-end exploit drained user wallets, and that traders still came back for the midterm results, with the platform keeping most of its value locked and its trading volume. Source: cryptopolitan.com

The Ecosystem

Missouri Prediction Market Fight Escalates With Robinhood, Polymarket Suits: Gambling Insider

Robinhood and Polymarket filed separate federal lawsuits on Monday against Missouri officials, the latest complaints after September cease-and-desist letters, seeking to stop the state from enforcing gambling laws against their sports event contracts. QCX LLC, doing business as Polymarket US, filed a 40-page complaint on November 9 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Missouri against Attorney General Catherine Hanaway and five Missouri Gaming Commission members; it alleges Commodity Exchange Act preemption and a Commerce Clause violation from rules that limit online licenses to in-state excursion boats, in-state pro teams, or their operators, require servers in Missouri, and require bettors to be physically present, and those allegations have not been tested in court. Robinhood's complaint follows a November 6 warning from the state's solicitor general that officials intended to seek a court order compelling compliance within three days. Hanaway filed counterclaims the same day against Novig under the federal Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act, the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act, and state public-nuisance law, seeking an injunction on payments connected to sports wagering, restitution, and civil penalties of up to $1,000 per violation. Crypto.com's OG.com and Polymarket also filed federal suits Monday in Arkansas, citing the state's November 4 lawsuit against Kalshi as an imminent enforcement threat. Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin has called Kalshi a "straightforward gambling platform" and "a pocket-sized bookie." Source: gamblinginsider.com Source: rg.org

CFTC's Sports Event Contract Rule Draws Just 11 Comments So Far as Deadline Nears: Gambling Insider

The CFTC's proposed rule to put sports event contracts inside the federal definition of swaps has drawn 11 public comments, with the comment deadline on November 18, and major operators, state regulators, and tribal interests had not yet weighed in. The proposal would explicitly include event contracts on sports, politics, cultural events, and weather as swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act, beside a companion interim final rule that excludes casino-style gambling products, including qualifying state-regulated sportsbook wagers, from that definition. Finalizing the rule would not necessarily settle whether federal law overrides state gambling limits or whether every sports contract can be listed; several petitions, including one from New Jersey, ask the U.S. Supreme Court to intervene. Almost all of the 11 submissions support federal oversight. A Utah trader, writing after Robinhood blocked him from opening new sports, political, and cultural positions, said Utah traders should not lose access to a federally regulated market because a state has redefined, for its own purposes, what a swap is. Another trader wrote that there is no logical reason for a 500-leg combo on a federally regulated exchange, and proposed limiting sports contracts to broader outcomes and raising the minimum age for sports contracts to 21; only one of the 11 submissions explicitly calls for withdrawing the proposal. Source: gamblinginsider.com

Coinbase, Kalshi, Kraken parent back CFTC in legal fight over stock perpetual futures: CoinDesk

After applications from Coinbase, Kalshi, and Kraken parent Payward to list U.S. stock perpetual futures were deemed approved on November 2, those three joined DRW Holdings, Jump Trading, and Paradigm in a letter in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York supporting the CFTC against a suit by the Capital Markets Alliance. The CMA, which the report says is backed by traditional-finance firms including Cboe and Citadel Securities, challenged CFTC approval of open-ended stock futures and argued equity products of that kind belong with the SEC. Perpetual futures in the filing's description have no expiry date, unlike traditional futures. A CFTC spokesperson, emailing CoinDesk when the CMA sued on November 3, called the action troubling and said markets should be governed by law, not litigation campaigns intended to block innovation. Source: coindesk.com Source: cryptonews.net

How It Works: Noise Traders: Why Uninformed Money Matters

In that model, governance improves the liquidity of a company's shares only when the market also contains noise traders the authors describe as semi-informed. How strong the effect is depends on how much those traders take part in transactions. Governance and that participation have what the model calls a synergistic effect on liquidity. Take the semi-informed flow away, and the liquidity channel the authors are tracking does not appear.

The step that matters is the counterparty. A better-informed trader can act on a signal only if someone else will take the other side. If everyone is waiting on the same information, there is no trade, and the governance improvement never shows up as liquidity. The 2020 paper is a model of listed stocks, not a measurement of a prediction-market contract. What it isolates is still readable on a contract: money that is uninformed, or only partly informed, is not only a source of error. In this account it is a condition for anyone else to trade at all.

A 2015 field study in the International Journal of Financial Management looks for that mix in Indian equities. The authors use daily trading data plus intra-day prices, volumes, and trade times at one-day and five-day intervals for the Nifty-Fifty firms listed on both the National Stock Exchange and the Bombay Stock Exchange, and they take market returns from the NSE Nifty and the BSE Sensex. They ask whether variation in returns includes noise-trader risk, and whether informed traders' short-run arbitrage pushes them into long-short positions that hedge it. The paper argues that noise has a systematic component and a firm-specific component, and that both vary over time. Those components include what the authors separate as idiosyncratic shocks and noise shocks. At lag periods, a long-short position across these markets can hedge the fundamental systematic shock and the fundamental firm-specific shock, and may detach the noise shock from them. Once the same stocks are traded on long-short horizons, the long-short return is what exposes the noise that runs across names. Set next to the same exercise two years earlier, the abstract's reported pattern is directional rather than a published coefficient: intra-day returns from the one-day and five-day data impound significant noise; daily returns show high exposure; weekly returns show moderate exposure. In the GARCH models, the conditional volatility of those long-short returns shows that the time-varying idiosyncratic noise is highly persistent when noise traders are present. The study's closing claim is that, during the trading day, prices impound information and noise together.

A 2016 paper in Qualitative Research in Financial Markets pushes on the habit of treating every individual trader as a dumb noise trader. Experienced researchers and practitioners rated the mathematical and verbal reasoning demands of investment tasks used in earlier studies. On that assessment, individual investors can perform a number of complex cognitive actions, especially those that demand higher-order verbal reasoning, and they look more limited on tasks that demand greater mathematical reasoning. The authors add that the math-heavy tasks are the ones considered more relevant to performance. They also limit their own result. The measurement is indirect, so they will not claim a definite score for retail competence; a direct test would be a laboratory experiment, and they say that data was not available for the retail investors in view. They could analyze ten tasks, which limits statistical power and blocks a deeper deductive test. Their practical notes stay on disclosure: illustrate key investor documents mostly in words, tell people what studies of this kind found, and offer online math practice built for individual investors. That is not a trading instruction.

The three papers do not draw one portrait, and forcing one would misread them. The 2020 piece is a model, and its noise trader is semi-informed, not empty of information. The 2015 study finds noise that stays persistent inside the trading day in one equity market during a recovery; it does not size the effect on an event contract. The 2016 authors dispute the undifferentiated "dumb noise trader" label, then mark the narrow base under their own dispute: ten tasks, no direct performance test. A listener can still read a contract price differently. A move can be information, or it can be the other side of an order that was never about the fact on the screen. In the 2020 account, liquidity is partly supplied by that second group. In the 2015 account, the noise need not be gone by the close. In the 2016 assessment, the people in that flow are uneven — more capable on verbal reasoning than on the math that performance is said to require — not uniformly lost. None of this identifies which side of a live contract is the informed side. It says a price is a trade, and a trade needs a counterparty who may not share the reason for being there.

The twenty fifteen study finds noise that stays persistent inside the day in one equity market during a recovery. It does not size that effect on an event contract. The twenty sixteen authors reject a blanket label, then mark how narrow their own rejection is: ten tasks, no direct performance test. What you can still do with a contract price is smaller than a slogan and more useful. A move can be information, or it can be the other side of an order that was never about the fact you are watching. Liquidity, in the twenty twenty account, is partly supplied by that second group. Persistence, in the twenty fifteen account, means the noise need not be gone by the close. The twenty sixteen assessment says the people in that flow are uneven, stronger on verbal reasoning than on the math performance is said to require, not uniformly lost. None of this names which side of a live contract is the informed side. A price is a trade, and a trade needs a counterparty who may not share your reason for being there. Before we go, New Jersey's petition is among those asking the Supreme Court to take the question of whether federal law overrides state gambling limits on these contracts.
