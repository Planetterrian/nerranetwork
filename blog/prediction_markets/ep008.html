Prediction Markets Daily

A dozen jobs against 1.8 million is the casino lobby's case against prediction markets.

What You Need to Know: The American Gaming Association says it is on a "war-time footing" over prediction markets in the United States. President Bill Miller made that case at the G2E conference in Las Vegas, and the chief executives of MGM, Caesars and Wynn said the markets should be state-licensed. The fight he described is about state gambling law, tribal sovereignty, taxes and who may buy an event contract.

Top Story

At the G2E conference in Las Vegas, American Gaming Association president Bill Miller said the U.S. casino trade group is on a "war-time footing" over the rise of prediction markets. He said the markets are "trying to prop themselves up," ignore state laws, tax systems and tribal casino sovereignty, and that regulated operators "come in through the front door" while "well-funded bad guys" have "kicked in the back door." The CEOs of MGM, Caesars and Wynn, whom TheLines.com described as Nevada's largest gambling operators, backed him and said prediction markets should be state-licensed. Miller said regulated U.S. gaming "supports 1.8 million [American] jobs," against "maybe a dozen jobs" from what he called the "cashmere hoodie bros in Brooklyn." TheLines.com reported his further case that the markets stretch the Commodity Exchange Act past its original intent, frame gambling as investing, and can be bought at 18 because they are regulated federally as derivatives, against 21 at many U.S. online betting sites. Source: thelines.com

The Ecosystem

Kalshi files to end a volume incentive program: The Defiant

Kalshi has filed to end its volume incentive program, The Defiant reports, a filing rather than a completed shutdown. The Block reports the move amid wash trading allegations and says monthly volume reached $52.98 billion in September as of September 29, an all-time high, with September data still incomplete. Source: thedefiant.io

CFTC sends an event-contracts proposal to the White House: Law360

Law360 reported on September 30, 2026, that the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has sent an event-contracts swaps proposal to the White House. That step is a proposal under review, not a final rule. Source: law360.com

Forecasters map an AI IPO philanthropy windfall: Metaculus

In a September 29 post, Metaculus said its forecasters expect more than $100 billion in pledges tied to Anthropic and OpenAI IPO wealth, but only about $26 billion of that to reach charities by 2031. The same post cited outside figures from @nanransohoff of $37 billion or more a year, from @georgecaweiner of $12 billion to $32 billion to donor-advised funds, and from @cogcelia of $37.9 billion from seven Anthropic co-founders. Metaculus said it reached its own figures with a visual mapping tool called Radiant. Those are forecaster estimates, not a traded contract price. Source: x.com

Tiger Global and Dragoneer are in talks on Kalshi's raise: Reuters

Reuters reported on September 29, citing sources, that Tiger Global and Dragoneer are in talks to invest in Kalshi's new $1 billion fundraise. Talks are not a closed investment. Source: reuters.com

NCPG director resigns as a Kalshi partnership is reviewed: Gambling News

Gambling News reports that the NCPG director has resigned as a Kalshi partnership is under review. Source: gamblingnews.com

Polymarket listed a new contract on September 30 asking whether Jeff Bezos will go to space before 2028, a named-person spaceflight question rather than an election or a game. The listing post does not carry a price. Source: x.com

How It Works: The Law: Event Contracts, Regulators and the Courts

An event contract is a promise with a price. The buyer pays now. If the stated event happens, the contract pays a fixed sum. If it does not, the contract pays nothing. In the United States, that shape is what pulls the instrument toward the Commodity Exchange Act and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. State authorities have treated the same instrument as gambling. Which label wins is not settled.

A 2026 Note in the Boston College Law Review says political prediction markets have existed in limited formats since 1988 and returned to public view in the 2024 elections, when platforms such as Kalshi offered trades on United States presidential and congressional races. The Note asks whether those trades are legal. It records that the CFTC said no and a federal district court said yes. Examining the statutory source of the agency's power to review trades based on the outcome of events, sometimes called event contracts, the Note concludes that the law is contradictory and unclear. It also concludes that political prediction markets benefit the public, and it recommends new Commodity Exchange Act language that would narrow the CFTC's authority to review event contracts and clarify the public-interest standard used in that review. That recommendation is the Note's. It is not an enacted amendment.

The same definitional problem appears under other statutes. A 2015 article in the Victoria University of Wellington Law Review examines New Zealand's Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, which it calls the most substantial overhaul of that country's securities law in recent history, and asks how the Act treats the prediction market iPredict. Its conclusion is narrow: iPredict can operate under the Act only if the Financial Markets Authority declares that its contracts are derivatives and grants substantial exemptions from regulatory compliance. The author then recommends a more coherent approach by analogy with the Act's prescribed intermediary service licences.

For someone reading a market, the label decides the pipe the contract travels through: who may list it, who may be shut out, and which public-interest test a regulator applies. The Boston College Note's finding is that, on political event contracts in the United States, that test still sits in contradictory statute. What is unsettled should be read as unsettled.
