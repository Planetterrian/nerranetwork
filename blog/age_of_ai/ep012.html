The Age of AI · Episode 12

Thor Hesselberg

A recruitment founder counters AI deepfakes flooding hiring pipelines by building portable verified credentials that replace self-reported CVs with employer-confirmed trust scores.

In their own words

WorkID talk

What you need to know

Thor Hesselberg described how, in one remote tech role, half of the eight shortlisted candidates from a pool of fifty turned out to be fabricated personas who appeared on video calls as avatars from different nationalities than claimed. That breakdown of trust at Decca People led him to build WorkID, which replaces self-reported CVs with portable profiles backed by direct employer confirmations and background probability checks. The result shifts screening from proving identity to assessing actual fit.

What we talked about
  • Thor noted that “humans are not perfect” and that perfectly aligned resumes with no gaps or inconsistencies often flagged synthetic applications during initial ATS review.
  • Recently established LinkedIn profiles that still appeared polished prompted deeper creation-date checks once the “too good to be true” instinct appeared.
  • Verification is moved to the front of the pipeline so that only candidates whose claims can be confirmed reach screening calls, reducing fake profiles without cutting overall volume.
  • Trust scores are presented in tiers rather than a single percentage; scores above 80 signal a candidate worth prioritizing when paired with skill-match data.
  • Every human confirmation is run through background algorithms that examine IP address, time of day, and open-source corroboration before the score is updated.
  • The system also verifies companies and their representatives because fake employers have begun posting roles to harvest candidate data.
  • Hiring cycles at client companies shortened from an average of six weeks with six-to-eight candidates to roughly two weeks with three candidates once early trust was established.

Who makes this

Patrick Novak

Patrick Novak created the Nerra Network and runs it from Vancouver. He chooses what the shows cover, writes the instructions they are made from, and listens to what comes out. No episode of anything here carries an ad or a sponsor.

On this show he is also the editor. He reads every application himself and decides whether an interview happens, and he reviews the finished episode before it is assembled — a gate with no timer, so nothing has ever published because a review ran late. Mira conducts every interview; when a guest has asked for him he joins as co-host, and either way he never answers for the guest afterwards: the guest reads their own transcript and decides whether it ships.

Mira is his design. He writes and tunes the instructions she works from — how she opens, what she chases, when to stop talking — sets the editorial rules she is held to, and listens to what she did afterwards to decide what changes next time. No model is retrained; the work is the brief she is given and the judgement about what to change in it.
