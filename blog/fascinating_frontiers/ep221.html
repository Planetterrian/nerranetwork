Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. APOD Captures Desert Stairway Under Milky Way — NASA Breaking

    A wide-angle composite shows a stairway sculpture in Chile's Atacama Desert with the Milky Way rising behind it. The image includes the dark lane of the Milky Way, a band of zodiacal light containing Spica, green and red airglow near the horizon, and the Small Magellanic Cloud at the top right. The photo also frames the Andes Mountains, Licancabur volcano, and lights from San Pedro de Atacama on the horizon. It was captured in July 2025 to align the staircase with the near-vertical Milky Way band. The featured image is credited to photographer Julien Looten and appears as the Astronomy Picture of the Day for October 13, 2026. The composition highlights how the sculpture sits just in front of the Milky Way's dense band while zodiacal light extends upward on the left side. Source: science.nasa.gov

  2. Primordial Helium Matches Big Bang Predictions — Phys.org Space

    Astronomers distinguish primordial helium formed in the first minutes after the Big Bang from helium later produced in stars. The early universe held roughly three parts hydrogen to one part helium by mass, with all heavier elements created later through stellar fusion or supernovae. These heavier elements remain so scarce that astronomers still classify them collectively as metals. New observations of the primordial fraction continue to align with standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis calculations. The measurements come from spectral lines in the oldest gas clouds that preserve the original abundance ratios. Distinguishing the two helium sources requires careful accounting of stellar contributions over cosmic time. Source: phys.org

  3. Dying Sun May Disrupt Outer Planets Sooner — Phys.org Space

    New models show the Sun's mass loss could destabilize some outer planets on a timescale of roughly one billion years rather than a quintillion. The change shortens earlier estimates by many orders of magnitude for the most distant worlds. Researchers published the revised timeline in The Astrophysical Journal Letters after recalculating orbital evolution during the Sun's red-giant phase. The simulations track how gradual mass shedding weakens gravitational binding for planets at large distances. Small perturbations that once took immense periods to grow now produce instability on far shorter cosmic timescales. The work focuses specifically on the outer solar system where effects accumulate most noticeably. Source: phys.org

  4. Moon Dust Could Charge Astronauts Like Capacitors — Space.com

    NASA is seeking ideas to prevent static electricity from building up on spacesuits and equipment during lunar surface operations. The Moon's dry, dusty environment allows charge to accumulate on astronauts, who could then discharge into spacecraft or tools. The agency highlighted the risk in a call for mitigation concepts ahead of planned Artemis landings. Dry regolith readily transfers charge in the absence of atmospheric moisture. Astronauts moving across the surface could act as walking capacitors capable of delivering unexpected shocks to hardware. The request for proposals targets both suit materials and operational procedures to reduce the hazard. Source: space.com

  5. JPL Opens Doors for Rare Public Tours — Pasadena Star News

    NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory is offering limited public tours on select dates this month. Visitors will see mission control rooms and spacecraft assembly areas normally restricted to staff and invited guests. The laboratory is marking the openings as a special outreach effort. The tours provide direct views of ongoing mission operations and hardware integration work. Access remains tightly controlled even during the open periods. The initiative aims to connect the public with the laboratory's current planetary and astrophysics projects. Source: pasadenastarnews.com

  6. Gujarat Science City Runs Rocket Workshops for Students — Ahmedabad Mirror

    Science City is hosting World Space Week activities centered on model rocketry, telescope building, and talks about satellite uses in daily life. Between one thousand and twelve hundred students are expected to take part in the hands-on sessions. The program includes a Scientist Circle discussion on how space technology supports communication, weather forecasting, and navigation. Workshops cover rocket design principles and propulsion fundamentals. A separate activity lets participants build simple telescopes. The events run through the week with an emphasis on practical demonstrations of space applications. Source: ahmedabadmirror.com

  7. Bengaluru Marks 70 Years Since Sputnik With Space History Reflections — Hindustan Times

    The city, headquarters of India's space program since the 1970s, is noting the anniversary of the world's first satellite launch. The article traces Indian space research from early ionosphere studies in the 1920s through the founding of key institutes by Homi Bhabha and Vikram Sarabhai. It also recalls the 1967 Outer Space Treaty that entered into force during the same October window now celebrated as World Space Week. SK Mitra conducted pioneering ionosphere experiments in the 1920s. Meghnad Saha and CV Raman advanced related principles in the 1930s. Bhabha established the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in 1945 while Sarabhai founded the Physical Research Laboratory in 1947. Source: hindustantimes.com

Cosmic Spotlight

The revised solar-mass-loss models show outer planets could face orbital instability far earlier than prior quintillion-year estimates. The key change comes from updated calculations of how the Sun's gradual shedding of mass alters gravitational binding over time. Researchers ran the new simulations specifically for the most distant members of the system, where small perturbations grow over long periods. The work appears in The Astrophysical Journal Letters and focuses on the red-giant phase when mass loss accelerates. One open question is whether similar mass-loss effects could unbind planets around other stars at different evolutionary stages. The models also highlight how sensitive distant orbits remain to even modest changes in central mass.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How the Early Universe Locked In Its Hydrogen-Helium Ratio

Picture a volume the size of a large city block at one second after the Big Bang; that single block already contained enough energy to form every atom now in the observable universe. In the next two minutes the temperature fell from billions of degrees to roughly one billion, allowing protons and neutrons to fuse into the first nuclei before the universe cooled too far for further reactions. Only about one helium nucleus formed for every twelve hydrogen nuclei, fixing the 75-to-25 percent mix we still measure today. That ratio depends on the exact expansion rate and the number of neutrino species present in those first moments. We can still detect the imprint through the relative strengths of spectral lines in the oldest gas clouds, yet no one has explained why the measured value sits so precisely on the theoretical curve rather than deviating by even a few percent. The mismatch, if it appears in future data, would force a revision of the standard model itself. Observations continue to test whether additional physics operated during those first minutes.

Space continues to deliver precise new measurements that refine our picture of both the universe's beginning and its eventual end.
