Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Asteroid Encounters Draw Attention This Weekend — Ars Technica

    2. Two separate asteroid flybys occurred over the weekend, each passing within a few lunar distances of Earth. Ground-based observatories tracked the objects to refine their orbits and physical properties. The events highlight the growing catalog of near-Earth asteroids monitored for potential future encounters. Observers used both radar and optical telescopes to gather data during the close approaches. Future campaigns will focus on similar passes expected later this year.

  2. Chandra Marks 250 Years of America With Color Images — NASA

    3. Four new composite views combine Chandra X-ray data with optical and infrared observations to produce red, white, and blue cosmic scenes. Three of the images include sonified versions that translate X-ray brightness into audible tones. The release pairs with broader NASA efforts linking Apollo heritage to the Artemis program. Viewers can explore the full set on the Chandra mission site. Additional data products will appear in coming weeks.

  3. Falcon 9 Completes Transporter-17 With 81 Payloads — Spaceflight Now

    4. The rocket lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base carrying dozens of small satellites into Sun-synchronous orbit. Deployment lasted roughly two and a half hours and included fire-detection sensors, military technology demonstrators, and 3D printers. The first stage landed on a droneship in the Pacific after separation. Several payloads remain unclaimed by public owners at this time. Follow-on missions in the Transporter series are already manifested.

  4. Parker Probe Links Dust Grains to Coronal Heating — Space.com

    5. Charged dust particles riding magnetic plasma waves have been detected in the Sun’s outer atmosphere. The observations come from NASA’s Parker Solar Probe during multiple close approaches. The grains appear to interact with waves that may transfer energy into the corona, helping explain why temperatures there reach millions of degrees. Researchers are now modeling how the dust-wave coupling scales with distance from the Sun. Additional passes will test whether the mechanism holds at even lower altitudes.

  5. Isar Aerospace Secures Canadian Launch Site — SpaceNews

    6. The German company signed an agreement to develop a launch pad for its Spectrum rocket on Canadian territory. The deal is tied to a separate submarine contract between Canada and Germany. Spectrum is designed as a small-lift vehicle capable of serving both commercial and government customers. Site preparation is expected to begin once regulatory approvals are complete. The facility would mark Isar’s first North American operational base.

  6. Moon, Saturn, and Neptune Visible Before Dawn — Astronomy Magazine

    7. The last-quarter Moon passes roughly seven degrees north of Saturn in the early-morning sky. Neptune lies nearby and can be spotted with binoculars under dark conditions. Observers are also encouraged to look for the Coma Star Cluster in the same region. The geometry changes quickly each morning as the Moon continues its orbit. Charts and timing details appear in the magazine’s weekly observing column.

  7. MTG-I2 Weather Satellite Begins Launch Campaign — ESA

    8. The second Meteosat Third Generation Imager satellite, weighing 3,800 kilograms, is undergoing final checks ahead of an Ariane 6 flight. Once in geostationary orbit it will deliver higher-resolution imagery and faster updates for European and North African forecasts. Journalists may apply by 16 July to attend the launch in French Guiana. The satellite’s infrared channels will improve detection of rapidly developing storms. Data validation will continue for several months after arrival on station.

  8. NASA Opens Year-Long Moon and Mars Habitat Simulation — Times of India

    9. Applications are now open for crews willing to live inside a closed habitat for twelve months. The study will test medical monitoring, food systems, and psychological support protocols. Selected participants will simulate surface operations without leaving the facility. Results are intended to inform both Artemis lunar stays and eventual Mars missions. Deadlines and eligibility criteria are posted on the agency’s human research website.

  9. Interstellar Comet Offers Clues to Cosmic Noon — Phys.org

    10. Comet 3I/Atlas, the third confirmed interstellar object, has been observed in detail by multiple NASA facilities. Its composition and trajectory provide a snapshot of material from another star system formed during the universe’s peak star-formation era. Spectra show unusual ratios of certain volatiles compared with Solar System comets. Continued tracking will reveal how the object’s surface evolves as it exits the inner solar system. Archival searches are underway for additional interstellar visitors.

  10. Retractable Tunnels Proposed for Mars Surface Ops — Universe Today

    11. A University of Michigan team designed pressurized, actuated tunnels that would link habitats to rovers and science stations. The concept was submitted to NASA’s 2026 Moon to Mars academic challenge. Once deployed, the tunnels would eliminate the need for repeated suit-up cycles during surface traverses. Prototypes are being tested in analog environments on Earth. The team will present further refinements at upcoming NASA workshops.

  11. Double-Ring Galaxy NGC 7098 Captured in Detail — Astronomy Magazine

    12. The barred spiral lies about 100 million light-years away in Octans and displays a bright inner ring surrounded by a fainter outer ring. High-resolution imaging from Chile reveals tightly wound spiral arms wrapped around the central bar. The structure is thought to result from dynamical resonances within the galaxy’s disk. Amateur and professional observers continue to monitor the system for transient events. The image appears in the magazine’s current picture-of-the-day series.

  12. Canadian Astronaut Jeremy Hansen Announces Retirement — CBC

    13. Hansen, who flew aboard Artemis II, is stepping down from active duty at the Canadian Space Agency. His departure follows a career that included training for lunar missions and public outreach across Canada. The agency has not yet named a replacement for his flight-assigned role. Hansen’s experience will remain available through advisory positions. Future Canadian astronaut selections are expected later this decade.

  13. ISS Crew Photographs 2026 World Cup Host Cities — NASA

    14. Astronauts captured nighttime images of several stadiums and urban areas preparing for the upcoming tournament. The views from 250 miles up show city lights and infrastructure changes visible from orbit. The photo series continues a long tradition of documenting major sporting events from the station. Images are being released through NASA’s Earth Observatory. Additional passes over host regions are planned through the summer.

  14. Cartilage and Blood-Pressure Studies Begin on Station — NASA

    15. Expedition 74 crew members started experiments examining cartilage tissue engineering and cardiovascular responses in microgravity. The work uses specialized incubators and monitoring equipment already aboard the outpost. Parallel maintenance tasks on spacesuits and cargo transfers continued throughout the day. Data from these investigations will feed into long-term health protocols for future missions. Weekly science summaries are posted on the NASA space station blog.

  15. Micro-Sculptures of Indian Scientists Head to Orbit — The Hindu

    16. Tiny carvings of legendary researchers have been placed inside a needle’s eye and will fly aboard the Vikram-1 rocket. The payload demonstrates precision manufacturing techniques suitable for future space artifacts. The launch is scheduled as part of India’s growing small-satellite manifest. Post-flight analysis will assess material durability in the space environment. Similar cultural payloads are under consideration for subsequent missions.

Cosmic Spotlight

Parker Solar Probe measurements show charged dust grains being carried on magnetic plasma waves inside the Sun’s corona. The interaction appears to channel energy into the surrounding plasma, offering one concrete pathway that could explain temperatures millions of degrees hotter than the visible surface. Wave amplitudes and grain sizes were recorded during the spacecraft’s closest approaches, allowing direct comparison with models. Follow-up passes at even lower perihelia will test whether the same coupling persists closer to the Sun. How much of the corona’s excess heat can ultimately be traced to this dust-wave process remains the open question.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Dust Grains Survive in the Solar Corona

If the Sun were scaled to the size of a basketball sitting in downtown Toronto, its corona would extend outward past the CN Tower yet remain almost completely invisible to the eye. Dust grains roughly a micrometer across somehow persist inside this million-degree plasma long enough to be observed by Parker Solar Probe. The grains are swept along by Alfvén waves whose magnetic tension acts like an invisible rail, shielding the particles from immediate vaporization. At 6.9 solar radii the probe recorded wave speeds near 200 km/s while the grains themselves moved at a few tens of km/s relative to the wave crests. The exact balance between radiative cooling, collisional heating, and magnetic confinement is still unknown; models cannot yet predict how far inward the grains can travel before they are destroyed. Solving that limit would tell us whether the same mechanism operates in the atmospheres of other stars.

New data keep arriving from every corner of the solar system.
