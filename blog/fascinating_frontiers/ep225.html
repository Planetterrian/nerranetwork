Astronomer leads global team on NASA mission to early universe — Phys.org Space

A University of Missouri astronomer heads a team of 130 researchers from 62 institutions, including NASA, Caltech, Princeton and Harvard. The mission aims to capture an unprecedented view of the early universe. The effort combines expertise across multiple universities and agencies to push observations of cosmic dawn further than prior surveys. The project draws on resources from the California Institute of Technology, Princeton University and Harvard University alongside NASA centers. No specific launch date or instrument name appears in the announcement, yet the scale of the collaboration marks a new level of coordination for early-universe studies. The team will coordinate observations and data analysis across dozens of sites to build a unified picture of the first galaxies and their environments. Source: phys.org

SpaceX Crew-12 commander reflects on seeing Earth from space — Space.com RSS

The commander of SpaceX's Crew-12 mission described how viewing Earth from orbit changes perspective on its fragility. The astronauts expressed a wish that more people could experience the view of our planet as a small outpost of life. The comments came after their recent return from the International Space Station. The mission placed four crew members aboard the station for an extended period before splashdown. Their reflections highlight the personal impact of seeing the planet's thin atmosphere and limited habitable zones from above. The crew noted that the experience underscores how isolated and delicate the biosphere appears against the backdrop of space. Source: space.com

NASA's Hubble spies spiral's edge — Phys.org Space

Hubble captured an edge-on view of the intermediate spiral galaxy NGC 1827, located roughly 37 million light-years away in Columba. The image shows bright-blue star-forming regions along the disk and dark dust lanes. This observation is part of a survey targeting 260 satellite galaxies around 22 Milky Way-like hosts within 32 million light-years, with 111 already observed and 149 orbits remaining. The Advanced Camera for Surveys recorded the data, revealing a foreground star with diffraction spikes between Earth and the galaxy. Low-mass satellite galaxies in the program help test cosmological models on small scales where predictions have historically struggled. The remaining targets will require additional Hubble time to complete the full sample of faint companions. Source: phys.org

Blue Origin committing big to New Glenn with four pads and 150 upper stages/year — Ars Technica

Blue Origin is investing in four launch pads, three landing ships, and capacity for up to 150 upper stages per year. The scale of the commitment targets high launch volume for the New Glenn rocket. The moves mark a shift toward sustained operations beyond earlier development phases. The company has also added three dedicated landing ships to support rapid reuse. These infrastructure choices position New Glenn for frequent missions once the vehicle reaches operational cadence. The investment includes ground systems sized for multiple simultaneous vehicle flows and recovery operations. Source: arstechnica.com

In the Search for Exoplanets, Some Failures Are A Success — Universe Today

Astronomers used archival data plus new radial velocity measurements to search for planets around the nearby binary star 70 Oph AB. No planets were found, but the work ruled out Jupiter-mass worlds at certain distances. The precise limits leave room for smaller Earth-sized planets and will guide deeper searches with future instruments. Decades of existing observations were combined with fresh measurements to tighten the constraints. The non-detection therefore narrows the parameter space that next-generation telescopes must examine. The combined dataset improves the precision of orbital solutions for the binary pair itself. Source: universetoday.com

A distant supercluster of galaxies points to self-interacting dark matter — Phys.org Space

Seven billion light-years away, the cluster nicknamed El Gordo contains two colliding regions with a combined mass exceeding two quadrillion solar masses. Its size is unusually large for that early epoch. The finding raises questions about how such massive structures assembled so quickly after the Big Bang. Gravitational lensing of background galaxies supplied the mass measurement. The ongoing merger between the two cores offers a rare laboratory for testing whether dark matter particles interact with one another. The lensing maps also reveal how the visible galaxies are distributed relative to the total mass peak. Source: phys.org

New 3D model reveals how shadows reshape chemistry in stellar nurseries — Phys.org Space

An international team led by Dr Brandt Gaches from the University of Duisburg-Essen built a high-resolution 3D model of stellar nurseries. The model demonstrates how the uneven structure of these clouds creates shadows that alter local chemistry and change the infrared emission seen by the James Webb Space Telescope. The work appears in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics. Shadows cast by dense filaments shift the balance of molecules that JWST detects, offering a new way to interpret observations of star-forming regions. The simulation captures both the geometry of the clouds and the resulting chemical gradients at scales previously averaged out in simpler models. The team compared the synthetic observations directly against real JWST spectra to validate the shadow-driven chemistry. Source: phys.org

Plants shift gene activity within minutes of entering spaceflight — Phys.org Space

A University of Florida study captured the earliest stages of biological adaptation in plants during a commercial suborbital mission. Published in the journal npj Microgravity, the experiment flew aboard Blue Origin's New Shepard NS-26 flight on August 29, 2024. Plants begin responding to spaceflight almost immediately after leaving Earth. Gene activity changed within minutes of microgravity exposure, revealing rapid molecular adjustments. The data provide the first detailed timeline of how plant cells sense and react to the transition from Earth gravity to weightlessness. The experiment tracked multiple gene pathways simultaneously to identify which ones activate first in the new environment. Source: phys.org

The search around 70 Oph AB shows how non-detections still tighten constraints on possible planets. By combining decades of radial velocity data with fresh observations, the team excluded large companions at key separations while leaving smaller worlds viable. Those tighter limits now focus future telescope time on the systems most likely to host Earth-sized planets. The work demonstrates that ruling out certain masses at certain distances is itself a measurable advance in the catalog of nearby stars. Future instruments can now prioritize targets where Earth-mass planets remain possible rather than scanning broadly. The refined orbital parameters for the binary also reduce uncertainty in any future planet searches around similar systems.

Cosmic Deep Dive: Self-Interacting Dark Matter

Picture a mass equal to two quadrillion Suns squeezed into a region whose diameter matches the distance between New York and Chicago repeated only a few times; that is the scale of the El Gordo supercluster observed when the universe was less than half its current age. The object sits seven billion light-years away and consists of two dense cores still colliding, an event whose violence should have scattered ordinary matter long ago unless an unseen component supplies extra gravitational cohesion.

If dark matter particles passed through one another without exchanging momentum, the cores would have separated and the structure would have dispersed. Instead the observed distribution stays tightly bound, implying the particles do interact during close encounters. That interaction slows the cores' separation and lets gravity pull in additional material faster than standard cold-dark-matter simulations allow. The result is a giant cluster forming earlier than most models predict for that cosmic epoch.

Astronomers measured the two-quadrillion-solar-mass total by mapping how light from more distant galaxies bends around the cluster, a gravitational-lensing signal strong enough to trace the full mass distribution directly. The same maps show the dark-matter cores offset from the visible galaxies in a pattern that matches computer runs only when a small self-interaction cross-section is included. The offset distance and the persistence of the bound cores together constrain how strongly the particles must scatter.

The precise strength of that interaction remains unknown, and no laboratory detector has yet registered a particle that behaves this way at cosmic densities. The open question is whether the interaction strength required to explain El Gordo also reproduces every other massive cluster observed at similar redshifts, or whether this object is an outlier that will force a revision of how quickly the first giant structures could form after the Big Bang. Resolving that question will require both deeper lensing maps of additional high-redshift clusters and refined simulations that track particle scattering across cosmic time.

New observations continue to refine our picture of both nearby worlds and the distant universe.

Picture that pile of mass packed into a span like the distance from New York to Chicago, repeated only a few times. The observation catches the universe at less than half the age it has now. Two dense cores are still slamming together, and that kind of collision should have flung ordinary matter apart unless something unseen kept the gravity locked. If dark-matter particles slid through each other without exchanging any momentum, those cores would have pulled apart and the whole structure would have come undone. The distribution stays tightly bound instead, which implies the particles do interact when they pass close. That scattering slows the cores as they try to separate, and gravity then rakes in extra material faster than ordinary cold-dark-matter computer runs allow. A giant cluster therefore appears earlier than most of those runs predict for that epoch. The maps place the dark-matter cores offset from the shining galaxies, a pattern that matching simulations reproduce only when a small self-interaction cross-section is switched on. How far that offset runs, and the fact the cores remain bound at all, together set a bound on how strongly the particles must scatter. Nobody yet knows the exact strength, and no laboratory detector has recorded a particle that behaves this way at the densities found in clusters. The live question is whether the same interaction also fits every other massive cluster seen at similar redshifts, or whether El Gordo is an outlier. An outlier would force a rewrite of how fast the first giant structures were allowed to grow after the Big Bang. Deeper lensing maps of more high-redshift clusters, plus simulations that follow particle scattering across cosmic time, are what it will take to settle that.
