  2. Comet Tempel 2 Shares Frame with Messier Objects — NASA Breaking

    3. The August 8 Astronomy Picture of the Day shows periodic comet 10P/Tempel 2 passing near several well-known Messier galaxies and nebulae. The image captures the comet’s faint coma against a backdrop of deep-sky targets. Tempel 2 reaches perihelion every 5.5 years and currently lies in the constellation Taurus. Observers with modest telescopes can still locate it under dark skies this month. The comet’s nucleus is estimated at a few kilometres across and releases dust and gas as it approaches the Sun. Source: science.nasa.gov

    4. NASA Administrator Bill Nelson stated that nuclear fission systems will supply the sustained energy needed for long-duration Mars surface operations. The approach would provide kilowatts of electricity independent of sunlight or dust storms. Current development focuses on a 10-kilowatt-class fission surface power unit. Engineers plan ground testing before any flight demonstration. The system is designed to operate continuously through the Martian night and during dust events that reduce solar panel output. Source: Deseret News

  5. Voyager 2 Receives Another Year of Instrument Power — Space.com

    6. NASA engineers have adjusted Voyager 2’s power management so its remaining science instruments can operate through at least 2027. The 48-year-old probe is now in interstellar space and continues to return data on the heliopause boundary. The latest change involved turning off non-essential heaters and reallocating wattage from the radioisotope thermoelectric generator. No new hardware failures have been reported since the adjustment. The spacecraft’s instruments now measure plasma waves and magnetic fields far beyond the Sun’s influence. Source: space.com

  6. Canada Commits to Full Canadarm3 Development — spaceq.ca

    7. The Canadian Space Agency has confirmed continued funding for the Canadarm3 robotic system destined for the Lunar Gateway.   7. Webb Data Reveal Previously Unknown High-Redshift Arc — Universe Today

    8. Astronomer Homer Dávila Gutierrez identified a gravitational arc candidate in the galaxy cluster MACS J0308.9+2645 using public James Webb Space Telescope images. The feature shows a photometric redshift near 4.4 and had not been catalogued before. The discovery paper is under review at Publications of the Astronomical Society of Japan. Follow-up spectroscopy will be needed to confirm the distance and magnification. The arc appears stretched by the cluster’s mass, offering a magnified glimpse of an early galaxy. Source: universetoday.com

  9. JWST “Little Red Dots” May Evolve into Spiral Galaxies — Space.com

    10. New analysis suggests the compact red objects discovered by the James Webb Space Telescope could represent an early stage in spiral galaxy formation. Their spectral features indicate dense stellar cores surrounded by gas that may later settle into disks. The objects appear at redshifts between 4 and 7. Further observations will test whether they grow through mergers or in-place star formation. The findings come from detailed examination of Webb’s near-infrared spectra. Source: space.com

  10. Galaxy Spins Preserve Early-Universe Torque Record — Phys.org Space

    11. Researchers report that the rotation directions of both spiral and elliptical galaxies still carry imprints from tidal torques exerted by surrounding matter in the early universe. The study compared observed spin alignments with predictions from tidal torque theory. Data came from large spectroscopic surveys covering millions of galaxies. The findings support the idea that initial conditions set billions of years ago remain detectable today. Alignment statistics were measured across multiple redshift bins to trace the signal’s persistence. Source: phys.org

  11. Uranus Bow Shock Varies Daily Due to Extreme Tilt — Phys.org Space

    12. Uranus’s 98-degree axial tilt causes its magnetosphere to rotate nearly perpendicular to the incoming solar wind, producing a bow shock that expands and contracts once per planetary day. The ice giant’s offset magnetic field adds further asymmetry. Spacecraft measurements from Voyager 2 still provide the only in-situ data. Models now predict the shock front can move by several planetary radii over 17 hours. The daily variation creates a dynamic boundary unlike the steadier shocks at other planets. Source: phys.org

  12. Mars Helicopter Prototypes to Carry Instrument Capes — Space.com

    13. NASA’s SkyFall helicopter concepts will incorporate lightweight “capes” containing sensors and communication hardware. The fabric-like layers are designed to survive dust and temperature extremes while adding minimal mass. Each cape integrates with the rotorcraft’s power and avionics systems. The technology aims to extend mission range beyond current Ingenuity capabilities. The capes also provide thermal regulation during the cold Martian nights. Source: space.com

  13. LINK Spacecraft Team Works to Resolve Onboard Anomaly — Space.com

    14. Engineers are troubleshooting a propulsion or attitude-control issue on the private LINK spacecraft launched last month to service a NASA telescope. The vehicle remains in a stable orbit while teams upload diagnostic commands. Mission managers have not yet set a new rendezvous timeline. The spacecraft was intended to demonstrate commercial satellite servicing techniques. Ground teams continue to monitor telemetry for clues to the anomaly’s origin. Source: space.com

  14. Mission to Rescue Falling NASA Observatory Encounters Delays — CNN

    15. A privately funded mission aimed at boosting a declining NASA astrophysics satellite has run into its own technical setbacks. The rescue spacecraft must perform a precise rendezvous and attachment before the target observatory loses too much altitude. Teams are evaluating alternative docking approaches. No revised launch or rendezvous date has been announced. The observatory continues its slow orbital decay while engineers refine the rescue plan. Source: Google News

  15. COSPAR Assembly Examines Engineering for Next Space Science Era — EurekAlert!

    16. Industry and academic leaders gathered at the 46th COSPAR Scientific Assembly to discuss how new engineering approaches can enable future space science missions. Sessions covered propulsion, materials, and autonomy for deep-space observatories and planetary probes. Participants highlighted the need for tighter integration between instrument teams and spacecraft designers. The meeting continues through the week in Busan, South Korea. Discussions also addressed radiation-hardened electronics and long-duration power systems. Source: EurekAlert!

Cosmic Deep Dive: How a Planet’s Tilt Rewrites Its Bow Shock

Uranus orbits at an average distance of 2.9 billion kilometres, yet its magnetosphere experiences the solar wind’s push on a 17-hour cycle because the planet rolls on its side like a barrel. Imagine standing on the surface while a 400-kilometre-per-second stream of charged particles slams into a magnetic field that itself spins perpendicular to the flow; the bow shock can shift inward or outward by several planetary radii within one rotation. Voyager 2’s 1986 flyby recorded only a single moment of this breathing motion, leaving the full daily pattern to be modelled from limited data. The planet’s magnetic axis is also offset by roughly 60 degrees from its spin axis, so the shock front never settles into the stable shape seen at Earth or Jupiter. We can calculate the geometry with increasing precision, but the key unanswered question remains how these repeated compressions and expansions influence the rate at which atmospheric ions escape into space and whether any radiation belts can persist under such variable pressure.

New data keep arriving from both fresh launches and decades-old probes; the sky is far from finished with us.
