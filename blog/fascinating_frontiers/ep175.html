Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Comet Caught Photo-Bombing Archival Telescope Images — Universe Today

    2. Japanese researchers examining data from the Subaru Telescope's 8.2-meter mirror found comet 28P/Neujmin had passed through existing frames at just the right angle. The geometry allowed them to measure the comet's surface properties directly. The work forms part of a larger effort to understand why some comets and asteroids share similar traits and what those similarities imply for Solar System formation. The Subaru images captured reflectance data normally hidden by dust and gas. Follow-up observations will test whether other "naked" comets can be characterized the same way.

  2. Rapid Chemical Pathways Build Complex Molecules in Space — Phys.org Space

    3. University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa chemists identified faster routes for large carbon-based molecules to assemble under space conditions. The findings address a long-standing question about how the building blocks of organic chemistry grow in interstellar environments. Laboratory simulations showed reaction sequences that operate on shorter timescales than previously modeled. The team traced specific reaction pathways that produce complex aromatics without requiring extreme conditions. Next steps involve matching the laboratory signatures against spectra from star-forming regions.

  3. Lower Eyelid Changes Linked to Long-Duration Spaceflight — Universe Today

    4. A new study isolates possible mechanisms that cause astronauts' lower eyelids to lose tone after extended time in microgravity. The changes are associated with shifts in vision that some crew members report. Researchers examined how fluid redistribution and tissue response interact during orbital flight.   5. Lava-World Atmospheres Challenge Escape Models — Universe Today

    6. Stanford researchers built a model showing how close-in lava-covered planets can retain atmospheres despite intense stellar radiation. The work questions earlier assumptions about the cosmic shoreline that separates worlds with and without atmospheres. Observations of these extreme cases provide new tests for atmospheric-loss theory. The simulations incorporate surface outgassing balanced against stellar wind stripping. Follow-up spectroscopy will check whether the predicted retention holds across additional targets.

  7. Swift Observatory Faces Uncontrolled Reentry — Space.com

    8. A private spacecraft could not capture NASA's Swift space telescope, leaving the observatory to reenter naturally. Details on timing and location remain limited while teams assess the orbit. Swift has operated for nearly two decades, delivering gamma-ray and X-ray observations of transient events. Engineers continue to monitor the spacecraft's systems until the final descent. The mission's long record of burst detections stands as one of its primary legacies.

    9. NASA Science published a series of stories detailing the Sentinel-6B ocean-surface topography mission. Cosmic Spotlight

Lava-covered exoplanets close to their stars are somehow keeping atmospheres that models once predicted would be stripped away. Stanford's new simulations show that surface outgassing and stellar wind interactions can balance each other in ways not previously calculated. The result forces a fresh look at which worlds might retain the gases needed for further chemistry. Observations of these extreme cases now serve as direct tests for atmospheric retention theories. What surface or magnetic process actually tips the balance on these extreme planets?

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Chance Alignments Expose Comet Surfaces

An 8.2-meter telescope mirror can record the surface of a comet only a few kilometers across when the viewing angle aligns perfectly across hundreds of millions of kilometers of space. In the Subaru archive the nucleus of 28P/Neujmin crossed the field at a phase angle that sent sunlight straight back from bare rock instead of scattering through the usual dust shroud. That brief window yielded reflectance measurements normally masked by the coma, showing a surface whose spectral signature resembles certain asteroids more than typical active comets. The geometry lasted only minutes in the data set yet supplied the exact illumination needed to separate intrinsic rock properties from the surrounding cloud of ice and dust. Such alignments occur when the comet's orbit and the telescope's pointing happen to intersect at the right moment, turning routine survey images into unplanned surface studies. The discovery raises the question of whether other dormant comets hide similar asteroid-like exteriors that only become visible under equally rare conditions. We still do not know whether this naked appearance marks a common end state for comets that have shed their outer layers or whether it identifies a separate population formed closer to the Sun.

Space keeps delivering new angles on old questions.
