Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. AMD Readies Chip for Fifteen-Year Space Missions — Tech Critter

    AMD is preparing its Versal AI Core adaptive system-on-chip for missions lasting up to fifteen years in space. The chip includes radiation-tolerant features and high-performance processing for onboard autonomy. Engineers are testing it against the thermal and radiation stresses of long-duration orbits. The design supports extended operations where traditional processors would fail. Source: tech-critter.com

  2. Deep Image Reveals Sombrero Galaxy Tidal Streams — NASA Breaking

    A seven-day exposure from Namibia captured faint tidal streams around M104. The structures include a diagonal ring and extended halo stars that point to past collisions. Light from the galaxy takes thirty million years to reach Earth. The galaxy spans one hundred fifty thousand light years. The longer exposure reveals a hairy halo beyond the familiar dust brim. Source: science.nasa.gov

  3. Anorthosite Massif Stands Out in Southern Madagascar — NASA Breaking

    Astronauts photographed the Saririaky anorthosite massif from the International Space Station on August twenty-eighth. The bright intrusive rock covers one hundred square kilometers and formed at least six hundred million years ago. It sits inside a ductile shear zone linked to the assembly of Gondwana. The massif lies within rocks that record the collision of ancient continents. Source: science.nasa.gov

  4. Western Australia Student Selected for NASA Program — Perth is OK!

    Alaxandrea O’Brien becomes the first university student from Western Australia chosen for the National Indigenous Space Academy. She studies environmental engineering at the University of Western Australia and will work at the NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Eight First Nations students are participating this year. She has mentored others through programs including the Polly Farmer Foundation and CSIRO Indigenous Women’s STEM Academy. Source: perthisok.com

  5. UAE Launches Altair-1 and Ships Asteroid Probe — EnterpriseAM

    The UAE placed the first satellite of a planned ten-satellite constellation into orbit. The Technology Innovation Institute completed its first fully home-built deep-space probe for a two-thousand-twenty-eight launch toward the asteroid belt. The probe carries an AI-capable data processing unit with potential uses in Earth observation and defense. The constellation will support commercial and scientific goals. Source: enterpriseam.com

  6. All-Filipino Crew Finishes Analog Mission — Philippine Space Agency

    An all-Filipino crew completed a simulated analog mission in Batangas. President Marcos met the analog astronauts and representatives from the Philippine Space Agency. The exercise tested procedures for future crewed operations. The mission advanced local expertise in crewed simulation techniques. Source: philsa.gov.ph

  7. Project Tektite Documentary Highlights Undersea Training — stthomassource.com

    FriendsVINP produced a documentary on Project Tektite, the nineteen-sixties undersea habitat program used to train astronauts. The film examines how saturation diving techniques supported space mission preparation. The project demonstrated long-duration living in extreme environments. Source: stthomassource.com

  8. Gamma-Ray Data Tighten Dark Matter Limits — Phys.org Space

    Researchers analyzed gamma-ray observations of the inner Milky Way to set new upper limits on dark matter annihilation. The study constrains possible particle masses and interaction cross-sections. The limits come from detailed mapping of the galactic center region. Source: phys.org

  9. Engineer Maps Gaps in NASA Moon Return Plans — NASA Breaking

    Richard Spolzino describes his goal of pointing to hardware and knowing his work contributed to it. The interview outlines remaining technical and schedule challenges for Artemis landings. He focuses on practical contributions rather than titles or specific missions. Source: nasa.gov

  10. XRISM Detects Hidden X-Ray Phase After Neutron Star Merger — Phys.org Space

    An international team found a previously unseen high-energy phase following a compact star merger. The observation came from data collected by the XRISM observatory. The phase reveals new details about the aftermath of such events. Source: phys.org

  11. Nations Advance Plans for Lunar Nuclear Reactors — nytimes.com

    Multiple space agencies are developing small nuclear reactors to power future Moon bases. The designs aim to provide continuous energy through the lunar night. The reactors would support sustained human presence on the surface. Source: nytimes.com

  12. Starship Version Four Hot-Staging Options Analyzed — r/spacex

    Engineers are evaluating three booster engine configurations for hot-staging on the stretched Starship vehicle. Options include shutting down to seven engines, throttling nine engines, or keeping all thirteen center engines at minimum throttle. The choices affect separation dynamics and boostback burn requirements. Source: reddit.com

  13. Enceladus Plume Grains Show Chemical Diversity — Phys.org Space

    Ice grains ejected from Enceladus fractures carry varying chemical signatures from the subsurface ocean. The diversity arises from different formation depths and interaction times with the water. The grains offer a direct sample of the moon’s hidden ocean. Source: phys.org

  14. Japan Space Chief Discusses Autonomy Goals — Anadolu Ajansı

    Japan’s space chief described national plans to develop independent launch and exploration capabilities amid growing international competition. The interview outlined ongoing projects and technology priorities. The focus includes building domestic infrastructure for future missions. Source: aa.com.tr

  15. Sputnik Launch Anniversary Recalled — Astronomy

    On October fourth nineteen fifty-seven the Soviet Union launched Sputnik one, the first artificial satellite. The sphere measured fifty-eight centimeters across and weighed eighty-four kilograms. The launch marked the start of the space age. Source: astronomy.com

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Anorthosite Forms on Earth and the Moon

Picture a rock body the size of a modest city district yet formed from a single slow-cooling magma chamber six hundred million years ago. On Earth the Saririaky massif in Madagascar reached the surface after tectonic forces sheared and uplifted the surrounding crust during the assembly of Gondwana. The same mineral, anorthosite, makes up the bright lunar highlands that formed more than four billion years ago when the Moon’s global magma ocean crystallized. Scientists compare the two because lunar samples are limited while terrestrial outcrops let them study crystal settling and chemical layering at human scale. The process begins when dense minerals sink and lighter plagioclase crystals float and accumulate into thick layers. One remaining puzzle is why Earth anorthosites appear in discrete massifs while the Moon produced a planet-wide crust of the same rock. Researchers continue to examine how the cooling rates and pressures differed between the two bodies.
