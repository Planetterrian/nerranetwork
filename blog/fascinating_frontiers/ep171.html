Cosmic Spotlight

The seventeen-spacecraft campaign on a single coronal mass ejection stands out because it turns a fleeting solar event into a three-dimensional measurement. By sampling the same plasma cloud from widely separated vantage points, scientists can now trace how the eruption’s magnetic field evolves as it travels outward. The next step is to apply these calibrated models to future events so that Earth-directed forecasts improve in both lead time and accuracy. What remains unclear is whether every eruption behaves this way or whether this one was unusually well sampled.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Multiple Vantage Points Reveal Solar Storm Structure

A single coronal mass ejection can carry several billion tonnes of magnetised plasma outward at speeds exceeding 1 000 kilometres per second, enough material to fill the volume of roughly 100 Earths if compressed to sea-level density. When seventeen spacecraft positioned from near-Earth orbit to beyond Mars all record the same event, each records a slightly different slice of the expanding cloud, much like weather stations spaced across a continent capturing one storm front from different elevations and latitudes. The combined dataset shows the magnetic flux rope inside the eruption stays intact over distances greater than one astronomical unit, a scale that single-point observers had only inferred from statistical averages. Temperature and density readings differ sharply depending on whether a spacecraft passes through the dense core or the more diffuse flanks, revealing internal structure that earlier models treated as uniform. This distributed view also exposes how the eruption interacts with the ambient solar wind, slowing and deflecting in ways that affect arrival times at Earth by many hours. Temperature gradients measured across the cloud reach several hundred thousand kelvin between core and edge, numbers that constrain the amount of heating supplied by magnetic reconnection during transit. Yet the precise trigger that decides whether a given eruption remains coherent or fragments during its journey is still unknown, leaving forecasters without a reliable way to predict which events will deliver their full energy to our planet.
