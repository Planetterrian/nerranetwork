Magnetar forms when two neutron stars touch — Phys.org

High-resolution simulations on Japan’s Fugaku supercomputer tracked low-mass neutron star binaries with initial fields around 3 trillion gauss. Within milliseconds of contact the neutron fluid hit Kelvin-Helmholtz instability and mixed so rapidly that the merged star’s field grew more than 300 times stronger. The run began just before collision and ended shortly after, capturing the full amplification in roughly one millisecond. The work appears on the arXiv as “A magnetar formation in binary neutron star merger.” Most neutron stars exceed the Chandrasekhar limit of white dwarfs yet remain below the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit near 2.6 solar masses, allowing some mergers to produce a single neutron star rather than a black hole. The initial magnetic fields sat in the upper range of typical neutron stars, and the instability mixed material so thoroughly that the amplified field could influence the remnant’s structure within the first millisecond. Source: phys.org

Galileo Second Generation gains reprogrammable signal module — European Space Agency

The new module, already tested in Toulouse, will generate multiple navigation signals in real time and accept in-orbit updates to create new ones. It pairs with the radio-frequency output module next year before integration into the first flight satellite. Twelve G2 satellites will join the constellation, each carrying electric propulsion, inter-satellite links, and an enhanced navigation antenna. Airbus Defence and Space has six satellites at various integration stages in its Friedrichshafen hall, while navigation antennas recently completed evaluation in Madrid. The module reads timing data from advanced atomic clocks and produces signals that can be made more robust against interference or tuned for faster acquisition. Source: esa.int

Supercomputer rover FLIP set for late-2026 lunar launch — Space.com

Astrolab’s FLEX Lunar Innovation Platform carries a supercomputer and will test tires, batteries, radios, and cameras identical to those on the upcoming crewed lunar terrain vehicle. The rover flies as a payload on Voyager Technologies’ Griffin Mission One and will operate at the lunar south pole. Data will inform the design of Astrolab’s CLV-1 before crewed Artemis landings. VIPER, NASA’s own south-polar rover, is fully assembled at Johnson Space Center and will travel more than 18 miles across permanently shadowed craters after its 2027 landing. Astrolab engineers will soon evaluate CLV-1’s airless wheels in NASA Glenn’s Simulated Lunar Operations laboratory. Source: space.com

The simulation illustrates how the ship would approach the tower from above after the booster has already been secured. Source: teslarati.com

Pulsar slowdown unmasks rare three-star system — Phys.org

A neutron star spinning more than 300 times per second appeared to brake unusually fast, revealing gravitational influence from two companion stars. The configuration is the first known pulsar in a hierarchical triple with this spin rate. Further timing observations are expected to pin down the companions’ masses and orbits. The rapid apparent slowdown arises because the pulsar’s motion around the common center of mass periodically changes the arrival times of its pulses as seen from Earth. Source: phys.org

Valles Marineris still reshaping Mars today — Phys.org

Columbia University and Imperial College London researchers mapped young fault scarps inside the solar system’s largest canyon system. The scarps indicate ongoing tectonic extension driven by the planet’s cooling interior. Activity continues billions of years after the canyon first formed. The measurements show that the rate of shrinkage remains small yet sufficient to trigger occasional landslides along steep slopes. Source: phys.org

JWST reveals protostellar jets inside NGC 7129 in Cepheus — NASA

The telescope imaged atomic and molecular hydrogen in NGC 7129, highlighting jets driven by embedded protostars. Golden regions trace hot atomic gas while red areas mark cooler shocked molecular hydrogen. The data show how outflows stir and heat the surrounding cloud. The image captures both the bright atomic hydrogen in the central zones and the extended molecular hydrogen shocked by the jets. Source: nasa.gov

Ancient rains chart prebiotic pathways on Mars — Universe Today

A new model traces how formaldehyde, delivered by rainfall billions of years ago, could have concentrated in surface minerals. The resulting map highlights regions where prebiotic chemistry may have been most active. Future landers could target these sites to search for chemical precursors to life. The study focuses on how early martian precipitation transported and deposited the key prebiotic molecule. Source: universetoday.com

Wearable patch tested for eyedrop delivery in microgravity — Phys.org

KAIST engineers built a flexible patch that releases medication directly onto the eye without relying on gravity. The device was developed with Seoul National University Bundang Hospital and published in Sensors and Actuators B: Chemical. It addresses a practical barrier for long-duration spaceflight crews. The patch was designed to maintain consistent delivery even when surface tension and lack of sedimentation dominate fluid behavior. Source: phys.org

OSIRIS-APEX enters fourth year of extended mission — r/space

The spacecraft continues its journey toward asteroid Apophis after successfully returning samples from Bennu. Mission teams are now refining the 2029 encounter plan while the probe operates on its second extended mission. The extended timeline allows additional observations of near-Earth asteroids before the close approach to Apophis. Source: reddit.com

Water changes atomic structure under planetary extremes — Phys.org

Laboratory tests show water molecules rearrange into new configurations when subjected to pressures and temperatures found inside ice giants and super-Earths. The altered bonding changes density, conductivity, and optical properties. These phases help explain interior models of distant worlds. The experiments demonstrate that water can adopt structures never observed under Earth-surface conditions. Source: phys.org

Bayeux Tapestry records 1066 Halley’s Comet passage — Phys.org

The embroidery depicts Harold and onlookers beneath the comet with the caption “Isti mirant stella.” Norman artists framed the apparition as an omen of Harold’s downfall and the coming invasion. The panel remains one of the earliest European visual records of the comet. The comet’s tail and position are shown relative to the invasion fleet in the tapestry’s border. Source: phys.org

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Turbulence Turns Weak Fields into Magnetar Strength

Picture two city-sized spheres, each denser than an atomic nucleus, spiraling together at hundreds of kilometers per second. When they finally touch, the contact zone does not simply smash together; it shears like two rivers colliding at right angles. That shear triggers the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, the same rolling billows you see when wind lifts smoke from a candle. Inside the neutron-star fluid the rolls stretch and fold any embedded magnetic field lines the way taffy is pulled, packing more lines into the same volume and therefore raising the field strength. The Fugaku runs showed this folding completed its work in roughly one millisecond, fast enough for the new field to influence the merger’s later evolution before the remnant has time to settle.

The initial fields in the simulation sat near three trillion gauss, already thousands of times stronger than anything we can manufacture on Earth. After the instability swept through, the merged object carried more than three hundred times that value. The amplification is not uniform; it begins in a thin turbulent layer and then spreads outward as the mixed fluid circulates. Because the process is so rapid, it can change the remnant’s internal pressure support and possibly decide whether the object stays stable or collapses further. The simulations assumed identical masses for the two stars and ran from moments before contact until shortly after the instability had done its work.

What remains unknown is how far the turbulence can push the field before it saturates or reconnects. The simulation stopped once the initial burst of mixing ended; longer runs or different mass ratios might reveal whether additional amplification occurs or whether the field begins to resist further stretching. Observers cannot yet measure the internal field of a freshly merged neutron star directly, so the models stand as the clearest prediction we have for how at least some magnetars come into being. The work also shows that the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism operates even when the starting fields are already strong, suggesting the process could be widespread among the subset of mergers that remain below the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit. Further simulations that extend past the first millisecond will be needed to determine whether the field continues to grow or whether reconnection begins to limit the gain.

We can see the instability develop in the models, but nobody knows exactly how the amplified field redistributes angular momentum inside the remnant or whether it changes the likelihood of a later collapse to a black hole.

New measurements keep rewriting what compact-object mergers can do.
