Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Rare Lunar Meteorites Could Fill Gaps In Moon’s Evolution — Universe Today

    Only 835 lunar meteorites have been verified, representing 0.7 percent of all meteorites that fall to Earth. Apollo missions returned 382 kilograms of samples while three Soviet Luna missions and China’s Chang’e 5 and 6 missions added roughly 4 kilograms combined. Most lunar meteorites originate from randomly distributed locations across the Moon, including the far side, unlike the Apollo sites that landed in or near the geochemically anomalous Procellarum KREEP Terrane. Researchers use the chemistry and age of these meteorites to test the lunar magma ocean hypothesis that a global ocean of magma covered the Moon early in its history. The first identified lunar meteorites, Yamato-791197 and Yamato-793169, were collected in Antarctica in 1979. Lunar meteorites arrive after spending years to tens of thousands of years in Earth orbit or up to tens of millions of years in solar orbit. Randy Korotev of Washington University in St. Louis noted that Apollo landing sites were chosen without orbital mineralogical data, leaving five of six missions inside one unusual region. Katherine Joy of the University of Manchester emphasized that meteorites supply pieces of ancient crust potentially from the far side, allowing tests of whether near-side and far-side crust formed identically. Source: universetoday.com

  2. Astronomers may have discovered the first moon beyond our Solar System — Science Daily

    Observations with ESO’s Very Large Telescope uncovered evidence of an enormous moon-like body orbiting the brown dwarf in the CD-35 2722 system. The exosatellite has at least the mass of Jupiter while the brown dwarf it circles exceeds 30 Jupiter masses. Lead author Kevin Hoy described the arrangement as super weird compared with our own Solar System. The team applied the radial velocity method with the CRIRES+ instrument to measure tiny changes in the brown dwarf’s motion caused by the orbiting body. If confirmed, the find would mark the first detection of a natural satellite outside our Solar System and blur traditional definitions of planets and moons. Alice Zurlo noted the satellite is a giant gaseous body orbiting a highly massive companion several times the mass of Jupiter. The system centers on a star of roughly half the Sun’s mass, with the brown dwarf and its satellite forming a nested hierarchy unlike anything in our Solar System. Source: sciencedaily.com

  3. NASA Safety Panel Urges Architecture Review Over Lunar Lander Refueling Demands — SatNews Publishers

    NASA’s Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel warned that unproven in-space cryogenic propellant transfer poses acute technical risks for Artemis lunar landings. Panel members noted planning estimates of around 22 Starship tanker launches to fuel an orbital depot, though SpaceX has disputed that figure. The panel plans to issue a formal recommendation urging NASA to reassess its landing mission architecture ahead of upcoming program updates. The warning arrives as NASA restructures Artemis with an Earth-orbit test for Artemis III in mid-2027 and a deferred crewed landing on Artemis IV in early 2028. Former astronaut Charlie Precourt highlighted that dozens of rapid cryogenic transfers introduce compounding failure modes into human lunar flights. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman is scheduled to deliver an agency-wide Artemis update that must address the refueling bottleneck. Source: satnews.com

  4. How flare ribbons reveal hidden solar explosions on the sun — Phys.org Space

    Researchers applied a new model to an M6.5-class solar flare observed on June 22, 2015, by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph satellite. The model quantifies how flare ribbon substructure evolves and links those changes to fragmentation in the reconnecting current sheet in the corona. Flare ribbons form when high-energy particles slam back into the chromosphere along magnetic field lines. The team found that energy deposition occurs in irregular bursts rather than a smooth process, with ribbons breaking into fragments of varying sizes. Lead author Marcel Corchado Albelo noted the work provides the first experimental demonstration of a physical relationship between observed ribbon shapes and the structure of the energy conversion region. Co-author Maria Kazachenko stated that simulations had long predicted ribbon boundary behavior but lacked observational confirmation until this quantitative test. Source: phys.org

  5. Medusa's eye, Halloween fireballs and more: 5 skywatching targets to look out for this spooky season — Space.com

    The Pleiades star cluster reaches its highest point at midnight during the Halloween period, a timing once linked by ancient Celtic druids to Samhain when the boundary between living and dead was thought thinnest. Observers in the United States can locate the cluster by tracing upward from Orion’s Belt to the orange star Aldebaran, then looking less than 15 degrees higher. The Demon Star Algol in Perseus appears to wink every 2.867 days as one star eclipses the other, dimming by a factor of 3.3 for roughly 10 hours at minimum. The constellation Cetus, representing a sea monster from Greek myth, shines in the southeastern sky with its head formed by a circlet of stars. Around the full moon on October 26, bright rays from the craters Tycho, Copernicus, and Kepler create spiderweb-like patterns across the lunar surface. The Taurid meteor shower, produced by debris from Comet 2P/Encke, offers a chance to spot bright slow-moving fireballs streaking away from a radiant near the Pleiades. Source: space.com

The conversation covers hiring demand driven by lunar missions, increasing low-Earth orbit activity, and new commercial space companies entering the market.   7. Oct. 24, 1601: The death of Tycho Brahe — Astronomy

    Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe died in Prague on October 24, 1601, after constructing large quadrants, an armillary sphere, a sextant, and a large globe for precise pre-telescopic observations. He ranked second only to Galileo in a 2022 Astronomy magazine list of the twenty greatest astronomers. Brahe measured the 1572 supernova and the Great Comet of 1577, finding no parallax and placing both objects beyond the Moon. His meticulous stellar and planetary position data passed to Johannes Kepler, enabling the derivation of the laws of planetary motion that later underpinned Isaac Newton’s work. Brahe had discovered early in his career that existing published positions were grossly inaccurate and devoted his life to correcting them through sustained instrumental measurements. Source: astronomy.com

  8. NASA adopting Apollo's urgency to build a moon base and land astronauts within 2 years — Phys.org Space

    NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman confirmed that Artemis IV remains scheduled for a 2028 crewed lunar landing while an Earth-orbit docking test for Artemis III is set for mid-2027. The test mission will require three rapid launches from Cape Canaveral so that Orion can practice automated rendezvous with both Blue Origin’s Blue Moon and SpaceX’s Starship Human Landing System. Isaacman described the current effort as adopting the same urgent approach that took NASA from Apollo 7 splashdown to Apollo 11 in nine months. A lunar base near the south pole is expected to see its first lander attempt by year’s end, with additional rovers and orbiters following so that the first crewed landing site already contains surface mobility assets. Lighter sortie suits will be tested on the International Space Station next year to reduce complexity for initial moonwalks. Source: phys.org

Cosmic Spotlight

The possible exosatellite in the CD-35 2722 system challenges how astronomers classify objects that sit at the boundary between planets and moons. Its mass equals or exceeds Jupiter’s while it orbits a brown dwarf more than thirty times Jupiter’s mass, creating a hierarchy in which a planet-scale body circles another planet-scale body that itself orbits a star. Lead author Kevin Hoy and collaborator Alice Zurlo note the arrangement forces a rethink of Solar-System-based labels because the satellite is clearly massive enough to be considered a planet under some schemes yet does not orbit a star directly. Radial-velocity measurements with CRIRES+ on the Very Large Telescope supplied the key evidence by tracking the brown dwarf’s slight wobble. Confirmation would open a new category of observations for giant satellites around substellar objects and would require updated frameworks for distinguishing moons from planets when both orbit within the same system.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Flare Ribbons Map Magnetic Reconnection in the Solar Corona

A single solar flare can release energy equivalent to the simultaneous detonation of millions of hydrogen bombs, yet the process that converts stored magnetic energy into that outburst remains hidden behind the Sun’s bright surface. Imagine riding a magnetic field line as it twists and shears under the relentless motion of the Sun’s plasma; eventually the line can no longer tolerate the accumulated stress and snaps, reconnecting into a simpler configuration while liberating enormous heat and accelerated particles. Those particles stream downward along newly formed field lines and slam into the denser chromosphere, lighting up bright ribbons whose fine structure records the fragmentation occurring far above in the corona. On 22 June 2015 an M6.5 flare was captured by the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph, revealing that the ribbons did not brighten uniformly but instead broke into irregular fragments of varying sizes, depositing energy in discrete bursts rather than a continuous flood. Researchers built a quantitative model that links the observed spatial complexity of those ribbon boundaries directly to the breakup of the reconnecting current sheet, providing the first observational test of three-dimensional flare simulations that had long predicted such substructure. The measurement matters because it turns the ribbons into a remote diagnostic of coronal physics that cannot be observed directly. One persistent mystery is why the fragmentation occurs at the particular scales recorded in the data; the model reproduces the pattern yet leaves open the question of which instability or turbulence process sets the fragment sizes that ultimately control how and where the Sun’s magnetic energy is released.

Des chercheurs ont appliqué un nouveau modèle à une éruption solaire de classe M six virgule cinq observée le vingt-deux juin deux mille quinze par le satellite Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph.

Le modèle quantifie comment la sous-structure des rubans d’éruption évolue et relie ces changements à la fragmentation dans la nappe de courant en reconnexion dans la couronne, rapporte Phys point org.
