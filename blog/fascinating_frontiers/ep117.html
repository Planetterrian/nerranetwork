Cosmic Spotlight

NASA’s planned robotic boost of the Swift observatory extends a mission that has already operated for more than two decades. The spacecraft’s ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes have recorded thousands of gamma-ray bursts and other high-energy transients. Raising the orbit will delay the point at which atmospheric drag becomes unmanageable. Engineers must execute the rendezvous with centimetre-level precision to avoid damaging the observatory. The effort demonstrates how commercial vehicles can now perform tasks once reserved for dedicated agency missions. What remains unclear is how many additional years of observations the boost will ultimately deliver before a final deorbit.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Interstellar Atoms Brake the Solar Wind

If the Sun were a basketball in downtown Toronto, the heliopause would sit roughly at the edge of the Greater Toronto Area—yet the solar wind crossing that distance slows by tens of kilometres per second. Neutral atoms drifting in from interstellar space slip past the Sun’s magnetic shield, then collide with charged solar-wind particles and steal momentum through charge-exchange reactions. New Horizons recorded this gradual deceleration only after travelling beyond 30 astronomical units, where the density of interstellar neutrals finally becomes appreciable. The process unfolds over years rather than days, turning the outer heliosphere into a vast, low-friction drag zone. Exactly how the resulting pickup ions and secondary energetic neutral atoms redistribute energy across the boundary remains an open puzzle that future interstellar probes will need to map in three dimensions.

New data keep arriving from the edge of the solar system and the surface of Mars—plenty to keep watching.
