Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. UNOOSA Registrations Found on UN Docs Site — Jonathan McDowell

    2. Orbital analyst Jonathan McDowell reports that fresh satellite registration documents remain unavailable through the standard UNOOSA website. The same files appear on the main UN documents portal when the correct document ID string is entered. Access still requires manual guessing of identifiers rather than a searchable index. Older records have also become reachable after a tip from another user on how to navigate the archive. The workaround restores visibility into recent launches without waiting for an official interface update. Users must still enter precise strings to retrieve each new filing. No automated search function exists on the documents portal for these entries.

  2. Starship Flight 13 Targets July 16 Liftoff — SpaceX

    3. SpaceX has begun preparations for the thirteenth integrated flight test of Starship. The vehicle could launch as early as July 16 from Starbase in Texas. This test continues the program’s push toward rapid reuse and eventual orbital refueling demonstrations. Remember, the show covered Starship & Mars on Ep128 — today’s update moves the flight-test cadence forward and keeps the open question of reuse intervals in view. Engineers will watch propellant transfer performance and booster catch attempts on this flight. The mission profile includes multiple engine burns after separation. Data from this flight will inform the next set of hardware changes.

  3. BBC Highlights Eight Close Calls in Human Spaceflight — BBC Sky at Night Magazine

    4. A new feature lists eight documented incidents in which astronauts survived life-threatening failures during flight. The cases span multiple nations and decades, underscoring how thin the margin remains between routine operations and catastrophe. Each example includes the specific system failure that occurred and the steps crews took to recover. The article draws on mission transcripts and post-flight reports rather than speculation. Readers see the exact sequence of events that turned potential disasters into safe returns. The incidents cover both American and Soviet-era missions. No new analysis is added beyond the historical record.

  4. Pakistan Eyes 2035 Moon Mission With NASA Help — satnews.com

    5. Pakistan has formally asked NASA to explore cooperation on a lunar landing targeted for 2035. The proposal includes shared technology development and possible joint science instruments. Officials have not released cost estimates or a detailed timeline. The request marks the country’s first public step toward independent crewed lunar capability. Discussions would focus on lander systems and surface operations. No formal agreement has been signed yet. The approach would build on existing bilateral space science exchanges.

  5. Mars “Ghost Snow” Falls as Dry Ice and Vanishes — r/astronomy

    6. In the planet’s polar regions, carbon dioxide freezes and precipitates as fine crystals that sublimate before reaching the surface. NASA’s MARSIS radar has mapped layers of this dry ice several kilometers thick. Beneath the ice, the same instrument has detected what appears to be liquid water that has persisted for billions of years. The snowfall occurs only where temperatures drop below minus 125 °C. The radar reflections distinguish the ice layers from underlying rock and liquid. No surface deposit forms because the crystals disappear in the thin atmosphere. The process repeats each Martian winter.

  6. Bulgaria’s Second Cosmonaut Dies at 74 — collectSPACE.com

    7. Aleksandr Aleksandrov, who flew under the Interkosmos program in 1988, passed away on July 11. He became the second Bulgarian citizen to reach orbit after Georgi Ivanov. Aleksandrov’s mission on Soyuz TM-5 included Earth-observation and materials-science experiments. Colleagues remember him for his work training later crews in microgravity procedures. His flight lasted eight days and returned safely to Earth. The Interkosmos program gave allied nations access to Soviet spacecraft. Aleksandrov remained active in space education after retirement.

  7. First Satellite-Linked Dog Collar Uses Starlink — Space.com

    8. A new collar combines GPS with SpaceX’s direct-to-cell Starlink network to maintain contact anywhere on Earth. The device can send location updates and short messages even when traditional cellular coverage is absent. Developers say the collar will enter limited production later this year. It is the first commercial pet product to rely on the emerging satellite-to-phone constellation. The hardware uses the same frequency bands planned for future smartphone connections. Battery life supports multi-day tracking without recharging. Field tests confirmed signal acquisition in remote areas.

  8. University of Alberta Finds Two Record-Distant Quasars — r/astronomy

    9. Astronomers have identified two quasars farther from Earth than any previously confirmed objects. The discovery relied on spectroscopic follow-up of candidates selected from wide-field surveys. Both objects are powered by supermassive black holes accreting at high rates. Their light provides new probes of conditions in the universe less than a billion years after the Big Bang. The spectra show strong emission lines shifted far into the infrared. Additional candidates from the same survey are under review. The findings extend the known reach of luminous early black holes.

  9. Permanent Magnets Proposed for Deep-Space Radiation Shield — Phys.org Space

    10. Researchers from Italy and Germany have modeled a lightweight permanent-magnet array that could deflect solar energetic particles. The design avoids the power demands of superconducting coils and the mass penalty of water shielding. Computer simulations show the static field can reduce crew exposure during solar storms by a measurable fraction. The team’s preprint explores field geometry needed to cover a small habitat module. The magnets would be mounted externally on the spacecraft structure. No cooling systems or electrical current are required. The study compares several magnet configurations for coverage efficiency.

  10. Chandra Maps Uneven Expansion of Young Supernova Remnant — Phys.org Space

    11. Dutch astronomers used Chandra X-ray Observatory data to track the shock front of G292.0+1.8. The remnant, rich in oxygen, shows faster expansion along some axes than others. The asymmetry suggests the original explosion was not perfectly spherical. Continued monitoring will test whether the pattern persists as the remnant ages. The X-ray images resolve the forward shock in multiple energy bands. Proper-motion measurements over several years reveal the velocity differences. The remnant lies several thousand light-years away in the southern sky.

  11. White House Names Avi Loeb to Lead New UFO Panel — Space

    12. Harvard astronomer Avi Loeb has been appointed to head a fresh federal study group on unidentified aerial phenomena. The panel will review sensor data and witness reports with an emphasis on scientific methods. Loeb previously led the Galileo Project, which searches for technological signatures from interstellar objects. The new group’s first report is expected within a year. The appointment brings an astronomer with experience in anomaly detection to the effort. Data sources will include both government and civilian instruments. The panel operates independently of prior UAP task forces.

  12. NASA Chandra Reveals Magnetic Structure of Distant Pulsar — r/astronomy

    13. The Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer has mapped the magnetic field geometry around a pulsar nicknamed the “lighthouse.” Polarization measurements show the field lines twist in ways that match theoretical predictions for a rotating neutron star. The data cover multiple rotation phases and provide the first direct view of the emission region’s orientation. Follow-up observations are planned to test whether the pattern changes over months. The pulsar emits beams that sweep across the line of sight like a lighthouse. IXPE’s detectors record the angle of polarized X-rays from each phase. The results constrain models of the magnetosphere.

  13. Amateur Captures High-Frame-Rate Moon Animation — r/astronomy

    14. A Nikon Z8 paired with a Takahashi TSA-120 telescope recorded 5,400 raw frames of the lunar surface. The sequence was aligned and stacked to produce a short time-lapse showing changing shadows across craters. The mount used was a ZWO AM5 in high-frame-rate mode at 20 frames per second. Processing combined PIPP, AutoStakkert, and Photoshop before final animation in After Effects. The best 10 percent of frames were selected for the final stack. The animation reveals subtle topographic relief under changing illumination. The observer used a 2.4× Barlow lens for additional magnification.

  14. Iraq Observer Images West Veil Nebula — r/astronomy

    15. From Baghdad, a Seestar S50 telescope collected 12 hours of luminance data on NGC 6960 under light-pollution filters. The resulting 1.3× mosaic reveals fine filamentary structure in the supernova remnant. Stacking and processing were completed in PixInsight and Photoshop. The image demonstrates that useful deep-sky data can be gathered from an urban site. The Veil complex lies about 1,500 light-years away in Cygnus. The observer used an EQ mode mount to track the field. Total integration time reached the equivalent of multiple clear nights.

  15. Romanian Astrophotographer Records Fireworks Galaxy — r/astronomy

    16. A 200 mm Newtonian telescope gathered 26 hours of luminance, 7 hours of RGB, and 2.5 hours of H-alpha on NGC 6946. Two cooled CMOS cameras operating at –15 °C captured the data from a Bortle 4 location. The final image shows star-forming regions and dust lanes across the spiral arms. Processing used PixInsight, Seti Astro tools, and Photoshop. The galaxy lies roughly 25 million light-years away in Cepheus. The long luminance exposure brings out faint outer structures. The H-alpha data highlight active star-forming complexes.

Cosmic Spotlight

NASA’s Chandra X-ray data on supernova remnant G292.0+1.8 show the shock wave racing outward at noticeably different speeds in different directions. The asymmetry points back to an explosion that was itself lopsided, not a perfectly round blast. Continued X-ray monitoring will reveal whether that pattern holds as the remnant sweeps up more interstellar material. The oxygen-rich composition further constrains the progenitor star’s final stages. Proper-motion measurements across multiple epochs will tighten the age estimate. What initial conditions inside the star produced such an uneven detonation?

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Magnetic Fields Channel Radiation Around Small Habitats

A neutron star squeezes the mass of the Sun into a sphere roughly 20 kilometres across; a teaspoon of its material would weigh as much as a mountain on Earth. Permanent magnets proposed for spacecraft create a comparable but far weaker static field that can nudge charged particles away from a crew module without any electrical power. Picture riding alongside a solar energetic particle as it approaches the habitat: instead of a straight path, the proton curves along the field lines and misses the thin shell by tens of metres. The geometry that works best is a simple dipole whose poles sit outside the living quarters, producing a protective bubble whose radius grows with the square root of the magnet strength. Even a modest field of a few tesla can reduce the dose from a typical solar storm by 30 to 40 percent according to the new simulations. The magnets would be arranged in a compact array mounted on the exterior of a small habitat, creating a localized deflection zone that follows the module wherever it travels. The remaining puzzle is whether the same arrangement leaves gaps at the “cusps” near the magnetic poles, where particles could still thread through and reach the crew.

New records keep appearing at both the cosmic edge and the launch pad.
