  1. Perseverance Sets New Distance Record on Mars — Universe Today

    NASA's Perseverance Rover has reached another milestone. It now holds the record for the longest distance travelled on another world, meaning Mars and Earth's Moon. It's all thanks the ingenuity behind AutoNav, it's hazard-detection driving system. It's a win for science, as more distance travelled means more scientific objectives are reached.   2. NASA Previews Two Spacewalks to Finish ISS Solar Array Upgrades — NASA

    NASA will preview two upcoming spacewalks to complete solar array installation and power augmentation of the International Space Station during a news conference at 2 p.m. EDT, Tuesday, Oct. 20, from NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston. NASA will stream the event through a variety of platforms. During U.S. spacewalk 100, NASA astronaut Anil Menon and CSA astronaut Joshua Kutryk will exit the station’s Quest airlock to install and deploy an International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array on the 2A electrical power channel. During U.S. spacewalk 101, Menon and Kutryk will install and deploy the eighth and final IROSA on the 3B channel of the space station, completing the power augmentation upgrades. The final two roll-out solar arrays will complete the space station’s power system augmentation. Together the eight roll-out arrays will increase available power by up to 30 percent, boosting the station’s electric power generation capacity from 160 kilowatts to approximately 215 kilowatts. The spacewalks will be the 285th and 286th conducted in support of space station assembly, maintenance, and upgrades. Source: nasa.gov

  3. Chandra Reveals Cause of Treble Clef Radio Structure in Galaxy Cluster — NASA

    Astronomers have used NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory to break down the cause of a remarkable formation of radio waves nicknamed the “Treble Clef.” The researchers discovered the Treble Clef is likely caused by an outburst from a supermassive black hole, followed by a collision of two galaxy clusters. A paper describing the findings, led by Andrea Botteon of the National Institute for Astrophysics in Italy, published Wednesday in the Astronomy & Astrophysics journal. Chandra and eROSITA found diffuse hot gas at the location of the Treble Clef, showing that a galaxy cluster hosts the object. The researchers also determined the galaxy cluster is in fact two clusters in the process of merging. Radio data from LOFAR revealed the musical shape after an earlier black-hole outburst drove charged particles outward at close to the speed of light and the later collision reenergized the cloud. The authors concluded that the Treble Clef likely formed in a two-stage process. The direct collision between the two clusters occurred a few hundred million years ago, and the clusters are now moving away from each other. Source: science.nasa.gov

    The edge of the Sun appears darker than its central region. This effect is known as limb darkening and tells us about the solar atmosphere. This same limb darkening effect has now been observed on several nearby stars and could help us improve our understanding of stellar atmospheres. Source: universetoday.com

Cosmic Spotlight

Perseverance’s record drive distance comes from repeated use of its AutoNav system, which lets the rover analyze terrain and choose safe paths without waiting for commands from Earth. Each successful segment adds to the total distance while also delivering new images and rock samples for study. The achievement shows how onboard decision-making can multiply the scientific return of a single mission by allowing continuous progress across boulder-strewn plains. AutoNav processes hazard data in real time, turning what would have been long pauses into steady forward motion. That capability directly enabled the distance milestone reported today.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Galaxy Cluster Mergers Re-Energize Radio Emission

Imagine two city-sized clouds of hot gas, each containing hundreds of galaxies, sliding past each other at hundreds of kilometers per second across a distance that would take light a million years to cross. That is the scale of the collision now reshaping the radio sky inside SDSS J1038+4849’s distant cluster. The event compresses and re-energizes particles that have already traveled near light speed for millions of years, turning a faded radio glow back into a structure that looks, from our vantage point, like a giant musical clef.

The process begins with a supermassive black hole at the center of one cluster. Its earlier outburst flings clouds of charged particles and magnetic fields outward at relativistic speeds. Those particles radiate brightly for a time, then lose energy and fade. When the second cluster arrives, the resulting shock waves and compression act like a cosmic bellows, squeezing the old particle population and boosting its radio output once more. Chandra and eROSITA data show the hot gas is no longer symmetrical; the brightest X-ray residuals lie along the left-right axis of the collision, confirming the direction of impact. Radio maps from LOFAR trace the revived particles into the treble-clef shape. The entire sequence played out a few hundred million years ago, and the clusters are now drifting apart again.

What remains unknown is why only some merging clusters produce such clean, recognizable radio shapes while others do not. The same black-hole-plus-collision recipe should operate elsewhere, yet the Treble Clef stands out. Differences in magnetic-field strength, the exact timing between the outburst and the merger, or the viewing angle from Earth could all matter. Astronomers are now searching for more examples at similar redshifts to test which variable decides whether a faded radio cloud sings again or stays silent.

New findings this week continue to sharpen our view of both nearby worlds and distant stellar processes.
