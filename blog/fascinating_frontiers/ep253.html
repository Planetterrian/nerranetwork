New models suggest dark energy flipped from negative to positive 9.5 billion years ago.

Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Unseen companion likely a 2.2-solar-mass neutron star — Phys.org Space

    Astronomers identified a red giant star with an unseen binary companion using data from LAMOST and APOGEE. The companion's mass falls between 1.8 and 2.8 solar masses, with a best estimate of 2.2 solar masses. The system, called J0008+0128, completes an orbit in about 3.3 years on a near-circular path. No light was detected from the companion, ruling out a regular star. Three hundred thousand POSYDON simulations of isolated binaries failed to reproduce the system's masses and orbital properties. The researchers conclude the companion is most likely a neutron star pushing the upper mass limit. Source: phys.org

  2. Dark energy may have flipped sign 9.5 billion years ago — ScienceDaily Space & Time

    Scientists exploring an alternative gravity theory found dark energy could have switched from negative to positive roughly 9.5 billion years ago. The reversal would change how cosmic expansion is understood. The finding emerged from an overlooked branch of the theory rather than standard models. Source: sciencedaily.com

  3. Gemini and DECam capture young Type Ia supernova in NGC 1433 — Phys.org Space

    A star's explosive demise added a new feature to the barred spiral galaxy NGC 1433, 50 million light-years away. Images from the Dark Energy Camera and Gemini South show supernova SN 2026ahvu changing appearance from night to night against its galactic surroundings. The event was recorded in the southern constellation Horologium. Source: phys.org

  4. JWST spectra reveal formation history of Uranus's innermost moons — AAS Nova

    New spectra from JWST uncover potential formation histories of Uranus’s ring-moon system. The observations target the innermost moons and their troubled past. Source: aasnova.org

  5. Dense galaxy clusters throttle star formation — Universe Today

    New research examined a particularly dense galaxy cluster to find clues about why star formation has slowed since the Cosmic Noon. The study identifies mechanisms fostered by cluster density that remove or heat the hydrogen gas needed for new stars. Source: universetoday.com

  6. Tensegrity-based robotic slugs could crawl on alien worlds — Universe Today

    Engineers at Colorado State University’s Adaptive Robotics Lab developed a shape-shifting robot using tensegrity principles from architecture. The design allows navigation over jagged boulders or through tight crevices where wheels and rotors struggle. A recent paper in Nature Machine Intelligence describes the morphology. Source: universetoday.com

  7. Gold nanoparticles boost signal in astrobiology mass spectrometry — AAS Nova

    Applying gold nanoparticles enhances the signal from astrobiologically relevant molecules such as histidine and vitamin K1. Silver nanoparticles proved ineffective in the same tests. The technique is detailed in a Planet. Sci. J. paper by Nikita J. Boeren and colleagues. Source: doi.org

  8. Radio signals traced to white dwarf and sun-like star pair — Space.com

    Astronomers traced mystery radio signals to a cosmic odd couple consisting of a white dwarf and a sun-like star. The detection demonstrates that radio wavelengths can reveal stellar storms more than 5,000 light-years away. Source: space.com

  9. Euclid releases first data toward 3D sky map — Phys.org Space

    ESA’s Euclid has produced early results including a small image covering 60 square degrees of the sky. The data release serves as a stepping stone to the mission’s full 3D map. Past observations already showed targets such as the Horsehead Nebula and the Perseus cluster. Source: phys.org

  10. Theoretical framework merges gravitational waves with Newtonian tides — Universe Today

    A researcher at Freie Universität Berlin extended the classical model of lunar tides to include the cross polarization of gravitational waves. The work asks what traces of dynamic spacetime changes might appear in high-precision tidal measurements. The Earth–Moon system offers a precise testbed because its tidal effects are already well described and measured. Source: universetoday.com

Also Today

Rocket Lab completes 100th launch with Japanese radar satellite: Electron lifted off from New Zealand carrying an iQPS synthetic aperture radar satellite to 575 kilometers altitude. Source: space.com

NASA plans Voyager 1 and 2 status update event for November 19: The event will include mission updates, science from interstellar space, and a preview of an upcoming NASA+ documentary. Source: nasa.gov

Cosmic Spotlight

Gemini astronaut photos from 1966 helped make the case for dedicated Earth-observation satellites. Lovell and Aldrin captured images of the Red Sea and Nile valley during Gemini XII, demonstrating the value of orbital imagery for studying Earth’s surface. Those pictures directly influenced William T. Pecora’s push for what became the Landsat program. The photos showed scientists could monitor crops, flooding, and land use from space. Source: nasa.gov

Cosmic Deep Dive: The Mass Gap Between Neutron Stars and Black Holes

Neutron stars form from the cores of exploded high-mass stars and pack roughly 1.4 solar masses into a sphere only about 20 kilometers across. Black holes, by contrast, typically begin at five solar masses and climb from there. Between these two populations sits a sparsely populated region from roughly 2.5 to 5 solar masses where few objects have been found. The gap matters because it marks the boundary where the equations describing matter under extreme pressure cease to support a stable neutron star and instead allow collapse into a black hole. Astronomers still lack a precise measurement of that transition mass because the relevant physics inside these objects remains poorly understood.

A recent study published in Science Advances identified a candidate that may help pin down the limit. Using spectra from LAMOST and APOGEE, researchers found a red giant star orbited by an unseen companion whose mass is estimated at 2.2 solar masses, with an upper range reaching 2.8 solar masses. The binary completes an orbit every 3.3 years on a surprisingly circular path, and no light is detected from the companion itself. Three hundred thousand simulations of isolated binary evolution failed to reproduce the observed combination of mass, separation, and circular orbit, suggesting the system formed through an unusual pathway or that current models miss key physics.

What remains unknown is whether objects in this mass range are always neutron stars that formed with unusually high birth masses or whether some represent the lightest black holes. A single additional detection with a measured radius or a clear signature of a solid surface would settle the question by showing whether the object can support itself against further collapse. Until then, each new system like J0008+0128 narrows the range of allowed equations of state for ultra-dense matter.

Space keeps delivering surprises that rewrite what we thought we knew.
