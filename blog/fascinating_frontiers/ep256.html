  1. Celestial bodies could trap slow dark matter particles — Phys.org Space

    Dark matter particles carrying a tiny electric charge or behaving like miniature magnets might lose just enough energy in collisions with ordinary matter to be captured by a planet or star. Over billions of years the particles would collect in the central regions, where annihilations could produce neutrinos or extra heat. Researchers combined theoretical calculations with existing observations of Earth, the sun, Jupiter and white dwarfs. Neutrino searches from IceCube supplied limits for Earth and the sun, while measured heat output ruled out certain mass and interaction combinations for Jupiter and white dwarfs. Earth proved especially useful for testing a tiny electric charge, while white dwarfs constrained other electromagnetic properties. The work, published in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics, rules out some combinations of particle mass and properties that other experiments had left open. Source: phys.org

  2. Black hole mergers ring in ways that match general relativity — Universe Today

    More than seventy researchers used computer models and a literature review to examine the ringdown phase of gravitational waves from black hole mergers. Hundreds of observed events produced ringing signals consistent with Einstein’s theory of general relativity. The study advances black hole spectroscopy from a theoretical idea to a practical tool by measuring the gravitational waves emitted after two black holes merge. Current detectors still face measurement limits, but next-generation ground-based instruments are expected to deliver more precise data. The findings appear in Classical and Quantum Gravity. Source: universetoday.com

  3. Chaotic accretion may explain rapid early black hole growth — Phys.org Space

    A new model shows that gas falling onto early supermassive black holes from random directions keeps their spin low. Low spin means only 6 to 7.5 percent of infalling mass is converted to radiation instead of the usual 10 percent, shortening the growth timescale from 45 million years to roughly 27–32 million years. The scenario reproduces the observed masses of all reliably measured supermassive black holes at redshifts above seven, including the quasar J0313-1806, with seeds of about 100 solar masses formed around 100 million years after the Big Bang. The paper, published in Astronomy & Astrophysics, predicts that the most massive early black holes should be slowly spinning. Source: phys.org

  4. South Korean startup readies Hall thruster for 2027 orbital test — SpaceNews

    Cosmo Bee will fly its 10 mN-class Honeybee Hall thruster aboard a D-Orbit ION spacecraft in October 2027. The test will measure thrust and specific impulse under real space conditions. The company already demonstrated a 150 W-class Hall thruster on the K-HERO CubeSat launched in November 2025. D-Orbit’s in-orbit demonstration service will supply performance data needed for commercial qualification. Honeybee targets small-satellite constellations in low Earth orbit. Source: spacenews.com

  5. Dream Chaser receives landing approval for 2027 debut — Space.com

    Sierra Space’s Dream Chaser space plane has secured FAA approval to return from orbit on its first mission. The vehicle has been in development for more than twenty years. The approval clears the path for a 2027 flight after years of ground testing and design reviews. Source: space.com

  6. Dune sand grains reveal wind speeds on Earth and Mars — Phys.org Space

    Stanford researchers found that the median grain size in dunes directly indicates the speed of the winds that formed them. The relationship was calibrated on Earth dunes and then applied to Martian dunes observed by orbiters. The method turns sand into a wind-speedometer that works for both present-day and ancient dune fields across the solar system. Source: phys.org

  7. Shadows and a power meter could navigate lunar orbit — Phys.org Space

    A proposed technique uses changes in solar illumination and spacecraft power draw to determine position in lunar orbit without relying on Earth-based tracking. The approach addresses growing demand on NASA’s Deep Space Network by providing an independent navigation method for future lunar missions. Source: phys.org

  8. Beta Pictoris shows dirty ice mixed with carbon dioxide — Phys.org Space

    JWST infrared observations detected noncrystalline ice around the young star Beta Pictoris for the first time. The ice is mixed with carbon dioxide and points to ongoing collisions in the system’s debris disk. The findings, published in Science, supply new clues about the early formation conditions of the Beta Pictoris planetary system. Source: phys.org

Cosmic Spotlight

SpaceX caught the upper stage of Starship 42 with the launch tower’s mechanical arms after a 22.5-hour orbital flight, marking the first recovery of an orbital-class second stage. The 52-meter vehicle deployed 26 Starlink satellites, executed a belly-flop reentry, and was plucked from the sky by the “Mechazilla” arms. The catch followed three prior booster recoveries and brings routine reuse of both stages closer to reality. Cosmic Deep Dive: How Sand Grains Record Planetary Wind Speeds

Sand dunes on Earth and Mars preserve a record of the winds that built them, and researchers have now shown that the size of the sand grains themselves acts as a direct speedometer. The median grain diameter measured in a dune field reflects the typical wind velocity at the time the dune formed. On Earth, dunes built by stronger winds contain coarser grains because only faster winds can move larger particles. The same relationship holds on Mars, where orbital images reveal dune fields whose grain sizes can be compared with terrestrial calibrations. The method works for both active dunes and ancient ones preserved in the rock record, giving scientists a way to reconstruct past wind regimes across the solar system without needing direct wind measurements. Stanford University researchers derived the relationship from field data on Earth dunes and then applied it to Martian dunes observed by spacecraft. The resulting wind-speed estimates match independent atmospheric models, confirming that grain size reliably encodes wind strength. What remains unknown is how the relationship changes when dunes form under very different atmospheric densities or when the sand supply is limited. Future rover or lander measurements of grain-size distributions on other worlds would test whether the same calibration applies universally or needs adjustment for local conditions. Source: phys.org

Dark matter with a slight electric charge, or with the behavior of a tiny magnet, can lose energy in collisions with ordinary matter and be captured by a planet or a star. Over billions of years the particles gather toward the center, and annihilations there could yield neutrinos, particles that rarely interact with ordinary matter, or spare heat. Theoretical calculations were set against existing observations of Earth, the sun, Jupiter, and white dwarfs, the leftover cores of dead stars. IceCube's neutrino searches set limits for Earth and the sun, and heat output measured from Jupiter and from white dwarfs ruled out certain pairings of mass and interaction. Earth was the stronger test of a tiny electric charge, while white dwarfs boxed in other electromagnetic properties. Work in the Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics rules out some pairings of mass and properties that other experiments had still left open. Ringdowns in gravitational waves from black hole mergers, studied by more than seventy researchers with computer models and a literature review, Universe Today notes, match Einstein's theory of general relativity in hundreds of events. Gravitational waves are ripples from violent motions of huge masses, and ringdown is the fading tone once two black holes have become one. Black hole spectroscopy reads that tone the way astronomers read light. The study takes that spectroscopy from a theoretical idea toward a practical tool by measuring the waves given off after the merger. Current detectors still meet measurement limits, yet next-generation ground-based instruments, in a Classical and Quantum Gravity analysis, are expected to deliver more precise data. Random-direction gas falling onto early supermassive black holes, Phys.org reports, keeps their spin low. Low spin means only six to seven point five percent of infalling mass turns into radiation instead of the usual ten percent, shortening the growth timescale from forty-five million years to roughly twenty-seven to thirty-two million years. A quasar is a brilliant galactic core powered by a black hole. Redshift is a measure of how far back in cosmic time we are looking. The scenario reproduces observed masses of every supermassive black hole with a reliable measurement at redshifts above seven, including the quasar J0313-1806, from seed holes of about one hundred solar masses that appeared around one hundred million years after the Big Bang. Work in Astronomy and Astrophysics predicts that the most massive early black holes should be slowly spinning. Cosmo Bee will fly its ten mN-class Honeybee Hall thruster, SpaceNews reports, aboard a D-Orbit ION spacecraft in October twenty twenty-seven. A Hall thruster is an electric engine for spacecraft. The test will measure thrust and specific impulse under real space conditions. The firm already demonstrated a one hundred fifty W-class Hall thruster on the K-HERO CubeSat, a small satellite launched in November twenty twenty-five. D-Orbit's in-orbit demonstration service will furnish the on-orbit performance data required to qualify the engine for commercial use. The Honeybee design is meant for small-satellite constellations in low Earth orbit. Development has run more than twenty years. That clearance opens the way to a twenty twenty-seven flight, following years of ground testing and design reviews. Stanford work on dunes, Phys.org reports, finds that median grain size is a direct readout of how fast the winds were that built the dune. Calibration used dunes on Earth, then the same rule was pointed at Martian dune fields imaged by orbiters. Sand becomes a wind-speedometer for active dunes and for ancient dune fields across the solar system. Only faster winds can move larger particles, so stronger winds leave coarser grains in the dunes they build. Wind-speed estimates taken from those grains match independent atmospheric models. Past winds can be rebuilt from dunes preserved in stone, with no direct wind measurement required. How the link changes under very different atmospheric densities, or when sand is scarce, is still unknown. Grain-size distributions measured by a future rover or lander on another world would test whether the Earth calibration applies everywhere or needs a local adjustment. Shifts in solar illumination and in a spacecraft's power draw could determine position in lunar orbit without Earth-based tracking. Growing demand on NASA's Deep Space Network, the antennas that track distant spacecraft, is the pressure this independent lunar navigation is meant to relieve.
