Cosmic Deep Dive: How Transmission Spectroscopy Reveals Exoplanet Atmospheres

If Earth were placed 48 light-years away, its atmosphere would span less than one ten-thousandth the width of the star it orbits, yet that sliver is exactly what astronomers measure. When a planet crosses its star, a tiny fraction of starlight filters through the upper atmosphere; molecules there absorb specific wavelengths, imprinting a barcode on the spectrum that reaches our telescopes. The James Webb Space Telescope records these changes at precisions better than 100 parts per million across dozens of transits, enough to distinguish between a bare rock and one wrapped in a detectable gaseous envelope. The surprising number is the scale: the signal from an Earth-sized atmosphere at that distance is equivalent to blocking an extra 0.01 percent of the star’s light for a few hours. Temperature gradients within the atmosphere further modulate the depth of absorption lines, revealing whether the gas is heated from above or below. Yet even with that detection in hand, we still cannot tell whether the atmosphere is being continuously replenished by volcanic outgassing or is a remnant left over from formation. The next generation of extremely large ground-based telescopes may resolve this ambiguity by capturing higher-resolution spectra during single transits.
