Thanks for joining me on Fascinating Frontiers, episode eighty-six. It’s May thirty-first, twenty twenty-six, and we’re looking back across the past week to see which missions advanced, which datasets landed, and what those steps actually mean for the work ahead. Space X’s redesigned Starship V3 reached space on its first flight last week, and the engineering teams are now deep in the post-flight review. That review will shape the return-to-flight attempt, which is meant to show clearer gains in engine performance and in how the booster is brought back under control. Regulators still have to sign off before any new date is locked in, yet hardware is already being stacked at the Texas site so the next test can move forward without delay. What matters here is that each completed flight narrows the remaining unknowns around orbital refueling and how quickly a booster can be readied again. From that launch cadence we turn to the sky itself. nassa’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite has finished an all-sky catalog that lists thousands of planets around stars close enough for follow-up work. The map shows how planet occurrence changes with stellar type and with location inside the galaxy, and the transit signals were checked against background stars to keep false positives low. Astronomers now have a ready list they can hand to larger telescopes for atmosphere studies, and the next step is to line those targets up with ground-based radial-velocity measurements so masses and orbits can be pinned down more tightly. Shifting from distant worlds to our own, MAVEN’s latest passes over Mars picked up plasma behavior that doesn’t match earlier models. The data show pockets where the solar wind presses in differently, and that detail helps trace how the planet lost most of its original atmosphere. More orbiter passes are planned so the same regions can be sampled again, and the full set of measurements will feed into papers once processing finishes. Commercial work offers a different angle. Orbit Fab and Thales Alenia are studying how a docking port can link with electric propulsion systems that run on xenon or similar gases. The tests look at mechanical fit, fluid transfer, and electrical interfaces so future servicing missions can top up propellant without bringing the satellite home. Results from this effort will set the practical limits for what kinds of spacecraft can be refueled in orbit. The U K’s new monitoring system adds another layer of protection. It pulls tracking data together with defensive steps that cover both collision risk and radio interference. Fresh images released this week show the network now reaches farther across the orbital environment, giving operators earlier notice when something drifts too close or starts to jam signals. Back on the ground, a plasma-based cleaning method is being tested for fabrics that would otherwise need water that simply won’t exist on long surface missions. Lab runs show the ionized gas breaks down organic bits at temperatures low enough for spacecraft use, which could stretch the life of the few sets of clothing crews can carry. Next work will check whether repeated exposures weaken the materials themselves. Meanwhile the Shenzhou-22 return capsule brought back another round of station samples covering materials, fluids, and biology. Ground labs are starting the detailed analysis, and this flight marks the tenth recovery supporting Tiangong research, with more returns already scheduled through the rest of the year. From returned hardware we move to planet formation still in progress. Researchers at the University of Warwick are using alma images to weigh young planets by the gaps they carve in their dusty disks. Gap width and depth line up with planet mass in the models, letting astronomers study systems that are still embedded in gas and therefore only a few million years old. The method sidesteps the need to spot the planets directly, which opens a much earlier window on growth. E S A’s Smile spacecraft has settled into its long elliptical orbit after launch. From that path the instruments will watch the solar wind meeting Earth’s magnetosphere, swinging repeatedly between the dayside boundary and the stretched-out tail so both regions are covered in a single orbit. Farther afield, construction has begun on a new facility in western China that will collect spectra from thousands of galaxies at once, giving large-scale surveys a faster way to map how galaxies move and evolve. Closer to home, May closes with a second full moon rising on the thirtieth. The geometry is tight, so the moment the Moon clears the horizon is brief and depends on local skyline and exact timing. Photographers planning to catch the low, large appearance are already checking sites because atmospheric conditions can change how the disk looks from one minute to the next. A few days earlier, on the twenty-fifth, Mercury, Venus, and Mars lined up low in the evening sky. The grouping was short-lived, giving observers a narrow chance to see the three planets together before orbital motion pulled them apart again. That wraps the week’s developments. The next Starship flight will show whether booster recovery has improved, TESS targets will start moving into atmosphere studies, and MAVEN’s plasma measurements will keep feeding atmospheric-loss models.
