Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. NASA Awards Four Lunar Lander Missions to Three Firms — SpaceNews

    2. NASA selected Astrobotic, Firefly Space, and Intuitive Machines for the deliveries. The contracts total nearly $600 million and cover four separate landings. Astrobotic receives $297.9 million for two missions, while Firefly Space and Intuitive Machines each receive one mission worth $144.2 million and $148.3 million respectively. These flights will carry science instruments and technology demonstrations to study the lunar environment. The selections support planning for a future crewed lunar base. Next steps include final mission design reviews before hardware integration begins. Source: spacenews.com

  2. NASA Weighs Sending Perseverance Backup Rover to Moon — Phys.org

    3. The agency is evaluating the PROMISE rover, a full-scale engineering model of Perseverance, for a lunar south pole mission. PROMISE would reuse hardware and software lessons from both Curiosity and Perseverance. The concept aims to accelerate surface exploration ahead of crewed landings. No final decision has been made, but the option remains under active study. Engineers are assessing how the rover’s nuclear power system would perform in the lunar cold traps. Further analysis will determine whether the existing test unit can be repurposed without major redesign. Source: phys.org

  3. New NASA Audit Flags Ongoing Starliner Delays — Space.com

    4. The audit concludes NASA will need additional commercial crew flights to the ISS through 2030. It highlights persistent technical issues with Boeing’s Starliner spacecraft. The report examines how certification setbacks have affected the overall crew rotation schedule. NASA continues to rely on SpaceX Crew Dragon vehicles in the meantime. The agency has not set a new target date for Starliner’s first crewed flight. Follow-up reviews will track whether Boeing meets revised performance milestones. Source: space.com

  4. Study Maps How Human Muscle Breaks Down in Weightlessness — Universe Today

    5. NASA-supported researchers built a ground-based model that reproduces the molecular effects of microgravity. The work shows that muscle cells lose strength through disrupted signaling pathways long before visible atrophy appears. Removing the constant mechanical load from gravity triggers these changes at the cellular level. The same model may help scientists understand muscle loss in other conditions on Earth. Data from the experiments are now being compared against astronaut samples. Future tests will examine whether targeted interventions can slow the process. Source: universetoday.com

  5. New Model Shows Dying Stars Receive Thousands of Tiny Kicks — Universe Today

    6. Asymmetric mass loss during the final stages of stellar evolution can impart small recoil impulses. Over hundreds of thousands of years these impulses accumulate and give the resulting white dwarf a noticeable velocity. The mechanism helps explain why many wide binary systems separate once one star becomes a white dwarf. The model predicts typical speeds reached after roughly ten thousand individual ejection events. Observers can now search for matching velocity signatures in nearby white dwarf populations. Additional simulations will test how different mass-loss patterns affect the final drift. Source: universetoday.com

  6. Survey Finds Most Void Galaxies Live in Isolation — Universe Today

    7. The CAVITY project mapped galaxy groups inside cosmic voids using a friends-of-friends algorithm. Results show the large majority of void galaxies have no companions. When groups do appear they remain small and loosely bound regardless of local density. The findings challenge assumptions that environment alone drives galaxy clustering. Researchers are now examining whether internal processes such as gas availability play a larger role. Follow-up observations will target the few groups detected to measure their dark matter content. Source: universetoday.com

  7. Euclid Telescope Image Reveals Thousands of Hidden Exoplanets — European Space Agency

    8. A new mosaic of the Milky Way’s center contains tens of millions of stars captured in 26 hours. Within the dense field, astronomers expect to detect planets through gravitational microlensing events. The telescope measures tiny, temporary brightening caused when a foreground star and planet pass in front of a background star. Euclid was originally designed to study dark matter and dark energy but is now also mapping galactic structure. The data will yield planet masses and orbital distances that are difficult to obtain by other methods. Additional fields will be processed in the coming months to expand the sample. Source: esa.int

  8. UC Irvine Team Finds New Earth-Sized Exoplanet — UC Irvine News

    9. Astronomers identified the planet through radial velocity measurements of its host star. The world orbits within the star’s habitable zone, where liquid water could exist on the surface. Follow-up observations are planned to determine the planet’s mass and density. The discovery adds to the growing catalog of nearby rocky worlds suitable for atmospheric study. The team used data from ground-based telescopes to confirm the signal. Further measurements will check for additional planets in the same system. Source: Google News

  9. Keck Observatory Discovers Third Galaxy Lacking Dark Matter — Universe Today

    10. The galaxy belongs to a linear structure possibly formed during a past galactic collision. Its visible mass alone appears sufficient to explain its observed motions. This marks only the third known example of a dark-matter-deficient galaxy. The finding raises questions about how such objects can remain stable without the usual gravitational scaffolding. Additional spectroscopy will map the galaxy’s stellar velocities in greater detail. Similar searches are underway in other filamentary structures. Source: universetoday.com

  10. ESA Details Instruments for Proposed Enceladus Lander — Universe Today

    11. The mission concept targets the moon’s south-polar region where water vapor plumes erupt from the subsurface ocean. Proposed instruments would sample plume material for organic compounds and biosignatures. The lander would operate in the extremely cold environment near the tiger-stripe fractures. Mission planners are evaluating how to protect sensitive electronics from ice particles and radiation. A 2050 launch date remains under discussion within ESA’s long-term science program. Technology development for the sampling system is already underway. Source: universetoday.com

  11. Submillimeter Array Captures Earliest GRB Observations at mm Wavelengths — Phys.org

    12. On January 26, 2026, the array responded within minutes of a gamma-ray burst alert. The rapid-response system recorded the event at millimeter and submillimeter frequencies for the first time. These early data reveal the initial evolution of the relativistic jet and surrounding material. The observations were published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. The demonstration opens a new window for studying the most energetic cosmic explosions. Additional rapid-response triggers are now being scheduled. Source: phys.org

  12. Astronauts Repair Joint on Canadarm2 Robotic Arm — Spaceflight Now

    13. Flight controllers detected an electrical current spike in one of the arm’s seven joints on May 27. The anomaly prevented normal movement of the 17.6-meter structure. Crew members performed a series of diagnostic tests and mechanical adjustments during a spacewalk. The arm is now operating within expected parameters again. The repair extended the operational life of a critical piece of ISS infrastructure. Engineers continue to monitor the joint for any recurrence. Source: spaceflightnow.com

  13. Blue Origin Shifts New Glenn Integration to Hybrid Approach — Spaceflight Now

    14. The company is redesigning ground operations after the May 28 launch failure. Future vehicles will use a mix of horizontal and vertical integration steps. The change aims to improve access during final assembly and testing. An investigation into the previous flight remains active. Blue Origin has not announced a revised target date for the next launch attempt. New procedures will be validated during upcoming static-fire tests. Source: spaceflightnow.com

  14. Venus Shines Beneath Leo’s Sickle This Evening — Astronomy Magazine

    15. The planet reaches magnitude –4.1 and remains visible in the western sky after sunset. It sits roughly 15 degrees above the horizon an hour after the Sun sets. Observers can locate it below the backwards-question-mark pattern of stars that forms Leo’s head. No other bright planet currently shares that part of the sky. The configuration will persist through the coming weeks as Venus continues its eastward motion. Binoculars will show the planet’s phase and nearby stars more clearly. Source: astronomy.com

  15. ESA Completes CAVES Training Course in Italian Cave System — European Space Agency

    16. Five participants from three space agencies finished the two-week analog mission. The course simulates the isolation, limited communication, and technical challenges of long-duration spaceflight. Crews conducted geological sampling, equipment maintenance, and daily planning under cave conditions. The training helps prepare astronauts for future missions to the Moon and Mars. Data collected during the exercise will inform future habitat design studies. Additional CAVES sessions are scheduled later this year. Source: esa.int

Cosmic Spotlight

A new model shows that dying Sun-like stars can receive thousands of tiny velocity kicks as they shed their outer layers. Each asymmetric ejection of gas imparts a small recoil, and after roughly ten thousand such events the resulting white dwarf drifts at a measurable speed through space. The process neatly accounts for why many wide binary systems separate once one member becomes a white dwarf. Observers can now look for matching velocity patterns among nearby white dwarfs to test the prediction. The finding also raises the possibility that some apparently single white dwarfs were once members of disrupted binaries. What fraction of white dwarfs in the solar neighborhood carry these accumulated kicks remains unknown.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Asymmetric Mass Loss Gives White Dwarfs Unexpected Speed

If you could watch a Sun-like star in its final million years, you would see it shed mass in sudden, uneven bursts rather than a smooth wind. Each burst carries away a fraction of the star’s outer envelope in one direction, and the remaining core receives a tiny opposing shove. Over hundreds of thousands of years these thousands of small recoils add up; after roughly ten thousand events the white dwarf can reach speeds of several kilometers per second. The mechanism operates on timescales far longer than any single human lifetime yet leaves an observable signature in the motions of white dwarfs today. We can measure the resulting velocities with precise spectroscopy, but no one yet knows exactly what fraction of the mass must leave asymmetrically to match the observed speeds. The same process may also explain why some apparently single white dwarfs show chemical traces of a former companion that was flung away.

New lunar hardware and fresh views of stellar endings keep the frontier moving forward.
