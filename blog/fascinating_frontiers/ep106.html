Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Galileo Adds Quasi-Pilot Signal for Low-Power Devices — European Space Agency

    2. The European Space Agency has activated the E5a Quasi Pilot component across 12 Galileo satellites. This new signal element reduces acquisition time and power draw for receivers in Internet of Things networks and smart-city sensors. Engineers designed it specifically for mass-market devices that cannot sustain continuous high-power tracking. The change expands Galileo’s reach into applications previously limited to stronger GNSS signals. Future firmware updates on additional satellites will extend the capability further. Source: esa.int

  2. NASA Advances DAPHNE Mission for Space-Weather Studies — NASA

    3. NASA has selected the Dynamic Atmosphere-Ionosphere Explorer mission concept to move into Phase B development. DAPHNE will measure how solar activity and atmospheric dynamics couple to shape the near-Earth space environment. Data from the mission are expected to improve forecasts that protect GPS signals and satellites in low Earth orbit. Instruments will target energy transfer between the lower atmosphere and the ionosphere. The selection follows a competitive review of proposals addressing space-weather impacts on technology. Source: nasa.gov

  3. Tsinghua University Plans Apophis Flyby Mission — SpaceNews

    4. A spacecraft led by Tsinghua University will rendezvous with asteroid Apophis during its 2029 Earth encounter. The mission joins planned efforts by other space agencies to study the 340-metre object at close range. Scientists aim to measure surface composition and any structural changes induced by tidal forces. The university-developed probe will carry remote-sensing instruments tailored for the brief flyby window. Launch timing remains under study to meet the 2029 arrival. Source: spacenews.com

  4. Startup Offers Commercial Parabolic Flights — SpaceNews

    5. Mu-g Technologies has acquired an aircraft to begin selling reduced-gravity flights to researchers and private customers. The move comes as NASA evaluates options for replacing its own microgravity aircraft. Parabolic arcs will provide 20-second periods of weightlessness for experiments in fluid dynamics and materials science. The company plans initial operations from European airports. Demand is driven by growing interest in short-duration microgravity access outside government programs. Source: spacenews.com

  5. Lucy Flyby Reveals Water-Altered Asteroid — Space.com

    6. Spectral data from NASA’s Lucy spacecraft show evidence of liquid-water alteration on asteroid Donaldjohanson. The 4-kilometre body formed farther from the Sun before migrating inward. Carbonate and phyllosilicate signatures indicate aqueous processing occurred early in its history. The findings help refine models of how water and organics were distributed in the inner main belt. Additional Lucy encounters will test whether similar alteration appears on other targets. Source: space.com

  6. Curiosity Examines Distinct Rock Bands on Mount Sharp — NASA

    7. The Curiosity rover is traversing layered outcrops on Mount Sharp that display contrasting textures and colours. Each band records different depositional or diagenetic conditions from ancient Gale crater lake environments. Scientists are mapping grain size, mineralogy, and fracture patterns to reconstruct changing lake chemistry. The current campaign focuses on a series of finely laminated units at the base of one prominent ridge. Data will feed into broader reconstructions of Mars’ early climate. Source: science.nasa.gov

  7. Webb Detects Salty Clouds on Cold Exoplanet — Space.com

    8. Observations with the James Webb Space Telescope reveal salt-bearing clouds in the atmosphere of a planet previously called the Pink Planet. The clouds form at temperatures low enough for ammonium hydrosulfide and other salts to condense. Spectral features appear only in transmission data taken during transit, confirming their presence high in the atmosphere. The detection adds a new class of condensates to models of cold giant-planet chemistry. Follow-up observations will map cloud distribution across the terminator. Source: space.com

  8. Study Links Long Gamma-Ray Bursts to Collapsing Stars — Phys.org

    9. New analysis from Los Alamos National Laboratory supports the view that long-duration gamma-ray bursts arise from the collapse of massive stars rather than neutron-star mergers. The team examined burst durations, host-galaxy metallicities, and afterglow properties across dozens of events. Results show consistency with the collapsar model in which a rapidly spinning black hole powers relativistic jets. The findings narrow the range of progenitor scenarios still under debate. Additional multi-messenger data will test the conclusion further. Source: phys.org

  9. Energetic Neutral Atoms Proposed to Map Uranus’ Magnetosphere — Phys.org

    10. Researchers suggest that instruments detecting energetic neutral atoms could reveal the structure of Uranus’ unusual magnetic field during a future mission. The atoms form when charged particles in the magnetosphere capture electrons from neutral gas and travel in straight lines to a spacecraft detector. This technique has mapped magnetospheres at Earth and Saturn and could clarify how Uranus’ tilted, offset field channels plasma. Proposed payloads would combine these measurements with plasma and magnetic-field sensors. The approach offers remote sensing of regions difficult to reach with in-situ instruments alone. Source: phys.org

  10. Donaldjohanson Shows Tumbling Rotation — Phys.org

    11. Southwest Research Institute scientists report that asteroid Donaldjohanson rotates about two axes simultaneously. The body completes one end-over-end rotation every 10.5 Earth days while its spin axis wobbles on a 26.5-day cycle. Light-curve data from the Lucy spacecraft flyby provided the measurements. The complex motion likely results from the asteroid’s irregular shape and internal mass distribution. Future shape models will refine predictions of its long-term rotational evolution. Source: phys.org

  11. Ancient Meteorite Impact Linked to Gold in Australia — Astronomy Magazine

    12. A new study traces concentrations of gold in Western Australian rocks to a large meteorite strike more than two billion years ago. The impact melted crustal material and allowed gold to concentrate in impact-related hydrothermal systems. Geochemical mapping shows the gold signature aligns with the proposed crater location. The work adds to evidence that extraterrestrial collisions have influenced ore deposits on Earth. Further dating of impact glasses will tighten the timeline. Source: astronomy.com

  12. High-Energy Neutrino Traced to Dusty Star-Forming Galaxy — Space.com

    13. Astronomers have associated a high-energy neutrino event with a distant galaxy nicknamed the Shadow Blaster. The galaxy’s intense star formation produces cosmic rays whose interactions generate neutrinos. If confirmed, this would mark the first direct link between an individual dusty starburst galaxy and a neutrino detection. IceCube observatory data combined with multi-wavelength observations narrowed the source position. Additional events will test whether such galaxies form a significant neutrino population. Source: space.com

  13. Pacific Seafloor Rock Contains Kilonova Plutonium — Universe Today

    14. A ferromanganese crust dredged from the Pacific in 1976 holds a few hundred atoms of plutonium isotopes produced in a neutron-star merger. The material settled onto Earth more than 100 million years after the event, then was incorporated into the growing nodule. Isotopic ratios match predictions for r-process nucleosynthesis in kilonovae. The find provides a rare terrestrial sample of debris from such mergers. Further searches in other ocean cores may locate additional signatures. Source: universetoday.com

  14. Fermi Data Suggest Paired Supernova Remnants — Phys.org

    15. Fermi Large Area Telescope observations indicate two supernova remnants may have originated from stars that once orbited each other. The first explosion ejected its companion, which later exploded after traveling thousands of years. Spatial and velocity data align with this binary-disruption scenario. The remnants lie in a region where such pairings are statistically rare. Continued gamma-ray monitoring will search for similar sibling systems. Source: phys.org

  15. Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer Identifies Four Cosmic-Ray Populations — Phys.org

    16. Data from the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer on the International Space Station reveal four distinct groups of cosmic rays spanning 20 elements. The populations differ in spectral shape and elemental ratios, challenging current propagation models. One group shows enhanced secondary-to-primary ratios consistent with extended confinement in the galaxy. The findings come from more than a decade of continuous measurements above the atmosphere. Upcoming instrument upgrades aim to extend the energy reach. Source: phys.org

Cosmic Spotlight

The discovery of plutonium isotopes in a Pacific seafloor nodule directly ties an ancient neutron-star merger to material now on Earth. The atoms survived the journey across the galaxy and the subsequent geological processing that formed the ferromanganese crust. Their presence confirms that kilonovae can deliver long-lived r-process elements to planets. The same process likely seeded other heavy elements found in terrestrial rocks. What fraction of Earth’s heaviest elements arrived this way remains an open measurement.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Kilonova Debris Reaches Earth

If the Sun were the size of a basketball, the nearest neutron-star merger would have occurred roughly 3 000 kilometres away—yet its ejected material still found its way into a rock on our ocean floor. When two neutron stars collide, they fling out neutron-rich matter at 10–30 % the speed of light; rapid neutron capture builds elements heavier than iron within seconds. That debris expands into a vast, radioactive cloud whose slowest-moving atoms can drift for tens of millions of years before encountering a molecular cloud that collapses into new stars and planets. In the case recorded in the Pacific nodule, a few hundred plutonium atoms arrived after traveling across the Milky Way and settling through 100 million years of Earth’s geological cycling. The lingering question is whether every planetary system receives a comparable sprinkling of these exotic nuclei or whether our sample is unusually well preserved.

New observations keep rewriting what counts as ordinary in the cosmos.
