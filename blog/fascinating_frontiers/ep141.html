Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Young Rift Flanks Point to Active Venus Tectonics — Phys.org

    2. Recent mapping of rift flanks on Venus shows surfaces that formed within the last few hundred thousand years. The features appear alongside known volcanic sites, suggesting ongoing internal heat flow. Researchers used radar data from earlier orbiter passes to measure flank slopes and compare them with older terrain. The work challenges the long-held view that Venus has been geologically quiet for hundreds of millions of years. Next steps include higher-resolution imaging from upcoming missions to pinpoint active vents.

  2. Air Force Takes Over Military GPS Receiver Program — SpaceNews

    3. The Air Force Life Cycle Management Center will now manage development and sustainment of receivers for the encrypted military GPS signal. The move follows earlier program delays and cost growth. The receivers provide positioning and timing for weapons systems and secure communications. Officials expect the center’s oversight to stabilize requirements and speed integration across platforms. Delivery milestones for the next receiver generation remain under review.

  3. Moon Cement Gains Strength After Six Months on ISS — The Times of India

    4. A cement-like material designed for lunar construction was exposed outside the station for six months. Upon return the samples measured up to 35 percent stronger than identical controls kept on Earth. The improvement is attributed to vacuum exposure and thermal cycling that altered the material’s microstructure. The experiment tests whether lunar regolith can be sintered into durable building blocks without Earth-supplied water. Further tests will examine radiation effects on the same formulation.

  4. Sentinel-2 Captures Australia’s Agricultural Patchwork — ESA Activities

    5. The Copernicus Sentinel-2 satellite imaged the Goldfields–Esperance region in Western Australia. The false-color view highlights rectangular crop and pasture fields set against red soils and scattered salt lakes. Data from the mission’s multispectral instrument allow tracking of vegetation health and irrigation patterns across thousands of square kilometers. The scene demonstrates how routine Sentinel passes support agricultural monitoring in arid zones.

  5. Two New Galileo Satellites Now Operational — ESA Activities

    6. Two satellites have completed commissioning and joined the Galileo navigation constellation. Their addition improves coverage redundancy and signal availability for European users. Four first-generation satellites remain to be launched, with the next pair scheduled for later this year. The expanded constellation strengthens Europe’s independent positioning, navigation, and timing capability.

  6. Spin-Reversing Comet Risks Breakup — Universe Today

    7. Observations show a comet whose rotation has reversed, likely from uneven outgassing torques. The changing spin could stress the nucleus enough to cause fragmentation on future solar approaches. The object is one of many studied to understand how comets evolve after repeated heating cycles. Continued monitoring will track whether the spin state stabilizes or accelerates breakup.

  7. Curiosity Team Reflects on Rover Engineering Legacy — NASA Breaking

    8. The Curiosity blog for sols 4954–4960 highlights collaboration between science planners and rover engineers at the University of New Brunswick. The APXS instrument continues to measure rock chemistry while engineers manage power and mobility trade-offs. Past and present engineering contributions are credited with keeping the rover productive after more than a decade on Mars. The post notes upcoming drive and drill plans that depend on this ongoing coordination.

  8. Hubble Finds First “Missing” Black Hole in Star Cluster — Universe Today

    9. Twenty years of Hubble archival images combined with James Webb Space Telescope infrared data revealed a black hole in a well-studied globular cluster. The detection relied on measuring stellar velocities that could only be explained by an unseen massive object. This marks the first confirmed stellar-mass black hole in such an environment where dynamical ejection was long predicted. Follow-up spectroscopy will refine the mass estimate and test formation models.

  9. GAO Flags NASA Cost and Schedule Risks — Aviation Week

    10. A new Government Accountability Office report notes progress on several NASA programs while warning of continued cost growth and launch delays. The assessment covers major projects including human spaceflight and science missions. Agency officials are reviewing the findings to adjust baselines and contractor oversight. The report will inform upcoming congressional budget deliberations.

  10. Helix Nebula Imaged in 24 Hours of Exposure — Astronomy Magazine

    11. An image of NGC 7293, the Helix Nebula, was assembled from 24.3 hours of Hα, OIII, and LRGB data taken at Farm Tivoli, Namibia. The planetary nebula lies roughly 650 light-years away and surrounds a hot white dwarf. The long integration reveals faint outer shells and intricate filamentary structure. The result demonstrates what dedicated amateur equipment can achieve under dark southern skies.

  11. SETI May Miss Signals on Unexplored Radio Frequencies — Phys.org

    12. Astronomers note that searches for extraterrestrial intelligence have largely avoided a portion of the radio spectrum that could carry artificial signals. New receiver technology now allows efficient coverage of those frequencies. Pilot observations are planned to test whether previously inaccessible channels contain narrowband emissions. The approach could expand the parameter space for technosignature hunts.

  12. NASA Telescope Reveals Previously Hidden Exoplanet — FOX Weather

    13. A NASA space telescope used a novel detection method to identify a planet that had evaded earlier surveys. The technique compensates for stellar activity that masked the planet’s gravitational signature. Confirmation came after multiple observing seasons ruled out false positives. The discovery adds to the census of worlds orbiting stars already known to host planets.

  13. DOE Supplies HALEU Fuel for Space Missions — Metal Tech News

    14. The Department of Energy has allocated high-assay low-enriched uranium for upcoming space nuclear power systems. The fuel will support propulsion and surface power concepts that require higher operating temperatures than traditional reactors. Delivery timelines align with planned demonstration flights later this decade. The move reduces reliance on foreign fuel sources for U.S. space nuclear projects.

  14. SunRISE Mission Switches to Falcon Heavy — Space.com

    15. NASA reassigned the SunRISE heliophysics mission from a Vulcan Centaur to a SpaceX Falcon Heavy. The change follows earlier vehicle delays and allows the six-satellite radio interferometer to reach its planned orbit sooner. The mission will study solar radio bursts from a vantage 1.5 million kilometers from Earth. Launch is now expected on the new vehicle in the coming year.

  15. MAVEN Links Mars Auroras to Miniature Magnetic Process — Phys.org

    16. Data from the MAVEN orbiter show that certain Martian auroras form through magnetic reconnection, a process scaled down from Earth’s magnetosphere. The observations captured electrons accelerated along crustal magnetic fields in the southern hemisphere. The findings explain why auroras appear even without a global magnetic field. Continued MAVEN measurements will map how often reconnection occurs under varying solar wind conditions.

Cosmic Spotlight

Hubble’s detection of the first confirmed stellar-mass black hole inside a globular cluster relied on two decades of proper-motion measurements plus fresh infrared imaging. The unseen object’s gravitational influence on surrounding stars produced velocity signatures that no other explanation fits. This single find supports models predicting that many clusters once held more black holes than they do today, most having been ejected through dynamical interactions. The next step is targeted spectroscopy to pin down the black hole’s mass and test whether similar objects lurk in other clusters already imaged by Hubble and Webb.

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Stellar Velocities Reveal Hidden Black Holes

If every star in a globular cluster moved exactly as expected from visible mass alone, the cluster would fly apart or collapse; instead, precise tracking shows a handful of stars whipping around at speeds that require an invisible central mass of several solar masses. Picture riding alongside one of those stars for a century: its path bends sharply every few years as it swings past an unseen companion, while neighboring stars trace gentler arcs around the cluster’s overall center of mass. Hubble’s repeated imaging over twenty years measures those tiny shifts in position—down to a few milliarcseconds—turning the cluster into a vast natural laboratory where orbital mechanics acts like a balance scale. The method works because a black hole’s gravity dominates within a few light-days even though its event horizon is only tens of kilometers across, so the stellar “test particles” reveal its presence long before any light escapes the horizon itself. Yet the same data leave open a stubborn question: why do some clusters appear to retain their black holes while others seem to have ejected nearly all of them through three-body encounters billions of years ago?

New data keep reshaping how we read the sky—one precise measurement at a time.
