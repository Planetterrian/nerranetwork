Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Falcon 9 Sends 24 Starlink Satellites to Orbit — Spaceflight Now

    2. A Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base at 1635 UTC on August 8 carrying 24 Starlink satellites. The mission marked another step in the ongoing west-coast launch cadence for the constellation. Deployment of the satellites was confirmed shortly after separation from the second stage. The flight used a previously flown booster that returned to a droneship in the Pacific. Liftoff occurred at 9:35 a.m. PDT, which converts to 12:35 p.m. EDT. The launch is designated Starlink Group 17-38.

  2. Self-Driving Mars Rover Breaks Distance Records — Ars Technica

    3. NASA’s newest Mars rover employs autonomous driving software that lets it cover more ground each day without constant commands from Earth. The system has already allowed the vehicle to surpass every prior rover’s total distance traveled. Onboard navigation uses cameras and hazard-detection algorithms to plot safe paths across rough terrain. Engineers report the rover continues to operate well beyond its original design goals. Remember, we covered robotic exploration yesterday — today’s news moves that forward because the autonomy is now delivering record distances.

  3. Sun’s Magnetic Poles Set to Reverse This Year — r/space

    4. The Sun undergoes a full magnetic pole reversal roughly every 11 years as part of its natural cycle. The upcoming flip will affect the structure of the heliosphere but is not expected to disrupt life on Earth. Strong solar activity during the reversal can still produce intense geomagnetic storms capable of damaging satellites and power grids. Observers note that widespread power outages could briefly reduce light pollution and reveal brighter auroras. The inversion occurs as part of the regular 11-year solar cycle.

  4. Andromeda Galaxy Captured in New Image — r/space

    5. The Andromeda Galaxy lies 2.5 million light-years from Earth and is the nearest large spiral galaxy to the Milky Way. A recent photograph highlights its bright central bulge and sweeping spiral arms. The image was taken with standard camera equipment under dark skies. Andromeda remains a key target for studying how galaxies similar to our own evolve over time. The photo was submitted by user Resolute_Protector6 and credited to Arthur Debons via Unsplash.

  5. APOD Highlights Contemplating the Sun — NASA Science

    6. NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day for August 9 features a striking view of the Sun’s surface and atmosphere. The image shows active regions, prominences, and the textured photosphere in fine detail. Daily APOD selections draw from both professional observatories and skilled amateur astrophotographers. The series continues to introduce new audiences to solar phenomena. The post appeared first on the NASA Science website.

  6. Webb Telescope Detects Hidden Exoplanet Beta Pictoris d — curiosmos.com

    7. The James Webb Space Telescope has identified a previously unseen planet orbiting the young star Beta Pictoris. The world appears in infrared data that penetrates the dusty debris disk surrounding the star. Its presence helps astronomers map the architecture of this nearby planetary system. Follow-up observations are planned to refine the planet’s orbit and mass. The discovery was reported through the curiosmos.com news feed.

  7. Intel Proposes Orbital Data Centers for Satellite Networks — r/space

    8. A concept from Intel envisions placing powerful computing nodes in higher orbits to manage thousands of simple low-Earth-orbit satellites. The two-tier architecture would move data processing away from the crowded lower constellation layers. Proponents argue the approach could reduce latency and power demands on individual satellites. The idea remains in early study phase. The proposal was submitted by user esporx on r/space.

  8. Brown Dwarf Hosts 0.9-Jupiter-Mass Companion — r/space

    9. Astronomers report a planetary-mass object of roughly 0.9 Jupiter masses circling a brown dwarf that itself contains 29 Jupiter masses. No consensus yet exists on whether the companion should be classified as a planet or a moon. The detection adds to the small sample of substellar systems with known companions. Further spectroscopy may clarify formation pathways. The abstract of the paper appeared in Nature and was discussed by user CurtisLeow.

  9. Northrop Grumman and CSA Adapt Gateway Hardware for Lunar Base — SpaceNews

    10. Northrop Grumman and the Canadian Space Agency are repurposing elements originally developed for NASA’s lunar Gateway station. The hardware will now support a planned surface lunar base instead. Few technical details have been released about the new applications. The shift reflects changing priorities in NASA’s lunar exploration roadmap. The report appeared first on the SpaceNews website.

  10. Infographic Maps Every Lunar Surface Mission — r/space

    11. A new visualization plots every spacecraft that has reached the Moon’s surface from 1959 to the present. The graphic uses NASA elevation data to show landing sites and rover traverse routes. Mission statistics are included for each entry. The project compiles decades of exploration into a single reference image. The infographic was submitted by user Informental.

  11. Reduced Gravity Alters Human Movement Patterns — Phys.org Space

    12. Texas A&M researchers supported by NASA are testing how people perform everyday tasks when gravity is lowered. Early results indicate subjects adopt simpler, more economical motion strategies. The experiments use varying levels of simulated reduced gravity to mimic lunar and Martian conditions. Data will help design future habitats and tools. The multiphase project explores movement from reaching for objects to tying shoelaces.

  12. Hipparcos Satellite Launched 37 Years Ago — Astronomy

    13. ESA’s Hipparcos mission lifted off on August 8, 1989, to perform high-precision astrometry. A rocket-engine failure left the satellite in the wrong orbit, yet operators still extracted valuable star-position data. The mission pioneered space-based measurement of stellar distances and motions. Its catalog remains a foundational reference for modern astronomy. The article continues on the Astronomy Magazine website.

  13. Crescent Moon to Pass Near Mars Before Dawn — Space.com

    14. On August 9, early risers can see a slender crescent Moon close to the red planet in the constellation Taurus. The pairing will be visible low in the eastern sky before sunrise. No special equipment is required for the conjunction. Observers are encouraged to watch from locations with clear eastern horizons. The event occurs in the predawn hours.

  14. Partial Solar Eclipse Visible Across North America on Aug. 12 — Space.com

    15. The Moon will take a noticeable bite out of the Sun for viewers in parts of the United States and Canada on August 12. The eclipse will not reach totality but still offers a safe observing opportunity with proper filters. Maximum coverage varies by location. Organizers remind the public never to look directly at the Sun without certified solar filters. The event will be visible to millions across the continent.

  15. Sky-Watcher SolarQuest Mount Simplifies Solar Viewing — Space.com

    16. The SolarQuest mount automatically locates and tracks the Sun for small telescopes. Designed for quick setup, it removes the need for manual alignment during solar observing sessions. The mount supports both visual use and eclipse photography. Reviewers note its compact size makes it convenient for travel. The product is positioned as a grab-and-go solution for solar astronomy.

This approach trades some risk for far greater daily progress across the Martian surface. The same technology could one day support sample-collection traverses or scouting routes for crewed missions. Cosmic Deep Dive: The Sun’s 11-Year Magnetic Flip

The Sun’s magnetic field reverses direction every 11 years, a cycle whose scale is hard to picture until you map it onto something familiar. Picture a star whose surface alone spans 1.4 million kilometres flipping its entire magnetic layout the way a handheld compass needle swings, except this star holds 330,000 times Earth’s mass and its field lines extend past Neptune. During the reversal the Sun’s north and south magnetic poles trade places, driven by the twisting motion of plasma deep inside the star where differential rotation winds up the field until it becomes unstable and snaps into the opposite orientation. One concrete number captures the stakes: the reversal coincides with solar maximum, when the number of sunspots can climb above 150 and the solar wind carries roughly twice the magnetic flux it does at minimum. Magnetographs track the poles migrating across the surface, and forecasters can time the flip to within months, yet no model fully explains why the cycle length stays so steady while the strength of each reversal varies. We can watch the poles migrate using magnetographs and even predict the timing to within a few months, yet no model fully explains why the cycle length stays so steady while the strength of each reversal varies. What hidden clock inside the Sun keeps resetting the field on such a regular timetable?
