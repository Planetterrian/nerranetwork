NASA’s next flagship observatory has reached Florida for final checks before an August launch on a Falcon Heavy.

Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope arrives in Florida — Spaceflight Now

    2. The $4.3 billion observatory touched down at Kennedy Space Center on June 21. It will spend roughly 70 days in prelaunch processing before riding a SpaceX Falcon Heavy no earlier than August 30. The telescope’s wide-field infrared instrument is designed to survey large sky areas for exoplanets and dark energy measurements. Engineers will now integrate the spacecraft with its launch adapter and run final environmental tests. Watch for the first images from the observatory once it reaches its L2 halo orbit.

  2. SpaceX has launched more satellites than all others combined — Space.com

    3. The company’s cumulative orbital deliveries now exceed the total from every other launch provider in history. The gap is expected to keep growing as Starlink missions continue at a high cadence. This dominance stems from reusable Falcon 9 first stages that return to landing platforms or ground pads after each flight. No other operator has matched the flight rate or payload capacity achieved so far. Future Starship flights could widen the margin further once they enter regular service.

  3. Blueprint outlines steps to assess Mars terraforming — Space.com

    4. Researchers have mapped the measurements needed to judge whether warming the Red Planet is feasible. The plan focuses on releasing greenhouse gases or deploying orbital mirrors to raise surface temperatures enough for liquid water. Key unknowns include how much carbon dioxide remains trapped in the polar caps and regolith. Data from current orbiters and future landers will feed into climate models that test each scenario. The study provides a clear checklist rather than claiming success is assured.

  4. Secret Starfall capsule targets in-space manufacturing — Teslarati

    5. SpaceX is preparing a disk-shaped reentry vehicle for a demonstration flight. The capsule is sized to return small batches of materials processed in microgravity. No prior commercial reentry system has offered routine, low-cost sample return from orbit. Engineers are targeting markets such as fiber optics and pharmaceuticals that benefit from extended weightless processing. A successful test would open a new class of orbital production missions.

  5. Bering Sea shows early summer ice breakup — NASA

    6. Satellite views captured drifting ice fragments near Saint Lawrence and Nunivak islands. Colorful sediment plumes around the Yukon Delta mark the seasonal transition. The imagery comes from instruments monitoring Arctic sea-ice extent and ocean color. These observations help track how quickly open water appears each year. Continued monitoring will show whether the pattern repeats in coming seasons.

  6. Earth microbes survive simulated Mars conditions — Phys.org

    7. Experiments exposed microorganisms to low pressure, cold temperatures, and radiation levels modeled on the Martian surface. Several strains remained viable after the exposure period. The work also found that the same microbes triggered weaker immune responses in test subjects. Results suggest that any future sample-return hardware must account for hardy terrestrial contaminants. The findings apply equally to protecting Earth from potential Martian material.

  7. North America Nebula climbs high after midnight — Astronomy Magazine

    8. NGC 7000 in Cygnus reaches nearly 70 degrees elevation in the eastern sky by 2 a.m. local daylight time. The emission nebula’s shape resembles the continent of North America when viewed through narrowband filters. Observers with modest telescopes or binoculars can locate it by sweeping from Deneb toward the Cygnus star cloud. The object is a favorite summer target because it lies in a rich Milky Way field. Clear, dark skies away from city lights give the best contrast.

  8. Appalachian region traces path from coal to orbit — Cardinal News

    9. The story follows how communities once centered on coal extraction are now contributing components and talent to space programs. Local manufacturers supply precision parts for launch vehicles and satellites. Workforce programs are retraining miners and engineers for aerospace roles. The transition illustrates one regional example of economic shifts tied to the space economy. More suppliers from the area are expected as launch demand grows.

  9. Jackal satellite reached orbit on May Falcon 9 rideshare — Jonathan McDowell

    10. The small spacecraft launched aboard the CAS500-2 mission on May 3. It joined a cluster of other secondary payloads released into low Earth orbit. Tracking data confirm the satellite is operating as planned. Such rideshare flights allow rapid deployment of technology demonstrators without dedicated rockets. Additional details on its mission objectives are still limited.

  10. Astroscale secures capital for satellite-servicing missions — SpaceNews

    11. The company is moving from single demonstration flights to a sustained series of on-orbit servicing operations. The new funds support development of rendezvous and capture hardware for multiple clients. Servicing spacecraft could extend the life of commercial and government satellites already in orbit. The shift marks a step toward routine orbital maintenance rather than one-off tests. Next milestones include hardware qualification and customer contracts.

  11. Seminars highlight upcoming infrared and AGN research — NASA

    12. The IR STIG PRIMA series features Bogdan Pastrav of the Institute of Space Science on June 22. Separate AGN and cosmic-origins sessions run later in the week with early-career presenters. Topics range from galaxy evolution to new instrumentation concepts. The talks are open to the community via the P-CAST webpage. Recordings will be archived for later viewing.

  12. Community workshop set for September to shape future missions — NASA

    13. The Ad ASTRA event runs September 1–3 and will gather input on the next generation of large astrophysics observatories. Participants will help define science priorities and technology needs. The workshop is organized by NASA’s Astrophysics Division. Outcomes are expected to feed into the upcoming decadal survey process. Registration details appear on the Cosmic Origins program site.

  13. AI’s role in research raises questions about originality — Nature

    14. A new commentary examines whether large language models will favor rapid but conventional results over novel ideas. Reviewers and funders may need new incentives to reward unexpected findings. The piece notes that model training data already reflect past publication patterns. Without deliberate changes, the diversity of scientific questions could narrow. The discussion continues among journal editors and funding agencies.

  14. Sky watchers can catch the North America Nebula this week — Astronomy Magazine

    15. The object is best seen after midnight when Cygnus is high overhead. Narrowband imaging reveals the glowing hydrogen regions that form the nebula’s outline. Even small telescopes show the dark lane representing the “Gulf of Mexico.” Conditions are favorable through the end of the month. Observers are encouraged to try both visual and photographic approaches.

  15. Pathogen studies inform planetary-protection protocols — Phys.org

    16. The dissertation work tested how simulated space travel alters microbial behavior. Results indicate that some Earth organisms could persist on icy worlds where liquid water exists. The research also has implications for crew health on long-duration missions. Supervisors note the dual use of the data for both extraterrestrial and terrestrial applications. Follow-on experiments are planned with additional microbe strains.

Cosmic Spotlight

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope has now arrived at Kennedy Space Center for its final prelaunch campaign. The observatory will spend about seventy days undergoing integration and testing before its scheduled Falcon Heavy liftoff. Its wide-field instrument is built to capture thousands of galaxies in single exposures, enabling statistical studies of dark energy and exoplanet microlensing events. Once at L2, the telescope will begin a five-year survey that complements JWST’s narrower but deeper fields. The arrival marks the shift from construction to operations readiness. What calibration targets will engineers choose first once the telescope reaches its halo orbit?

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Constellations Maintain Safe Orbital Spacing

If every Starlink satellite were the size of a city bus, the shells they occupy would still be separated by only a few tens of kilometers vertically. Here is what actually happens when two satellites approach: ground operators upload a small thrust command days ahead, nudging one craft a few hundred meters aside so their paths never intersect. The adjustment uses less than a kilogram of propellant per year per satellite because the relative velocities are modest and the warning time is long. At 550 km altitude the orbital period is roughly 95 minutes, so each satellite passes over any given ground point 15 times a day. The real puzzle is how operators will keep the same spacing once thousands more satellites from competing constellations join the same altitude bands. We can already compute the probability of close approaches, but no one yet knows the practical limit before active coordination becomes impossible.

New hardware and fresh data keep arriving—watch this space.

Ground operators upload a small thrust command days ahead when two satellites approach. The nudge moves one craft a few hundred meters aside so their paths never intersect. The adjustment uses less than a kilogram of propellant per year per satellite because the relative velocities are modest and the warning time is long. At five hundred fifty kilometers altitude the orbital period is roughly ninety-five minutes, so each satellite passes over any given ground point fifteen times a day. The real puzzle is how operators will keep the same spacing once thousands more satellites from competing constellations join the same altitude bands. We can already compute the probability of close approaches, but no one yet knows the practical limit before active coordination becomes impossible.
