A Canadian university lab is teaming up with the Canadian Space Agency to explore how living systems could manufacture materials beyond Earth.

Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. University lab partners with Canadian Space Agency on biomanufacturing — Lethbridge News Now

    2. The University of Lethbridge lab will investigate how microorganisms and engineered cells can produce useful compounds and materials in microgravity or on other planetary surfaces. The project focuses on extraterrestrial biomanufacturing techniques that could support long-duration missions by generating food, pharmaceuticals, or construction materials on site. Researchers aim to understand how altered gravity and radiation environments affect cellular processes used in production. The collaboration builds on existing Canadian expertise in space biology and seeks to develop closed-loop systems that reduce reliance on resupply from Earth. Next steps include ground-based simulations followed by potential experiments on the International Space Station. The work draws on the lab’s prior studies of cellular responses to space conditions. Canadian Space Agency support will help translate ground findings into flight-ready hardware. Results could inform how future crews sustain themselves without constant cargo deliveries.

  2. MIT team wins first prize in NASA LunaRecycle Challenge Phase 2 — NASA Breaking

    3. The Composites for Extraterrestrial Recycling By Engineering the Reuse and Upcycling of Zotek (CERBERUZ) team from MIT received $775,000 for its system that converts mixed waste into reusable powder. The approach grinds fabrics, plastics, foam, and metals into a fine material that can be reformed into new parts or structures during lunar or deep-space missions. NASA designed the challenge to address the growing problem of waste accumulation on long-duration flights where every kilogram of trash must be managed without return to Earth. The winning technology emphasizes upcycling rather than simple disposal, turning discarded items into feedstock for additive manufacturing. The CERBERUZ team includes undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral students at MIT. Follow-on work will test the process in relevant environments to refine particle size and material properties. The award reflects NASA’s priority on sustainable logistics for crews operating far from Earth.

  3. Jeremy Hansen receives U.S. Space Medal of Honor as first non-NASA recipient — The Lethbridge Herald

    4. Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen was awarded the Congressional Space Medal of Honor for his role on the Artemis II crew that flew around the Moon. The honor marks the first time the medal has gone to someone outside NASA’s astronaut corps. Hansen’s selection highlights the international nature of the Artemis program and Canada’s contributions through the Canadian Space Agency. The medal recognizes the crew’s successful demonstration of Orion spacecraft systems in lunar orbit. Future Artemis missions will build on this flight to advance crewed lunar landing capabilities. Hansen becomes the first non-NASA astronaut to receive the distinction. The award ceremony took place at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston.

  4. Virgin Galactic opens public vote to name next spaceplane — Astronomy

    5. Virgin Galactic extended the deadline for naming its next-generation spaceplane, giving the public until Thursday to submit and vote on suggestions. The company retired its earlier SpaceShipTwo vehicle after completing its first commercial flight in 2023 and has not conducted another since 2024. The new vehicles are intended for suborbital research and tourism flights. The naming contest aims to engage the public in the company’s next phase of operations. Results will be announced after the voting period closes. The extension allows more time for enthusiasts to participate in shaping the identity of the upcoming fleet.

  5. SpaceX recovers Starship from Indian Ocean and begins return to Texas — Phys.org Space

    6. SpaceX completed recovery of the most recent Starship test article from the Indian Ocean roughly a month after its launch. The spacecraft is now being transported back to Starbase for inspection and analysis. Engineers will examine the vehicle’s heat shield and structure to assess performance during reentry and splashdown. This marks the largest salvage operation the company has conducted for a Starship prototype. Data from the recovered hardware will inform upgrades ahead of future test flights. The operation demonstrates the feasibility of retrieving large orbital vehicles after water landings. Post-flight analysis will focus on thermal protection system integrity and structural loads.

  6. NASA releases replay videos of Sentinel-6B launch — NASA Science

    7. The agency posted launch footage of Sentinel-6B, which lifted off aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Vandenberg Space Force Base. The satellite continues the long-term record of sea-level measurements begun by earlier missions in the series. Sentinel-6B will monitor ocean height, temperature, and salinity to support climate and weather research. The videos allow the public to review the full ascent sequence and spacecraft separation. The mission extends the Jason series of ocean-observing satellites that have operated since 1992. Data from Sentinel-6B will feed into models used by weather forecasters and climate scientists worldwide.

  7. President Trump addresses Expedition 75 crew from Johnson Space Center — NASA Science

    8. President Trump spoke with the Expedition 75 astronauts after presenting the Congressional Space Medal of Honor to the Artemis II crew at the same location. The conversation covered ongoing station operations and future exploration goals. Expedition 75 continues science and maintenance activities aboard the International Space Station. The call occurred during the president’s visit to NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas. Crew members discussed daily research tasks and preparations for upcoming resupply missions. The exchange highlighted coordination between national leadership and orbital operations.

Cosmic Spotlight

The MIT CERBERUZ team’s waste-to-powder system turns everyday trash into manufacturing feedstock by grinding mixed materials to a uniform particle size. This approach could let crews on the Moon or Mars turn packaging and worn equipment into new tools or habitat components without waiting for resupply. The $775,000 award funds further testing to ensure the powder works reliably in additive manufacturing systems under lunar gravity and vacuum conditions. The team’s process handles fabrics, plastics, foam, and metals together, avoiding the need for separate sorting streams that would add complexity on long missions. What remains unclear is how radiation exposure over months or years will alter the recycled material’s strength and chemical stability once it leaves Earth’s protective environment.

Cosmic Deep Dive: Turning Trash Into Tools Off Earth

Imagine every kilogram of waste generated on a lunar base must either be stored forever or turned into something useful, because lifting replacement materials from Earth costs thousands of dollars per kilogram. The LunaRecycle challenge winner shows one path forward: grind mixed plastics, fabrics, and metals into a fine powder that can be fed into 3D printers to create new parts on demand. In practice, a single crew member might produce several kilograms of trash each week; over a year that adds up to hundreds of kilograms that could instead become brackets, insulation panels, or even simple tools. The surprising number is the $775,000 prize itself, which reflects how seriously NASA takes the logistics problem of sustaining crews far from resupply. The CERBERUZ approach avoids pre-sorting by treating all common spacecraft materials as a single feedstock stream. Yet the open question is whether repeated grinding and reforming will preserve the mechanical properties needed for structural parts, or whether radiation and thermal cycling will make the recycled material brittle in ways we cannot yet predict from ground tests alone.

Space news continues to show steady progress on the practical challenges of living and working beyond Earth.
