Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. ESA Fuels Photosynthesis Satellite for September Launch — ESA Activities

    2. Europe’s newest eye on the health of Earth’s vegetation has taken another crucial step towards orbit. The European Space Agency’s FLEX satellite has been fuelled at Europe’s Spaceport in French Guiana. The spacecraft will measure fluorescence from vegetation to track plant health and photosynthetic activity across Earth. Launch is set for 15 September at 03:21 CEST, which corresponds to 14 September at 22:21 local time. FLEX carries a single instrument designed to detect faint light emitted by plants during photosynthesis. Engineers will now complete final checks before the satellite moves to the launch pad. The mission will operate in low Earth orbit and deliver daily global maps of vegetation stress.

  2. Commercial Rescue Attempt for Swift Observatory Ends in Failure — People.com

    3. NASA has confirmed that the LINK spacecraft will not boost the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory to a higher orbit. An ongoing attitude control issue on the commercial vehicle prevented the planned capture and reboost. The observatory will remain in its current orbit and is expected to re-enter the atmosphere later this year. Swift has provided ultraviolet and X-ray observations of gamma-ray bursts and other transients since 2004. No alternative reboost option is currently available. The decision follows an extended period during which teams assessed whether the commercial spacecraft could still complete its task.

  3. Roman Telescope Will See a Different Slice of Light Than Hubble or Webb — NASA

    4. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will observe from near-infrared into some visible wavelengths. Hubble covers near-infrared through ultraviolet while Webb spans near-infrared to mid-infrared. Each instrument therefore captures distinct features of the same cosmic targets. Roman’s wide-field near-infrared camera is optimized for large-scale surveys of galaxies and exoplanets. The differences in wavelength coverage allow complementary data sets when all three telescopes study overlapping sky regions. Mission planners note that the combination of perspectives strengthens studies of cosmic evolution and exoplanet atmospheres.

  4. Large Iceberg Drifts Unusually Far South in Denmark Strait — NASA Science

    5. An iceberg originating from northeastern Greenland traveled more than 1,000 kilometers south during summer 2026. The drift path took the berg through the Denmark Strait between Greenland and Iceland. NASA Earth Observatory imagery tracked the iceberg’s movement and melt. Such long-distance drifts are uncommon for icebergs from that source region. The observations help refine models of iceberg trajectories and their impact on ocean circulation. Researchers continue to monitor how these events influence local salinity and marine ecosystems.

  5. Nuclear Power Studied to Keep Future Lunar Missions Warm at Night — WESH

    6. Engineers are examining radioisotope power systems to supply heat and electricity during the two-week lunar night. Surface temperatures drop below minus 180 degrees Celsius, halting most solar-powered operations. Nuclear sources would allow landers and rovers to continue working through the darkness. The approach builds on systems already used on Mars rovers and outer-planet probes. Development focuses on compact, lightweight units suitable for crewed and robotic missions. The technology could extend surface operations at the lunar south pole where sunlight is limited for extended periods.

  6. Voyager 1 Nears One Light-Day From Earth — NASA Science

    7. The spacecraft is approaching the milestone distance light travels in a single day. That distance equals roughly 26 billion kilometers. Voyager 1 has been traveling outward since 1977 and currently moves at about 17 kilometers per second relative to the Sun. Mission teams use the benchmark to illustrate the scale of interstellar space. No other human-made object has reached this range. The milestone underscores how far the probe has traveled while still returning data from beyond the heliopause.

  7. Eartheye Space Signs Analytics Partnership With IN2 — SpaceNews

    8. The companies signed a memorandum of understanding on 19 August giving IN2 access to Eartheye’s multi-sensor satellite tasking. IN2 will use the data for consulting projects that require Earth-observation imagery and analysis. The agreement expands commercial use of Eartheye’s constellation without requiring new satellite hardware. Both organizations expect the partnership to support clients needing rapid tasking and derived products from multiple sensors.

  8. Earth Microbes Could Survive in Lunar South Pole Shadows — Phys.org Space

    9. NASA researchers modeled conditions in permanently shadowed craters near the lunar south pole. Some terrestrial microbes could remain viable in these cold, dark niches if carried by future landers. The study highlights the need for planetary-protection measures during crewed exploration. Survival would depend on protection from radiation and extreme temperature swings. The findings inform ongoing discussions about contamination risks at potential Artemis landing sites.

  9. Chinese Company LandSpace Achieves First Orbital Booster Landing — Spaceflight Now

    10. LandSpace’s ZhuQue-3 rocket completed a controlled landing using landing legs on 19 August. The flight marks the first time a Chinese commercial firm has returned an orbital-class booster. The vehicle touched down after delivering its payload, demonstrating reusability technology. The successful recovery represents a milestone for China’s emerging private launch sector.

  10. New Method Proposed to Detect Space Resources Without Landing — Phys.org Space

    11. A SETI Institute team is developing the Interworld Slingshot concept to identify surface materials from orbit. The technique would use targeted impacts or gas releases to loft dust for spectroscopic analysis. The approach avoids the cost and complexity of drilling or sample return. Early modeling focuses on the Moon and near-Earth asteroids. Lead researcher Pablo Sobron and colleagues are refining the method to work with existing orbital platforms.

  11. Liquid Nitrogen May Be Rising Through Cracks on Pluto — Universe Today

    12. New Horizons images show evidence that liquid nitrogen is moving upward along fractures in Sputnik Planitia. The process could explain dark streaks and surface wetting observed along the northern edge of the heart-shaped plain. The finding adds to understanding of Pluto’s ongoing geological activity driven by subsurface heat. Data from the 2015 flyby continue to yield new insights into the dwarf planet’s interior dynamics.

  12. Plasma “Laundry Gun” Designed to Sterilize Astronaut Clothing — Space.com

    13. A pen-sized device fires plasma to kill microbes on fabrics without water or detergents. The technology targets odor-causing bacteria that accumulate during long missions. Laboratory tests showed effective sterilization on common spaceflight textiles. The tool could reduce the mass of clothing carried on future deep-space flights. Developers are now evaluating integration pathways for use aboard the International Space Station and future exploration vehicles.

Cosmic Spotlight

NASA’s Swift Observatory rescue mission has ended without a reboost, leaving the satellite on a collision course with Earth’s atmosphere later this year. The commercial LINK spacecraft could not overcome its attitude control problem in time to perform the capture maneuver. Swift has operated for more than two decades, delivering rapid ultraviolet and X-ray follow-up of cosmic explosions. Its loss will remove one of the few instruments capable of immediate multi-wavelength observations of transient events. What follow-up observations will astronomers prioritize once Swift is no longer available?

Cosmic Deep Dive: Crossing One Light-Day

One light-day equals the distance light covers in twenty-four hours—roughly 26 billion kilometers, or about 650 times the average Earth-Moon distance. Imagine riding alongside a photon as it leaves Earth: in one second it has already passed the Moon, in eight minutes it reaches the Sun, yet a full day later it is still only a tiny fraction of the way to the nearest star. Voyager 1, now traveling at 17 kilometers per second, will need another 20,000 years to cover that same interval at its current speed. The milestone therefore marks not just distance but the immense gulf between our fastest spacecraft and the rest of the galaxy. We can calculate the number precisely, yet we still do not know how the solar wind and interstellar medium interact across that final stretch of Voyager’s journey.

New instruments and missions keep revealing how little we still know about the systems closest to home and farthest away.
