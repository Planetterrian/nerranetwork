Top 12 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. ExoMars spots hydrogen chloride over Martian fissures — European Space Agency

    The ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter detected hydrogen chloride above volcanic fissures in the Alba Mons region. The gas appeared in early northern summer, outside the usual seasonal pattern tied to dust and water cycles. Fresh narrow fissures hundreds of meters long showed color changes consistent with deposits from escaping gases. Researchers link the release to hot magma beneath the surface. The finding suggests Mars retains enough internal heat for volcanic activity until recently or still today. An international team led by Alexander Trokhimovskiy and co-lead author Daniel Mège analyzed data from the Atmospheric Chemistry Suite infrared spectrometers and the CaSSIS camera. The team ruled out atmospheric chemistry cycles after examining all possible chemical processes. They concluded the seepage connects to a dormant magma chamber beneath the surface. The discoloration around the fissures matches deposits seen around fumaroles on Earth. The Trace Gas Orbiter spotted the handful of molecules while continuously monitoring the whole Martian atmosphere from orbit. Source: x.com Source: phys.org

  2. NASA’s Swift observatory reenters over Indonesia — Qazinform

    The 1,450-kilogram Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory reentered at 7:39 a.m. EST over northern Sumatra. The spacecraft ended 22 years of gamma-ray burst and supernova observations after an attempted orbit boost failed. Most of the craft was expected to burn up, with debris injury risk estimated at one in 2,100. Its final observation occurred October 20. The observatory was launched in November 2004 for an initial two-year mission but operated nearly two decades beyond that. It was renamed in 2018 to honor principal investigator Neil Gehrels. Shawn Domagal-Goldman, director of NASA’s Astrophysics Division, noted that Swift delivered an incredible return on investment. The reentry followed a private spacecraft attempt in July that lost control and could not raise the orbit. Source: qazinform.com

  3. NASA unveils X-70 Fractal experimental aircraft — The Aviationist

    The crewed X-70 Fractal will test rapidly reconfigurable flight-control technology. It cruises at Mach 0.75, reaches 45,000 feet, and offers more than 1,500 nautical miles range. Takeoff weight is 10,000 pounds, with up to 2,000 pounds for internal or external payloads. First flight is expected within two years. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman announced the aircraft at the UP.Summit at Kennedy Space Center. The modular design allows rapid swaps of internal and external payloads. Laurie Grindle, director of the Aeronautics Division, said the X-70 joins a storied legacy of X-planes that advanced American aviation. The aircraft will explore maneuverability in ways never before demonstrated in crewed flight. Isaacman noted the science fiction future imagined as children is ours to build. Source: theaviationist.com Source: nasa.gov

  4. NASA awards Ka-band communications contracts — Aviation Week

    Three firms received contracts worth up to $1.065 billion to develop commercial Ka-band relay services. Astranis was awarded up to $264.3 million, Global Enterprises Solutions up to $340.4 million, and Viasat up to $460.9 million. The work will replace the aging Tracking and Data Relay Satellite fleet. Each firm-fixed price contract gives NASA options to advance from concept development to orbital flight demonstration. The initial performance period extends through May 2027, with further options through 2029. The selections support NASA’s decision to retire the TDRS network and adopt commercial services. Seven TDRS satellites remain active supporting the International Space Station and Hubble until decommissioning. Source: aviationweek.com

  5. Kutryk and Menon complete final ISS solar array spacewalk — Space.com RSS

    Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk and NASA’s Anil Menon installed the eighth and last roll-out solar array. The pair finished the upgrade that raises station power from 160 to about 215 kilowatts. The roughly seven-hour spacewalk began at 8:30 a.m. EST. Kutryk became the fifth Canadian to perform a spacewalk on October 29 when the duo installed seven of the eight arrays. Today’s extravehicular activity was the 100th U.S. spacewalk from the ISS and the first for a Canadian since 2019. The five-year effort to augment the station’s power generating capacity is now complete. Kutryk and Menon are sharing the station with five other crew members from NASA, CSA, and Roscosmos. Source: space.com Source: kelownadailycourier.ca

  6. Starfish Otter spacecraft loses contact — Aviation Week

    The Otter spacecraft lost its communications link during commissioning. The loss was traced to an issue in the fault protection system. The mission was testing propulsion, navigation, and inspection technologies for orbital debris under a $15 million NASA SBIR Phase III agreement. The spacecraft launched October 1 from Vandenberg Space Force Base. Technologies integrated included the Manta robot arm, Nautilus docking mechanism, Cetacean navigation software, and Cephalopod guidance software. NASA and Starfish determined the two-way link could not be re-established. Future Otter missions will continue gathering data on in-space servicing technologies. Source: aviationweek.com

  7. Alex MacDonald named Canadian Space Agency president — SpaceQ

    Alex MacDonald, NASA’s former chief economist, was appointed CSA president. He succeeds Lisa Campbell and acting president Jean-Claude Piedboeuf. MacDonald has urged faster procurement and a shift toward lunar surface robotics after NASA paused the Lunar Gateway. He spent 17 years at NASA, starting at Ames Research Center and later serving as founding program executive of the Emerging Space Office. MacDonald authored the 2017 book The Long Space Age and has advocated for Canada to buy rides on NASA Commercial Lunar Payload Services landers in 2027 and 2028. He also highlighted opportunities in space nuclear power, medical systems, and food production for Canadian firms. Source: spaceq.ca Source: kelownadailycourier.ca

  8. China details crewed lunar landing plan — Universe Today

    Deputy chief designer Wang Xiang outlined China’s crewed Moon landing architecture. The plan includes new launch vehicles and a lunar lander to place astronauts on the surface. The announcement came on November 3 from the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation. Source: universetoday.com

  9. TOI-561 b shows persistent atmosphere despite molten surface — Inkorr

    The ultra-short-period planet TOI-561 b maintains an atmosphere despite a global magma ocean. The finding challenges models that predict rapid atmospheric loss on such worlds. The planet orbits its star so closely that its surface is a molten rock ocean. Source: inkorr.com

  10. Enceladus appears in silhouette with active plumes — NASA Breaking

    Cassini captured the 500-kilometer moon backlit by the Sun, revealing south-polar plumes that feed Saturn’s E ring. Pandora, 80 kilometers across, appears faintly lit by Saturnlight beyond the rings. The image from November 1, 2009 shows the icy surface reflecting light like fresh snow. The plumes were first discovered by Cassini in 2005 and are likely connected to an ocean beneath the ice shell. Source: science.nasa.gov

  11. UT San Antonio receives NASA grant for lunar soil study — UT San Antonio Today

    NASA awarded the University of Texas at San Antonio $1 million to study icy lunar regolith properties. The research will examine how ice behaves in lunar soil samples. Source: news.utsa.edu

  12. Cramer discusses commercial space future at UND summit — Senator Kevin Cramer

    Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota hosted Rocket Lab leadership to discuss the future of the commercial space industry. The summit focused on industry growth and policy support. Source: cramer.senate.gov

Cosmic Spotlight

Cassini’s 2009 image shows Enceladus backlit by the Sun, with its south-polar plumes clearly visible against the darkness. The 500-kilometer icy moon supplies material to Saturn’s faint E ring while its surface reflects light like fresh snow. Beyond the rings, 80-kilometer Pandora glows faintly from reflected Saturnlight. The view captures one of the solar system’s most active ocean worlds in dramatic profile. The plumes continuously spew into space and were first spotted by Cassini in 2005. Scientists believe they connect directly to a subsurface ocean. Source: science.nasa.gov

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Backlit Imaging Reveals Hidden Activity on Icy Moons

Imagine standing on the night side of Saturn and looking toward the Sun with a 500-kilometer ball of ice hanging between you and the light. That is the perspective Cassini had on November 1, 2009 when it captured Enceladus in silhouette. The geometry turns the moon into a dark disk edged by brilliant sunlight, and any material leaving its surface becomes visible as bright streaks against the black of space. Enceladus is only 500 kilometers across, yet its south-polar plumes stretch hundreds of kilometers into the vacuum, feeding particles directly into Saturn’s outermost E ring. The same image shows 80-kilometer Pandora glowing faintly from Saturnlight reflected off the planet’s icy rings, a reminder that even small moons can be lit by their parent world when the Sun is behind them. Cassini’s cameras recorded the scene while the spacecraft itself moved at several kilometers per second, yet the long exposure still froze the plumes in place because the particles were moving outward at roughly the escape speed from Enceladus’ weak gravity. The surface itself appears as reflective as fresh snow, an effect produced by the constant rain of tiny ice grains falling back from the plumes. Scientists first spotted these jets in 2005 and quickly realized they could not be explained by sunlight alone; the only plausible source was liquid water beneath the ice shell kept warm by tidal flexing from Saturn. The 2009 backlit view remains one of the clearest demonstrations that the activity is ongoing and localized at the south pole. What still puzzles researchers is why the plumes appear in discrete jets rather than a uniform sheet, and whether the fractures that feed them open and close with Enceladus’ orbital cycle around Saturn. Future missions equipped with mass spectrometers and higher-resolution imagers could sample the plume material directly and map the fractures in three dimensions, but until then the silhouette images remain the most direct evidence that this small moon is venting its interior ocean into space.
