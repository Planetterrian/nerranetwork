Top 15 Space & Astronomy Stories

  1. NASA cancels Gateway and upper stage amid major delays — Ars Technica

    2. An analysis shows the canceled Exploration Upper Stage and Gateway elements were years behind schedule. The inspector general report details how NASA had already spent heavily on a simple Orion stage adapter before termination. Remember, the show covered Artemis on June 24 — today’s development clarifies why the agency shifted resources away from those elements. The move frees budget for other lunar landing hardware still in development. Watch for the next inspector general update on remaining Artemis program milestones. Source: arstechnica.com

  2. Euclid delivers largest visible-light image of Milky Way center — European Space Agency

    3. The Euclid mission released its widest and sharpest view yet of the galactic center in visible wavelengths. The image covers a crowded field packed with stars and dust that ground telescopes struggle to resolve. Euclid’s wide-field camera captured the data during routine survey operations. Scientists will use the mosaic to map stellar populations and dust lanes across the inner galaxy. Future data releases will add near-infrared layers to the same region. Source: esa.int

  3. Canada outlines path to greater space leadership — Financial Post

    4. A new analysis argues Canada can expand its role through targeted investments in robotics and Earth observation. The report highlights existing strengths in satellite technology and astronaut contributions. It recommends closer coordination between government, universities, and industry. No specific funding figures are attached to the proposals. Next steps depend on upcoming federal budget decisions. Source: Google News

  4. Space Shuttle Endeavour placed on permanent display in California — Phys.org

    5. The orbiter that flew 25 missions now stands vertically at the California Science Center. Workers completed the final stacking and unveiling this week. The display shows Endeavour mated with its external tank and solid rocket boosters. Visitors can view the vehicle from multiple levels inside the Samuel Oschin Pavilion. The installation marks the end of the shuttle’s post-flight journey. Source: phys.org

  5. Australian crater confirmed as Earth’s oldest known impact site — Space.com

    6. The structure in Western Australia formed roughly three billion years ago. Earlier dating left its age uncertain, but new analysis of shocked minerals and impact melt settled the timeline. The crater’s preservation offers a rare window into early Earth bombardment. Researchers compared it with younger, better-studied sites to confirm the ancient date. Additional fieldwork will search for similar old structures elsewhere. Source: space.com

  6. China readies Long March 10B for first flight and booster recovery — SpaceNews

    7. The new rocket is scheduled to lift off in July from Wenchang. Engineers plan to attempt a controlled splashdown of the first stage at sea. The mission will test both the core vehicle and recovery hardware. Success would mark a step toward reusable heavy-lift capability. Airspace notices have already been issued for the launch window. Source: spacenews.com

  7. ESA opens European Flight Ticket Initiative to more providers — European Space Agency

    8. The program now accepts proposals from additional European launch companies. It aims to give small satellites and technology demonstrators regular rides. Earlier rounds were limited to a smaller set of operators. Selected missions will receive funded flight opportunities on qualifying vehicles. The next call for proposals is expected later this year. Source: esa.int

  8. Saturn’s moon Iapetus passes north of the ringed planet — Astronomy Magazine

    9. The two-toned moon reaches 1.9 arcminutes due north of Saturn on June 25. Iapetus is currently midway between its eastern and western elongations. Observers with small telescopes can watch the slow westward drift over coming weeks. The moon’s unusual brightness contrast makes it easy to track against the planet’s glare. The geometry repeats every 79 days. Source: astronomy.com

  9. TESS finds two of the puffiest planets yet detected — NASA

    10. The planets are Jupiter-sized yet have densities comparable to cotton candy. Data from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite revealed their extremely low masses relative to size. Follow-up observations refined the radius and mass measurements. The worlds orbit stars bright enough for additional atmospheric study. Researchers will next examine whether the low densities result from intense stellar heating. Source: science.nasa.gov

  10. EarthCARE cloud radar data now improve global weather forecasts — European Space Agency

    11. The satellite’s cloud profiling radar is being assimilated into the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts system. This marks the first time space-based cloud radar observations have been used operationally in a global model. The measurements help refine predictions of cloud cover and precipitation. Data flow began after months of validation against ground-based instruments. Forecasters expect incremental gains in medium-range accuracy. Source: esa.int

  11. Binary star system defies standard evolution models — Universe Today

    12. A Korean team identified a pair locked in an orbit too rapid for current theory. One star appears to be stripping material from its companion at an unexpected rate. The system was caught during a slow, violent mass-transfer phase. Spectra show unusual abundance patterns that standard models cannot reproduce. Continued monitoring will test whether the pair will merge or stabilize. Source: universetoday.com

  12. Black Sea shows milky turquoise from phytoplankton bloom — NASA

    13. Spring and summer 2026 brought a widespread bloom visible from orbit. The milky blue hue spread through the sea and connected waterways. Satellite imagery tracked the bloom’s growth and movement over several weeks. Phytoplankton concentrations reached levels high enough to alter surface color dramatically. Similar events help scientists study nutrient cycling in enclosed basins. Source: science.nasa.gov

  13. Event horizon “fingerprints” detected around a black hole for first time — Phys.org

    14. Researchers identified subtle signatures in light that trace the boundary where escape velocity equals the speed of light. The detection relied on high-resolution observations of nearby accreting material. The pattern matches predictions from general relativity but had never been isolated before. The work used data from multiple ground-based telescopes. Follow-up campaigns will search for the same signatures in other systems. Source: phys.org

  14. Star TOI-5882 shows evidence it recently swallowed a planet — Universe Today

    15. A team from the United States and Chile found chemical traces left by the engulfed world. The star already hosts a brown-dwarf companion that may have perturbed the planet’s orbit. Spectra reveal unusual lithium and refractory-element ratios consistent with planetary material. The event likely occurred within the past few million years. Additional radial-velocity measurements will check for any remaining planets. Source: universetoday.com

  15. Perseverance confirms organic carbon in Bright Angel rocks on Mars — Phys.org

    16. A new paper in Science Advances details unambiguous detection of organic molecules in two samples. The rocks were collected from the Bright Angel formation in September 2025. Instruments on the rover measured carbon abundance and molecular structure directly. The findings strengthen the case for past habitable conditions but do not prove biology. The samples remain cached for eventual return to Earth. Source: phys.org

Cosmic Spotlight

The first detection of event-horizon fingerprints comes from careful analysis of light emitted by gas spiraling near the boundary. Telescopes recorded tiny, repeating brightness changes that match computer models of photons skirting the point of no return. Because the signal is so faint, the team combined data from several observatories to pull it above the noise. The result gives astronomers a new way to test whether real black holes behave exactly as predicted. What other subtle signatures might still be hiding in existing observations of accreting systems?

Cosmic Deep Dive: How Gravity Traps Light at an Event Horizon

If you could shrink Earth’s mass into a sphere just 1.8 centimeters across, its surface gravity would equal the speed of light and nothing could climb back out. Photons emitted from that surface would lose all their energy trying to escape, stretching into infinite redshift. The actual event horizon of a stellar-mass black hole sits only a few kilometers from its center, yet the same rule applies. We can watch gas orbit just outside this boundary and see its light dim and stretch, but we still cannot explain exactly how information about what fell in is preserved or lost.

New observations keep rewriting what we thought we knew about the universe’s extremes.
