Booster 23's rollout to Massey for cryo testing keeps the next Starship hardware cycle on schedule.

By Patrick October 13, 2026 Ep 129 5 min read Listen to podcast
Listen in

Top News

  1. Super Heavy Booster 23 moved to Massey for initial cryogenic proof testing

    The booster arrived at the Starbase Texas test site on October 13. Cryogenic proof testing verifies tank integrity under liquid oxygen and methane loads before any hot-fire campaign. This step precedes stacking and flight preparations for upcoming Starship missions. Observers documented the vehicle already positioned for tanking operations shortly after rollout. Source: x.com

  2. Starship Flight 15 will attempt to catch the Ship upper stage with tower arms

    Elon Musk stated at the X Takeover event that Flight 15 remains four to six weeks away and will likely occur just before Thanksgiving. The change list is shorter than prior flights, though some modifications still require internal tank access. Flight 16 is planned to refly both the booster and the Ship. Starship V3 targets 100 metric tons to orbit in a fully reusable configuration, a figure that requires intact Ship recovery. Musk addressed roughly 2,000 attendees at Giga Texas on Saturday. SpaceX has caught Super Heavy boosters several times but has never caught a Starship upper stage, with every prior Ship ending its flight in the ocean. Source: keeptrack.space

  3. Falcon 9 launches Starlink 15-25 mission from Vandenberg, first Starlink flight in more than three weeks

    Booster B1088-20 lifted off from SLC-4E carrying 27 V2 Mini Optimized satellites into a 70-degree orbit. The mission marked the first Starlink launch since mid-September. SpaceX has now launched 12,988 Starlink satellites total, with 11,131 still in orbit and 11,117 operational. Vandenberg Space Force Base logged its 69th launch of the year with this flight from SLC-4E. Source: keeptrack.space Source: space.com Source: vandenberg.spaceforce.mil

  4. CRS-35 flies on Falcon 9 booster B1096 making its eighth flight

    Cargo Dragon C211 is on its fourth mission after prior flights on CRS-26, CRS-29, and CRS-33. Liftoff from SLC-40 was targeted for 6:33 a.m. EDT, with docking planned at the zenith port of Harmony around 1500 UTC on October 15. The spacecraft will remain docked for about a month, completing SpaceX’s 35th Commercial Resupply Services mission. Booster B1096 flew previously on IMAP, CRS-34, NROL-77, NROL-95, GPS-III 9, Kuiper Falcon 01 and Starlink 6-87. The port became available after Crew Dragon Freedom returned the four Crew-12 astronauts on October 8. Source: keeptrack.space

  5. EON Space Labs’ Mira optical payload reaches orbit aboard Falcon 9 Transporter-18

    The Hyderabad startup’s flagship earth-observation payload launched on October 2 and is expected to deliver first images within ten days. EON Space Labs holds an Indian patent for Mira and has developed germanium-free LWIR lenses using synthetically grown domestic materials, cutting lead times from six months to six weeks. The company is working with more than seven space customers and has received interest from roughly 120 firms for its aerial EONIR systems. A handheld imaging system combining SWIR, LWIR and day cameras has already undergone trials aboard Indian Navy ships, identifying vessels at around 30 km in low-visibility conditions. Source: yourstory.com

  6. Eight upcoming Starlink conjunctions appear on the SOCRATES high-probability list

    None of the events is rated high risk. One report is flagged as suspect due to physically implausible upstream numbers and is treated as stale or erroneous. These assessments help operators prioritize collision-avoidance maneuvers only when warranted by reliable data. Source: keeptrack.space

  7. One Starlink satellite carries an active reentry prediction

    The satellite is expected to reenter in the coming days as part of normal end-of-life disposal. Controlled reentries limit debris generation and keep the constellation population aligned with licensed orbital shells. Source: keeptrack.space

The Counterpoint

Starship cargo missions face a reported $650 million insurance gap because current policies cannot cover the full value of payloads on early flights. Insurers cite limited flight heritage and high replacement costs for large orbital assets. Closing the gap would require either demonstrated reliability through repeated successful recoveries or new risk-sharing structures with launch customers. Source: benzinga.com

Engineering Deep Dive

Cryogenic proof testing of a Super Heavy booster tank set begins by chilling the structure to liquid oxygen and methane temperatures while monitoring for leaks and deformation. Stainless steel 304L contracts roughly 0.3 percent linearly between ambient and 90 K, generating hoop and longitudinal stresses that must remain below yield across every weld and flange. The test therefore applies internal pressure at cryogenic conditions to expose any micro-cracks or porosity that would only appear once the material reaches flight temperatures. Passing the proof allows the vehicle to proceed to ambient and then cryogenic proof on the second tank, followed by a combined tanking test that verifies the common bulkhead and feed-line interfaces under simultaneous LOX and LCH4 loads. Each successful cycle reduces the probability of a thermal-induced leak during the first hot-fire or flight by confirming that the manufacturing processes—particularly the friction-stir welds and flange seals—maintain integrity after repeated thermal cycling. Data from these tests also calibrate finite-element models used to predict fatigue life across the targeted ten-plus flights per booster.

The Idiot Index for a finished booster tank measures how many times the delivered article costs compared with the raw stainless plate, weld wire, and forming energy. Cryogenic proof testing collapses that index by catching defects before they reach the launch pad, where a single leak could destroy both the vehicle and the test stand. Because the same tanks must survive multiple flights, the proof campaign effectively amortizes the cost of each additional cycle across the entire reuse program. Finite-element updates derived from strain-gauge readings during the test directly inform the next design iteration of the tank domes and skirt interfaces. In this way the test functions as both a gate and a data source, tightening the manufacturing loop without requiring flight hardware to serve as the first proof article.

When the booster later returns from flight, the same cryogenic test sequence can be repeated at lower pressure to confirm that fatigue has not introduced new leak paths. The 0.3 percent contraction figure remains the dominant driver of residual stress, so any change in weld schedule or flange preload must be re-validated at 90 K before the vehicle is cleared for another mission. This closed-loop approach keeps the per-flight marginal cost of the booster low even as cumulative flight count climbs.
