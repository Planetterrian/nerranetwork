Crew Dragon Grace docked with the ISS after seven hours and fifty-five minutes, the fastest US crewed flight to the station.

Top News

  1. Crew Dragon Grace sets US record for fastest crewed arrival at the ISS

    Crew Dragon Grace lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 11:10 a.m. EDT on October 1 and docked to the forward port of the Harmony module at 7:05 p.m. EDT, completing the trip in seven hours and fifty-five minutes. NASA described the time as the shortest launch-to-docking interval achieved by any US spacecraft. The previous US crewed mark stood at fourteen hours and forty-three minutes on the Crew-11 mission. SpaceX program director Julianna Scheiman attributed the result to favorable station orbital geometry rather than a change in vehicle performance. The four-person Crew-13 crew will remain aboard the station for approximately six months. The capsule carried NASA astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk, and Roscosmos cosmonaut Sergey Teteryatnikov. NASA’s blog posted the docking notice at 7:13 p.m. EDT. The previous US cargo record was twelve hours and thirty-three minutes set by CRS-31 in November 2024. Source: shattered.io

  2. SpaceX supplier STMicroelectronics raises full-year sales outlook on satellite and AI demand

    STMicroelectronics cited stronger-than-expected orders from satellite and artificial-intelligence customers when it lifted its 2026 revenue guidance. The company supplies power and sensor components used on multiple SpaceX vehicles and Starlink satellites. Management linked the upward revision directly to rising production volumes in both segments. The forecast update came during the firm’s quarterly earnings release. Satellite demand includes components for both commercial Starlink and government Starshield variants. Source: wsj.com

  3. SpaceX completes pre-launch static-fire campaign on Starship 42 and Super Heavy

    Teams finished single-engine and multi-engine static fires plus integrated simulations on Ship 42 and its booster ahead of Flight 15. Video released by the company shows the vehicles remaining on the pad after each test with no visible damage to engines or structures. The campaign clears the vehicles for the next flight-test window. The tests included full-duration burns on the Raptor engines installed on both stages. Ground crews confirmed propellant loading and tanking sequences performed as expected. Source: teslaoracle.com

  4. Bloomberg examines unresolved technical questions around Starship orbital refueling

    The article outlines the sequence of tanker flights, rendezvous timing, and propellant-transfer hardware still required before a crewed lunar mission can proceed. It notes that no public test has yet demonstrated the full quantity of propellant transfer needed for Artemis III. Engineers continue to iterate on docking seals and transfer lines inside the payload bay. The refueling architecture requires multiple tanker vehicles to deliver cryogenic propellant to an orbiting depot stage. Precise timing of rendezvous and transfer operations remains a key open parameter for mission planners. Source: bloomberg.com

  5. Tesla next-generation Roadster displayed at SpaceX Hawthorne headquarters

    The vehicle appeared inside the Hawthorne facility two weeks after its public unveiling. Observers noted the placement next to Starship hardware displays. No official statement linked the display to any joint engineering program. The Roadster was positioned near production tooling areas used for Falcon and Dragon hardware. The sighting occurred during routine facility tours for employees and visitors. Source: teslarati.com

  6. NASASpaceflight compares historical launch-site build times to SpaceX Louisiana plans

    The analysis places Kennedy Space Center construction at five-and-a-half years from groundbreaking to first launch and Starbase Texas at roughly four-and-a-half years so far. SpaceX has stated it intends to begin launches from the new Louisiana site within three years of site selection. The comparison highlights differences in permitting, workforce scale, and vertical-integration methods. Kennedy’s Vehicle Assembly Building required three years to complete four high bays for Saturn-class vehicles. Launch Complex 39A took twenty-two months to reach structural completion before outfitting began. Source: nasaspaceflight.com

  7. Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile petition FCC to remove 290 MHz from upcoming satellite spectrum allocation

    The carriers filed comments urging the commission to exclude the band before a vote scheduled for Thursday. They argue the spectrum should remain reserved for terrestrial 5G rather than shared with non-geostationary satellite systems. Starlink’s direct-to-cell service would use portions of the band under discussion. The petition requests the FCC reduce the proposed satellite allocation by 290 MHz. The filing was submitted ahead of the scheduled commission meeting. Source: teslarati.com

  8. SpaceX pauses Florida Falcon launches for six weeks to prepare Starship infrastructure

    The company notified regulators it will stand down from Cape Canaveral and Vandenberg operations while ground crews complete Starship-related work at Starbase. The pause aligns with the upcoming Flight 15 test campaign. No Falcon missions are currently scheduled during the interval. The stand-down period begins immediately and extends through mid-December. Regulators received the notification as part of routine launch manifest updates. Source: gadgetreview.com

Engineering Deep Dive

The seven-hour-fifty-five-minute transit of Crew Dragon Grace from liftoff to docking illustrates how orbital mechanics can be exploited once a launch vehicle reaches a precise insertion state. Dragon performed a series of phasing burns that placed it on a trajectory whose argument of perigee and nodal alignment matched the station’s position at arrival. Because the station travels at roughly 7.66 km/s, even small differences in launch azimuth or upper-stage cutoff velocity shift the relative phasing by hundreds of kilometers per orbit. The short transit therefore required both an on-time liftoff and a second-stage performance that left the capsule with the correct energy and plane. Earlier US crewed flights averaged twelve to fourteen hours because their launch windows were set by daylight or lighting constraints rather than minimum-time rendezvous geometry.

The same physical trade-off appears in the historical record: Soyuz achieved a three-hour docking in 2020 by launching into a near-co-planar orbit from Baikonur, but US launches from Florida face a different inclination and range-safety corridor that historically forced longer coast phases. Dragon’s record therefore reflects a convergence of vehicle capability, ground-track geometry, and operational willingness to accept a narrow daily window. Scheiman noted that the station happened to be in an opportune spot, making the geometry unusually favorable on that particular day. If Starship refueling demonstrations later allow crew vehicles to depart from higher-energy orbits, the same principles will determine whether transit times can be driven below six hours on a routine basis. The limiting factor remains the precision of the initial insertion burn and the ability to execute the subsequent phasing maneuvers with minimal propellant margin.

Historical comparisons show that the previous US crewed minimum of fourteen hours and forty-three minutes on Crew-11 still required multiple orbits to close the phase angle. The seven-hour-fifty-five-minute flight compressed that sequence by accepting a tighter launch window and relying on the Falcon 9 second stage to deliver a more exact velocity vector. This approach trades operational flexibility for reduced crew time in the confined capsule environment. Future vehicles with greater propellant reserves could relax the window constraint while preserving the short transit.
