Top News

  1. Starship Flight 15 advances toward November static fire

    Ship 42 completed a cryoproof test at Massey’s after engines were removed to address an RVac software glitch isolated to one engine computer. Flight 15 now carries a NET date of November 8 and requires static fires on both the booster and Ship before launch. Flight 16 is aimed for December and could mark the first Starship liftoff from LC-39A. The campaign is expected to deliver only five Starship launches in 2026 during the Block 2 to Block 3 transition. SpaceX still projects hundreds of annual launches from Starbase in later years. Musk noted that the RVac issue was a software failure on a single unit and did not appear on other engines during Flight 14. The volume of changes between flights ranges from hundreds to thousands, though the current campaign involves fewer modifications than some earlier ones. Source: nasaspaceflight.com

  2. White House directed Starshield speed throttling in Ukraine

    Reports indicate the White House ordered limits on Starshield data rates that caused Ukrainian drones to lose guidance during combat operations. The restrictions capped speeds near 100 mph and affected deep-strike and interceptor missions. Neither the White House nor Elon Musk has claimed responsibility for the change. Republicans in Congress have requested formal explanations of the decision chain. The throttling occurred after field tests showed the speed limits reduced effectiveness of interceptor drones and helicopters. Starshield remains distinct from the commercial Starlink constellation in both hardware and access controls. Source: techtimes.com

  3. Musk says permits blocked giant pyramid headquarters

    Elon Musk posted that permits for a proposed pyramid-shaped SpaceX headquarters proved impossible to obtain. He separately suggested the structure would be visually striking. The comments appear tied to ongoing expansion planning at Starbase and other sites. Musk also floated the idea of universal high income in a separate post on the same day. Both remarks came amid continued discussion of facility growth needed to support higher launch cadence. Source: x.com

  4. SpaceX Q3 earnings preview highlights AI and Starlink split

    Analysts forecast $12.89 billion revenue and $6.62 billion adjusted EBITDA for the quarter ending Tuesday, marking the first profitable period since the June IPO. Roughly $6.4 billion is expected from AI-related activity and $4.9 billion from Starlink, which is projected to reach 14.3 million subscribers. Capital spending is estimated at $19.6 billion, producing negative free cash flow near $11.6 billion. The results will be webcast at 4:30 p.m. ET. The quarter would represent roughly 65 percent growth from the second quarter. Management continues to target more than 2 gigawatts of compute capacity by year end and a $100 billion annualized revenue run rate. Source: teslarati.com

  5. Ex-Landspace VP launches full-flow staged combustion startup

    A former Landspace vice president has founded a new firm focused on replicating SpaceX’s full-flow staged combustion cycle engine architecture. The venture secured backing from Ningbo’s 8.6-billion-yuan industrial investment base. The effort targets the same propellant cycle used in Raptor. Full-flow staged combustion offers higher efficiency by routing both fuel and oxidizer through the turbopumps before combustion. The new company aims to close the performance gap that has kept most competitors on simpler gas-generator or staged-combustion cycles. Source: eu.36kr.com

  6. Gwynne Shotwell follows Tesla on X amid merger speculation

    SpaceX president and COO Gwynne Shotwell began following Tesla’s official account, a move noted by observers tracking potential combination talks. Shotwell has previously stated that SpaceX personnel have already shifted into xAI roles. No company has confirmed merger discussions. The follow occurred as Barclays analyst Dan Levy outlined an all-stock deal structure that would require a vote by Tesla’s December 6 annual meeting. Shotwell now oversees operations that include Grok and the company’s AI compute buildout. Source: teslarati.com

  7. Musk floats universal high income concept

    Elon Musk posted the phrase “universal high income” without further detail on the platform. The remark follows recent comments on AI-driven economic shifts. Musk has linked similar ideas to productivity gains from advanced AI systems. The post appeared alongside separate comments on headquarters design and permitting challenges. Source: x.com

  8. Robertson Stephens raises SpaceX holdings

    Robertson Stephens Wealth Management LLC increased its position in SPCX shares, according to a filing reported by MarketBeat. The move contrasts with a separate filing showing Curated Wealth Partners LLC reduced its stake. Both filings were disclosed on the same day and reflect differing institutional views on the stock after its June listing. SPCX closed Friday at $146.03. Source: marketbeat.com

The Counterpoint

A new Chinese startup backed by an 8.6-billion-yuan industrial fund is explicitly targeting full-flow staged combustion cycle engines identical to Raptor architecture. Success would give a state-supported competitor the same propellant-cycle performance SpaceX spent years qualifying. Regulatory or export-control responses could slow the effort, but the capital commitment signals a direct technical challenge. The venture’s focus on replicating the exact cycle used in Raptor highlights how the performance advantages of full-flow staged combustion are now viewed as a benchmark worth matching at scale. Source: eu.36kr.com

Engineering Deep Dive

A student team just flew a 105-metre reusable rocket hop on a 1.2 kN bipropellant engine built for less than 250,000 Swiss francs. That hop demonstrates basic throttle control, guidance, and soft landing at roughly 100 kg scale. An orbital-class first stage must also survive hypersonic reentry heating, execute high-energy boostback and entry burns after traveling above 100 km, and manage multi-engine control across orbital staging margins. Energy and thermal loads therefore scale by orders of magnitude beyond the student demonstrator. The gap explains why most teams remain stuck at pure hops rather than operational reuse; the prediction is falsified only if a comparable low-budget effort achieves full orbital recovery or if reentry physics prove unexpectedly trivial.

The Swiss Colibri demonstrator used an in-house F-100 engine producing 1.2 kN of thrust and completed its entire flight in 60 seconds while translating 30 metres laterally before touchdown. At that scale the dominant challenges are control authority and propellant management during the short ascent and descent. An orbital booster like Falcon 9’s first stage or Starship’s Super Heavy must instead dissipate kinetic energy from velocities exceeding Mach 5, manage heat fluxes that can exceed 1 MW per square metre, and execute precise burns with engines that have already delivered thousands of kilonewtons for minutes. The raw-material cost of the student vehicle stayed under 250,000 Swiss francs because the structure, avionics, and engine were all built at laboratory scale with minimal qualification testing. Scaling those same functions to a vehicle that returns from orbit multiplies both the material and certification costs by factors that current reuse programs are still working to compress.

The Idiot Index for orbital reuse therefore sits far higher than for a 105-metre hop. The finished first-stage booster costs many times its raw propellant, metal, and electronics inputs because of the thermal-protection system, the structural margins required for reentry, and the repeated qualification of every engine and valve after each flight. Reusing that hardware twenty or thirty times collapses the per-flight cost only after the initial development and qualification investment has been amortized. The Swiss flight shows that the core closed-loop control problem is solvable at small scale with modest resources. The remaining engineering distance lies in surviving the energy environment of orbital return and in manufacturing the vehicle at a cadence that makes the reuse economics close.
