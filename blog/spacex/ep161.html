SpaceX plans a $74 billion investment in supercomputing campuses to power its AI ambitions.

By Patrick November 13, 2026 Ep 161 7 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents
Top News Also Today Community Buzz The Counterpoint Engineering Deep Dive Market Watch

Top News

  1. SpaceX plans $74 billion investment in Texas and Arizona supercomputing campuses

    The company will split the spend across two sites, with more than $24 billion allocated to the Texas location near Starbase. The campuses will support large-scale AI training and inference workloads tied to the xAI and Grok stack. Local opposition in Arizona has gathered over 2,400 petition signatures against one proposed $50 billion component that would create 6,500 jobs. SpaceX stated the projects will expand local tax bases and create jobs while addressing decades of prior environmental debris at the Texas site. The Texas allocation includes a $24 billion AI computing campus at Starbase, while the Arizona site in Cochise County carries a larger proposed figure that has drawn community pushback. Company statements emphasize that development will include cleanup of scrap metal, discarded appliances, old tanks, fiberglass insulation, construction materials, and thousands of illegally dumped tires accumulated since the 1960s. The overall plan positions the campuses as infrastructure to back both SpaceX operations and the broader Musk compute ecosystem. Source: govconwire.com

  2. Ukraine reports Starshield speed restrictions caused 100 percent mission failure rate

    Ukrainian officials said restrictions escalated from 10 percent of missions affected on the first day to 50 percent on the second and 100 percent on the third. The limits cap speed at 161 kilometers per hour, blocking interceptor drones from matching incoming Russian Shahed threats. SpaceX stated there have been no changes to the service. President Zelenskyy described the limits as systematic rather than random errors. The restrictions have complicated efforts to intercept jet-powered attack drones over the front line and Russian-occupied territory. Ukrainian military personnel investigated the disruptions and concluded they were not technical errors. SpaceX told Axios that the account of new limitations is false. Source: united24media.com

  3. Blue Origin will not bid on NASA’s commercial low Earth orbit destination contract

    CEO Dave Limp told employees the company’s priorities remain New Glenn development and Blue Moon. The decision leaves the CLD competition to other bidders for whom the award would be more consequential. Blue Origin has focused resources on completing its New Glenn heavy-lift vehicle and the Blue Moon lunar lander. The company views the orbital station contract as lower priority compared with those core programs. Industry observers note that the CLD award carries greater weight for smaller commercial providers than for Blue Origin. Source: x.com

  4. SpaceX commits $220 million to Brownsville water and wastewater infrastructure

    The pledge is the largest private contribution in the city’s history and targets long-term drinking water reliability for the region around Starbase. The investment addresses one of the area’s most pressing challenges by expanding supply capacity for generations of residents. SpaceX framed the commitment as part of its broader infrastructure work in the Rio Grande Valley. The funds will support both water and wastewater systems serving Brownsville and Cameron County. Source: x.com

  5. SpaceX begins environmental cleanup at the Starbase supercompute site

    Decades of unauthorized dumping since the 1960s left scrap metal, appliances, tanks, insulation, construction debris, and thousands of tires on the property. Removal work is underway as site preparation proceeds. The cleanup targets material dating back to the 1960s that accumulated through unauthorized activity. SpaceX described the effort as an opportunity to address long-standing environmental neglect during development of the supercompute campus. Source: x.com

  6. SpaceX commits to sharing infrastructure and tax-base benefits with Texas and Arizona communities

    The company highlighted job creation, local tax contributions, and coordinated planning from the Rio Grande Valley to Cochise County as the supercomputing campuses advance. Statements emphasized that the projects extend beyond hardware to include community-level investments. SpaceX noted that development will increase local tax bases while expanding critical infrastructure in both states. The commitments cover the full corridor from southern Texas to southern Arizona. Source: x.com

  7. TO THE NEW partners with SpaceXSI to integrate Cursor into enterprise AI workflows

    The collaboration will embed the AI coding platform into larger enterprise environments where Grok models are already in use. The partnership targets organizations seeking to scale agent-based development workflows. Cursor’s integration will allow teams to coordinate tasks across multiple users and agents within shared workspaces. The move extends Grok’s reach into enterprise coding environments through the established Cursor interface. Source: smbtech.au

  8. ULA rolls Vulcan to the pad for Friday’s Amazon Leo LV-01 mission

    The VC6L configuration will launch from SLC-41 at Cape Canaveral while Starship Flight 15 is scheduled from Texas the same day. The mission supports Amazon’s Project Kuiper constellation buildout. ULA’s vehicle will use its most powerful configuration to date for the Leo LV-01 payload. The dual-launch day underscores the busy manifest for both companies on November 13. Source: floridatoday.com

SpaceX backs child protection initiative with 750 free Starlink kits: Drive Tesla Canada reports the kits will connect children’s homes across Africa. Source: driveteslacanada.ca

The Counterpoint

Ukraine’s military has documented three consecutive days of Starshield speed restrictions that it says rendered every affected drone interception mission unsuccessful. SpaceX maintains no service changes occurred. The dispute raises questions about how quickly disputed limits can be verified or lifted when the customer is a foreign government relying on the system for real-time operations. Ukrainian President Zelenskyy stated that the speed cap at 161 kilometers per hour has already allowed some Russian strikes to penetrate defenses. European Commission officials have called on SpaceX to restore full operations as soon as possible. Source: kyivpost.com

Engineering Deep Dive

The planned double catch on Flight 15 requires the Ship upper stage to survive a full orbital mission, 14 revolutions, and a high-energy reentry before threading itself between the tower arms roughly 22 hours after launch. The booster, by contrast, returns after only six and a half minutes and has already demonstrated three successful tower catches on earlier flights. The Ship’s thermal protection system must therefore handle peak heating rates far higher than the booster while preserving tile integrity for immediate reuse, a constraint that drove the decision to fly the first Block 3 ships with additional instrumentation and thicker tile bonds. Reusing both stages without ocean recovery or extensive post-flight work collapses the marginal cost per flight toward the propellant price alone; each avoided ocean recovery operation removes weeks of logistics, salt-water exposure, and tile replacement labor. The 124-meter stack’s 80-year reusability goal therefore hinges on whether the upper-stage heat shield and leg-less catch geometry can match the reliability already shown by the booster. Early Block 3 data from Flights 12–14 showed progressive improvements in ascent engine reliability and payload deployment, yet every booster still experienced at least one engine anomaly during boostback or landing. Those results set the minimum bar Flight 15 must clear for both stages if the program is to declare the first fully reusable orbital launch system. On Flight 12 the first Block 3 booster suffered boostback issues and was lost during landing burn. Ship 39 lost a Raptor Vacuum engine on ascent yet still deployed its payload and achieved an on-target Indian Ocean splashdown. Flight 13 saw Booster 20 complete a clean ascent and boostback burn before ice ingestion shortened the landing burn, resulting in a Gulf impact. Ship 40 flew cleanly, deployed 20 Starlink V3 satellites, and returned intact for later tile study. Flight 14 marked the first orbital Starship mission, with Booster 21 losing two Raptors yet achieving a soft splashdown and Ship 41 losing one Raptor Vacuum engine before deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites and executing a contingency reentry. The data from those flights directly informs the 22-hour profile and dual-catch attempt now planned for Flight 15.
