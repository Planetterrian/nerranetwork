Top News

  1. FAA weighs new SpaceX Starship flight path, emergency landing zones — San Antonio Express-News

    2. The FAA is evaluating an updated Starship trajectory and designated ocean emergency zones for upcoming flights. The review covers both ascent corridors and contingency landing areas to support higher flight rates from Starbase. Updated paths would affect overflight restrictions and public-safety calculations. The agency has not set a timeline for a final decision. Source: Google News

  2. Amazon goes to FCC again, seeks approval to launch 5,105 satellites; aims to compete with SpaceX’s Starlink Mobile — The Times of India

    3. Amazon filed a new application with the FCC for 5,105 satellites to expand its Kuiper constellation. The request targets direct-to-cell service to challenge Starlink Mobile coverage. The filing follows earlier Kuiper authorizations and focuses on spectrum and orbital slots. No launch schedule was included in the submission. Source: Google News

  3. Starship heat shield aces reentry test on Flight 13, floats intact in Indian Ocean — eciks.org

    4. The Ship upper stage completed reentry with its heat-shield tiles remaining attached and functional. The stage then performed a controlled soft splashdown in the Indian Ocean. Post-flight imagery shows the tile array survived peak heating without major loss. This outcome directly informs tile-attachment and material choices for future vehicles. Source: Google News

  4. Elon Musk reveals SpaceX performed secret Starship test on Flight 13 — Teslarati

    5. Musk disclosed that Flight 13 included an unannounced test on a specific section of the Ship. The test ran alongside the primary mission objectives that were all reported met. Details on the test hardware or measurements remain limited to the public statement. The addition demonstrates incremental data collection during nominal flights. Source: Google News

  5. SpaceX Logs Thirteenth Starship Launch, First Starlink V3 Test — Broadband Breakfast

    6. Flight 13 carried the first on-orbit test of a Starlink V3 payload. The mission profile included the standard ascent, separation, and Ship reentry sequence. V3 hardware aims at higher bandwidth and power compared with prior generations. No further payload performance data has been released. Source: Google News

  6. Launch Preview: Falcon 9 and Chang Zheng rockets set to launch this week — NASASpaceflight

    7. Five orbital launches are scheduled worldwide, including at least one Falcon 9. The Falcon mission targets a Starlink deployment from Vandenberg. Chinese Chang Zheng vehicles fill the remainder of the manifest. No booster flight counts or turnaround targets were specified for the Falcon flight. Source: nasaspaceflight.com

  7. SpaceX plans 2 California rocket launches this week at Vandenberg — Ventura County Star

    8. Two Falcon 9 missions are slated from Vandenberg Space Force Base. Both target polar orbits and will use previously flown boosters. Ground operations are already underway at the pad. The schedule depends on final range and weather approvals. Source: Google News

  8. Orbital Data Centers Need Inspection Too: Orbes Signs First Escort Mission for a Commercial ODC — Tech Times

    9. Orbes has contracted the first inspection escort for a commercial orbital data center. The mission will demonstrate close-proximity servicing and monitoring around an active ODC platform. No operator name or orbital parameters were disclosed. The contract marks an early step toward routine on-orbit infrastructure support. Source: Google News

Engineering Deep Dive

The Ship upper stage on Flight 13 demonstrated that its current tile array can survive peak reentry heating while preserving attachment integrity all the way to a soft ocean landing. From first principles, the heat-shield problem reduces to balancing three constraints: incident heat flux, material ablation or thermal conductivity, and the mechanical loads transmitted through the attachment points during both ascent vibration and atmospheric deceleration. The successful outcome shows the tile-to-structure interface now carries margin against both thermal expansion mismatch and shear from the plasma flow. That margin directly lowers the risk of tile loss on the next flight, which would otherwise require either thicker tiles or a redesign of the attachment geometry. If the same interface survives a second reentry without refurbishment, the effective cost per use of the thermal-protection system drops toward the raw material and forming cost of the tiles themselves rather than the full installed assembly price. The next data point to watch is whether the recovered Ship shows any localized tile degradation that would force a change in material formulation or attachment spacing before the first attempted booster catch.

Following the Flight 13 data released yesterday, the intact heat-shield performance moves the program one step closer to answering whether the Ship can achieve multiple orbital reentries with minimal ground intervention. The plasma environment during reentry imposes heat fluxes that can exceed several megawatts per square meter for tens of seconds; the tiles must reject or absorb that energy while the underlying structure stays below its temperature limit. Attachment points must also resist the dynamic pressure and vibration that peak during the hypersonic phase. The current design appears to have closed the gap between the theoretical minimum mass of ceramic material needed for insulation and the added mass required for mechanical retention and gap fillers. Closing that gap further would reduce the Idiot Index of the thermal-protection system—the ratio of finished installed cost to the raw material cost of the tiles—bringing it nearer to the economics of simple formed ceramics rather than precision aerospace assemblies. Engineers will next examine whether the same tile layout can tolerate the higher entry velocities planned for future missions that carry heavier payloads or target different orbital inclinations.

If the recovered hardware confirms uniform tile survival, the program gains a clearer path to testing the Ship on a second flight without tile replacement. That step would validate the manufacturing repeatability of the attachment method at scale and reduce the turnaround time between flights. Any localized wear patterns observed on the Indian Ocean recovery would instead point to specific adjustments in tile thickness, coating, or spacing before the design is frozen for higher-cadence operations. The data also informs parallel work on the Super Heavy booster, whose own thermal-protection needs differ because of its lower velocity at stage separation. Together these results narrow the remaining unknowns around full-vehicle reuse before the first tower-catch attempt on a subsequent flight.
