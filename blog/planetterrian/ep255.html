Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

  1. Large behavioral trial tests 55 ways to build carbon-price support — Phys.org

    One hundred eight researchers ran a preregistered experiment with 20,000 U.S. participants on the Prolific platform. Each person received one of 55 randomly assigned interventions or a control condition. Eleven interventions produced a detectable increase in support or real-world action such as donations. Writing a letter to a fictional future person ranked among the strongest. Imagining one’s own life decades ahead with or without climate action also raised donations. Information that carbon pricing reduces emissions or recycles revenue to climate protection showed measurable effects. The average effect across all designs remained small yet statistically significant. Source: phys.org

  2. Alfalfa forms root nodules without rhizobia via cytokinin-NIN module — Phys.org

    Researchers at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology identified a cytokinin–NODULE INCEPTION regulatory module that drives spontaneous nodule formation in alfalfa. The work shows how the plant initiates nodules independently of bacterial partners. The finding clarifies an alternative pathway for nodule organogenesis in this forage crop. Source: phys.org

  3. First known deep-sea-only salp species described from Monterey Bay — Phys.org

    MBARI underwater robots collected specimens of an unfamiliar salp observed more than twenty years ago. Larry Madin led the formal description of Vitreosalpa gemini, the glass salp. The species lives exclusively in deep water and represents the first salp with that habitat restriction. Source: phys.org

  4. Detailed workflow enables programmable chromosome editing in plants — Phys.org

    A group led by Gao Caixia at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology published an experimental protocol in Nature Protocols. The platform allows targeted chromosome manipulation in plant genomes. The method supplies step-by-step guidance for researchers seeking to rearrange plant chromosomes. Source: phys.org

  5. Electron microscopy captures dynamic changes in catalyst nanoparticles — Phys.org

    University of Nottingham researchers tracked nanoscale transformations inside working catalysts. The observations reveal structural shifts that occur during operation. The data may guide design of more efficient catalysts for cleaner fuels. Source: phys.org

  6. Vagus nerve stimulation alters gut microbiome to reduce epilepsy seizures — @EricTopol

    Stimulation of the vagus nerve changes microbial composition in ways that lessen seizure activity. The link was reported in Cell Host & Microbe. The pathway runs from nerve signaling through intestinal bacteria to brain excitability. Source: cell.com

  7. Systemic inflammation triggers T-cell exhaustion through negative feedback — @EricTopol

    Widespread inflammatory signals drive T cells into an exhausted state. The mechanism forms a self-reinforcing loop reported in Immunity. The finding connects body-wide inflammation to impaired immune responses. Source: cell.com

  8. Nerve cells maintain local protein supply to monitor mitochondria — Phys.org

    Angelika Harbauer’s group at the Max Planck Institute for Biological Intelligence showed that neurons produce a mitochondrial quality-control protein near their mitochondria. The strategy mirrors on-site tracer production in hospitals. It allows rapid damage checks without long-distance transport. Source: phys.org

  9. Nasal mRNA drops restore memory performance in aging and Alzheimer’s-model mice — ScienceDaily

    The treatment stimulates production of a neuron-protecting protein without clearing plaques. Treated older mice regained youthful learning and memory scores. Alzheimer’s-model mice showed prevented cognitive decline. The approach uses technology similar to mRNA vaccines. Source: sciencedaily.com

  10. Fourth Natsal survey maps shifts in British sexual behavior since 1990 — Phys.org

    The National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles collected data from a large national sample. Results appear in five papers in The Lancet. Sexual and reproductive health needs remain substantial and varied across age groups. Source: phys.org

  11. Microplastics detected in 95 of 120 fish sampled off Oman coast — Phys.org

    Sultan Qaboos University researchers examined gills and digestive tracts of fish from the Sea of Oman near Sohar. Microplastics appeared in nearly 80 percent of the sample. The study supplies a baseline for future monitoring. Source: phys.org

  12. Glycine clusters reach exceptional stability at exactly six molecules — Phys.org

    Bar-Ilan University researchers found that clusters of six glycine molecules become markedly more stable than other sizes. The molecules switch to zwitterion form upon assembly. This structural change enables stronger intermolecular bonds. Source: phys.org

Also Today

New chemical route achieves first total synthesis of antitussive alkaloid neotuberostemonine: Phys.org

Researchers completed the first total synthesis of the plant alkaloid neotuberostemonine after nearly thirty years of attempts and clarified how nitrogen geometry contributes to oxidative instability. Source: phys.org

Laser light maps electron accumulation in stacked atomically thin semiconductors: Phys.org

A multi-institutional Japanese team used laser illumination to track how electrons move and accumulate when semiconductors are layered, offering a route to lower-power electronics. Source: phys.org

Planetterrian Spotlight

Flowers in Cellini’s Narcissus sculpture link botany and political ambition: Phys.org

A new analysis shows that the flowers depicted on Benvenuto Cellini’s Narcissus connect emerging botanical science with Renaissance artists’ competition and Cosimo I de’ Medici’s efforts to strengthen his rule in Florence. The study traces how accurate plant representation served both artistic and political goals during the period. Source: phys.org

Science Deep Dive: Why exactly six glycine molecules form an unusually stable cluster

Most people assume molecular clusters grow steadily more stable as more units join, yet clusters of the amino acid glycine show a sharp peak at precisely six molecules. The stability arises because each glycine switches from its neutral form to a charged zwitterion once the six-member cluster assembles, allowing stronger electrostatic bonds between neighbors. This internal rearrangement happens only at that specific size and explains why smaller or larger groupings fall apart more readily. The finding comes from controlled cluster experiments at Bar-Ilan University that isolated and compared different numbers of glycine units. The same structural flip may operate in other small peptides, altering how we predict which assemblies persist in solution or on surfaces. Researchers can now test whether forcing or blocking the zwitterion transition changes cluster lifetime in different solvents. Knowing the exact size at which this switch occurs lets chemists design more durable molecular scaffolds without adding extra stabilizing groups.

New measurements keep reshaping what counts as basic biology.
