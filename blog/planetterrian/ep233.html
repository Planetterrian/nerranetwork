Top 12 Science & Health Discoveries

  1. Fire losses to U.S. buildings could nearly triple by 2060 without new protections — Phys.org

    Researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder's Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences reconstructed fire risk to structures from 1945 to 2023 using historical burned-area and development data. They combined that record with weather, climate and housing-density projections to model outcomes through 2060. Under moderate climate mitigation and no new adaptations, the model projects up to 449,100 buildings damaged or destroyed. The largest relative increase in risk appears in the central United States, where grassland fires are rising. In the western states, 30 percent of census tracts facing the steepest risk increases already show high poverty and housing burden. The work appears in Nature Communications. Virginia Iglesias, the lead author, stated that this is a call for action because doing nothing about climate change, building patterns or community involvement will drive fire risk significantly higher. The eastern U.S. experiences fires that are just as destructive as those in the west, yet communities there remain less prepared because they have seen fewer destructive events historically. Colorado adopted a new wildfire resiliency code in July 2025 aimed at reducing risk to life and property, though code changes alone are insufficient without addressing social vulnerability. Iglesias noted that risk is not only about where fires occur but also whether communities have resources to reduce impacts and recover. The results highlight that action requires targeted investment so policies and building codes are applied where they will have the greatest impact. Source: phys.org

  2. Study dives into lesser-known climate impacts of global reforestation — Phys.org

    Simon Fraser University researchers used an Earth-system model to trace non-CO₂ effects of large-scale forestation. Forests absorb more sunlight than grasslands, especially in snowy regions, and the resulting warmer air can increase atmospheric water vapor. When that air crosses oceans, it raises sea-surface temperatures and evaporation, amplifying the greenhouse effect. Oceans absorb the added heat slowly, so the full remote warming unfolds over centuries. The study was published in Earth System Dynamics. Pierre Etienne Banville, the lead author and a master's student in SFU Geography, explained that large-scale forestation alters land surface properties and leads to temperature changes that travel beyond planting sites. Banville added that the benefit of CO₂ removal occurs relatively quickly as trees grow, yet the non-CO₂ effects may creep up more slowly and offset some of that cooling. The work shows that awareness of these non-CO₂ effects and their differing time frames is essential when developing policies for nature-based solutions. Banville emphasized that achieving climate goals by a specific date requires accounting for all effects that will occur. Source: phys.org

  3. Climate change is making the tropics hotter — Phys.org

    Researchers examined long-term records from La Selva in Costa Rica and El Verde in Puerto Rico. Average temperatures changed little, yet days exceeding the 95th-percentile heat threshold rose from eight per year to thirty at La Selva by 2024. La Selva also recorded more extreme rainfall events, while El Verde saw an increase in dry days from 152 to 170 per year. The findings were published in PLOS Climate. Alonso Ramirez, lead author and professor at North Carolina State University, noted that instances of rainfall exceeding the 99th percentile at La Selva rose from around one event per year between 1983 and 1985 to six per year in 2024. Marcelo Ardon, co-author and professor at NC State, observed that one site is getting wetter and the other drier despite their relative proximity, which was surprising given expectations of similar trends. Ramirez stated that conventional wisdom holds that protected tropical areas should show little change from anthropogenic impacts, yet these research hotspots demonstrate clear shifts in climate and ecosystem functioning. Co-authors included researchers from Universidad de San Carlos de Guatemala, The Jones Center at Ichauway and NC State University. Source: phys.org

  4. UK storm surges have far-flung, global climate connections — Phys.org

    Cambridge University scientists analyzed seventy years of sea-level records around northwestern Europe. They isolated extreme surge events and used machine learning to group coastlines that experience them together. Low pressure over Iceland relative to the Azores shifts the jet stream northward and directs surges toward northern and central U.K. coasts. The opposite pressure pattern routes storms southward. The study appears in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans. Lisanne Blok, the lead author from Cambridge Earth Sciences, found that the largest influence on storm surge location comes from atmospheric pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere and specifically the North Atlantic. Blok noted that the relationships were clearer than expected, especially across large distances, and that identifying emerging conditions in the Atlantic could allow forecasters to pinpoint which coastal stretches should prepare for flooding. The North Sea's narrow funnel shape and shallow basin amplify surge heights, placing many low-lying communities at heightened risk. Blok added that storms driving extreme surges in southwest England tend to originate off eastern Canada, while those affecting the North Sea and Scotland often come from off Greenland. Source: phys.org

  5. Microbes at the end of the Earth: Antarctica's hidden biodiversity — Phys.org

    A study published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences compared Antarctic Arthrobacter strains with isolates from the Atacama Desert, Svalbard and the Tibetan Plateau. Distinct Antarctic lineages emerged that appear absent elsewhere, consistent with fifteen million years of geographic isolation by the circumpolar current. Soil temperatures at the sites range from 8 °C in summer to below −25 °C in winter. The same adaptations that allow survival may yield new antimicrobial compounds. Caray A Walker and Melody Clark, the authors, noted that Antarctic organisms have evolved remarkable solutions to extreme cold, desiccation and high UV-B exposure. Their earlier research cultured bacteria from eight Antarctic marine invertebrate species and found that two strains inhibited growth of epidemic methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Micrococcus luteus and Candida albicans in laboratory tests. A separate study monitored antimicrobial resistance around the Rothera Research Station and found that treated wastewater has only a limited effect on surrounding marine microbial communities, though the bacteria adapted easily to warmer temperatures in lab conditions. These microbes play a fundamental role in nutrient cycling and form the base of Antarctic food webs. Source: phys.org

  6. Gas inside us could fight wound infections, moth study shows — Phys.org

    University of Reading researchers loaded nitric oxide into organosilica nanoparticles that release the gas slowly over hours. One hour after infecting wax-moth larvae with skin-infection bacteria, they injected the particles. Nine in ten treated larvae survived three days, compared with roughly six in ten untreated larvae. In separate flask tests the particles reduced bacterial counts by four to five orders of magnitude. The work was published in the Journal of Drug Delivery Science and Technology. Vitaliy Khutoryanskiy, the lead researcher, explained that nitric oxide attacks germs on several fronts at once, making it harder for bacteria to develop resistance, and that the particles address the challenge of storing and releasing the gas slowly. Wax moth larvae weigh about a quarter of a gram yet fight germs in ways similar to humans, allowing tests of both safety and effectiveness while reducing reliance on mammalian experiments. Khutoryanskiy's team is developing tests that use insects and other invertebrates to study new medicines and drug delivery systems. The findings suggest the approach could supply a non-antibiotic option for infected wounds where conventional treatments struggle. Source: phys.org

  7. ‘Three-parent IVF’ was supposed to prevent mitochondrial disease. Studies are raising doubts — STAT News

    Eight healthy children born in the U.K. last year demonstrated that mitochondrial replacement techniques can lower transmission of disease-causing mitochondrial mutations. Subsequent U.K. data and small pilot studies elsewhere have raised questions about whether preventing mitochondrial disease is the optimal first clinical use of the method. Regulators in Australia are preparing their own national trial of the approach. The technique combines genes from a mother and father with mutation-free DNA in the mitochondria of a donor egg. Emerging data have prompted scientists and ethicists to rethink potential risks and benefits before wider adoption. Source: statnews.com

  8. Scientists discover new Alzheimer’s drug that repairs DNA damage and reduces brain inflammation — ScienceDaily

    King's College London tested KCL-286, an oral small molecule originally developed for spinal-cord injury, in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease. The compound activates a protein in the retinoic-acid pathway and reduced both DNA double-strand breaks and brain inflammation. KCL-286 has already completed Phase 1 human safety trials for its original indication. Professor Jonathan Corcoran noted that the drug is first-in-class and orally bioavailable, which will dramatically cut the traditional multi-year timeline for new drug development. Dr. Maria Goncalves, who project-managed the development, stated that the findings demonstrate KCL-286 targets DNA damage and reduces inflammation, two processes that occur very early in Alzheimer's progression. The approach differs from treatments focused primarily on amyloid-beta or tau by addressing additional early biological changes. Earlier research linked disruptions in the retinoic-acid pathway to amyloid-beta deposits in rat brains, suggesting the pathway's relevance to the disease. Source: sciencedaily.com

  9. Chinese PhD students in clinical medicine can graduate without a thesis — Nature

    Draft policy documents released on 22 October allow professional master’s and PhD students in clinical medicine to submit a practical achievement such as a clinical-case analysis instead of a thesis. The change follows a similar pathway introduced for engineering students in 2024. Universities will set their own assessment standards for the new route. Futao Huang, a researcher of higher-education policy at Hiroshima University, said the reform is a step in the right direction because students preparing for professional careers should be trained and assessed in ways that reflect those careers. Zhou Feifei, a spinal surgeon at Peking University Third Hospital, noted that the new pathway means students can be assessed on the contribution their research will bring to patients and society. One student, Tie Dazhao, became the first to graduate with a practical PhD in clinical medicine in June after developing an artificial-intelligence-empowered software program to help physicians classify a rare spine condition. Liping Duan, chief physician of gastroenterology at Peking University Third Hospital, said the biggest challenge will be creating a uniform standard to assess practical achievements across different medical fields. Source: nature.com

The nitric-oxide nanoparticle work stands out because it moved beyond laboratory killing assays to a live Galleria mellonella infection model. Larvae weigh roughly a quarter gram yet mount immune responses comparable to those in vertebrates, allowing researchers to test both safety and efficacy while reducing mammalian experiments. The particles release the gas over several hours, addressing nitric oxide’s rapid breakdown. If the approach scales, it could supply a non-antibiotic option for wound infections where resistance is rising. The next required step is mammalian toxicity and dosing data before any human trial. Khutoryanskiy emphasized that with bacteria becoming harder to kill with antibiotics, treatments that work in a different way are urgently needed, and nitric oxide’s multi-front attack on germs makes resistance development more difficult.

Science Deep Dive: How microbes become unique in isolated extremes

Most people assume microbes travel so easily through air, water and wind that identical species appear wherever environmental conditions match. In Antarctica the picture differs sharply because the continent’s long geographic isolation has allowed certain bacterial lineages to evolve separately from populations elsewhere. The circumpolar current has kept Antarctica effectively cut off from other landmasses for ten to fifteen million years, a period far longer than the roughly one and a half million years the Arctic has been cold while remaining connected to surrounding continents. This extended separation, combined with extreme conditions such as rapid temperature swings, intense ultraviolet radiation and scarce nutrients, creates selective pressures that favor specialized survival traits not required in less isolated cold regions. Researchers compared strains of the bacterium Arthrobacter collected from Antarctic soils with samples from the Atacama Desert, Svalbard and the Tibetan Plateau and identified distinct genetic clusters present only in the Antarctic isolates, supporting the idea that these microbes evolved in place rather than arriving recently from elsewhere. These same adaptations that permit survival in such harsh settings also appear to produce compounds with potential medical applications, including bacteria cultured from Antarctic marine invertebrates that inhibited growth of clinically important resistant pathogens in laboratory tests. A separate investigation around the Rothera Research Station found that treated wastewater exerts only limited influence on surrounding marine microbial communities, yet the bacteria readily adapted to warmer temperatures when grown in the lab, hinting that rising global temperatures could alter the very conditions that have maintained this microbial uniqueness. Continued warming therefore risks changing the selective environment that has preserved these isolated lineages, making current surveys of Antarctic microbial diversity a necessary baseline for detecting future shifts. Documenting which strains exist only on the continent also raises practical questions about conservation priorities and the risk of introducing non-native microorganisms through increased human activity such as tourism and scientific operations.

New findings on fire risk, climate feedbacks and isolated microbes give concrete numbers to track in coming years.
