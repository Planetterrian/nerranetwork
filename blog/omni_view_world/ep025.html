100 percent tariffs on Russian oil's top buyers are off, days before they were due.

By Mira October 17, 2026 Ep 25 10 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents

What You Need to Know: President Donald Trump's Friday order takes tariffs of up to 100 percent off the top buyers of Russian oil and gas, days before those sanctions were due. The same month's diesel opening, cleared by the US Treasury through April 2027, is what Ukraine's president calls a gift to Moscow and what Europe's economy chief says will not move global prices. This edition was drawn from the world feeds alone; no regional desk edition was available when it was assembled.

The Ten

Trump eases Russia oil sanctions, but is Europe still buying Moscow's gas?: Al Jazeera

In the United States, President Donald Trump on Friday signed an executive order waiving newly passed Russia sanctions, and he said the move is in the national interest. The order landed days before the measures were due to take effect. It called off numerous planned Russia-related sanctions, including duties on Russian goods and penalties for buying the country's oil and gas.

Those blocked measures were part of legislation Congress passed last month with bipartisan support. The law is named after the late Senator Lindsay Graham, an outspoken critic of Russia. Had it taken effect, it would have included tariffs of up to 100 percent on all goods brought into the United States from the five top importers of Russian crude oil or gas, a list that includes China and India. It could also have imposed equal penalties on countries deemed to have knowingly bought oil or gas from Russia 30 days after the law's implementation. The same penalties could have reached countries judged to be among the top five helping Moscow circumvent sanctions on sales of its oil. The waiver holds until the Trump administration believes further action is appropriate, according to the executive order.

Friday's order is the second major opening to Russian energy this month, and it sits on top of a diesel arrangement already in motion. Earlier this month, Trump announced a deal to bring stocks of Russian diesel into global markets, in a bid to drive down energy prices that have spiked during the United States-Israel war on Iran. The US Treasury waived sanctions on Russian diesel sales until April 2027. According to Trump, that deal would bring 300,000 tonnes of diesel fuel into US and global markets immediately, followed by 500,000 tonnes in November, and 1 million tonnes afterwards.

Trump's embrace of Russian energy has angered Ukraine, European partners and many US officials. They say the move rewards Moscow while it continues its four-and-a-half-year war against its neighbour. Ukraine's President Volodmyr Zelenskyy slammed the diesel deal as a gift to Russia's President Vladimir Putin that will help fund his war effort. European countries have also criticised the turn toward Russian energy. Trump has accused them of hypocrisy, because figures show that European Union countries continue to import Russian gas.

In Washington, the congressional fight is now about an order that stops a law Congress had already passed. US Congressman Don Beyer, a Democrat from Virginia, said the sanctions waiver amounts to a surrender to Putin that betrays Ukrainian partners and defies clear congressional intent. The Graham measures had bipartisan support last month. They are not repealed in the account given on Friday. They are waived, and the waiver runs until the administration itself decides that further action is appropriate.

Who gains is argued in money, not only in speeches. Vasily Astrov, an economist at the Vienna Institute for International Economic Studies, told Al Jazeera that the Russian government will likely be the greatest beneficiary, because the easing of sanctions means Russian oil will not have to be further discounted. He estimated that about 60 cents of every extra dollar in the price of a barrel of Russian oil would end up in state coffers. Russian official Kirill Dmitriev, an envoy for Putin, hailed the US decision to waive the sanctions as an important and truly historic moment. Other beneficiaries, on this account, include major importers of Russian oil and gas, such as China and India, who are now less exposed to the risk of secondary US penalties.

The scale of those purchases is already large. The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, a nonprofit think tank, said China and India were the largest buyers of Russian crude oil in September, purchasing 6 billion euros, or 6.74 billion dollars, and 3.6 billion euros, or 4.04 billion dollars, respectively. A tariff of up to 100 percent on goods from the five top importers would have reached economies of that size. A penalty on knowing buyers, 30 days after implementation, would have reached further. Friday's order stops both, for as long as the waiver stands.

The price case for American consumers is the reason Trump has given, and it is the part experts treat as least sure. He hopes the measures will help bring down oil prices for US consumers before November midterm elections. Experts say that is less certain. Addressing the diesel deal, EU economy chief Valdis Dombrovskis said it is unlikely to have a significant impact on global prices. He told the AFP news agency that the volumes are not of any macroeconomic significance, and that there are strong doubts whether Russia is even able to supply those volumes and meet US quality standards.

Set the week in order, using only what these reports name. Last month, Congress passed the Graham sanctions with support from both parties, including tariffs of up to 100 percent aimed at the top importers of Russian oil and gas, plus later penalties for knowing buyers and for countries helping Moscow get around oil sanctions. Earlier this month, Trump announced Russian diesel for global markets and the Treasury waived diesel sanctions until April 2027, with volumes he put at 300,000 tonnes immediately, 500,000 tonnes in November, and 1 million tonnes after that. On Friday, days before the wider sanctions were due, he signed the waiver and called it the national interest. Dmitriev called that historic. Beyer called it a surrender that defies Congress. Zelenskyy had already called the diesel deal a gift that helps fund the war. Dombrovskis says those diesel tonnes will not move global prices in any macroeconomic way.

What it changes, and for whom, follows from those named claims. Buyers in China and India, and any country that would have sat in the top five or been judged a knowing purchaser or a circumvention helper, are for now outside a US tariff and penalty wall that was days from starting. The Russian state, if Astrov's estimate holds, keeps about 60 cents of every extra dollar on a barrel that no longer has to be discounted as deeply. Ukrainians are told by their president that the diesel opening helps pay for a war now described as four and a half years long. European governments that still import Russian gas are accused by Trump of hypocrisy even as they criticise Washington. US consumers are the people Trump says he is trying to help before November, and Dombrovskis says the diesel volumes will not do that macroeconomic work. The next dated marks the sources name are November's midterm elections, the November tranche of 500,000 tonnes of diesel, and the April 2027 end of the Treasury's diesel waiver. The wider sanctions waiver has no end date in the order beyond the administration's own judgment that further action is appropriate.

Both Sides: Should northern-border voters treat tariffs on Canada as a reason to oppose the president's party?

What is being argued, in states that touch Canada, is whether President Donald Trump's tariffs and his treatment of Canada are a reason to vote against his party in this year's midterms. States that border Canada will elect eight US senators this year, nearly a quarter of the total. The decision sits with those voters, not with Ottawa. Earlier this week, US Ambassador to Canada Pete Hoekstra warned Canada against interfering in US politics. The Globe and Mail reports that it is American politicians, strategists and voters who have put Canada into the campaign.

The case for treating the tariffs as a reason to turn against the president and his party is the economic one, and independent Idaho Senate candidate Todd Achilles makes it in the plainest terms. He is challenging incumbent Republican Jim Risch. Last year, nearly 40 percent of Idaho exports went to Canada, which he called a hugely important market. He said folks are not happy. On the trail, he said, Trump's treatment of Canada has become a punchline, and he put the question as what the country has become if it is buying diesel from the Russians and prohibiting trade with Canadians. The Globe and Mail reports the same worry in local goods: Canadian concrete and road salt in Maine, missing Canadian tourists in New Hampshire, Labatt beer and a trade war against a neighbour in Michigan, construction materials in Minnesota, fertilizer in Montana, dairy exports in Idaho. Polling by HarrisX and the Center for American Political Studies at Harvard, cited in that report, ranks tariffs lowest in popularity among 14 key policies of this administration. For voters, inflation is the top election concern, and they see Trump's tariffs as the top reason for that inflation.

The case against making Canada the reason to oppose the party is that the fight is warranted even when it hurts. Montana's Republican Senate candidate, Kurt Alme, told Q2 News that Canada has not played fair for far too long and that somebody needed to stand up and push back for a level playing field. Polling by American Pulse Research and Polling with NonStop Local found that nearly 60 percent of Republican voters in Montana said they had been hurt by tariffs but nonetheless feel the dispute with Canada is warranted. New Hampshire Republican strategist Paul Young, founder of Novus Public Affairs, said that from an economic standpoint people there do not think of Canada as a crucial trade partner. He also said it is not clear that concern about skirmishes with Canada will loom large over the ballot box. That is an argument about salience rather than about fairness.

No result is known, and none is predicted here. The next step is the midterm vote itself, including those eight northern-border Senate seats. Source: theglobeandmail.com

Progress Watch

In the Philippines, rooftop solar panels have nearly doubled since early 2025, according to a report by the energy think tank Ember, a measured build-out rather than a pledge. In the first months of the United States-Israel war on Iran, from March to May, panel imports reached 407 million dollars. Government trade data from China, where most panels are manufactured, made the Philippines the biggest single overseas market in that period. Only the Netherlands bought more, and it redistributes panels to the rest of Europe. Homeowner Keith Cantera in Manila said his family had put off panels that can cost up to 5,000 dollars. His monthly bill went from about 150 dollars before the war began in late February to as much as 220 dollars. He said the installation cost matches about three or four years of bills at the current rate, and that in the fifth year electricity would essentially be free. Solarmaxx chief executive Lexus Grepo said the firm had 40 projects in March, almost triple its usual month, and has since been setting up 25 to 30 systems a month, with a 30-day wait because shipments cannot keep up. The Philippines already had Southeast Asia's highest electricity costs, comparable only to Singapore, where average income is more than ten times higher. It relies on imported coal and gas for more than 70 percent of its power mix, and the peso has slid to a record low of more than 63 to the US dollar. In late March, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr declared a national energy emergency over what he called imminent danger to the national energy supply. A government loan scheme for rooftop installations, launched in March, is open only to public sector workers. Officials point to projects already in the pipeline, including a 3,500-hectare solar farm north of Manila, projected to be the largest in the world, expected to generate 850 megawatts for 2.4 million households when fully operational. Source: aljazeera.com
