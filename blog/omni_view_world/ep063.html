Reports of a nuclear satellite launch within sixty days push Nato to vow space defence.

The Ten Both Sides: Whether Nato should change space defence over Russian satellite reports

What You Need to Know: German media reports of a possible Russian nuclear-armed satellite, shared among Nato countries this month, drew a pledge from the alliance to defend the space element of its territory. Separately, Gaza health officials warned of a meningitis surge as they reported new deaths from Israeli strikes, and Nvidia restored Jensen Huang after President Donald Trump said he had pressed the board. This edition also draws on the Omni View Omni View North America desk.

The Ten

Nato chief vows defence against space threat after Russian nuclear satellite reports: BBC

In Germany, Nato secretary general Mark Rutte said the alliance will defend against threats in space after German reports that Russia is preparing to launch a nuclear-armed satellite. Public broadcasters NDR and WDR and the newspaper Süddeutscher Zeitung, citing anonymous German military and intelligence sources, said a satellite dubbed Sputnik-S was equipped with a nuclear warhead and could disrupt or destroy other satellites, including communications and observation craft. France 24 reported that the accounts cited unnamed German military and BND sources and described a possible launch in the coming weeks; the BBC said intelligence reports of a launch in the next sixty days had been shared among Nato countries this month, and that it was not clear which country wrote them. Chancellor Friedrich Merz did not confirm or deny the reports, called them not new yet no less shocking, and said they reveal the level of escalation Russia is evidently prepared to pursue, while government spokesman Stefan Kornelius said they were being taken very seriously and also warned against excessive nervousness over hypothetical dangers. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he had not seen the reports and dismissed stories citing unknown sources as hardly worth taking seriously; the Russian embassy in Berlin rejected what it called unfounded allegations and a propaganda campaign. Beside Merz in Berlin, Rutte said Nato will defend every inch of its territory, including space, that the alliance has a variety of response options, and that it will assess deterrence and defence and update them where necessary. Russia is a signatory to the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which bans weapons of mass destruction from orbit. European Space Agency head Josef Aschbacher called nuclear weapons in space the ultimate nightmare scenario, and Bleddyn Bowen of Durham University told the BBC a nuclear blast in space would have massive effects on more than 15,000 active satellites and would be indiscriminate enough to damage Russia's own space infrastructure. In 2024 the United States accused Russia of developing a space-based anti-satellite weapon with a nuclear warhead; Russia denied it, and President Vladimir Putin said then that Russia remained categorically against deploying nuclear weapons in space.

Ranks: militaries and societies that rely on shared satellites · a reported orbital nuclear weapon would threaten communications and observation craft · a claimed window of sixty days, not confirmed Source: bbc.co.uk

Israeli air strikes in Gaza kill 7 as doctors warn of meningitis outbreak: Al Jazeera

In Gaza, health officials reported at least seven Palestinians killed in Israeli strikes, including a child and police officers, as the Ministry of Health issued an epidemic alert over meningitis. Dr Nidal Ghoneim, deputy director general of primary care, said cases rose to 272 in October, more than eight times a typical month of 30 to 50, and that 15 people have died of the disease since the start of this year, including five in August, September and October. He said bacterial meningitis, the most serious form, reached 108 cases in October, against a usual 10 to 20 a month, and that another 100 cases have been recorded since the start of November. The ministry warned that the spread threatens thousands of people, particularly children, given deteriorating health and environmental conditions. Medical sources said three Palestinians, including a child, were killed in a strike on the Wadi Gaza area in the centre of the Strip. The Palestinian news agency Wafa said an Israeli drone strike on al-Mawasi, west of Khan Younis, killed three and wounded five, and Al Jazeera's Hind Khoudary reported that those three were police officers. Wafa also said a person died of injuries after being targeted in Jabalia camp in northern Gaza, and that two civilians were killed Monday night in an air strike on Gaza City. Khoudary said dozens more were injured across the Strip and that strikes have continued daily even though a ceasefire was agreed.

Ranks: people in Gaza, especially children · deaths reported today and a meningitis rate far above the usual month · immediate Source: aljazeera.com

Trump faces backlash after saying he 'convinced' Nvidia to reinstate CEO Jensen Huang: BBC

In the United States, Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive less than two weeks after he was forced to resign over extramarital affairs, one of them with a suspected Chinese spy, after pressure from President Donald Trump. Trump said he made the deal with the board, which had rejected Huang's initial offer to return, and told CNBC the board asked for assurance on the national security threat and was given assurances based on everything officials know. He said it is a threat to lose Huang, not to have him, and when asked whether stock losses after the resignation influenced him, he said probably so. Huang resigned on 12 November; last Tuesday the board said it did not intend to reinstate him, and chairman Harvey Jones later said the return was in the best interests of stockholders, employees, partners and customers. The board said it had input from senior levels of the US government and was confident the reinstatement posed no national security risk. The BBC says the comeback added almost $1 trillion to Nvidia's market value as the share price jumped 31 percent, lifting other tech shares and Asian markets while European markets were flat, though the price remains down over the past month amid wider concerns about the AI industry. Trump and Nvidia face backlash from lawmakers, and Huang is due before Congress next week over his relationship with Alice Huang, alleged to be a spy for China; China has denied the spying allegations. On Monday the Commerce Department said it was preparing to blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore-based Nvidia customer where Alice Huang, no relation, was chief executive. A BBC review of disclosures found Trump bought Nvidia shares worth between $23 million and $86 million over two years, including a $265,000 purchase and sales of as much as $15,000 in September, in 85 separate purchases; Huang, 63, co-founded the company in 1993.

Ranks: users of AI chips and US national-security oversight · the person who leads the central AI chipmaker is back, with a congressional hearing next · this week, hearing next week Source: bbc.co.uk

How the AI trade could trap even cautious investors: DW

In Germany, asset manager Gerd Kommer told DW that a portfolio with 70 percent in one country, the United States, does not reasonably represent the global economy, which he said is more resilient than any national economy. He said investors need exposure to every sector and that no single sector should make up more than a quarter of a portfolio. DW reports that flagship index funds buy shares according to market value, so the larger a company grows, the more of it they hold, and that these funds hold pensions and other savings of hundreds of millions of passive investors. Nine of the top 10 holdings of the MSCI All Country World Index, which covers more than 2,400 firms in 47 markets, are US Big Tech names, and the other is TSMC, which produces 90 percent of high-end AI semiconductors. Those 10 holdings make up nearly a quarter of the index, and nearly two-thirds of its value is in US-listed companies even though they are only about a fifth of its firms. In MSCI's Emerging Market index, the top 10 make up 37.5 percent of value, TSMC alone almost 16 percent, and tech, chip and internet firms are eight of the largest holdings, beside one Chinese bank and one Indian bank. Dimitri Vayanos of the London School of Economics told DW his model suggests flows into index funds have larger price effects when the large stocks are in the same industry, because optimistic buying is reinforced when funds add to winners. Economists told DW that investors looking for shelter from the AI trade are finding few places to go, partly because of how these indexes work.

Ranks: hundreds of millions of passive savers · their global funds are heavily tied to a few AI-linked firms · already the case in current indexes Source: dw.com

Quebec court rules MNAs must swear fealty to the King: The Globe and Mail

In Canada, Quebec Superior Court Justice Ian Demers ruled that members of the provincial legislature must take an oath of allegiance to King Charles III before they can assume their duties in the National Assembly. He granted an emergency application to suspend a 2022 law that had abolished the requirement to swear fealty to the British monarch. Demers wrote that, contrary to what it may seem, the oath plays a vital role in democracy. He added that the oath acknowledges the primacy of the Constitution, personified by the King. After the October 5 election, the province's 127 legislators swore allegiance only to the people of Quebec. The National Assembly is set to resume next week. The Globe and Mail reported the ruling in a decision released Tuesday.

Ranks: Quebec's 127 legislators and the voters they represent · they cannot sit without the royal oath the 2022 law had dropped · before the assembly resumes next week Source: theglobeandmail.com

Both Sides: Whether Nato should change space defence over Russian satellite reports

Nato is deciding how to treat German media reports, shared among allies this month, that Russia may launch a nuclear-armed satellite within sixty days. Secretary general Mark Rutte said the alliance will assess deterrence and defence and update them where necessary.

The case for treating the reports as a reason to harden space defence starts with Rutte: space is part of defending every inch of Nato territory, and he said the alliance has a variety of response options. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's strongest point is that the reports, which he neither confirmed nor denied, reveal an escalation Russia is evidently prepared to pursue, even if they are not new. Josef Aschbacher, head of the European Space Agency, called nuclear weapons in space the ultimate nightmare scenario. Bleddyn Bowen of Durham University gave the practical stake: more than 15,000 active satellites, and a blast would be indiscriminate.

The case against acting on the reports as if a launch were established starts with Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov: he had not seen them, and stories citing unknown sources are hardly worth taking seriously. The Russian embassy in Berlin said the claims are unfounded allegations and part of a propaganda campaign. German spokesman Stefan Kornelius, while saying the reports were taken very seriously, warned against excessive nervousness over hypothetical dangers. Russia has signed the 1967 Outer Space Treaty banning weapons of mass destruction from orbit, and in 2024, after the United States accused Moscow of developing a nuclear-armed anti-satellite weapon and Russia denied it, President Vladimir Putin said Russia was still categorically against nuclear weapons in space.

What happens next is the assessment Rutte described.

Some cookies keep the site working. Before you choose, Google gets only basic, cookie-free signals (for example, that a page loaded), with no cookies or IDs that identify you. With your OK you can also turn on: Analytics, which uses Google Analytics cookies to show us which pages and episodes are useful, and Ad measurement, which uses Google Ads to measure sign-ups from our ads. When you sign up from a Nerra Personal page, Ad measurement also sends Google a coded (hashed) version of your email. Change your mind any time from Cookie settings at the bottom of every page.
