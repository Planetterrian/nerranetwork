Two chambers of Congress flipped to Democrats, checking Trump on tariffs and the Iran war.

By Mira November 07, 2026 Ep 46 12 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents
The Ten Both Sides: Repealing tariffs on Canadian goods

The new majority can legislate and investigate, but it lacks the votes to override a veto, while strikes continue and Tehran says the Strait of Hormuz stays shut. This edition also draws on the Omni View Omni View North America desk.

The Ten

Five ways the midterm election results could reshape Donald Trump's America: The Globe and Mail

The United States has a Congress no longer run by President Donald Trump's party, and the argument over what that majority can do is already about prices, tariffs and a war. The Globe and Mail reported that this week's midterm vote swept Republicans from power and that Democrats won control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives. VPM News said Democrats won strong majorities in both chambers. The Globe and Mail called the result a political upheaval unlike any seen in generations, and framed the morning after as revival for Democrats and a deepening political dusk for Mr. Trump.

For the past two years, the Globe and Mail wrote, Congress under Republican control largely stood as a passive bystander to Mr. Trump. Democratic control, in that account, gives the party strength to pass legislation, reject judges, investigate wrongdoing and counter attempts by the president to rule by executive fiat. The same report states the limit in the same breath: Democrats hold insufficient numbers to overcome his veto. The paper asks, without answering, whether the country gets a hard-fought stalemate between branches seeking mutual obstruction, or room to address living costs, corruption and federal debt grown to astronomical proportions, while both parties prepare for the end of his presidency. It said five themes are likely to dominate the new day. The first is cost.

Senate Leader Chuck Schumer promised this week that the first priority for victorious Democrats is to "reduce people's costs and work the legislative process so it does." He did not say how. Candidates had spent months decrying tariffs, and some returned to that theme on election night as they began looking toward a legislative agenda. Sherrod Brown in Ohio said, "We will fight to end these tariffs." Josh Turek in Iowa said Mr. Trump's tariffs "are decimating markets, killing farmers and hurting our rural communities." Adam Hamilton, the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Kansas since 1932, said every Kansan has been "hurt by the tariffs." Those are campaign and leadership statements, not statutes.

The tariff map, as the Globe and Mail sets it out, is wide. Mr. Trump has levied tariffs on about 60 countries under the auspices of seeking to eliminate "forced labour" from countries such as Switzerland and Canada, in addition to a raft of measures on metals and vehicles and, more recently, 50 per cent taxes on tens of billions of dollars in imports from Canada, the northern neighbour. Democrats, the paper wrote, have little choice but to try to reverse some of those measures, because the campaign was built on the cost of them.

Democratic campaign strategist Chuck Rocha worked on campaigns that won 21 Congressional seats in the midterm vote. He made 251 ads, virtually all of them on cost, corruption and the overreach of immigration enforcement. In many, he said, "we put the tariffs and the war in the same ads, as the two reasons driving up cost." For that reason alone, he said, "I do think Congress will do something around tariffs." He also named the constraint in the next breath: "The problem with the tariff argument is that the President has so much control over tariffs." On whether lawmakers can force a change, he said, "I don't know the legality policy-wise." Democrats have said they are determined to try.

Jamie Raskin, a Maryland congressman, told CBC's Canadian audience that Congress would tackle tariffs "within the first 100 days" of the party's return to legislative power. Ron Wyden, a Democratic senator from Oregon, told The Wall Street Journal: "There is overwhelming support for repealing the tariffs on products from Canada." One option the Globe and Mail describes is to strike down the legal authority used by the White House to tax imports. An obvious candidate, the paper says, would be Section 338, the provision under the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act that Mr. Trump used for the first time in U.S. history to impose 50-per-cent tariffs on goods from Canada this year. Congress could also codify the Supreme Court decision that struck down his earlier use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose tariffs. Neither step has been taken. No repeal vote is on a calendar in these reports. Raskin's clock is a promise of the first 100 days, not a scheduled sitting.

The New York Times reported a separate caution for Canada: while U.S. voters largely oppose the trade war with Canada, many Democrats and their supporters have protectionist leanings, and America's rebuke of Mr. Trump will not swiftly restore Canada-U.S. harmony. That is the paper's reading of the new majority, not a finding that a tariff bill has failed. France 24 reported that, in this era of divided government, Democratic lawmakers say their priorities will be tackling the rising cost of living, investigating alleged corruption, and tightening oversight of ICE agents. Antonia Kerrigan reported that account. Alleged corruption is the word the lawmakers' stated priority uses; the reports here do not set out a charge, a defendant or a finding.

The White House answered the vote with a staff change, not a tariff retreat. The Guardian reported that on Friday afternoon Mr. Trump abruptly announced on his Truth Social platform that Stephen Miller would replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff, and that he confirmed Wiles had been ousted. The Guardian describes Miller as his most trusted adviser and as the architect of his sweeping immigration crackdown, the record the appointment puts at the centre of the West Wing. It reported that Democrats and Republicans blasted the promotion. Trump explaining the change beyond the announcement that Wiles was out and Miller was in.

Virginia, in VPM News's tally, did not move as one bloc even as the national count did. The Associated Press called the 1st Congressional District for Democrat Shannon Taylor over longtime Republican Representative Rob Wittman; VPM said that call came Thursday, with Keyris Manzanares reporting it. Republican Representative John McGuire held the 5th against Tom Perriello. Billy Shields reported that McGuire withstood what VPM called the national blue wave and retained the seat. Eugene Vindman won reelection in Virginia's 7th District. Voters signed off on all three constitutional amendments. On Tuesday night Jahd Khalil, recording from his car in Henrico County, reported voters approving those three state amendments. VPM aired Governor Abigail Spanberger, at a virtual press conference, talking about a statewide shift toward Democrats, and reported Jackie McDonnough's win in a Richmond School Board race as unexpected. Gregory Six won a special election to remain Hanover County sheriff.

Certification is still a process, not a finished national headline. VPM explained that before winners are declared, local election officers review and verify the totals for each voting machine. Those totals are collected and given to the general registrar and the local electoral board. Local electoral boards certify results and declare winners for local offices and issues. The State Board of Elections verifies statewide races, constitutional amendments, General Assembly elections and federal races such as Congress. Electoral Board Chair Bill Pantele said of the Richmond canvass, "It's done when it's done, and when it's done right." He said the board would work through the weekend if that is what it takes, because November elections with higher turnouts mean more work, including provisional and absentee ballots as well as regular votes. Much of the national attention on Tuesday night, VPM noted, sat on Congress; by Wednesday, Richmond's job was canvassing and verifying what was cast locally.

The war that Rocha put beside tariffs in those ads is still being fought, and Saturday's reporting is about strikes and a waterway, not about a ceasefire. ASHARQ AL-AWSAT, citing army spokesman Mohammad Akraminia through the state news agency IRNA, reported that Iran's military is "stronger" and more motivated than when the United States and Israel started the war in February. Akraminia said, according to IRNA: "Today, the Islamic Republic's armed forces... having gained experience from direct confrontation with the United States and the Israeli regime, are on higher alert and are stronger than they were at the beginning of the war." He added that they "now possess greater motivation, commitment, and self-confidence." The war began when the United States and Israel carried out a massive wave of strikes in late February and has been deadlocked for months.

Tehran has sought to limit traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, which normally carries about one-fifth of global oil consumption. According to AFP, as carried by ASHARQ AL-AWSAT, Akraminia said Iranian armed forces retained control over the strategic waterway and that it would remain closed until the United States accepted Tehran's demands. That is the spokesman's claim about control and about a condition for reopening, not a published shipping tally. Under the order of the Supreme Leader, he added, "the response of the armed forces will be immediate, effective, and without geographical limitations."

President Trump this week confirmed new strikes on Iran, after American media reported attacks on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. On Saturday the local Tasnim news agency reported that two missiles had struck Qeshm, adding that there was no official information so far on the damage. Iranian state television reported two explosions on the island. The Iranian army said early Saturday that its air defenses had "struck and destroyed" a U.S. drone that was flying over the island, according to state media. It did not say whether the island was attacked. Those are three accounts of the same morning: a presidential confirmation of new strikes, a local agency's report of two missiles with no official damage figure, and an army claim, via state media, that a drone was destroyed.

Anadolu Ajansı reported that Australia's premier urged the United States to de-escalate the Iran war after the Republican midterm losses. EA WorldView reported that its correspondent, speaking with Pakistan TV's Sana Maqbool, set out the vote's significance for Vice President J.D. Vance, described as an opponent of the war from its launch, who proposed reopening talks to Iran. The correspondent considered the possibility that the United States and Iran may converge on a settlement allowing Tehran to continue enriching uranium, albeit at lower levels, in exchange for significant relief from U.S. sanctions. The correspondent said that shape would resemble the 2015 nuclear agreement between Iran and the 5+1 powers, including the United States, the agreement the correspondent said Mr. Trump ripped up in 2018. That is an analyst's reading of a possible bargain, not a text tabled by either capital. The same piece's photograph caption places Mr. Vance before a quadrilateral meeting of the United States, Iran, Pakistan and Qatar at the Burgenstock resort above Lake Lucerne on June 21, 2026. No new round is dated in Saturday's reports.

What changes, and for whom, is therefore split by institution and by clock. Importers paying the Canadian tariff line, and the farmers and rural buyers named by Brown, Turek and Hamilton, are the people Democratic leaders say a cost bill would reach, if one is written inside Raskin's 100 days and if it survives a veto the Globe and Mail says this majority cannot override. Oil buyers are the people named by the scale of the strait: about one-fifth of global oil consumption normally moves there, and Tehran's army spokesman says the waterway stays under Iranian control until Washington accepts Iranian demands, while Washington has confirmed further strikes. Staff in the West Wing change now: Miller in, Wiles out, as of Friday's announcement. Virginia's local boards are still in the canvass, with Pantele's weekend as the near date, and the State Board still to verify the federal races. None of the outlets above reports a signed repeal, a ceasefire, or a date for talks.

Ranks: Voters, importers and oil buyers in the United States, Canada and beyond · a majority that can legislate and investigate but cannot override a veto, beside a war still claimed to be closing a strait that carries about one-fifth of global oil · immediate for staffing and strikes, and inside a stated first hundred days for any tariff bill Source: theglobeandmail.com · vpm.org · theguardian.com · france24.com · nytimes.com · english.aawsat.com · eaworldview.com · aa.com.tr

Both Sides: Repealing tariffs on Canadian goods

The decision in front of the new Congress is whether to repeal President Donald Trump's tariffs on goods from Canada, and whether it has a legal path that survives the presidency.

The case for repeal, as Democrats stated it, starts with household and farm costs rather than with a drafted statute. Chuck Schumer said the first priority is to reduce people's costs through the legislative process. Sherrod Brown said Democrats will fight to end the tariffs. Josh Turek said the tariffs are decimating markets, killing farmers and hurting rural communities. Adam Hamilton said every Kansan has been hurt by them. Ron Wyden told The Wall Street Journal there is overwhelming support for repealing the tariffs on products from Canada. Chuck Rocha said tariffs and the war were paired in ads as the two reasons driving up cost, which is why he thinks Congress will act. Their shared reason is price: the tariffs, in this telling, are a cause of the costs the new majority was elected to cut.

The case for leaving the tariffs where they are rests on the purpose under which they were imposed and on who holds the pen. The Globe and Mail reported that Mr. Trump levied tariffs on about 60 countries under the auspices of seeking to eliminate forced labour from countries including Switzerland and Canada, and that this year's 50 per cent taxes on tens of billions of dollars in Canadian imports used Section 338 of the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act for the first time in U.S. history. Rocha, who expects Congress to try, said the president has so much control over tariffs that he does not know the legality of a congressional change. The New York Times reported that many Democrats and their supporters have protectionist leanings even while U.S. voters largely oppose the trade war with Canada, and that the election will not swiftly restore harmony. The Globe and Mail also reported that Democrats lack the numbers to overcome a veto, so a repeal that passes one or both chambers can still stop at the White House. That limit is arithmetic in the paper's account, not a prediction of how Mr. Trump would use it.

No vote is dated. Raskin's clock is the first 100 days. Wyden described support, not a bill number. Schumer did not say how costs would come down. A Supreme Court decision has already struck down an earlier tariff use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, and the Globe and Mail says Congress could codify that ruling, or could try to strike Section 338. Both remain options on paper. Source: theglobeandmail.com · nytimes.com
