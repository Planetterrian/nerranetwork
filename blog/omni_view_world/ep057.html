Mary Peltola’s Alaska win gives Democrats a fifty-six to forty-four Senate edge.

By Mira November 18, 2026 Ep 57 11 min read Listen to podcast

The Ten Both Sides: Ukraine’s deep strikes inside Russia Progress Watch

The same day, President Trump halted some sanctions on Russia and announced U.S. purchases of Russian diesel, British inflation rose to 3.6 percent, and a World Court genocide case on Myanmar moved toward a Thursday ruling. This edition also draws on the Omni View Omni View North America desk.

The Ten

Mary Peltola flips Alaska seat, giving Democrats a 56-44 edge in Senate: The Washington Post

In the United States, the last unsettled Senate count of the midterms has now closed. The Washington Post reported that Mary Peltola flipped Alaska’s seat and that the win leaves Democrats with a 56-44 edge in the Senate. MS NOW reported that she unseated Senator Dan Sullivan, so the seat changes hands rather than staying with the incumbent the network named. The BBC reported that confirmation of her victory arrived two weeks after midterm counting began. The BBC said that confirmation caps what it called the party’s rout of the Republicans in the midterm Senate election. Those accounts describe a finished count, not a projection and not a forecast of any later contest. The balance those outlets state is fifty-six Democratic seats to forty-four on the other side, a margin that sets the chamber Democrats will organize. For Alaskans, the person who will hold the state’s Senate seat has changed; for the rest of the country, the reported change is which party holds the majority and by how many seats. No outlet in today’s file predicted a later race from this result.

Ranks: the U.S. Senate’s party balance · Democrats hold a 56-44 edge · confirmed after two weeks of counting Source: washingtonpost.com · bbc.co.uk · ms.now

Putin Will Continue Ukraine War Despite Plans to Meet Trump, Intelligence Says: The New York Times

In the United States, a policy shift on Russia landed on the same day President Donald Trump is due to talk about Ukraine with the leaders of Russia and China. The New York Times reported that he has halted some sanctions on Russia and announced that the United States will buy Russian diesel. The same account said intelligence judges that President Vladimir Putin will continue the Ukraine war despite plans to meet Trump. The sanctions step, as the Times stated it, is a halt to some measures, not a claim that every sanction has been lifted. The diesel announcement is a purchase by the United States, named as Russian diesel, not a general description of world oil trade. Ukraine is the subject the Times said Trump plans to raise with those two leaders. For Ukrainians, the reported American moves are fewer sanctions on Russia and a U.S. buyer for Russian diesel, while the intelligence judgment in the headline is that the war does not stop because a meeting is planned. The Times placed that judgment beside the sanctions halt and the diesel announcement as the facts of the day, not as a prediction of what the leaders will sign.

Ranks: Ukraine’s war and U.S.-Russia energy trade · some sanctions halted and diesel purchases announced; war assessed as continuing · now, with talks planned Source: nytimes.com

UK inflation rises to 3.6% after petrol and diesel prices hit record high: The Guardian

In the United Kingdom, the Office for National Statistics said consumer-price inflation rose to 3.6 percent in October, from 3.5 percent in September, matching City economists’ forecasts and the highest headline rate in a year. Grant Fitzner, the ONS chief economist, said fuel prices were once again a major contributor, and that annual costs of raw materials and of goods leaving factories are still climbing, driven by higher crude oil and petrol prices. Petrol rose 5.2 pence a litre between September and October, to 174.7 pence, the highest in four years. Diesel rose 7 pence a litre, to 199.5 pence, the highest price on records dating to 1996. Motor fuel prices rose at an annual rate of 33.2 percent, the largest increase since July 2022, after Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The Guardian linked the October rise to fallout from the war in the Middle East. A Bank of England spokesperson said that conflict has caused energy prices and inflation to rise in recent months, and that inflation is expected to rise further by the middle of next year because of cost pressures already in the pipeline. The Bank said it raised bank rate from 3.75 percent to 4 percent two weeks ago to limit how far higher energy costs pass into other prices, and that it will keep setting rates so inflation returns to the 2 percent target and stays there. Forecasters cited in the account look for a peak close to 5 percent next year. Markets anticipate at least two quarter-point increases before the end of next year, and analysts said a rise could come as early as next month. Andy Burnham’s government has pledged household support. At last month’s budget, Chancellor John Healey announced a £100 winter handout for 20 million households. The October figures already include a 4 percent rise in the Ofgem energy price cap from the start of October. Cornwall Insight on Wednesday forecast a 26 percent jump in Great Britain energy bills from January, to £2,166 a year for a typical household. Core inflation was unchanged at 2.8 percent. Services inflation held at 3.7 percent, slightly under a City prediction of 3.8 percent. Food prices rose 0.9 percent, the slowest rate since September 2021, down from 1.3 percent in September. Air fares rose 1.8 percent between September and October on higher long-haul tickets, partly offset by cheaper European flights. Alcohol and tobacco prices rose 4.5 percent, influenced by the timing of a tobacco-duty increase at the start of the month.

Ranks: households across Britain · inflation at 3.6 percent, diesel at a record, bills forecast higher · October figures now, January bills forecast Source: theguardian.com

Fears grow over US sanctions on ICJ judges ahead of Myanmar genocide ruling: The Guardian

At the International Court of Justice, a genocide case brought over Myanmar is due for a ruling the Guardian described as set for Thursday, and lawyers and diplomats are already arguing about what a finding would mean. The Guardian reported growing fears that the United States could sanction judges, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court finds that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide. The definition the court must apply is acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, and proving that intent has been the hard point in earlier cases. Myanmar denies genocide and says its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents. Lawyers for Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017. Judges have taken advice on how to protect their assets from possible U.S. sanctions, on the view that a genocide ruling could be read in Washington as lowering the legal threshold. The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, described as close to the White House, has predicted that a genocide ruling will lead the United States to sanction the ICJ. The Guardian reported that the United States and Israel fear a final judgment may clarify how intent is established and so make it more likely the court later finds that Israel committed genocide in Gaza. The Gambia, as a litigant, appointed Pillay as an ad hoc judge; she is required to act as a jurist, not as an advocate for The Gambia. Mike Waltz, the U.S. ambassador to the United Nations, told Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges that Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponize the law against our troops,” and asserted that Pillay is tipping the scales to lower the threshold so it can later be used against American and allied soldiers “whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.” He said Washington sees her agenda and will watch the proceedings closely. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Friday that for “this global elite,” international law is an instrument to advance their interests “under the pretence of universal principles,” and he warned the International Criminal Court against quoting laws to “men with swords.” The United States has already sanctioned ICC judges after arrest warrants against Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, and has sanctioned the ICC as an institution it called a rogue court, citing fear the court could pursue U.S. troops. A 24-page submission by the United Kingdom and five other mainly European states argued that intent should be judged from a range of facts and circumstances, assessed comprehensively and holistically. Israel has criticised adverse ICJ advisory opinions and refused to implement 2024 orders to admit more aid into Gaza and to cooperate with Unrwa.

Ranks: Rohingya survivors and later genocide cases · a possible first state genocide finding, with U.S. sanction threats on judges · ruling described for Thursday Source: theguardian.com

Traumas of Sudan war lead to wave of drug addiction: Al Jazeera

In Sudan, a war that began in Khartoum in April 2023 has killed at least 59,000 people and forced 14 million from their homes in a country of 52 million, Al Jazeera reported. The fight is between the Sudanese armed forces and the Rapid Support Forces, former military allies, and the capital was an early centre of that struggle. Thousands in Khartoum State were trapped at home for weeks or forced to flee. The armed forces expelled the RSF from the region in May last year, and the capital has since become a haven for people leaving fighting elsewhere. Mohammed Awad was 17 when the war started. He told Al Jazeera he was pushed out of school and moved from state to state with his cousins looking for safety, and that he began taking drugs to forget what he saw in the first months, including friends and a cousin killed in front of him. He said he has entered rehabilitation centres many times, returned to addiction, takes pills and sometimes weed, and keeps failing when he tries to stop, though he wants to return to education. Government officials say insecurity and poverty have spread drug manufacturing and trade, particularly the amphetamine Captagon, and that areas now or formerly under RSF control are a particular problem. Lubna Ali, director of the Qadreen Center for Addiction Treatment, one of the few charitable centres dealing with the problem in Khartoum, said the centre kept providing medical care through the war and has seen a significant increase in people seeking treatment. Since the war broke out, the centre has treated 10,100 cases, from psychological support for war trauma to drug addiction, and Ali said 135 people have been treated in their recovery journey. Doctors in the capital told Al Jazeera demand for those services is growing. Young people, the report said, are especially exposed: violence, displacement, dead friends and relatives, interrupted school, and falling work. Weakened institutions and a worse security situation have undercut oversight and the health service’s ability to answer the addiction crisis.

Ranks: civilians in Sudan, including people seeking addiction care · at least 59,000 killed and 14 million displaced; treatment demand up · ongoing since April 2023 Source: aljazeera.com

EU proposes new €45 billion Ukraine loan to be repaid by Russia: France 24

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on Wednesday proposed a 45-billion-euro Ukraine loan for 2027, about two-thirds of Kyiv’s needs, repaid from future Russian reparations.

Ranks: Ukraine’s 2027 finances · a proposed loan for about two-thirds of needs · announced Wednesday, still a proposal Source: france24.com

US loses measles-free status as cases soar: France 24

The United States lost measles elimination status after 26 years, the Pan American Health Organization said Tuesday, and Bolivia and Mexico lost theirs.

Both Sides: Ukraine’s deep strikes inside Russia

At a gathering of Ukraine’s allies in Brussels on Tuesday, the question was whether Ukraine should keep striking deep inside Russia, and whether its backers should keep enabling those strikes. France 24 reported that Elbridge Colby, a top U.S. defence official, criticised Ukraine’s “deep strikes” against Russia. President Volodymyr Zelensky, at the same moment in the diplomatic calendar, urged Kyiv’s backers to send air defences without delay. The case against the deep strikes, as Colby put it by criticising them, is that the American official does not want that tactic to stand as the allies’ line. France 24 did not quote a longer military rationale from him. The case for pressing on, in the form France 24 reported, is the flat rejection from Germany and the United Kingdom, who hit back at the Americans’ suggestion. Their strongest stated position in that account is refusal: they did not accept the U.S. criticism. Zelensky’s request for air defences without delay is the Ukrainian government’s accompanying ask — protection at home while the argument over strikes inside Russia is unresolved. CNBC reported the same split as allies rejecting U.S. criticism of deep strikes ahead of a Trump-Putin-Xi summit. The next dated setting for the wider war talks is that summit, which other reporting placed on Thursday in Shenzhen, where President Trump has said the talks would cover the wars in Iran and Ukraine as well as nuclear disarmament. Nothing in today’s accounts records a vote that ends the strikes or a shipment that fills Zelensky’s air-defence request. Source: france24.com
