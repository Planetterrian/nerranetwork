Top 12 News Items

  1. Norway’s Wealth Fund Again Votes No to Musk’s Tesla Pay Package — Bloomberg.com

    Norway’s sovereign wealth fund voted against Elon Musk’s compensation package at Tesla for the second time.

    The fund has consistently opposed the package in prior shareholder votes. Source: bloomberg.com

  2. Tesla Files to Buy German Gear-Maker EMAG to Unblock Optimus — Dealroom

    Tesla filed with Germany’s antitrust authority to acquire EMAG, a machine-tool maker whose systems produce drive components for humanoid robots.

    The filing lists the deal as an asset purchase under case B5-132/26.

    EMAG employs about 220 people at its Salach site and has announced plans to cut 300 jobs.

    Tesla aims to reach one million Optimus units per year at Fremont and up to ten million at a future Giga Texas line.

    Tesla recently set up Tesla Machining Technologies Management SE with its address at EMAG’s headquarters.

    Germany has already supplied dozens of Optimus production sub-lines that were tested there before shipment to Fremont.

    Production of the Optimus Gen 3 design has begun at Fremont using floor space previously occupied by Model S and Model X lines.

    Musk has said Tesla will likely rent Optimus robots to customers rather than sell them outright. Source: app.dealroom.co

  3. Tesla’s New AI5 Chip May Not Reach Cars Until 2028 — TipRanks

    Tesla’s next-generation AI5 inference chip is not expected to enter production vehicles until 2028.

    The delay affects both unsupervised FSD capability and Optimus training and inference workloads that rely on higher-performance silicon.

    Current HW4 vehicles will continue to run the existing end-to-end model until the new hardware arrives. Source: tipranks.com

Tesla First Principles

The timeline for Tesla’s next inference chip now stretches into 2028, which shifts the conversation from hardware cadence to how much capability the existing fleet can still unlock. Current vehicles run the end-to-end model on HW4 hardware that was designed years earlier, yet the company continues to push software updates that improve perception and control without new silicon. That gap between chip arrival and software progress determines how quickly unsupervised FSD or Optimus training can scale on customer hardware already in the field. If the model keeps improving on today’s accelerators, the economic case for waiting on AI5 changes from a pure hardware bet to a question of utilization rates across millions of deployed computers. The same silicon also underpins Optimus inference, so any delay affects both the robotaxi network and the humanoid-robot cost curve at the same time. Vertical integration helps here because Tesla controls both the training clusters and the vehicle software stack, but it cannot shorten the semiconductor fabrication and qualification cycle that sits outside its walls. The practical result is that near-term gains will come from squeezing more performance out of the chips already shipping rather than from a new generation that remains years away.

The acquisition filing for EMAG reveals a different constraint on Optimus scaling. The German firm’s machines produce the precision gears and actuators required for humanoid drive systems, and Tesla has already tested dozens of sub-lines from that site before shipping them to Fremont. Bringing the capability in-house would give direct control over a production bottleneck that currently limits output to the Fremont line’s repurposed Model S and Model X floor space. EMAG’s own job cuts of roughly 300 positions at Salach signal that the supplier base is shrinking exactly when demand for those specialized tools is rising. The filing lists the transaction as an asset purchase under case B5-132/26, and the newly formed Tesla Machining Technologies Management SE is already registered at the same address. This move compresses the supply chain for the components that determine how many robots can be assembled per week once the Gen 3 design moves beyond initial low-volume runs.

Powerwall data limitations during islanded operation point to a software visibility gap rather than a hardware one. Owners lose granular consumption history the moment the home disconnects from the grid, even though the batteries continue to supply power. The same update that added manual battery preconditioning in version 2026.44.3.1 shows Tesla can push new controls through software, yet the outage data path remains unaddressed. Closing that loop would let owners see exactly how much energy the system delivered during an outage and how the battery state of charge changed minute by minute. Without it, the value of detailed home-energy analytics stops at the edge of the grid connection. The limitation is narrow but recurring for any customer who relies on the Powerwall for backup in regions with frequent weather-related outages.
