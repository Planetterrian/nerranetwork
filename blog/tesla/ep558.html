  1. Tesla's Fleet-Based GNSS Correction for Urban Driving Explained: TSLAming (X)

    2. A single vehicle in a glass canyon receives distorted satellite signals that drift its calculated position toward curbs or buildings. One car matches visible landmarks to a stored visual map and sends both its map pose and raw satellite measurements to a server. Nearby vehicles receive the paired data, combine it with their own readings, and use real-time kinematic processing to calculate relative position vectors that correct the drift. The updated pose returns through the server to surrounding cars, giving the fleet a stronger shared reference. This approach moves FSD positioning from isolated receivers to a moving network that improves accuracy at city scale without requiring direct line of sight between vehicles. Source: x.com

Tesla First Principles

Fleet-level sensor fusion changes the unit economics of perception in a way single-vehicle systems cannot match. When one car’s visual map pose and raw GNSS measurements travel to a server and return corrected by relative vectors from nearby vehicles, the cost of accuracy spreads across the entire local fleet instead of remaining locked inside each machine. That shift matters because urban GNSS errors are not random noise but repeatable geometry created by the same buildings and atmospheric conditions every day. A network that measures those errors collectively reduces the per-car compute and sensor burden required to stay inside a lane at highway speed.

The practical consequence appears first in dense corridors where individual fixes fail most often. A vehicle entering an intersection can inherit a sharper pose from cars already crossing it, even without direct camera overlap. This matters for FSD because regulatory approval for unsupervised operation will likely hinge on demonstrated performance under the worst local conditions rather than average conditions across a continent. The fleet approach supplies exactly that local calibration at marginal extra cost once the server and map infrastructure exist.

For the business, the same architecture lowers the hardware specification needed inside each new vehicle. If relative positioning from the group can compensate for some individual sensor drift, the company can delay or reduce the scope of next-generation onboard compute while still meeting safety targets. That option directly affects capital intensity and gross margin on the vehicle side, an outcome that matters more than any single software release when production volumes scale. The open question is how quickly regulators accept fleet-derived corrections as equivalent to independent sensing when liability questions arise.
