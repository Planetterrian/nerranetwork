Tesla First Principles

BYD now employs 1.02 million people, more than Toyota, GM, Ford and Tesla combined, yet the comparison reveals less about manufacturing strength than about how companies structure their operations. The Chinese firm added roughly 150,000 workers in ten months from the end of 2025, with 120,000 in research and development roles covering batteries, motors, electronic controls and semiconductors, while 960,000 staff remain in China and 60,000 work overseas. Tesla’s latest reported headcount stands at 134,785 worldwide. Toyota reported 390,927 employees as of March 2026. Ford ended 2025 with 169,000 and GM with 156,000.

Vertical integration explains much of the gap. BYD produces key components in-house across batteries, chips and motors, pulling in workers from its electronics contract manufacturing arm that alone employed 165,000 at the end of 2025. That structure inflates total employment without a direct link to vehicle output per person. Tesla’s smaller workforce focuses more narrowly on vehicle assembly, energy products and software, so raw headcount numbers mask differences in what each employee actually contributes to the final product. Volkswagen Group reported 662,942 employees at the end of 2025, placing it between BYD and the U.S. and Japanese groups. BYD’s hiring spree followed a drop to 869,600 at the close of 2025 after earlier peaks above 900,000.

The real question for investors is how these structures affect cost per unit and gross margin over time. A company that carries 170,000 more employees than its nearest rivals must either spread those costs across higher volumes or accept lower returns per vehicle. Tesla’s approach keeps the employee base smaller while still scaling software and energy businesses that do not require the same physical labor intensity, which may preserve margin flexibility even if absolute production numbers stay lower than a fully integrated rival. BYD sold about 4.6 million vehicles last year with 869,600 employees at year-end, equating to 5.3 vehicles per worker. The gap in headcount does not automatically translate to superior competitiveness when productivity metrics differ across business scopes.
