Tesla First Principles

Autonomous vehicles shift the safety burden from driver reaction time to sensor and software latency. Traditional cars rely on a human to notice an open door or a passenger still boarding, but a robotaxi must detect those conditions without any onboard observer. This forces every subsystem, including doors, to carry its own real-time verification layer. The requirement applies equally to opening and closing sequences, creating a continuous monitoring obligation that did not exist in driver-controlled vehicles.

The Cybercab door system illustrates the point. It keeps monitoring during the entire close cycle and can reverse motion within milliseconds if an obstruction appears. The same logic governs opening, disabling automatic movement when the path is blocked and requiring a deliberate hold-to-open command instead. These features add hardware and software complexity that conventional vehicles never needed. Validation now includes thousands of edge-case scenarios that previously fell to the driver.

The added intelligence raises unit cost and validation effort, yet it is non-negotiable once the driver is removed. Regulators evaluating the vehicle will treat door behavior as part of the overall safety case, not an afterthought. Manufacturers that treat doors as simple mechanical parts will face repeated certification delays when they attempt driverless operation. The engineering investment therefore becomes a direct input to both production cost and time-to-market for any future robotaxi platform.
