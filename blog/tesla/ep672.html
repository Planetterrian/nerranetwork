Tesla First Principles

Battery supply sets the hard ceiling on how many vehicles, energy products, and robots Tesla can build at once. Demand now pulls from passenger cars that already carry multi-year backlogs, Megapacks that are nearly sold out through 2027, and the much larger cell counts required by each Cybercab and Semi. The company is responding by scaling 4680 production at Giga Texas toward North America’s largest cell plant, adding LFP capacity in Nevada, building cathode and lithium facilities in Texas, and expanding pack lines in Berlin while locking in longer-term supplier contracts.

The constraint is not demand or factory floor space but the kilograms of cells that can be produced and qualified each month. Every additional 4680 line or LFP plant therefore multiplies the addressable volume across every program rather than just one. When cell output rises, the same increment supports more Model Ys, more Megapacks for data centers, and the first batches of Optimus units without forcing trade-offs between segments.

Vertical integration on cells also changes the cost curve. Internal production gives Tesla direct control over yield improvements and chemistry tweaks that outside suppliers would price at a premium. The result is lower cost per kilowatt-hour that flows straight into gross margin on vehicles, energy storage, and eventually the robot fleet, rather than remaining a supplier margin. Until cell capacity stops being the bottleneck, every other expansion plan remains gated by this single physical limit.
