  1. Tesla Patents Behind Autonomous Perception Magic — TSLAming (X)

Two patent applications, US 2026/0285363 A1 and US 2026/0127891 A1, address autonomous perception. One reconstructs and remembers the static road. The other reshapes virtual viewpoints to track moving objects like vehicles, cyclists, and pedestrians. The patents cover a system that turns surround-camera footage into a persistent overhead model of the road. This model keeps curbs, lanes, crosswalks, and junctions visible even when cameras lose sight of them due to obstructions. The approach relies on neural backbones that reduce images into visual features and a learned projection network that fuses them in a virtual bird’s-eye space. Source: x.com

Short Spot

Tesla First Principles

The economics of scaling humanoid robots like Optimus hinge on more than just assembly speed. Hand assembly remains the current bottleneck even as output rises nearly tenfold, because each unit still requires manual intervention for cooling systems, battery integration, and initial AI calibration. This keeps per-unit costs far above the target needed to compete with human labor in repetitive factory tasks.

Supply chain constraints compound the issue. Battery cells and actuators must reach consistent high-volume availability before weekly production can approach the thousands, let alone the twenty-thousand-unit goal. Until those components move from prototype sourcing to dedicated lines, the robot's theoretical advantage in 24-hour operation stays theoretical.

The practical path forward therefore runs through vertical integration on the hardware side rather than software alone. Tesla's experience with 4680 cells and structural packs shows how controlling the full stack can compress costs, but that same discipline has yet to be applied at scale to the robot's full bill of materials. Without it, external sales remain distant even if internal deployment begins next year.
