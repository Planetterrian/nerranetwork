Tesla First Principles

Taking a step back from today’s headlines, the start of physical construction on a dedicated Optimus factory challenges the assumption that Tesla’s robot program will remain a low-volume research effort for years to come. The decision to allocate prime Giga Texas real estate and begin steel erection signals that production scaling is now treated as an engineering and logistics problem rather than a distant possibility.

The facility’s stated capacity target of ten million units per year forces a different set of constraints than prototype work. At that volume, every process from casting and actuator assembly to final calibration must achieve automotive-level repeatability while remaining far cheaper per unit than current industrial robots. Physics and economics both push toward vertical integration of motors, gearboxes, and control electronics inside the same site, the same pattern Tesla applied to battery cells and powertrains.

If the factory reaches even a fraction of its design rate, Tesla gains a structural cost advantage that outside robot makers cannot easily match without similar scale. The practical implication is that future Optimus pricing and availability will be set by manufacturing throughput rather than by software breakthroughs alone, shifting the competitive question from “can it work” to “how fast can we build them.”
