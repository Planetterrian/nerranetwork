Tesla First Principles

Admitting that existing vehicle hardware cannot deliver full autonomy forces a clearer separation between software progress and silicon requirements. The current fleet can improve supervised features, yet unsupervised city driving still needs new inference chips and higher training throughput. This distinction moves the bottleneck from algorithm refinement to the pace of HW5 development and Dojo scaling. Capital plans must now account for both the cost of new chips and the timeline for installing them across production vehicles.

Once hardware limits are stated plainly, capital allocation decisions become more straightforward. Resources shift toward Cortex cluster expansion and custom silicon rather than repeated software releases on the same processors. The business implication is that gross margins on autonomy services will depend on when the next-generation chip reaches volume production and how quickly it can be installed across new vehicles. Investors tracking service revenue will watch the first vehicles equipped with the updated silicon as the clearest signal of when unsupervised capability can scale.

The same constraint applies to Optimus training. Humanoid robots need far higher inference performance per unit than today’s supervised FSD stack. Without the next silicon step, both programs risk extended timelines even if the underlying models continue to advance. The practical test will be whether Dojo throughput gains and HW5 sampling rates align with the 2027 production targets already set for both products. Any slippage in chip availability would directly affect unit economics and deployment speed for both autonomy services and humanoid sales.
