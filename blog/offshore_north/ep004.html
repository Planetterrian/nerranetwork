Ocean Race Atlantic Leaders Extend Gap — Episode 4

Team Malizia leads The Ocean Race Atlantic after recovering from a technical issue while a leading trio pulls ahead of the rest of the fleet in rough conditions.

Offshore North

Week ending: September 07, 2026

Team Malizia leads The Ocean Race Atlantic after recovering from a technical issue while a leading trio pulls ahead of the rest of the fleet in rough conditions.

The Canadian Boat

As of the team's most recent update on 2 September 2026, the boat's position after its early-August departure from Collingwood remains unconfirmed in public posts. Canada Ocean Racing's site was last updated 2 September with a summary of summer training; its News section was last updated 17 August with a television interview; its YouTube channel was last updated 31 August with a video on the Welland Canal transit.

Standing item: EMIRA IV is the IMOCA 60 previously raced as 11th Hour Racing Team — Mālama, which won The Ocean Race 2023 under Charlie Enright and finished third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe under Sébastien Simon despite a broken foil. The boat was built as a current-generation foiling design over 18 metres long and capable of speeds above 40 knots. It passed to Sébastien Simon after the 2023 race and carried him to that Vendée Globe podium on reduced foiling performance. Canada Ocean Racing took delivery in early 2025 and renamed it EMIRA IV for Scott Shawyer's campaign. The boat's history directly supplies the campaign's first foiling IMOCA platform ahead of the 2028 Vendée Globe start. Source: canadaoceanracing.com

The Fleet

In The Ocean Race Atlantic, a 3,000-nautical-mile event now at its midpoint, Paul Meilhat on United by the Ocean, Francesca Clapcich on Team Francesca Clapcich powered by 11th Hour Racing, and Kojiro Shiraishi on DMG MORI Global One have opened a gap of more than 430 miles over the rest of the six-boat IMOCA fleet after five days. The leaders have moved ahead of a low-pressure system that brought gusts above 30 knots and heavy slamming seas, while Boris Herrmann's Team Malizia recovered from an earlier technical problem to retain the overall lead. The conditions have made eating, sleeping and moving on board physically and mentally demanding. Meilhat described the fleet as at the halfway point with hopes for steadier weather ahead. The spread across hundreds of miles means small routing choices now carry larger consequences for the final standings into Lorient. Source: sail-world.com

Embrace the Challenge sustained a three-metre tear in its mainsail while reefing in 40-knot winds and has carried out repairs at sea to return to full speed. The damage occurred during the same low-pressure system that affected the entire fleet. Crews on the boat completed the repair without stopping, allowing the yacht to resume racing at competitive pace. The incident illustrates how equipment failures in heavy weather can erase hours or days of progress even when the boat itself remains intact. Source: sail-world.com

Sixteen solo sailors started the Golden Globe Race from Les Sables d'Olonne on 6 September for a non-stop, unassisted round-the-world voyage using only 1960s-era equipment and no modern electronics or routing. The edition follows the original 1968–69 rules with no satellite navigation, no weather routing assistance, and no outside support. Skippers must rely on sextant, paper charts, and self-sufficiency for the entire circumnavigation. The start marks the third running of the event since its revival. Source: sailorz.com

Four Ultim trimarans and eleven Ocean Fifty boats competed in the 24H Ultim, the final major offshore confrontation before the Route du Rhum starts on 1 November. The event served as a last high-speed test for the largest and fastest classes ahead of the Saint-Malo to Pointe-à-Pitre race. Both Ultim and Ocean Fifty fleets used the short-format race to refine setups and crew coordination under race conditions. The timing places the results directly into Route du Rhum preparation cycles for the boats involved. Source: voilesetvoiliers.ouest-france.fr

Plain Sailing

The physical demands on crews in a transatlantic race like The Ocean Race Atlantic come from the combination of constant motion and sleep disruption rather than from any single storm. When a low-pressure system passes, winds can jump from 15 knots to over 30 knots within hours, forcing repeated sail changes and reefing while the boat slams through short, steep waves. On a 60-foot IMOCA the motion is violent enough that simply moving from the cockpit to the galley or trying to prepare a freeze-dried meal becomes a full-body effort; crews often report going 24 hours or more without a proper hot meal or more than a few minutes of continuous rest. The six-boat fleet in this edition encountered exactly those conditions on day four and five, with gusts above 30 knots and seas that made every task slower and more exhausting.

Sleep is taken in short fragments because the boat must keep moving at high speed through the night. Even with an autopilot holding course, someone must remain alert for sudden wind shifts or equipment strain, and the noise of water slamming against the hull makes deeper rest difficult. Over five days these effects compound: reaction times slow, small errors in sail trim or routing add up, and the mental load of deciding whether to push harder or conserve the boat grows heavier. The leading trio of Meilhat, Clapcich and Shiraishi used the low-pressure system as a turning point, sailing a route that placed them ahead of the worst weather while the rest of the fleet remained inside it.

The leading trio's decision to pull ahead of the low-pressure system shows how these cumulative stresses shape strategy. By sailing a slightly longer route to clearer air they traded distance for a chance at steadier conditions and better rest, a calculation that becomes decisive when the fleet is already spread across hundreds of miles. The gap of more than 430 miles that opened after five days is the direct result of those choices made under fatigue. In ocean racing the boat that manages its crew's energy most effectively often gains the largest advantage once the weather settles.

The Countdown

797 days remain until the Vendée Globe start on 12 November 2028. The campaign's next concrete milestone is the Route du Rhum, which starts on 1 November 2026 from Saint-Malo.
