What This Show Is

Offshore ocean racing ranks among the world's great endurance sports yet reaches English-speaking audiences mostly as late, partial results stripped of context. This show supplies the missing layer: it explains what each development actually changes for the boats, the sailors, and the campaigns that matter.

Every four years a handful of people attempt to sail alone, non-stop and unassisted around the world. No Canadian has ever finished that race. Scott Shawyer and Canada Ocean Racing are preparing to change that at the Vendée Globe that starts on 12 November 2028. The show follows their attempt week by week and uses it as the door into the wider sport.

Each episode keeps the same shape. The Canadian Boat section tracks only what the team itself has published and states the direct consequence for the 2028 qualification path. The Fleet section covers the rest of offshore racing with the same consequence test. Plain Sailing explains one concept so listeners can understand the week's news without prior expertise. The Countdown gives the exact number of days left and the current qualification status drawn only from sourced facts.

Sources are named and linked every week. No hype is added, no play-by-play is given that will be stale by Monday, and nothing is stated that the sources do not support. Uncertainty is flagged inline. The audience is assumed to be enthusiastic but not expert; every piece of jargon receives a single plain-language sentence on first use and is then left alone.

Offshore North is part of the Nerra Network, an independent, ad-free collection of daily shows produced by two friends and covering technology, science, markets, world news and language learning in several languages at nerranetwork.com.

The Fleet

The Ocean Race Atlantic 2026 starts 1 September with six IMOCA entries sailing 3,600 nautical miles from New York to Lorient. Every crew must include two women, two men and one on-board reporter, creating the first 50/50 gender-parity offshore transatlantic race. The event serves as the main pre-event test before The Ocean Race round-the-world course begins in January 2027. Two new-generation IMOCAs already sailing will use the race as their first serious ocean test. Malizia 4 emerged from a three-team sistership collaboration between Team Malizia, TR Racing and Team Banque Populaire; it was designed by Antoine Koch with Finot-Conq and Gsea Design, is narrower and flatter than its predecessor, and carries more than 85,000 hours of build time. Skipper Boris Herrmann called the first week of sailing on the boat “love at first sail.” DMG MORI Global One follows a separate design route by Guillaume Verdier, built by Multiplast; its hull resembles the central hull of a trimaran and is intended to foil earlier while reducing slamming. Skipper Kojiro Shiraishi gave the designer the single brief “free mind, free ideas, no limits.” This race therefore supplies the first direct comparison between the two new boats under identical 50/50 crew rules and identical ocean conditions before the longer 2027 campaign. Source: sailingscuttlebutt.com

British America's Cup team GB1 has added kitefoiler Jemima Lines (née Crathorne) to its sailing squad. The announcement brings recent high-speed foiling experience directly into a campaign that will require precise handling of its eventual Cup boat. Lines joins an existing squad that must integrate new talent across multiple disciplines before the next Cup cycle. The move is a concrete personnel step rather than a results announcement, reflecting the multi-year timeline on which Cup teams assemble and train crews. No further details on her specific role or schedule were released with the announcement. Source: sail-world.com

Plain Sailing

Gender parity rules require mixed crews on offshore boats. In The Ocean Race Atlantic 2026 the rule is explicit: two women, two men and one on-board reporter on every entry. The requirement is not a suggestion; it is written into the notice of race and applies to every team that wants to start.

The rule changes how campaigns select sailors and how they train. A skipper can no longer fill a five-person watch system with the four most experienced offshore sailors available; at least two of those sailors must be women who meet the same physical and skill thresholds as their male teammates. That constraint affects hiring timelines, training blocks and even boat layout, because watch systems and berthing must now accommodate the mandated balance rather than the fastest available combination.

The practical effect is that teams must develop a wider bench of qualified offshore sailors earlier in the campaign cycle. For the two new IMOCAs already launched, the rule also means their first serious ocean miles will be sailed by crews that were assembled under the parity constraint rather than under an open selection process. The single sentence a listener could repeat is: the rule forces every team to solve the same talent-pool problem at the same time.

An IMOCA is a 60-foot monohull built to a strict class rule that permits foils, water ballast and other performance features while capping overall length, beam and other dimensions. The class rule itself does not dictate crew composition; that decision belongs to each race organiser. When an organiser adds a gender-parity clause, the constraint travels with the entry list rather than with the boat design. Teams therefore face two parallel problems: they must still optimise the boat for solo or short-handed sailing in later events, yet they must also prove the same boat works with a five-person mixed crew on the Atlantic course. The on-board reporter counts toward the total but does not sail the boat, so the four sailing positions must still satisfy the two-plus-two split. This creates a narrow window in which teams can test new equipment and new crew combinations simultaneously. Because the race starts on 1 September, any shortfall in qualified female offshore sailors must be solved before that date; there is no later qualifying event that can substitute for the experience these crews will gain. The same calendar pressure applies to every entry, which is why the rule is described as the first 50/50 gender-parity crewed transatlantic race in offshore sailing.
