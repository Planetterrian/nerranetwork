Workers in Metro Vancouver now need $28.55 an hour to cover essential costs.

By Mira November 19, 2026 Ep 58 11 min read Listen to podcast
Listen to this episode
Contents
Top Stories City & Province The Closer Look Also Today Getting Around Sports What's On Both Sides: Hotel rooms and a Mount Pleasant rental

What You Need to Know: Workers in Metro Vancouver now need $28.55 an hour to cover essential costs, BC Policy Solutions says in its 2026 living-wage report, up from $27.85 last year and $10.30 above the provincial minimum. Vancouver council has ordered funding for 100 new bus shelters by September 2027. An 87-year-old woman struck in a Dunbar crosswalk on Remembrance Day has died, and police are still looking for the driver.

Top Stories

Metro Vancouver's living wage has increased as everyday costs rise: Daily Hive

BC Policy Solutions, in its BC Living Wage 2026 report, calculates the hourly wage a full-time worker needs to cover essential expenses, avoid chronic financial stress, and take part in community life. In Metro Vancouver that figure is $28.55, a 2.5 per cent rise from 2025’s $27.85, and $10.30 above B.C.’s $18.25 minimum wage. The institute’s press release says the increase reflects wages not keeping up with the cost of living; it ties higher prices for food, fuel, clothing and footwear to the trade war with the U.S. and the war in Iran. It says 35 per cent of Metro Vancouver workers, more than half a million people, earn less than the living wage. Lead author Iglika Ivanova, co-executive director and senior economist, says the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation reports rent cuts and vacancies concentrated in new high-end buildings, while the market stays tight for lower-income households, especially families needing two or more bedrooms. Food, the second-largest expense in the living-wage budget, rose 4.1 per cent from last year, and out-of-pocket childcare for the living-wage family of four rose by nearly $160 a month. The report says the wage would have been higher without the Canadian Dental Care Plan, the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit and the BC Rental Assistance Program, and that ending the BC Climate Action Tax Credit hurt low-wage earners without children. Co-author Navdeep Singh Chhina, managing director of Living Wage BC, called on the incoming provincial government to invest in affordable housing, public transit and childcare. Squamish’s living wage is $29.05, up 3.8 per cent, and Whistler’s is $30.50, up 3 per cent; the lowest in B.C. is Grand Forks at $22.15, and Port Alberni is $23.75. Source: dailyhive.com Source: biv.com

Elderly victim of Vancouver hit-and-run dies in hospital: Vancouver Sun

An 87-year-old woman struck and seriously injured in Vancouver on Remembrance Day has died. Vancouver Police Department spokesperson Sgt. Adam Donaldson said she was crossing Dunbar Street at West 41st Avenue in a marked crosswalk when a white sedan turning off West 41st onto Dunbar hit her. She was taken to hospital and died on Tuesday, November 17. Donaldson said police are still searching for the driver and the vehicle, described as a two-door Mercedes C-Class, possibly white, grey or silver, with a model year between 2002 and 2005. Five days earlier, a 78-year-old woman was struck on Dunbar Street at West 34th Avenue in an unmarked crosswalk; that driver stopped and co-operated, and she died the next day. Source: vancouversun.com

B.C. lawsuit seeks millions in missing restaurant tips from fintech company: Global BC

A B.C. lawsuit seeks millions in missing restaurant tips from financial technology company XTM. In February, members of B.C.’s hospitality industry noticed XTM had stopped distributing money, and some said they were missing thousands of dollars. Source: globalnews.ca

Richmond unanimously rejects supervised consumption sites: Global BC

Richmond council unanimously rejected supervised consumption sites. It stopped short of approving a motion by Counc. Sheldon Starrett to ban them entirely on both public and private property. Source: globalnews.ca

Union bracing for layoffs amid B.C. Conservative plan to pause Massey Tunnel replacement: CBC British Columbia

A union representing crews on the George Massey Tunnel replacement project says workers are bracing for layoffs as they wait to see whether the incoming B.C. Conservative government switches the plan to a bridge. Source: cbc.ca

After Myles Gray's death, officer says describing police response as 'compassionate' was wrong: CBC British Columbia

One of seven Vancouver police officers involved in the fatal beating of Myles Gray says he was wrong to describe police conduct during the call as “incredibly compassionate” in an interview with an RCMP investigator in 2017. CBC reported the comment from a public hearing on November 18. Source: cbc.ca

The Closer Look

Vancouver City Council approves goal of 100 new TransLink bus shelters, aims to cover every stop where space allows by 2029: Daily Hive

Vancouver City Council has unanimously approved a plan to add a shelter with seating at 100 TransLink bus stops that lack one, with those shelters to be installed by September 2027. The amended motion from COPE councillor Sean Orr passed at the November 18, 2026 public meeting, the same night council approved all 11 member motions on the agenda. Staff must put funding for the first 100 locations into the 2027 budget, generally the busier suitable stops without shelters, and report back by April 2027 on options to put a shelter and bench at every stop where it physically fits, by December 2029.

Orr presented it as access for people who depend on the bus. He said someone with a disability, or a senior with mobility trouble, should not have to stand in the rain to go about daily business, and he said the amenities help ridership. He credited COPE colleague Solomon Yi-Kieran with leading the drafting. They estimate only about half of Vancouver’s stops have a shelter with a bench. Expanding shelters was a COPE platform promise, and Yi-Kieran has called transit improvements a core priority. He said that as a rider he knows waiting half an hour in the rain for the No. 3, the No. 8 or the No. 49 with no bench or cover, and that with rain more than 40 per cent of the year, about half of stops without a covered bench is not acceptable.

TransLink handles shelters on its own property, such as off-street exchanges. On sidewalk stops, Metro Vancouver cities generally install and maintain them. Mayor Pete Fry sought a fuller account of the ongoing work and spending to keep shelters clean, safe, accessible and usable. Green councillor Stephanie Smith amended the motion so staff weigh weather exposure, safety and barriers to access, including for seniors and people with disabilities, not boarding totals alone. Some stops have no shelter because the sidewalk is narrow, the curb sits close to a property line, or trees and utilities are in the way. After suggestions from ABC councillor Mike Klassen, staff must determine how much money is available, what TransLink and others might contribute, and how much more is needed, including possible reallocation inside the 2027–2030 capital plan. Astral/JCDecaux is the long-term street-furniture contractor: it installs, operates and maintains shelters and receives the advertising revenue. Staff will consider designs, that revenue and possible changes to the partnership, plus solar-powered lighting, garbage bins and safety features. Designs have generally not changed since the early 2000s, when the city signed the first 20-year contract.

Denis Agar, executive director of the transit advocacy group Movement, told council that a long wait with no bench is part of the mobility gap between people who can drive and people who cannot, and that the gap can be fixed. Orr pointed to Burnaby, which launched an expansion in 2023 aimed at 370 new shelters by 2030, choosing sites by ridership and by proximity to schools, seniors centres and civic facilities. A 2021 Burnaby staff report estimated about $30,000 to design, fabricate and install each lighted shelter, a figure likely higher after later price rises. Burnaby then had 968 stops, 231 with a shelter and 737 with only a bench or nothing; at the 2021 unit cost, covering the rest was roughly $22 million before later inflation. New Westminster is replacing 50 shelters through Pattison Outdoor by the end of 2026, adding 14 in January 2027 for a municipal total of 64, with a longer aim of about 100. Source: dailyhive.com

Both Sides: Hotel rooms and a Mount Pleasant rental

Yesterday’s new Vancouver council was already taking up reversals of the last term’s decisions. On Wednesday, November 18, COPE councillors Solomon Yi-Kieran and Chloe Leslie asked whether that reaches the Hotel Development Policy and a rezoning approved only in principle.

Michael Cook, chair of the Myron Manor Collective and a member of the Vancouver Tenants Union, asked the new council to repeal previously approved hotel bylaws and rezonings, especially his building, and send them back to staff. Myron Manor, at 75 East 8th Avenue, is a three-storey rental with more than two dozen units near the future Mount Pleasant SkyTrain station. On July 28, after a hearing over three dates, the previous council approved in principle a 21-storey tower with 190 hotel units and two storeys of industrial space, led by Lotus Capital Corporation and Nicola Real Estate. Cook’s stated reason is that rental homes should not be replaced by hotels. He asked council to repeal subsection 3.2.2.1(d) and replace it with a binding rule that no rental apartments in the city be replaced by hotels, and he proposed a municipal fund so tenant groups could buy buildings and convert them to co-operatives or community land trusts.

The policy, approved in April 2025 and last amended in May 2026, is the city’s tool for a net 10,000 hotel rooms over the coming decades. The City and Destination Vancouver say extra rooms are needed for tourism, jobs and major events. Destination Vancouver says the region, especially Vancouver, faces a room shortage visible in occupancy and overnight rates, and that the city’s hotel supply is lower than in the early 2000s even as short-term rentals were restricted. City staff have said Vancouver is on track against the room goal based on projects in the pipeline, while many remain proposals and approved projects may not be built. The policy also allows hotels mixed with rental or strata housing, which staff say has helped make new housing financially viable. Unite Here Local 40, which represents many hotel workers, has opposed several hotel proposals and policies and has launched court challenges; in July 2026 public hearings, then-ABC councillors accused the union and its supporters of filibuster tactics.

City staff told council that folding Cook’s requests into a tenant-rights motion would greatly expand the work, that repealing a rezoning is possible but “very complex,” and that council can also decline to enact a rezoning bylaw after an in-principle approval. They said they would brief council later on the policy and on subsection 3.2.2.1(d). A separate report on a 15-storey, 151-room hotel at 852-872 Seymour Street was withdrawn from the November 17 agenda; staff had urged a public hearing and then rejection because of impacts on adjacent towers. Source: dailyhive.com
