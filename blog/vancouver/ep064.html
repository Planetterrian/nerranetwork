Top Stories

California man ticketed for going 208 km/h on Sea-to-Sky Highway: Global BC

A 22-year-old man visiting from California was ticketed after RCMP recorded him at 208 km/h in a 90 km/h zone on the Sea-to-Sky Highway near Squamish earlier this month. CBC reports he was allegedly driving that speed in a BMW, which was impounded for seven days. The stop is a police enforcement case, not a court finding. Source: globalnews.ca Source: cbc.ca

Here's what B.C. laws and fees are changing in 2027: Daily Hive Vancouver

TransLink fares are set to rise 2 per cent in 2027, after 5 per cent increases. Daily Hive reports a one-zone cash fare would go from $3.50 to $3.55, a one-zone Compass fare from $2.85 to $3.00, and a one-zone monthly pass from $117.20 to $119.55, with the board voting the exact increase each year before it takes effect. A Metro Vancouver draft budget, not yet adopted, would lift water, sewer, garbage and parks charges from $897 to $914, a $17 or 1.9 per cent increase, about $76 a month; the previous board deferred the vote, and the new board's first meeting is Nov. 27. The maximum allowable rent increase for 2027 is 2.2 per cent, and landlords decide whether to use it. BC Hydro's 2027 rate increase is expected April 1, 2027, with its application due in February; its 2026 increase was 3.75 per cent, and it is seeking a two-year optional fixed-bill pilot from Nov. 1, 2027 to Oct. 31, 2029. BC Ferries' average annual fare increase is capped at 3.2 per cent until March 2028. The Bank of Canada raised its policy rate on Oct. 28 from 2.25 per cent to 2.5 per cent; Ratehub.ca reported variable rates up 25 basis points and the average five-year fixed rate up 43 basis points to 4.99 per cent, with the next decision on Dec. 9, 2026. Premier Lorne Doerkson has promised a $1,000 income-tax cut for families, low-income earners and seniors; that is a campaign promise, not a law in force. Source: dailyhive.com

Woman who died in Squamish boating tragedy loved life, animals, family says: Global BC

A woman died in a Squamish boating tragedy, Global BC reports. Friends say she was Femke Visser, a local horse guide and dogsled guide. They say she had gone out with neighbours to help animals and people stranded by the floodwater. Source: globalnews.ca

City & Province

Likely end to B.C. NDP housing reforms doesn't mean cities will revert to picket-fence neighbourhoods: Vancouver Sun

Premier Lorne Doerkson has pledged to rescind Bill 44, the 2023 NDP law that required municipalities over 5,000 residents to allow up to four units on a single-family lot and six near transit. Port Coquitlam Mayor Brad West introduced a motion this week directing staff to disregard new applications for those projects, pending clarification of the province's planned changes. A Vancouver Sun column reports that many mayors want land-use power back, while most say they still want density on their own terms and do not want to lose federal housing-accelerator funding, which requires communities over 10,000 to allow at least four units per property. Burnaby was approved for $44.1 million and has received $32.9 million; the city said Tuesday it is well-positioned for a final advance of about $11.2 million in 2027, subject to federal review, and does not anticipate the Bill 44 changes will stop it delivering housing. Mayor Mike Hurley, re-elected last month, said in an open letter this month that Burnaby led the opposition to a one-size-fits-all approach that ignored local infrastructure, schools and parking. The column notes Global News reported Toronto lost $10 million after limiting multiplexes to some neighbourhoods. Port Coquitlam did not get accelerator funding; West wrote Tuesday that the law worked against the city's townhome market, and new West Vancouver Mayor Kirk LaPointe said council heard both an appetite for change and an appetite to preserve neighbourhood character. Source: vancouversun.com

Surrey to audit how truck parking, retail cannabis permits awarded: CBC

Surrey city council has ordered a trio of new forensic audits into decisions by the previous administration, CBC reports. The reviews include how that administration awarded retail cannabis licences. They also cover permits to operate truck parking on city-owned land. Source: cbc.ca Source: cbc.ca

The Closer Look

New premier must overhaul youth mental-health system: Representative: Vancouver Sun

B.C.'s representative for children and youth, Jennifer Charlesworth, said Tuesday she wants the new Conservative government to change how youth mental health works in the province. She is asking for a stand-alone Youth Mental Health Act, which would be a first in Canada, or a youth-specific section in the Mental Health Act. She called the current Act archaic and said it focuses almost exclusively on adults. "The mental-health concerns of young people have been growing significantly," she said. "The most important thing for a new minister to understand is children and youth are different from adults."

She said the Act largely concentrates on detaining people in a crisis. She wants an expanded set of publicly funded, voluntary services that could stabilize young people in the community. Postmedia News reported this week that youth mental-health problems are rising and that young people frequently face long wait-lists. Charlesworth said that matches what parents tell her: "They asked for help over and over and over again, and they weren't able to access it. And then (their kids' mental health) got really serious."

Mandate letters from Premier Lorne Doerkson for Health Minister Ryan Peterson and the rest of cabinet were expected to be made public this week. Charlesworth plans to write to the ministers with her requested changes after she reads those letters.

She had already lobbied the previous NDP government. She said that government introduced some positive voluntary options, including Foundry Centres, but that demand and need still outstrip available services. Her recent report found an average of 1,600 teens ages 12 to 17 were involuntarily detained under the Mental Health Act each year between 2020 and 2025. That was 14 times the rate of youth detained by the justice system.

During the campaign, the Conservatives promised to review the Mental Health Act, reduce wait times and expand early services. Charlesworth said she supports those directions. She is more worried about a platform pledge to promote anti-drug education in schools, which she said has failed in the past, and about a promise to increase mandatory care for people who may be a risk to themselves or others. Too often, she said, community services are not there on release, and evidence suggests forced treatment can jeopardize long-term recovery and survival. "We need to build out a voluntary system before we rely too heavily on an involuntary one," she said.

Ania Wysocka, founder of Rootd, a panic-attack and anxiety-relief app she created in Victoria after a panic attack in university, said a separate Act would organize care more clearly because young people's needs differ from adults'. Anxiety, she said, can often show up first in the teen and early adult years. When young people are folded into a system built mainly around adults, she said, the focus tends to be crisis and treatment, "when what many of them need most is support early on, before things escalate." Both Sides: Absorbing Surrey's development company

Surrey council is deciding whether the city-owned Surrey City Development Corp. stays an arm's-length builder or is absorbed into city hall. The company was created in 2007, with the city as sole shareholder, to develop surplus land. Mayor Doug McCallum dissolved it in 2020. Former mayor Brenda Locke revived it in late 2022. By 2023 it was working on a former Safeway site across from city hall — Locke's promised 10,000-seat arena, luxury hotel, conference space, homes and retail — plus Centre Block beside Surrey Central station, including space for Simon Fraser University's medical school, the Cloverdale Fairgrounds redevelopment, two tower projects near Gateway station, and a high-density mixed-use site in Newton.

McCallum's case is that the move keeps a campaign promise. "This is one of our promises in the campaign to download the Surrey development corporation into city operations, so this is the actual start of it," he said Monday. Council voted this week to terminate four independent directors. As of Wednesday the board is three city employees: city manager Rob Costanzo, finance director Vipin Sachdeva, and chief capital programs officer Jeff Arason. His report says integration is relatively straightforward, but some projects need special handling because of existing partnerships and continuing obligations, and staff are identifying those. McCallum also promised in the campaign to cancel the arena, so whether that project continues is unresolved.

Coun. Harry Bains, the only councillor outside the mayor's majority, was the only vote against removing the directors, on Monday and at the Nov. 9 meeting where the first integration vote was held. His case is that absorption "risks losing flexibility, focus and approach that make it a valuable municipal development tool." He asked what happens to company staff and what severance would be. Engineering manager Scott Neuman said he would not discuss severance, citing privacy, and that the city is looking at chances to integrate staff where possible. Coun. Doug Elford declined to comment and deferred to the mayor. The dated step now on the record is Wednesday's reduced board, with staff still sorting project obligations. Source: vancouversun.com
