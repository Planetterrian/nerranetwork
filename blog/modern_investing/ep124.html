Strategy Spotlight

Credit-spread selling lets investors collect premium while defining risk, but exits must be mechanical to avoid emotional overrides. Today’s mixed signals from lower inflation prints and geopolitical headlines make rules-based exits especially relevant for Canadian accounts that already face currency and rate volatility. The straightforward rule is to exit any credit spread once the loss equals 100% of the credit originally received; a $13 credit therefore triggers a stop at $26. This approach removes discretion and works on any platform that supports options, including Questrade or Interactive Brokers. It has historically performed best in range-bound or slowly trending markets where premium decay outpaces small adverse moves, yet it can still lose when volatility spikes suddenly. Always size the position so a full stop-out equals no more than 1% of portfolio capital. The rule applies equally to vertical spreads on individual equities or index ETFs, and it pairs naturally with the current environment of contained PCE readings that suggest limited immediate rate-cut pressure. Canadian investors using TFSA accounts can implement the same stop discipline without triggering frequent-trading concerns because the rule is purely mechanical. Source: x.com

Investor Education: How to Read an Earnings Report

Imagine you bought a U.S. industrial name last week after an analyst raised estimates; your order filled at the open but the stock faded 4% by midday. The actual driver was not the headline EPS beat but the widening gap between reported revenue and free-cash-flow conversion that the transcript revealed only after the call. Revenue shows top-line growth, yet EPS can be flattered by one-time items or share buybacks, so always subtract non-recurring gains and check the cash-flow statement for rising receivables or inventory. Forward guidance matters more than the beat itself; management commentary on margins and capex often signals whether the beat is sustainable. Balance-sheet red flags include debt rising faster than EBITDA or cash balances shrinking quarter-over-quarter. Free-cash-flow yield above 8% combined with an earnings beat has historically preceded multi-quarter outperformance in rate-sensitive sectors. The common retail mistake is treating any EPS beat as automatic buy signal; the fix is to read the 10-Q cash-flow section and the forward paragraph before adding to a position. A second layer involves comparing year-over-year changes in operating cash flow versus net income to spot non-cash earnings inflation. When management raises full-year guidance after a beat, the transcript usually contains the precise language on whether the improvement stems from volume or pricing power. Investors who skip the balance-sheet footnotes often miss rising contingent liabilities that later pressure valuations. The practical sequence is to open the earnings release first for headline numbers, then the 10-Q for cash-flow reconciliation, and finally the prepared remarks for forward color.
