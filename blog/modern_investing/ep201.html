Strategy Spotlight

Single-stock ETFs let investors gain targeted exposure to names like Alphabet without buying the underlying shares outright. Today's launch of six PowerStock single-stock ETFs on the TSX, including GOOP tracking Alphabet, gives Canadian investors a new vehicle for concentrated bets inside registered accounts. Implementation starts with checking the ETF's premium or discount to NAV on your platform and sizing the position to match your existing sector limits. These products have historically performed best in low-volatility regimes where single-name momentum persists, but they amplify drawdowns when the underlying rotates. Always compare the ETF's management fee and liquidity against simply holding the stock or a broad sector ETF. The new funds also simplify foreign-exchange handling for U.S. names, removing the need to manage separate currency conversions on each trade. Canadian investors can therefore maintain precise weights in high-conviction names while staying within TFSA contribution limits and avoiding direct custody of U.S. shares. Liquidity and tracking error should be monitored closely in the first weeks of trading to ensure the ETF does not deviate materially from the underlying stock's performance. Source: kalkinemedia.com

Investor Education: Canadian Discount Brokerages Compared

Imagine you want to buy 100 shares of a TSX-listed energy name at the open; Wealthsimple Trade executes commission-free but routes through a single market maker that can widen the spread by 3-5 cents on less-liquid names during the first 15 minutes. Questrade charges $4.95-$9.95 per trade yet offers free ETF purchases and direct access to U.S. exchanges with real-time Level 2 data on its higher tier. Interactive Brokers posts the lowest margin rates at roughly 6.5% for balances under $100k and supports 150+ global markets, though its platform requires learning a steeper interface. Bank-owned brokerages such as TD Direct Investing integrate seamlessly with chequing accounts but levy $9.99 commissions plus higher foreign-exchange spreads on U.S. trades. The pro habit is to run a side-by-side cost calculator for your typical trade size and frequency before committing, then route larger U.S. orders through the lowest-fee desk. The misconception that “free is always cheapest” ignores hidden spreads and limited order types that can cost active investors more than a transparent commission. Canadian investors also need to verify which platforms support automatic dividend reinvestment inside registered accounts, because missing that feature can force manual purchases that incur extra commissions or FX fees. Finally, margin availability and interest rates matter for those who occasionally use leverage; Interactive Brokers' lower rates can save hundreds of dollars annually on a $50,000 borrowed balance compared with bank platforms, but only if the investor is comfortable navigating its order types and reporting tools.
