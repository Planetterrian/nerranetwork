Strategy Spotlight

Buying the dip replaces market-timing attempts when broad selloffs hit without fundamental deterioration in holdings. Today's geopolitical headlines and index declines create exactly that setup, where investors who add to quality names on weakness have historically captured rebounds once the shock passes. Implementation starts with a pre-built watchlist of holdings already in your TFSA or RRSP, then uses a simple rule: add only when price drops 5-8% from your last entry and volume exceeds the 20-day average. The approach worked best in 2018 and 2022 volatility spikes when investors maintained dry powder rather than selling into fear. Risks include catching a falling knife if the catalyst proves structural, so position size remains capped at 5% of the account per addition. Always confirm the dip aligns with your original thesis before executing. Source: fool.com

Investor Education: Listener Guide: Investing Your First $10,000

Imagine you just received a $10,000 windfall and want to put it to work in your TFSA before the contribution room resets. The first step is confirming your emergency fund covers three to six months of expenses in a high-interest savings account; only then move to investing. Most Canadians start with the TFSA over an RRSP because of tax-free growth and flexible withdrawals, then decide between a single all-equity ETF like VEQT for simplicity or a three-ETF mix of Canadian, US, and international equity for modest diversification. Open the account at Wealthsimple or Questrade, link your bank, and place the first purchase as a limit order during market hours rather than market order to avoid slippage. Set up automatic $200 bi-weekly contributions the same day so the psychological barrier of pressing “buy” is crossed once. Historical data shows broad equity markets have delivered roughly 7-8% annualized returns over long periods, yet single years can range from +30% to -20%, so expect volatility rather than smooth gains.
