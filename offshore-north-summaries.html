Ep 7: Route du Rhum Costs Climb as Canadian Campaign Prepares
Download Read transcript

Offshore North

Week ending: September 28, 2026

Organizers of the Route du Rhum 2026 will spend 1.1 million euros securing the Saint-Malo start site alone, a twenty-two-fold increase since 2014.

The Canadian Boat

As of the team's 2 September post, Emira IV was now leaving Canada and heading back to Europe.

Canada Ocean Racing released no new updates this week on the boat's position or preparations. The last known movement remains the transatlantic delivery to France after summer training on the Great Lakes. Scott Shawyer's 2 September post detailed the summer's learnings on systems and handling, but no further posts have appeared in the thirty-day window. The boat did not sail the Défi Azimut in Lorient earlier this month. With the Route du Rhum start now thirty-four days away, the campaign's focus shifts to final preparations in Europe.

Scott Shawyer spent the summer in Collingwood, Ontario, on Georgian Bay with Emira IV, a foiling IMOCA 60 designed by Guillaume Verdier and built by CDK Technologies. The boat arrived in Canada in spring 2026 after crossing the Atlantic and navigating the St. Lawrence Seaway and Welland Canal. Shawyer described the biggest learning as understanding how the entire boat works, from sails, rigging and foils to electrical systems, hydrogenerators, solar panels, alternators, sensors, computers and autopilot overlays. He learned these elements system by system and process by process in a controlled lake environment he has known for over forty years. On two heavy days his triceps seized on the grinder, which he traced to pushing rather than pulling because of a shoulder injury and to not drinking enough water with electrolytes, something he noted to monitor. He has not yet sailed Emira IV in open-ocean waves and swell and said he needs to remain humble. The support team named in the post included Martin Amescua, who has been with the boat for a long time, along with Gaby Bucau, Clovis Gautier, Giulina Bayat for rigging and systems, Alberto Zaccaria for electrical, computer and sensor systems, Alan Roberts for coaching on the Exocet routing system and autopilot overlays, and Nick Moloney as sailing coach. The post also noted that the boat handles remarkably well in short-period chop typical of the Great Lakes, with days of strong winds allowing quick sailing and lighter days providing time for manoeuvres. Emira IV departed the weekend of 8–9 August 2026 via the Welland Canal, earlier than the published mid-August plan. The campaign's next major milestone is the Route du Rhum starting 1 November 2026 from Saint-Malo, Shawyer's first solo transatlantic race on a foiling IMOCA.

Dan's lens: The pilot's frame for weather routing decisions on incomplete information at three in the morning applies directly to how Scott managed the boat's systems during heavy days on Lake Huron. Source: [canadaoceanracing.com](https://www.canadaoceanracing.com/thank-you-canada-what-scott-shawyer-has-learned-after-a-summer-of-intense-training-on-an-imoca-60/)

The Fleet

The Route du Rhum 2026 presentation in Paris revealed that securing the Saint-Malo site now costs 1.1 million euros, compared to 50,000 in 2014. Organizers also face a 400,000 euro budget for CSR measures, including a 100% electric village. The business model relies on sponsorship rather than TV rights or ticketing, and the start broadcast actually loses money at 500,000 euros. This escalation in costs shows how environmental and security requirements are reshaping the economics of major offshore events. The thirteenth edition will feature 118 solo sailors across six classes on one start line from Saint-Malo to Pointe-à-Pitre. OC Sport Pen Duick CEO Joseph Bizard noted that the event reaches millions of people every four years, with Saint-Malo welcoming 1.2 million visitors in 2022 and Guadeloupe seeing 300,000. The race does not rely on TV rights or ticketing, which normally account for 70 percent of such event budgets, and instead depends on sponsorship. Producing the start and finish broadcasts costs 500,000 euros, creating a loss on TV. Site security in Saint-Malo has risen sharply, while CSR investments now total around 400,000 euros, covering Natura 2000 site demarcation, carbon assessments, educational work on environmental issues, and a fully electric village to reduce the carbon footprint. These figures come from an economic press briefing held the morning of the official presentation on 22 September at the Folies Bergères in Paris. Source: [sailorz.com](https://sailorz.com/en/route-du-rhum-en/route-du-rhum-how-much-does-it-cost/)

Jackknife, the J/125 sailed by Sam and Andrew Hall, has entered the 2027 RORC Transatlantic Race, building on their third-place finish in the 2026 edition under IRC. The campaign moves from ISORA series success to a 3,000nm Atlantic crossing, demonstrating how consistent preparation allows a Corinthian crew to compete against larger yachts. The Halls previously campaigned the Lombard 46 Pata Negra on the Irish Sea and retained the overall ISORA title, with Sam also receiving the association's top youth award. Jackknife won IRC Two and finished third overall under IRC in the 2026 RORC Transatlantic Race, showing the boat's effectiveness over a full ocean crossing despite lacking the latest generation of offshore technology. The move from ISORA racing to the 3,000nm Atlantic crossing represents another step in a campaign built on consistency, preparation and crew work rather than outright speed. The boat has been a familiar competitor in Irish offshore racing, including the ISORA series, where the Halls developed their offshore programme. Source: [afloat.ie](https://afloat.ie/sail/events/rorc-transatlantic/item/73170-jackknife-returns-to-rorc-transatlantic-race)

In the 24H Ultim, SVR Lazartigue won the Ultim class while Edenred took the Ocean Fifty category. These short-format races serve as final tune-ups before the Route du Rhum, testing boat speed and crew work in a compressed window. The event took place on 26 September and provided the last major gathering of the fleet ahead of the November transatlantic start. SVR Lazartigue's victory in the Ultim division and Edenred's win in the Ocean Fifty class highlight the competitive depth in both the 32-metre flying trimarans and the 50-foot trimarans that will share the Route du Rhum start line with the IMOCA fleet. Source: [voilesetvoiliers.ouest-france.fr](https://voilesetvoiliers.ouest-france.fr/course-au-large/24h-ultim/24h-ultim-victoire-de-svr-lazartigue-en-ultim-et-edenred-en-ocean-fifty-8b804839-3aad-4beb-8e47-c59a1ddcaff2)

Plain Sailing

The Route du Rhum starts all classes on one line, from the 32-metre Ultims down to the Vintage monos. That single start line creates the festival atmosphere the race is known for, but it also means the smaller boats must navigate the same weather systems and traffic as the giants for the first forty-eight hours. The 118 entries this year will share the same departure from Saint-Malo, with the fastest boats pulling away quickly while the Class40s and Vintage entries settle into their own rhythms. This format rewards skippers who can manage their own race without being distracted by the leaders disappearing over the horizon. The six official classes are ULTIM, Ocean Fifty, IMOCA, Class40, Vintage Multi and Vintage Mono. Each class has its own target time, yet every boat crosses the same starting line at the same moment. For a solo skipper this means the first day and night are spent threading through a crowded fleet while the weather routing decisions that will shape the rest of the crossing are already being made. The format turns the departure into a single shared moment rather than staggered starts, which is why the race draws such large crowds in Saint-Malo and why the early hours often decide who stays in contention.

The Countdown

Seven hundred seventy-six days remain until the Vendée Globe start on 12 November 2028. The campaign's next milestone is the Route du Rhum race village opening in twenty-two days on 20 October.

Ep 6: Ruyant Withdraws from Route du Rhum
Download Read transcript

Offshore North

Week ending: September 21, 2026

Thomas Ruyant, the defending IMOCA champion for the Route du Rhum, has withdrawn from the November start because he could not secure a major sponsor for his new foiling boat.

The Canadian Boat

As of the team's 2 September post, EMIRA IV was leaving Canada and heading back to Europe. The post is Scott Shawyer's own account of the summer training on Georgian Bay. He described the biggest learning as understanding how the boat works system by system, including sails, rigging, foils, electrical systems, hydrogenerators, solar panels and autopilot overlays. Scott noted that after more than forty years sailing on Lake Huron, the water conditions held few surprises, but the boat itself did. He mentioned that on two heavy days his triceps seized on the grinder, which he traces to a shoulder injury and not drinking enough water with electrolytes. He added that he has still not sailed EMIRA IV in open-ocean waves and swell and needs to remain humble. The support team named includes Martin Amescua, who has been with the boat for a long time, Gaby Bucau, Clovis Gautier, Giulina Bayat for rigging and systems, Alberto Zaccaria for electrical, computer and sensor systems, Alan Roberts for coaching on the Exocet routing system and autopilot overlays, and Nick Moloney as sailing coach. The boat was not among the 14 IMOCAs that started the 48H Azimut in Lorient on 17 September. The next confirmed milestone for the campaign remains the Route du Rhum start on 1 November from Saint-Malo, Shawyer's first solo transatlantic on a foiling IMOCA. Source: [canadaoceanracing.com](https://www.canadaoceanracing.com/thank-you-canada-what-scott-shawyer-has-learned-after-a-summer-of-intense-training-on-an-imoca-60/)

The Fleet

Thomas Ruyant announced on 21 September that he will not start the Route du Rhum on 1 November. The defending IMOCA champion cited the failure to secure a major sponsor for his new foiling IMOCA and the roughly 2.5 million euros required for the race and ongoing operations. The boat, launched on 19 June, remains in storage in Lorient. Ruyant called the decision immensely disappointing, noting that the race has a particular place in his history, yet stated that his ambitions for the IMOCA circuit and the 2028 Vendée Globe remain unchanged, with the energy even stronger. He had sought major backing for about 2.5 million euros to participate competitively, and the team needs 4 to 5 million euros per year until the Vendée Globe. Other skippers, including Armel Tripon and Maxime Sorel, are also still seeking funding for the same start in a tense sponsorship market. The withdrawal leaves one fewer established name on the 26-boat IMOCA entry list for a race that already sits in a tight sponsorship market. Source: [lequipe.fr](https://www.lequipe.fr/Voile/Actualites/Tenant-du-titre-en-imoca-thomas-ruyant-renonce-a-la-route-du-rhum-faute-de-sponsor/1720261)

Anthony Marchand completed winter modifications on Actual Ultim 4 that included a taller mast at the class maximum height and a revised sail plan. The changes allow earlier take-off, more mast bend, and the ability to flatten the mainsail further without reefing early. Marchand described the work as preparation for his primary 2026 objective, the Route du Rhum on the 32-metre trimaran. He noted that the new mast allows proactive adjustments that were not possible with the previous wing mast, and the sail inventory was adjusted accordingly, with mainsail, J3 and J2 all at the same sail area. The modifications illustrate how even established Ultim teams continue to chase marginal gains in a class where small differences in rig behaviour can decide a solo Atlantic crossing. Source: [sailorz.com](https://sailorz.com/en/ultim-en/anthony-marchand-the-rhum-on-an-ultim-its-even-beyond-the-dream/)

Plain Sailing

An IMOCA campaign's annual operating budget typically runs between four and five million euros once a new boat is in the water. That figure covers the boat itself, shore team, spares, travel, insurance and the skipper's salary. Most of it must be raised from title sponsors who treat the campaign as media exposure rather than philanthropy. When a sponsor relationship ends or a new one cannot be closed in time, the boat stays ashore even if it is ready to race. The Route du Rhum entry list shows how quickly that arithmetic can remove a defending champion from a start line. Ruyant had launched his new boat in June but could not raise the necessary funds, highlighting the business reality behind even the most competitive campaigns. A skipper must also complete at least one Grade 2 solo race on the boat they will race in the Vendée Globe, finishing within twice the winner's time, and finish among the top 37 in the IMOCA Globe Series championship for a place in the 40-boat fleet.

The Countdown

Seven hundred eighty-three days remain until the Vendée Globe start on 12 November 2028. The campaign's next concrete milestone is the Route du Rhum start on 1 November 2026 from Saint-Malo.

Ep 5: Class40 leads Route du Rhum entries
Download Read transcript

Offshore North

Week ending: September 14, 2026

Class40 has drawn 49 entries for the upcoming Route du Rhum, making it the largest and most open class on the start line.

The Canadian Boat

The campaign's own channels show no new posts inside the seven-day window. Canada Ocean Racing's main site was last updated September 2 with a post on what Scott Shawyer learned after summer training on Georgian Bay. The news section was last updated August 17 and the YouTube channel August 31. The team confirmed the boat completed its first Canadian summer training programme based on Georgian Bay after arriving in Collingwood, Ontario at the start of June 2026. Delivery from France had included an Atlantic crossing followed by the St. Lawrence Seaway, the Welland Canal and the Great Lakes, with mechanical problems in the Seaway requiring repairs en route. The published plan had the boat remaining in Collingwood until roughly mid-August 2026 before returning across the Atlantic to France, though it in fact departed the weekend of 8–9 August 2026. No update on current position or routing has appeared since the August 31 YouTube post covering the Welland Canal transit.

Standing item: EMIRA IV is a current-generation foiling IMOCA 60 acquired by Canada Ocean Racing in 2025; under previous names it won The Ocean Race 2023 and finished third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe. Source: [canadaoceanracing.com](https://www.canadaoceanracing.com/)

The Fleet

Class40 has 49 boats entered for the Route du Rhum – Destination Guadeloupe that starts November 1 from Saint-Malo. The class is described as the biggest and most open on the start line. That entry count matters because Class40 serves as the main affordable step before IMOCA campaigns, and a large field increases the chance that strong performers will attract the funding needed to move up. The race covers roughly 3,500 nautical miles across the Atlantic to Pointe-à-Pitre in Guadeloupe. Organisers have noted the class draws a wide range of experience levels, from established professionals to emerging sailors seeking their first major solo offshore result. A crowded start line also raises the likelihood of close tactical battles in the early days when all classes share the same weather window. Source: [mysailing.com.au](https://www.mysailing.com.au/class40-is-the-biggest-and-most-open-class-in-the-upcoming-route-du-rhum/)

Boris Herrmann's Team Malizia crossed the line first as The Ocean Race Atlantic began. The crewed transatlantic event gives IMOCA teams a chance to test boats and routing in a format that still requires full crews rather than solo sailing. A strong result here can influence sponsor confidence and crew selections for the solo Route du Rhum that follows in November. The boat is a current-generation foiling IMOCA 60 that has previously competed in The Ocean Race Europe. Herrmann's team posted the result on its own site the day after the finish. The race itself forms part of the 2026 IMOCA calendar alongside the Vendée Arctique and the Route du Rhum. Source: [team-malizia.com](https://www.team-malizia.com/news/boris-herrmann-s-team-malizia-first-across-the-line-as-the-ocean-race-atlantic-begins)

Jérémie Beyou has stated he wants to move into the Ultim class. The 30-metre flying trimarans represent the next step above IMOCA in speed and cost. Any established IMOCA skipper shifting classes changes the talent pool available for future Vendée Globe campaigns and signals where sponsorship money is flowing. Beyou has a long record in the IMOCA class, including multiple Route du Rhum and Vendée Globe starts. The announcement appeared in the weekly Mercato column on Sailorz. Moving up requires securing a berth on one of the handful of Ultim trimarans currently campaigning, a step that typically involves multi-year sponsorship commitments. Source: [sailorz.com](https://sailorz.com/mercato/jeremie-beyou-veut-se-lancer-en-ultim/)

Plain Sailing

The Route du Rhum is the sport's great festival because every class starts on the same line on the same day. On November 1 at 13:02 local time, 117 solo skippers in six different boat types will leave Saint-Malo together and race across the Atlantic to Pointe-à-Pitre in Guadeloupe. The smallest are the 12-metre Class40s; the largest are the 30-metre Ultim trimarans that can exceed 40 knots. Because the start is massed rather than staggered, a spectator on the breakwater can watch every boat pass the same buoy in the same hour. That single start also forces every team to make the same weather-routing decisions at the same moment, which turns the first 48 hours into a live laboratory for how different designs handle the same conditions. The format is why the race draws the biggest crowds and the widest television coverage in the calendar. A Canadian skipper's first solo transatlantic on a foiling IMOCA will therefore unfold inside a much larger story that every other class is also writing at the same time. The race village opens three weeks earlier on 20 October, giving teams time to prepare boats and conduct final sea trials in the same waters the fleet will cross on race day. Six classes share the start line: Ultim, IMOCA, Ocean Fifty, Class40, Class 50 and the Rhum Multi category. The staggered arrival times at Pointe-à-Pitre reflect the huge speed differences between the fastest trimarans and the slowest monohulls, yet the shared departure creates a single narrative thread that runs through every result story. For an IMOCA skipper the first days are spent managing the transition from coastal waters into the open Atlantic while watching the larger Ultims pull away and the smaller Class40s stay close on corrected time. That mix of competition and spectacle is what makes the Route du Rhum the event most often cited by new fans as their entry point into the sport. The 2026 edition marks the thirteenth running since the inaugural race in 1978, when Canadian Mike Birch won the first edition by 98 seconds after 23 days at sea. Every subsequent edition has kept the same mass-start format, preserving the festival atmosphere even as boat speeds have more than doubled. For the Canadian campaign the race serves as both a competitive milestone and a public introduction to a wider audience that may not yet know the Vendée Globe story. The shared start line means the first weather system after departure will test every design philosophy at once, from the high-speed foilers to the more conservative displacement boats. That shared test is the reason the Route du Rhum remains the single most watched offshore event outside the Vendée Globe itself.

The Countdown

790 days remain until the Vendée Globe start on 12 November 2028. The campaign's next concrete milestone is the Route du Rhum, which starts 1 November 2026 from Saint-Malo.

Ep 4: Ocean Race Atlantic Leaders Extend Gap
Download Read transcript

Offshore North

Week ending: September 07, 2026

Team Malizia leads The Ocean Race Atlantic after recovering from a technical issue while a leading trio pulls ahead of the rest of the fleet in rough conditions.

The Canadian Boat

As of the team's most recent update on 2 September 2026, the boat's position after its early-August departure from Collingwood remains unconfirmed in public posts. Canada Ocean Racing's site was last updated 2 September with a summary of summer training; its News section was last updated 17 August with a television interview; its YouTube channel was last updated 31 August with a video on the Welland Canal transit.

Standing item: EMIRA IV is the IMOCA 60 previously raced as 11th Hour Racing Team — Mālama, which won The Ocean Race 2023 under Charlie Enright and finished third in the 2024–25 Vendée Globe under Sébastien Simon despite a broken foil. The boat was built as a current-generation foiling design over 18 metres long and capable of speeds above 40 knots. It passed to Sébastien Simon after the 2023 race and carried him to that Vendée Globe podium on reduced foiling performance. Canada Ocean Racing took delivery in early 2025 and renamed it EMIRA IV for Scott Shawyer's campaign. The boat's history directly supplies the campaign's first foiling IMOCA platform ahead of the 2028 Vendée Globe start. Source: [canadaoceanracing.com](https://www.canadaoceanracing.com/)

The Fleet

In The Ocean Race Atlantic, a 3,000-nautical-mile event now at its midpoint, Paul Meilhat on United by the Ocean, Francesca Clapcich on Team Francesca Clapcich powered by 11th Hour Racing, and Kojiro Shiraishi on DMG MORI Global One have opened a gap of more than 430 miles over the rest of the six-boat IMOCA fleet after five days. The leaders have moved ahead of a low-pressure system that brought gusts above 30 knots and heavy slamming seas, while Boris Herrmann's Team Malizia recovered from an earlier technical problem to retain the overall lead. The conditions have made eating, sleeping and moving on board physically and mentally demanding. Meilhat described the fleet as at the halfway point with hopes for steadier weather ahead. The spread across hundreds of miles means small routing choices now carry larger consequences for the final standings into Lorient. Source: [sail-world.com](https://www.sail-world.com/news/299527/The-Ocean-Race-Atlantic-2026-Day-5)

Embrace the Challenge sustained a three-metre tear in its mainsail while reefing in 40-knot winds and has carried out repairs at sea to return to full speed. The damage occurred during the same low-pressure system that affected the entire fleet. Crews on the boat completed the repair without stopping, allowing the yacht to resume racing at competitive pace. The incident illustrates how equipment failures in heavy weather can erase hours or days of progress even when the boat itself remains intact. Source: [sail-world.com](https://www.sail-world.com/news/299528/?source=rss)

Sixteen solo sailors started the Golden Globe Race from Les Sables d'Olonne on 6 September for a non-stop, unassisted round-the-world voyage using only 1960s-era equipment and no modern electronics or routing. The edition follows the original 1968–69 rules with no satellite navigation, no weather routing assistance, and no outside support. Skippers must rely on sextant, paper charts, and self-sufficiency for the entire circumnavigation. The start marks the third running of the event since its revival. Source: [sailorz.com](https://sailorz.com/en/golden-globe-race-en/the-golden-globe-race-sets-sail-again/)

Four Ultim trimarans and eleven Ocean Fifty boats competed in the 24H Ultim, the final major offshore confrontation before the Route du Rhum starts on 1 November. The event served as a last high-speed test for the largest and fastest classes ahead of the Saint-Malo to Pointe-à-Pitre race. Both Ultim and Ocean Fifty fleets used the short-format race to refine setups and crew coordination under race conditions. The timing places the results directly into Route du Rhum preparation cycles for the boats involved. Source: [voilesetvoiliers.ouest-france.fr](https://voilesetvoiliers.ouest-france.fr/course-au-large/24h-ultim-quatre-ultim-et-onze-ocean-fifty-pour-une-derniere-confrontation-avant-la-route-du-rhum-8273a5a9-4a7c-43f7-bcb3-25f787acd081)

Plain Sailing

The physical demands on crews in a transatlantic race like The Ocean Race Atlantic come from the combination of constant motion and sleep disruption rather than from any single storm. When a low-pressure system passes, winds can jump from 15 knots to over 30 knots within hours, forcing repeated sail changes and reefing while the boat slams through short, steep waves. On a 60-foot IMOCA the motion is violent enough that simply moving from the cockpit to the galley or trying to prepare a freeze-dried meal becomes a full-body effort; crews often report going 24 hours or more without a proper hot meal or more than a few minutes of continuous rest. The six-boat fleet in this edition encountered exactly those conditions on day four and five, with gusts above 30 knots and seas that made every task slower and more exhausting.

Sleep is taken in short fragments because the boat must keep moving at high speed through the night. Even with an autopilot holding course, someone must remain alert for sudden wind shifts or equipment strain, and the noise of water slamming against the hull makes deeper rest difficult. Over five days these effects compound: reaction times slow, small errors in sail trim or routing add up, and the mental load of deciding whether to push harder or conserve the boat grows heavier. The leading trio of Meilhat, Clapcich and Shiraishi used the low-pressure system as a turning point, sailing a route that placed them ahead of the worst weather while the rest of the fleet remained inside it.

The leading trio's decision to pull ahead of the low-pressure system shows how these cumulative stresses shape strategy. By sailing a slightly longer route to clearer air they traded distance for a chance at steadier conditions and better rest, a calculation that becomes decisive when the fleet is already spread across hundreds of miles. The gap of more than 430 miles that opened after five days is the direct result of those choices made under fatigue. In ocean racing the boat that manages its crew's energy most effectively often gains the largest advantage once the weather settles.

Plain Sailing

The Ocean Race Atlantic requires every boat to carry exactly two women and two men.

That 50/50 rule is not a suggestion; it is written into the notice of race and checked at the dock before the start. In practice it forces teams to build four-person watches that balance strength, weight, technical skill and decision-making across gender lines on a boat that still demands the same physical loads as any IMOCA—grinding winches, handling 40-knot squalls, and executing sail changes while the hull is flying on one or two foils. The six entries therefore represent the first systematic test of whether this enforced balance changes how a crew organises itself for a six-to-eight-day crossing.

Because the race is crewed rather than solo, the skipper’s role shifts from sole decision-maker to coordinator who must integrate four sets of observations and fatigue patterns. On a solo boat the skipper alone reads the weather, chooses the sail plan, and decides when to push or conserve; here those tasks are distributed, which changes both the speed of decisions and the risk of conflicting inputs at three in the morning. The format therefore tests both the human system (how quickly a mixed team learns to trust one another’s calls) and the technical system (how sail plans and routing change when four people can stand watch instead of one). Teams such as the Clapcich–Meilhat pairing and the all-Kiwi crew under Colman will reveal whether national or cross-border combinations handle that coordination differently.

The first edition therefore functions as a live experiment: results will show whether the gender-balance rule produces measurably different speeds, safety records or breakage rates compared with the all-male or open crews that dominate most IMOCA events. If the mixed boats finish with fewer structural issues or more consistent average speeds, organisers may consider widening the rule to other crewed series. If the opposite occurs, the data will still inform how future mixed events are structured. Either outcome supplies the class with concrete evidence on a format that has so far existed only in smaller classes and shorter races.

Plain Sailing

The Golden Globe Race and the Vendée Globe both send one person alone around the world without stopping, yet they test entirely different skills. The Golden Globe uses only boats designed before 1988 and forbids GPS, satellite phones, or weather-routing computers; skippers must navigate with a sextant and paper charts the way the original 1968 competitors did. The Vendée Globe, by contrast, is sailed in current-generation foiling IMOCAs that can exceed 40 knots and carry the full suite of modern satellite systems.

Because the boats are slower and the information poorer, a Golden Globe skipper’s daily decisions rest on what can be seen from the deck and what the barometer and paper weather maps suggest. That constraint turns every cloud build-up and wind shift into a high-stakes calculation made without the safety net of real-time forecasts or outside assistance. A skipper may spend weeks without speaking to another human, rationing food, water and energy while the boat moves at perhaps one-third the speed of an IMOCA. The absence of electronic autopilots and routing software also means the sailor must steer and trim continuously during daylight hours and accept whatever weather arrives rather than threading between systems.

The result is a race whose difficulty lies less in raw speed than in sustained self-reliance over roughly eight months at sea. A listener can picture it as the difference between driving a modern rally car with live telemetry and driving a 1960s vehicle across the same continents using only a road atlas and a compass. The Golden Globe therefore measures endurance of isolation and judgment under deliberately limited tools, while the Vendée Globe measures the ability to extract maximum performance from the most advanced offshore machines ever built. Both formats reward the same core trait—making correct decisions when no one else can help—but they remove different variables to isolate that trait. One removes technology; the other removes company. A listener who finishes this explanation can state that the Golden Globe’s eight-month duration and pre-1988 boats create a test of pure self-reliance that modern IMOCA racing deliberately replaces with speed and connectivity.

Plain Sailing

The Vendée Globe is the only round-the-world race that is solo, non-stop and unassisted. A skipper leaves Les Sables d’Olonne, sails south through the Atlantic, rounds the three great capes of the Southern Ocean, then returns the same way without any outside help and without ever stopping the boat. Sleep comes in fragments of 20 minutes while the autopilot steers.

No Canadian has ever finished the race. That single fact is why the campaign exists and why every qualifying race Shawyer sails on EMIRA IV now carries an extra layer: each completed Grade 2 solo event both banks points toward the 2028 entry list and proves the boat and skipper can survive the conditions that have already claimed lives in the fleet’s history.
