Today we explore the warm and expressive world of Russian feelings so you can share exactly how you feel in any moment.

Word of the Day

рад

Transliteration: rad

English meaning: glad / happy (short form, used like “I am glad”)

Example sentence: Я рад видеть тебя.

English translation: I am glad to see you.

Memory hook: It sounds like the start of “radiant” — when you’re рад, your face lights up!

Repeat after me: рад… рад.

Grammar Spotlight:

Short-form adjectives in Russian let you say how someone feels right now without extra words. They change slightly for gender (рад for he, рада for she) but stay simple.

Examples:

  • Он рад. (He is glad.)
  • Она грустная. (She is sad.)
  • Я устал. (I am tired — said by a male speaker.)

Unlike English, you often drop the verb “to be” entirely — Russians just say the feeling word and it’s understood.

Word Origins — Deep Dive: рад

This little word carries a surprising ancient link: it comes from the Proto-Slavic *radъ, which traces back to the same Indo-European root that gave English “ready” and even “ride.” Long ago the idea was “prepared and eager,” so when you say “я рад” you are literally saying you are ready and happy to see someone. A fun false-friend moment: it looks a bit like the English name “Rad” but has nothing to do with radiation! Here’s a quick memory trick — picture yourself already “ready” with a big smile whenever you feel рад.

Cultural Corner:

In Russian conversations people often answer “как дела?” with a short feeling word like “нормально” or “всё хорошо,” keeping things warm and direct. Sharing feelings openly is part of the friendly, heartfelt style you hear in everyday chats across Russia.

Practice Challenge:

For kids: Draw a face showing each feeling (рад, грустный, злой) and label it in Russian.

For adults: Next time you greet a friend in English, switch to Russian and say “Как дела?” then answer with one of today’s words — “Я рад!” or “Я устал.”

Grammar Spotlight:

In Russian, the verb “to eat” changes depending on who is doing the action. We say “я ем” (I eat), “ты ешь” (you eat), and “он/она ест” (he/she eats). This is different from English, where the verb stays almost the same. Example: Я ем сыр. (I eat cheese.) Ты ешь хлеб. (You eat bread.)

This word started in northern Chinese dialects as “chá,” traveled along the Silk Road through Central Asia, and entered Russian in the 17th century as the popular drink “чай.” But be careful — the Russian word “чай” sounds exactly like the English “chai” yet it simply means the same beverage, not a fancy latte! Here’s a memory trick: picture a steaming cup of “chay” arriving by caravan from China straight to a Russian samovar.

Cultural Corner:

Russians love sharing tea and simple snacks like bread with cheese or soup when guests visit. Offering food is a warm way to show hospitality and start a friendly conversation.

Ветер

Transliteration: VYEH-ter

English meaning: wind

Example sentence: Ветер дует сильно.

Example translation: The wind is blowing strongly.

It sounds a little like “weather” but means the moving air you feel on your face!

Transliteration: VYEH-ter

English: wind

Example sentence: Ветер холодный сегодня.

Example translation: The wind is cold today.

Memory hook: Think of a “veteran” sailor who knows every gust of wind.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Снег

English: snow

Example sentence: Снег падает на землю.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Облако

Transliteration: OB-la-ka

English: cloud

Example sentence: Большое облако плывёт по небу.

Example translation: A big cloud is floating across the sky.

Memory hook: Say “ob-la-ka” like “oh, block-a” – it blocks the sun!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Тепло

Transliteration: tep-LO

English: warm / warmth

Example sentence: Сегодня тепло на улице.

Example translation: It is warm outside today.

Memory hook: Sounds like “tepid” but means pleasantly warm.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Холодно

Transliteration: ha-LOD-na

English: cold (impersonal)

Example sentence: Зимой холодно в Сибири.

Example translation: It is cold in Siberia in winter.

Memory hook: “Ha-lod-na” – imagine a “halo” of frost around you.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Ливень

Transliteration: LEE-ven'

English: downpour / heavy rain

Example sentence: Ливень идёт весь день.

Example translation: A downpour is falling all day.

Memory hook: “Lee-ven” like a “living” river pouring from the sky.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Гроза

Transliteration: gra-ZA

English: thunderstorm

Example sentence: Гроза громкая и яркая.

Example translation: The thunderstorm is loud and bright.

Memory hook: “Gra-za” sounds like “graze” but brings lightning instead of cows.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): Температура

Transliteration: tem-pe-ra-TOO-ra

English: temperature

Example sentence: Какая температура на улице?

Example translation: What is the temperature outside?

Memory hook: Almost the same as English – just roll the “r”!

Grammar Spotlight:

Impersonal constructions with weather (using adverbs like тепло, холодно). In Russian we often say “It is warm” without saying “it” – just Тепло.

Examples:

  • Тепло. (It is warm.)
  • Холодно. (It is cold.)
  • Сегодня ветрено. (It is windy today.)

Unlike English “It is cold,” Russian drops the subject and uses the adverb form directly.

Word Origins — Deep Dive: Ветер

This word started in ancient Proto-Slavic as *větrъ and shares a very old root with English “wind” that goes all the way back to the same Indo-European source meaning “to blow.” It travelled through centuries of Slavic languages without changing much, while English kept a similar sound but spelled it differently. Be careful – the Russian “ветер” sounds nothing like the English “veteran” even though they share some letters! Here’s a memory trick: picture a veteran sailor shouting “VYEH-ter!” every time a gust hits the sails.

Cultural Corner:

Russians love to talk about the weather because it changes dramatically across their huge country – from snowy winters in Siberia to warm summers near the Black Sea. People often greet each other with a quick comment about the temperature or wind as a friendly way to start conversation.

Practice Challenge:

For kids: Draw a picture of today’s weather and label the sun, clouds, and wind with the new Russian words, then say each word out loud.

For adults: Look out the window right now and make one simple sentence in Russian about the weather using at least two new words.

Word of the Day

Transliteration: oo-CHEE-tyel

English meaning: teacher

Example sentence: Мой учитель говорит по-русски.

Example translation: My teacher speaks Russian.

Memory hook: Imagine your teacher cheering “You can do it!” — the “cheer” sound in “учитель” helps you remember the friendly guide at school.

Repeat after me: учитель… (pause) учитель.

Transliteration: SHKO-la

English: school

Example sentence: Я иду в школу.

Memory hook: “School” starts with the same “shk” sound as “school” in English — easy to remember!

Transliteration: KNEE-ga

English: book

Example sentence: Учитель читает книгу.

Example sentence: Дети пишут в тетради.

English: to read

  • Russian (Cyrillic): ручка

English: pen

Example sentence: Дай мне ручку, пожалуйста.

English: class / classroom

English: friend

Example sentence: Мой друг сидит рядом.

Example translation: My friend sits next to me.

Memory hook: Your “drook” is the buddy who “drums” up fun at school.

Grammar Spotlight:

Today we look at the verb “читать” (to read) in the present tense. Russian verbs change endings depending on who is doing the action.

  • Я читаю (I read)
  • Ты читаешь (you read)
  • Он/она читает (he/she reads)

Unlike English, which often keeps the verb the same (“I read, you read”), Russian adds different endings so the listener instantly knows who is acting.

Word Origins — Deep Dive: школа

No way — the Russian word for school started life meaning “free time”! It began in ancient Greek as skholē, which first described leisure or spare time for thinking and discussion. Greek scholars passed it to Latin as schola, then it travelled into Old Russian around the 17th century through Western European languages. But be careful — in English we also say “school,” yet the Russian version kept the original Greek “skh” sound while English softened it. Here’s a memory trick: picture Greek philosophers relaxing in their skholē, then suddenly deciding to turn their free time into a classroom — that same peaceful spot became школа!

Cultural Corner:

In Russia, the first day of school on September 1 is called “День знаний” (Knowledge Day). Children bring flowers to their teachers and the whole school gathers for a special assembly to celebrate the start of the new learning year.

Practice Challenge:

For kids: Draw a picture of your classroom and label three things using today’s words (учитель, книга, ручка).

For adults: Imagine you are meeting a new Russian classmate — introduce yourself and say one thing you like about school using at least two new words.

English meaning: penguin

English: hare

Transliteration: med-VYED'

English: bear

Example sentence: Медведь ест мёд.

Memory hook: “Med” means honey, so медведь is the “honey-knower”!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): корова

Transliteration: ka-RO-va

English: cow

Example sentence: Корова даёт молоко.

  • Russian (Cyrillic): собака

Transliteration: sa-BA-ka

English: dog

Example sentence: Собака бежит быстро.

Memory hook: “Sobaka” sounds like “so bark-a” — dogs bark!

  • Russian (Cyrillic): кошка

Transliteration: KOSH-ka

English: cat

Example sentence: Кошка спит на окне.

Example translation: The cat sleeps on the window.

Memory hook: “Koshka” is cozy like a cat curled up.

Grammar Spotlight:

Russian nouns have three genders: masculine, feminine, and neuter. Most animal words ending in a consonant are masculine (like волк), words ending in -а are usually feminine (like корова), and some ending in -о are neuter. Examples: Волк большой (The wolf is big — masculine). Корова большая (The cow is big — feminine). This is different from English, where we don’t change adjectives for gender.

Word Origins — Deep Dive: медведь

This word started in ancient Slavic languages as a clever way to avoid saying the real name of the bear, which people once feared. It literally breaks down to “med” (honey) + “ved” (knower), so it means “the one who knows honey.” Russian speakers used this polite nickname instead of the older word for bear. But be careful — the English word “bear” comes from a completely different root meaning “brown,” so it’s a false friend in sound and meaning. Here’s a memory trick: picture a bear sniffing out honey like a detective who always finds the sweet stuff.

Cultural Corner:

In Russian fairy tales, the bear is often a strong but kind character who lives in the forest and helps or tests the hero. Russians love stories like “Masha and the Bear,” where the bear is big and a little clumsy but friendly.

Practice Challenge:

For kids: Draw your favourite animal and say its Russian name out loud three times while colouring it. For adults: Look around your home or neighbourhood, point to any animal (even in a picture or on TV), and say the Russian word for it in a full sentence like “Это собака.”
