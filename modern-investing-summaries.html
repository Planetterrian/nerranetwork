Strategy Spotlight

Yield-curve steepness analysis helps investors decide whether to favor short-duration bonds or extend into longer maturities when central-bank decisions loom. The 2-year Treasury yield stood at 4.7494 percent while the 30-year reached 5.3200 percent, producing a 57-basis-point spread that indicates the market anticipates limited near-term easing. An investor opens a brokerage bond screener, selects investment-grade corporate issues maturing between 2028 and 2030, and compares each yield to the 2-year benchmark to locate names offering at least 80 basis points of additional carry over the short end. The same screener can be filtered for option-adjusted spreads so the investor sees how much extra compensation the market demands for credit risk at each maturity point. This technique draws directly from the forward curve embedded in the CME FedWatch Tool probabilities, where a 76.5 percent chance of a 25-basis-point hike by the December 9 meeting already prices modest further tightening. The concrete implementation step is to set a price alert on the 10-year/2-year spread at the 60-basis-point level and review any intermediate corporate holdings only if the spread widens before the next FOMC statement. The approach relies on the inverse relationship between yields and prices, so a steepening curve that lifts longer yields faster than shorter ones reduces the present value of distant cash flows on intermediate bonds. When the curve instead flattens because inflation data surprises higher, even carefully selected carry trades can lose ground as the entire curve shifts upward together. Source: cnbc.com

Investor Education: How Central-Bank Decisions Transmit to Corporate Bond Spreads

You hold a 5-year investment-grade corporate bond bought at a 120-basis-point spread over the 5-year Treasury. The Fed leaves rates unchanged but the chair’s statement hints at a higher neutral rate; within 90 minutes the Treasury yield rises 8 basis points while your corporate bond’s spread widens to 135 basis points, cutting the price by roughly 0.7 percent even though the policy rate itself did not move. The mechanism is that dealers widen credit spreads when they anticipate slower future rate cuts, because the present value of the issuer’s cash flows falls and the risk of refinancing at higher rates increases. A professional habit is to check the option-adjusted spread on your bond every time the FedWatch Tool probability of a December hike moves more than 10 percentage points; if the spread has already widened 15 basis points or more, you reduce the position size before the statement rather than after. The common misconception is that an unchanged policy rate means unchanged bond prices; the fix is to treat the forward curve and credit-spread reaction as the actual trade signal, not the headline rate decision itself. When the 10-year yield sits at 5.0134 percent and the 2-year at 4.7494 percent, any statement language that shifts the expected path of the federal funds rate changes the discount rate applied to every corporate cash flow beyond the next meeting. Investors who monitor only the spot rate miss the repricing that occurs in the 90-minute window after the press conference when the entire curve and the credit-spread matrix adjust simultaneously.

Strategy Spotlight

Custom benchmarking lets TFSA investors measure performance against a personalized ETF mix that reflects the exact dates and dollar amounts of their contributions rather than a single static index. The Globe and Mail article describes a retired Albertan who weights several ETFs by contribution timing to separate genuine outperformance from simple market beta. To implement it, pull your TFSA contribution history from your brokerage, assign each deposit to the ETF allocation you would have held at that moment, then calculate the blended return for comparison against your actual holdings. This approach works best when contribution dates cluster around market lows or highs because it removes the timing distortion that a plain TSX or S&P 500 benchmark introduces. It fails when investors cherry-pick favorable ETF weights after the fact or ignore rebalancing costs inside the account. The technique is especially useful for Canadian investors who add to their TFSA irregularly and want an honest read on whether active picks are adding value. By rebuilding the benchmark from actual cash-flow dates, investors can see whether a holding-company name like POW.TO truly beat what a contribution-weighted mix would have delivered over the same periods. The method also highlights how irregular additions during volatile stretches can make a static index look either too generous or too harsh depending on when money arrived in the account. Source: theglobeandmail.com

Investor Education: The Mechanics of Custom Benchmarking in Tax-Advantaged Accounts

If you held POW.TO in your TFSA and compared its return only to the TSX Composite, the gap might look large, yet weighting the benchmark to your actual contribution dates and amounts can shrink or reverse that gap. The mechanism works by treating each contribution as a separate "bucket" that earns the return of the chosen ETF mix from the day the money arrived, then summing the buckets to create a personalized hurdle rate. A pro habit is to export the contribution log once per quarter and run the weighted calculation in a simple spreadsheet before reviewing any individual holdings. The common misconception is that a single broad index already gives a fair performance picture; the fix is to rebuild the benchmark from the contribution timeline so the comparison reflects the cash flows you actually experienced. When contributions arrive in clusters near market peaks or troughs, the custom benchmark reveals whether outperformance came from security selection or simply from the timing of new capital entering the account. Investors who skip this step often credit or blame their picks for results that were largely driven by when they happened to add fresh TFSA room. Running the calculation quarterly also surfaces any drift between the intended ETF mix and the actual holdings, prompting rebalancing decisions that keep the performance test honest.

Investor Education: Options Basics for Income and Protection

Imagine you already own 100 shares of CIBC at the current price near $159 and want to generate extra cash without selling. You could sell a covered call at the $170 strike expiring in 30 days for a $1.80 premium. That premium is collected immediately and lowers your effective cost basis to $157.20, but it also caps upside if the shares rally above $170 by expiry. The mechanism works because the buyer of the call pays you for the right to buy your shares at the strike; if the stock stays below $170 you keep both the shares and the premium. A pro habit is to check the option chain’s open interest and bid-ask spread before placing the order—wide spreads on low-volume strikes can erase most of the income edge. The common misconception is that covered calls are “free money” with no downside; in reality they reduce but do not eliminate loss if the stock falls sharply, and they limit gains in strong rallies. The one-sentence fix is to size the position so the premium income offsets no more than 2–3% of potential downside before you would normally sell the shares anyway. Canadian investors can execute this strategy inside a TFSA or RRSP without triggering immediate tax consequences on the premium received. The delta of the $170 call is typically around 0.25, meaning the position behaves like 75 shares of long stock plus 25 shares of short stock for risk purposes. Time decay works in the seller’s favor, with roughly two-thirds of the premium eroding in the final two weeks if the stock remains range-bound. Protective puts function differently: buying a $150 put for $2.50 creates a collar when paired with a covered call, capping both downside below $147.50 and upside above $170. The key difference from naked options is that covered strategies require owning the underlying shares or cash to secure the put, which limits leverage and margin calls. Investor Education: Technical Analysis Fundamentals for Modern Investors

Suppose you bought shares of a TSX energy name at the open on October 19 expecting a rebound after the inflation print; the stock instead fell through its 50-day moving average by midday and kept sliding. The 50-day and 200-day moving averages act as dynamic support and resistance levels that many algorithms and institutional desks watch, so a break often triggers further selling from systematic strategies. RSI on the 14-day setting below 30 would have flagged oversold conditions, yet volume above the 20-day average would have been needed to confirm any bounce attempt. A pro habit is to mark the prior day’s low as the first support level and set an alert there rather than guessing direction. The common misconception is treating a moving-average cross as a guaranteed reversal signal; the fix is to require volume confirmation and a second indicator such as RSI before acting on the cross. Moving averages smooth price action over a chosen period so the 50-day line reflects the average closing price of the prior 50 sessions while the 200-day line captures a longer-term trend. When price trades above both averages the security is considered in an uptrend, but a sustained break below the 50-day often signals momentum loss that can accelerate if volume rises. RSI measures the speed and change of price movements on a 0-to-100 scale, with readings below 30 indicating the asset may be oversold and due for a technical rebound provided volume supports the move. MACD tracks the relationship between two exponential moving averages and generates buy or sell signals when its lines cross, yet these signals gain reliability only when accompanied by rising volume that shows genuine participation rather than low-conviction noise. Chart patterns such as double bottoms or flags can provide statistical edges for entry timing when they form at known support levels, but they fail more often than not without confirming volume and a clear invalidation point below the pattern low. The practical edge comes from using these tools together to time entries and exits rather than to predict overall direction, which remains the domain of fundamental analysis.
